# AUTOMOBILE POWER PLANT

## Module 1: ENGINES

### Topic: Valve and Valve Mechanism Systems - OHV, OHC, DOHC

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're going to dive into a crucial part of our internal combustion engines: the **valve and valve mechanism systems**. You know, engines are essentially air pumps, right? They need to breathe in fuel-air mixture and then exhale exhaust gases. And how do they do that? Through valves! So, understanding how these valves operate is fundamental to understanding the entire engine. This topic directly relates to our **Course Outcome 1 (CO1)**: "Understand constructional details and working of various internal combustion engine." We'll be looking at the "constructional details" of these valve systems and how they "work" to manage the engine's breathing.

Think of an engine's cylinder like a room. For the engine to run efficiently, this room needs to be perfectly sealed when the combustion happens. But it also needs precisely timed openings to let in the fresh fuel-air charge and let out the burnt gases. These openings are controlled by valves. And the systems that control these valves are what we call the **valve mechanism systems**.

Over the years, engineers have come up with different ways to design these systems, each with its own advantages and complexities. We'll focus on the three most common configurations: **Overhead Valve (OHV)**, **Overhead Camshaft (OHC)**, and **Double Overhead Camshaft (DOHC)**.

### 1. The Basic Function of Valves

Before we get into the different types, let's quickly recap what valves do. In a typical four-stroke engine, we have at least two valves per cylinder:

*   **Intake Valve:** This valve opens to allow the fuel-air mixture (in petrol engines) or just air (in diesel engines) to enter the cylinder during the intake stroke.
*   **Exhaust Valve:** This valve opens to allow the burnt exhaust gases to be pushed out of the cylinder during the exhaust stroke.

These valves are essentially spring-loaded poppet valves. Imagine a mushroom shape: the flat head acts as the sealing surface, and the stem is what gets pushed to open it. The valve seat, machined into the cylinder head, provides a perfect mating surface for the valve head to seal against. The **valve spring** is a powerful coil spring that keeps the valve tightly closed when it's not being actuated.

### 2. Valve Mechanism Systems: The Evolution and Types

The key difference between OHV, OHC, and DOHC lies in the **location of the camshaft** and **how it operates the valves**. The camshaft itself is a rotating shaft with lobes (cams) that push against components to open the valves. The efficiency and complexity of the mechanism depend heavily on this camshaft placement.

#### 2.1. Overhead Valve (OHV) Engine

Let's start with the classic design, the OHV. The name itself tells us a lot: the valves are located **overhead**, meaning they are in the cylinder head, directly above the combustion chamber. However, the **camshaft is located in the engine block**.

*   **How it works:** The camshaft, driven by the crankshaft (usually via a timing chain or gear), has lobes that push against **lifters** (also called tappets). These lifters ride on the cam lobes and move upwards. The lifters are connected to **pushrods**, which are long metal rods. These pushrods extend up to the cylinder head and push against one end of a **rocker arm**. The rocker arm pivots on a shaft or stud, and its other end pushes down on the valve stem, opening the valve. When the cam lobe rotates away, the valve spring closes the valve.

*   **Visualizing OHV:** Imagine a lever system. The camshaft is at the bottom (in the block), the rocker arm is like the fulcrum, and the pushrod is the effort arm pushing down on the valve. This entire system adds a bit of mechanical linkage between the camshaft and the valve.

*   **Advantages of OHV:**
    *   **Simpler and Cheaper to Manufacture:** With the camshaft in the block, the cylinder head design can be simpler, and the overall engine can be more compact, especially in terms of height. This was a significant advantage in earlier automotive designs.
    *   **Good Low-End Torque:** The pushrod design generally allows for simpler valve timing, which can be optimized for good torque at lower engine speeds.
    *   **Durability:** The pushrod system can be quite robust.

*   **Disadvantages of OHV:**
    *   **More Moving Parts:** The presence of lifters, pushrods, and rocker arms means more components, more friction, and more potential for wear and play.
    *   **Limited RPM Capability:** The extra mass of these components (pushrods and rocker arms) can lead to valve float at higher engine speeds. Valve float is when the valve doesn't close quickly enough due to inertia, causing it to bounce off its seat, disrupting combustion and potentially causing damage. Think of trying to clap your hands very fast; eventually, your hands can't move fast enough to keep up.
    *   **Less Efficient Breathing:** The indirect valve actuation can limit the maximum valve lift and duration, impacting the engine's ability to "breathe" efficiently at high RPMs.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Ganesan. V, in his book "Internal Combustion Engines," often discusses OHV as a foundational design, highlighting its mechanical simplicity and historical significance. Ramalingam K.K. also details the pushrod mechanism, emphasizing the forces involved.

*   **Real-world connection:** Many older American V8 engines, like those found in classic muscle cars and trucks, are famous for their OHV design. They produce great torque for towing or cruising.

#### 2.2. Overhead Camshaft (OHC) Engine

Now, let's move to the OHC design. Here, the **camshaft is located directly in the cylinder head**, right above the valves. This is a significant change from OHV.

*   **How it works:** The camshaft, driven by the crankshaft via a timing belt, timing chain, or gears, has lobes that directly push down on the valves. In its simplest OHC form, the cam lobes might push directly on the valve stem (or sometimes a small cap on the stem). Alternatively, the cam lobe might push on a **bucket tappet** (also called a shim-under-bucket or shim-over-bucket tappet), which then directly acts on the valve stem. This eliminates the need for pushrods and rocker arms in the traditional sense.

*   **Visualizing OHC:** Imagine the camshaft sitting directly on top of the valves. The cam lobe's shape directly dictates when and how much the valve opens. It's a much more direct connection.

*   **Advantages of OHC:**
    *   **Fewer Parts, Less Inertia:** By eliminating pushrods and rocker arms, there are fewer moving parts. This reduces mechanical friction and the overall mass that needs to be accelerated and decelerated, allowing for higher engine speeds (RPMs).
    *   **Better High-RPM Performance:** The direct valve actuation provides more precise valve control at high speeds, leading to better volumetric efficiency (the engine's ability to fill its cylinders with air and fuel) and thus better power output at higher RPMs.
    *   **Simpler Valve Adjustment:** In some OHC designs (especially shim-over-bucket), adjusting valve clearance can be simpler, as you're often just swapping shims.

*   **Disadvantages of OHC:**
    *   **More Complex Cylinder Head:** Placing the camshaft in the head makes the cylinder head design more complex and potentially wider, especially for V-engines where a camshaft is needed for each bank of cylinders.
    *   **Camshaft Drive Complexity:** Driving the camshaft from the crankshaft requires a timing belt, chain, or gears, which adds to the complexity and maintenance requirements.
    *   **Cost:** Generally more expensive to manufacture than OHV.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Rajput's "Internal Combustion Engines" likely details the OHC layout, emphasizing the direct action of the cam on the valve or tappet, and discussing the role of timing belts/chains. Heywood's "Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals" would provide in-depth analysis of the kinematic advantages and limitations.

*   **Real-world connection:** Most modern petrol engines, especially those in Japanese and European cars, use OHC designs. They offer a good balance of performance, fuel economy, and emissions. Think of your typical family car's engine; it's very likely OHC.

#### 2.3. Double Overhead Camshaft (DOHC) Engine

The DOHC is a refinement of the OHC concept. As the name suggests, it features **two camshafts per cylinder head**.

*   **How it works:** In a DOHC engine, one camshaft is typically dedicated to operating the **intake valves**, and the other camshaft is dedicated to operating the **exhaust valves**. Both camshafts are located overhead in the cylinder head. The cam lobes directly (or via bucket tappets) actuate their respective valves.

*   **Visualizing DOHC:** Imagine two rows of cams sitting above the valves. One set opens the intake valves, and the other set opens the exhaust valves. This allows for independent control over the intake and exhaust valve timing.

*   **Advantages of DOHC:**
    *   **Optimal Valve Control:** Having separate camshafts for intake and exhaust allows for more flexibility in designing the valve timing, lift, and duration for both intake and exhaust events. This means engineers can optimize the engine's breathing for peak performance across a wider RPM range.
    *   **Higher Power Output:** The precise control over valve events, combined with the ability to use more valves per cylinder (e.g., four valves per cylinder – two intake, two exhaust), significantly improves volumetric efficiency and allows for higher power output.
    *   **Efficient Breathing at High RPM:** The reduced inertia and direct actuation contribute to excellent high-RPM performance and responsiveness.
    *   **Enables Multi-Valve Designs:** DOHC is the preferred layout for engines with more than two valves per cylinder (e.g., 4 valves or even 5 valves per cylinder), which are common in high-performance engines.

*   **Disadvantages of DOHC:**
    *   **Increased Complexity and Cost:** Having two camshafts, their driving mechanisms, and the associated valvetrain components makes the cylinder head design the most complex and expensive of the three.
    *   **Wider Cylinder Head:** For V-engines, this means two camshafts per cylinder bank, further increasing the width of the engine.
    *   **More Critical Timing:** The precise synchronization of two camshafts requires robust timing systems.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Mathur & Sharma's "A course in internal combustion engines" would likely compare DOHC with OHC, highlighting the benefits of independent valve control. Heywood's text would delve into the thermodynamic advantages of multi-valve cylinder heads enabled by DOHC.

*   **Real-world connection:** High-performance sports cars, racing engines, and many modern, fuel-efficient passenger car engines utilize DOHC. Think of the engines in many modern hatchbacks, sedans, and performance vehicles – they almost universally use DOHC, often with 4 or 5 valves per cylinder.

### 4. Important Considerations and Exam Focus

When you're studying this, keep these points in mind for your exams:

*   **Comparison:** Be ready to compare and contrast OHV, OHC, and DOHC engines based on their camshaft location, components, advantages, and disadvantages. A comparative table is often a good way to summarize this.
*   **Diagrams:** Understanding how to sketch or interpret diagrams of these systems is crucial. Know where the camshaft, lifters, pushrods, rocker arms, and valves are located in each type.
*   **Terminology:** Make sure you can define and explain terms like valve float, volumetric efficiency, timing chain/belt, tappet, rocker arm, and pushrod.
*   **Application:** Understand why an engineer might choose one design over another. For example, OHV for simplicity and low-end torque, OHC for a balance of performance and cost, and DOHC for high-performance applications.
*   **Evolution:** Think of it as an evolution of design, moving from the simpler OHV to the more sophisticated OHC and DOHC for better performance and efficiency.

**Remember this:** The fundamental goal is to control the flow of gases into and out of the cylinder at the right time. The differences in OHV, OHC, and DOHC are primarily about *how* that control is achieved and the engineering trade-offs involved.

### 5. Summary of Key Differences

To reinforce, let's quickly summarize the core distinctions:

| Feature            | OHV (Overhead Valve)                      | OHC (Overhead Camshaft)                   | DOHC (Double Overhead Camshaft)             |
| :----------------- | :---------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------ |
| **Camshaft Location** | In Engine Block                           | In Cylinder Head                          | In Cylinder Head (Two per head)             |
| **Valve Actuation** | Cam -> Lifter -> Pushrod -> Rocker Arm -> Valve | Cam -> Bucket Tappet/Directly -> Valve    | Cam (Intake) -> Tappet -> Intake Valve; Cam (Exhaust) -> Tappet -> Exhaust Valve |
| **Key Components** | Pushrods, Rocker Arms, Lifters            | Bucket Tappets, (No pushrods/rockers)     | Two Camshafts, Bucket Tappets               |
| **Complexity**     | Relatively Simple                         | Moderate                                  | High                                        |
| **RPM Limit**      | Lower                                     | Higher                                    | Highest                                     |
| **Cost**           | Lower                                     | Moderate                                  | Higher                                      |
| **Primary Use**    | Older engines, trucks, some modern V8s    | Many modern passenger cars                | Performance cars, racing, modern efficient cars |

This covers the essential valve and valve mechanism systems. Mastering these concepts will give you a solid foundation for understanding engine operation.

## Sample Questions with Answers

**Q1. Explain the working principle of an OHV engine and list its main advantages and disadvantages.**

**Answer:**
An OHV (Overhead Valve) engine has its valves located in the cylinder head, but the camshaft is positioned within the engine block. The crankshaft drives the camshaft via a timing chain or gears. The rotating camshaft lobes push against lifters (tappets), which in turn move pushrods. These pushrods transfer the motion to rocker arms in the cylinder head. The rocker arms pivot, and their opposite end presses down on the valve stem, opening the valve against the force of the valve spring. When the cam lobe rotates away, the valve spring closes the valve.

**Advantages of OHV:**
*   **Simplicity and Cost:** Generally simpler to design and manufacture, leading to lower production costs.
*   **Compactness:** Can result in a shorter and lighter engine, especially in terms of cylinder head height.
*   **Good Low-End Torque:** The mechanism can be tuned for good torque characteristics at lower engine speeds.

**Disadvantages of OHV:**
*   **Limited RPM Capability:** The extra mass of pushrods and rocker arms can lead to valve float at high engine speeds.
*   **More Friction:** The additional components (lifters, pushrods, rocker arms) introduce more friction.
*   **Less Efficient Breathing:** The indirect valve actuation can limit valve lift and duration, impacting performance at high RPMs.

**Q2. What is the primary difference between OHC and DOHC engine configurations?**

**Answer:**
The primary difference lies in the **number of camshafts** located in the cylinder head.
*   An **OHC (Overhead Camshaft)** engine has **one camshaft** per cylinder head, which operates either the intake valves or the exhaust valves (or both if it's a shared design).
*   A **DOHC (Double Overhead Camshaft)** engine has **two camshafts** per cylinder head. Typically, one camshaft is dedicated to operating the intake valves, and the other is dedicated to operating the exhaust valves. This allows for more precise and independent control over the intake and exhaust valve events.

**Q3. Why does DOHC configuration generally allow for higher engine speeds and better performance compared to OHV?**

**Answer:**
DOHC engines allow for higher engine speeds and better performance primarily because:
1.  **Reduced Inertia:** By eliminating pushrods and rocker arms (inherent in OHV systems) and having camshafts directly overhead, the moving parts in the valvetrain have less mass and inertia. This allows the valves to open and close more rapidly and accurately at high RPMs without "valve float" (where the valve doesn't seat properly due to inertia).
2.  **Direct Valve Control:** The direct action of the cam lobe (or a simple tappet) on the valve stem provides more precise control over valve timing, lift, and duration.
3.  **Multi-Valve Potential:** DOHC is ideally suited for multi-valve cylinder heads (e.g., 4 or 5 valves per cylinder). More valves improve the engine's ability to "breathe" (volumetric efficiency) by allowing more air-fuel mixture into the cylinder and more exhaust gases out, which is crucial for high-RPM power output.

**Q4. Define "valve float" and explain in which engine configuration it is most likely to occur and why.**

**Answer:**
**Valve float** is a phenomenon where the inertia of the valve train components (like valves, springs, pushrods, rocker arms) becomes greater than the force exerted by the valve spring at very high engine speeds. This causes the valve to not close completely and rapidly against its seat. Instead, it may remain slightly open or bounce, leading to erratic valve operation, loss of compression, poor combustion, and potential damage to the engine.

Valve float is most likely to occur in **OHV (Overhead Valve)** engines. This is because the OHV design uses a longer and heavier valvetrain mechanism involving lifters, pushrods, and rocker arms. The additional mass and linkages in this system have higher inertia compared to the more direct actuation in OHC and DOHC designs. As engine speed increases, the momentum of these parts can overcome the closing force of the valve springs, resulting in valve float.
