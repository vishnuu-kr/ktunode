# AUTOMOBILE POWER PLANT: MODULE 2: FUEL SUPPLY SYSTEM

## Topic: The Fuel Tank – The Foundation of Our Engine's Lifeblood

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into the very beginning of our engine's journey – the fuel supply system. And at the heart of this system, quite literally holding the energy for our automobile's power plant, is the **fuel tank**. Think of it as the reservoir, the vital storage unit that keeps our engine running. Without a properly functioning fuel tank, well, the engine simply wouldn't get the fuel it needs to create that explosive power we rely on.

This topic might seem straightforward, but understanding its construction, purpose, and integral role is crucial for grasping how the entire fuel system operates. It directly connects to our **Course Outcome 1 (CO1)** by helping us understand the "constructional details and working of various internal combustion engines" by looking at one of its fundamental support systems. Furthermore, it lays the groundwork for **CO2**, which focuses on discussing the fuel system and air induction system. We can't discuss the fuel system without first understanding where the fuel itself is stored and delivered from.

### What is a Fuel Tank? Its Fundamental Role

At its simplest, the fuel tank is a container designed to safely store liquid fuel, typically gasoline or diesel, for use by the internal combustion engine. It's not just a simple metal box; it's a carefully engineered component that must meet several critical requirements.

*   **Storage:** Its primary function is to hold a significant quantity of fuel, providing a reserve for extended driving.
*   **Safety:** It must be designed to contain the fuel securely, preventing leaks, evaporation, and fire hazards. This is paramount, given the flammable nature of fuel.
*   **Delivery:** It needs to facilitate the delivery of fuel to the engine via the fuel pump, ensuring a consistent and clean supply.
*   **Ventilation:** It requires a system to manage pressure fluctuations caused by fuel consumption and temperature changes, preventing tank collapse or over-pressurization.

### Design and Construction: More Than Just a Container

When we look at the fuel tank, especially in modern vehicles, we'll see it’s made from specific materials and has various internal and external features.

**Materials of Construction:**

Historically, fuel tanks were made of steel. Steel is robust and relatively inexpensive. However, it's susceptible to corrosion, especially from water that can condense inside the tank or from external environmental factors. If you've ever seen an old car with rust flakes in the fuel filter, it’s often due to a decaying steel tank.

*   **Steel Tanks:** As mentioned, these are common in older vehicles. They are often coated internally to prevent rust. (Refer to Ganesan, V., Chapter 3 on Fuel Systems, for details on historical materials).
*   **Plastic (Polyethylene) Tanks:** Today, most new vehicles use plastic fuel tanks, typically made from high-density polyethylene (HDPE). Why the shift?
    *   **Corrosion Resistance:** Plastic doesn't rust! This is a massive advantage for longevity and preventing fuel contamination.
    *   **Weight Savings:** Plastic is significantly lighter than steel, contributing to better fuel economy.
    *   **Design Flexibility:** Plastic tanks can be molded into complex shapes, allowing manufacturers to utilize space more efficiently within the vehicle's chassis. Think about how the tank might fit around the exhaust system or the drive shaft – plastic makes this easier.
    *   **Impact Resistance:** Modern plastics are designed to be impact-resistant, offering good safety in the event of a collision.

The manufacturing process for plastic tanks often involves blow molding, where a molten plastic tube (a "parison") is inflated with air inside a mold to form the tank's shape.

**Key Internal and External Features:**

Let's think about what's *inside* and *attached* to the fuel tank that makes it work effectively.

1.  **Fuel Filler Neck:** This is the opening where you pour fuel. It's designed with specific diameters to prevent misfuelling (e.g., a smaller opening for unleaded petrol to prevent diesel being pumped in) and often has a flap or cap mechanism to seal it.

2.  **Fuel Pump and Sender Unit Assembly:** This is a crucial component, usually housed inside the tank.
    *   **Fuel Pump:** This electric pump draws fuel from the tank and pushes it towards the engine. In modern vehicles, it’s almost always an in-tank electric pump, which is quieter and more efficient. Older vehicles might have had mechanical pumps mounted externally. (Ganesan and Ramalingam K.K. both detail these different pump types).
    *   **Fuel Sender Unit:** This unit measures the fuel level in the tank and sends this information to the fuel gauge on your dashboard. It typically consists of a float connected to a variable resistor. As the fuel level changes, the float moves up or down, altering the resistance and thus the signal to the gauge. It's like a simple lever system with an electrical component. This directly relates to our understanding of how vehicle systems communicate information.

3.  **Baffles:** These are internal walls or partitions within the tank. They are not just randomly placed; they serve important purposes:
    *   **Preventing Fuel Sloshing:** When you accelerate, brake, or turn, the fuel inside the tank can slosh around. Baffles help to dampen this movement, ensuring a stable fuel supply to the pump and preventing the fuel pickup from being momentarily uncovered. Imagine trying to pour water from a bucket while on a rollercoaster – it would be all over the place! Baffles act like internal dividers to keep the fuel relatively still, even when the car is moving.
    *   **Stabilizing the Vehicle:** By reducing the shifting mass of the fuel, baffles contribute to better vehicle stability, especially during cornering.

4.  **Fuel Pickup (Strainer):** This is where the fuel pump draws fuel from. It's usually located at the lowest point of the tank and is fitted with a strainer or filter to prevent larger debris from entering the fuel pump and subsequently the engine's fuel lines. Think of it as the first line of defense against dirt.

5.  **Ventilation System (Breather System):** This is a critical safety and performance feature.
    *   **Purpose:** As fuel is consumed, a vacuum would form inside the tank, potentially causing it to collapse or making it difficult for the fuel pump to draw fuel. Conversely, changes in ambient temperature can cause pressure to build up. The vent system allows air to enter the tank as fuel is used and allows excess pressure to escape.
    *   **Evaporative Emission Control (EVAP) System:** In modern vehicles, this system is tightly integrated with the fuel tank's ventilation. Instead of venting fuel vapors directly to the atmosphere (which causes pollution), these vapors are captured and stored, usually in a charcoal canister. Later, they are routed back into the engine to be burned. This is a key aspect of emission control, linking our topic to **CO3**. The fuel tank is a significant source of evaporative emissions.

    The components of a typical EVAP system include:
    *   **Vent Valve:** Controls the flow of vapors from the tank to the charcoal canister.
    *   **Charcoal Canister:** Absorbs fuel vapors.
    *   **Purge Valve:** Opens to allow stored vapors to be drawn into the engine for combustion.

    (Heywood's "Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals" provides extensive detail on EVAP systems, as they are crucial for modern emissions standards.)

### Fuel Tank Location and Mounting

The fuel tank is typically located at the rear of the vehicle, underneath the passenger compartment. This placement is chosen for several reasons:

*   **Weight Distribution:** It helps balance the weight of the engine, which is usually at the front.
*   **Safety:** It provides some protection in a rear-end collision.
*   **Space Utilization:** It allows for more passenger and cargo space within the cabin.

The tank is securely mounted to the vehicle's chassis using strong straps or brackets, often with rubber isolators to reduce vibration transmitted to the vehicle body.

### Operational Considerations and Maintenance

*   **Fuel Quality:** The type of fuel stored in the tank directly impacts engine performance and longevity. Using the correct octane rating and fuel type (petrol vs. diesel) is essential.
*   **Keeping it Full:** While not strictly about the tank's construction, it's worth noting that running a fuel tank completely empty can sometimes draw sediment from the bottom of the tank into the fuel system. It's generally good practice to refuel before the "low fuel" light comes on, especially in older vehicles.
*   **Evaporative Emissions:** The EVAP system is vital. If there's a leak in the fuel tank or the EVAP system hoses, the "Check Engine" light might illuminate due to a fault code detected by the vehicle's computer. This often means a loose gas cap, a cracked hose, or a faulty vent valve. This is a practical example of how the fuel tank system integrates with the vehicle's overall diagnostics.

## Sample Questions and Answers

**1. Conceptual Question:** Explain the primary reasons why modern vehicles predominantly use plastic fuel tanks over steel ones.

**Answer:** Modern vehicles prefer plastic (polyethylene) fuel tanks primarily due to their **corrosion resistance**, which prevents rust and fuel contamination, leading to greater durability. Secondly, plastic tanks offer significant **weight savings** compared to steel, contributing to improved fuel economy. Lastly, their **design flexibility** allows for intricate shapes, enabling manufacturers to maximize space utilization within the vehicle chassis, a benefit not as easily achieved with traditional steel tanks.

**2. Exam-Oriented Question:** Describe the function of baffles within a fuel tank and their importance for vehicle operation.

**Answer:** Baffles are internal partitions within a fuel tank. Their primary function is to **reduce fuel sloshing** during vehicle maneuvers such as acceleration, braking, and cornering. This is crucial for two main reasons:
    *   **Consistent Fuel Supply:** They ensure that the fuel pickup, where the fuel pump draws fuel, remains submerged even when the fuel level is low or the vehicle is subjected to dynamic forces. This prevents fuel starvation to the engine.
    *   **Vehicle Stability:** By minimizing the shifting mass of the fuel, baffles contribute to better vehicle handling and stability, especially during turns.

**3. Application-Based Question:** If a vehicle's "Check Engine" light illuminates and the diagnostic code points to an EVAP system leak, what are the potential issues related to the fuel tank that a mechanic might investigate?

**Answer:** A "Check Engine" light indicating an EVAP system leak, with roots potentially in the fuel tank, suggests that fuel vapors are escaping into the atmosphere or not being properly contained. A mechanic would investigate:
    *   **Fuel Tank Cap:** Ensuring it is properly tightened or that the seal is intact. A loose or damaged cap is the most common cause.
    *   **Fuel Tank Material Integrity:** Checking for cracks or damage to the plastic tank itself, especially around mounting points or areas exposed to road debris.
    *   **EVAP System Hoses and Connections:** Inspecting all hoses running from the fuel tank to the charcoal canister and purge valve for cracks, disconnections, or blockages.
    *   **Vent Valve/Canister:** While not directly part of the tank, these are critical for the system's operation and are closely linked to the tank's venting.

**4. Conceptual Question (Relating to CO3):** How does the fuel tank contribute to controlling emissions in an automobile?

**Answer:** The fuel tank is a significant source of **evaporative emissions**, particularly volatile organic compounds (VOCs) released from the fuel. In modern vehicles, the fuel tank is integrated with an **Evaporative Emission Control (EVAP) system**. This system captures fuel vapors that would otherwise escape from the tank through ventilation. These captured vapors are stored in a charcoal canister and later directed back into the engine's intake manifold to be burned during normal operation. Thus, the fuel tank's design and its connection to the EVAP system play a vital role in reducing hydrocarbon emissions into the atmosphere.
