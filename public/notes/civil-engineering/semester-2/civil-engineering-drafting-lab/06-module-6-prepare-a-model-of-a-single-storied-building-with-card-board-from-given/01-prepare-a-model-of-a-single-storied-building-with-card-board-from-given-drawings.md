# CIVIL ENGINEERING DRAFTING LAB
## MODULE 6: PREPARE A MODEL OF A SINGLE STOREYED BUILDING WITH CARDBOARD FROM GIVEN DRAWINGS
### TOPIC: PREPARE A MODEL OF A SINGLE STOREYED BUILDING WITH CARDBOARD FROM GIVEN DRAWINGS (NOT EXPECTED TO COMPLETE IN THE LAB HOURS)

Welcome, everyone, to Module 6 of our Civil Engineering Drafting Lab! This module is a fantastic opportunity to bridge the gap between the abstract world of architectural drawings and the tangible reality of a built structure. We’re going to take those plans, elevations, and sections you’ve been meticulously drafting and bring them to life, albeit in miniature form, using cardboard.

Now, before we dive into the practicalities of cutting and pasting, let's understand *why* we're doing this.

### 1. Connecting with Course Outcomes

This activity directly supports several of our key course outcomes:

*   **CO1: Illustrate ability to organize civil engineering drawings systematically and professionally (K2)**
    While you've already learned to organize drawings, building a model requires you to **interpret** and **translate** that organized information into a physical form. You'll see how the plan, elevations, and sections all relate to each other to define the complete building. It reinforces the idea that drawings are not isolated pieces but a cohesive representation of a building. Think of it as seeing the blueprint come together in 3D!

*   **CO2: Illustrate the detailing of building components like doors, windows, roof trusses etc. (K2)**
    This is where the real fun is! As you cut out and assemble doors and windows, you’ll appreciate the scale and placement shown in your drawings. You’ll have to think about how a door opening is represented in elevation and how it translates to a cut-out in your model. The same applies to windows and even the subtle details of roof construction. You'll be essentially recreating these details in a physical sense.

*   **CO3: Develop the sketch of plan, front elevation and sectional elevation from line diagram. (K3)**
    Although this module starts with *given* drawings, the process of creating the model forces you to **understand** those drawings at a deeper level. You’ll be mentally (and physically!) deconstructing the building. This process reinforces your ability to visualize the building from its 2D representations. You'll see how a line on a plan becomes a wall, how an opening in an elevation becomes a void, and how a section cut reveals internal structure.

*   **CO4: Draft the plan, elevation and sectional views of the residential buildings, industrial buildings, and framed structures using software. (K3)**
    While this module focuses on cardboard models and not software drafting, the underlying principle is the same: **visualizing and representing a building**. The skills you hone here – understanding spatial relationships, component detailing, and overall form – are directly transferable to your software drafting. A good model builder often makes a good software drafter because they possess a strong spatial understanding.

### 2. The Foundation: Understanding the Drawings

Before we pick up a craft knife, the most crucial step is a thorough understanding of the given drawings. This is not a rushed process; it requires careful study. You'll typically be provided with:

*   **Plan:** This is the bird's-eye view, looking down on the building as if sliced horizontally at sill level (or sometimes lintel level). It shows the layout of rooms, walls, doors, windows, and other features in plan.
    *   **Analogy:** Imagine looking down at a cake from above, seeing all the layers and fillings. The plan shows you the building's "floor plan."
    *   **From our textbooks:** Both "Building Drawing and Detailing" by Dr. Balagopal T.S. Prabhu and "Building Planning and Drawing" by M.V. Chitawadagi S.S. Bhavikatti emphasize the importance of the plan as the primary document for understanding the building's layout and dimensions. They'll detail how wall thicknesses, door/window locations, and room dimensions are crucial elements from the plan.

*   **Elevations:** These are the views of the building from the outside, as if you were standing directly in front of each face (Front Elevation, Rear Elevation, Side Elevations). They show the external appearance, including the shape of the roof, the placement and design of doors and windows, and the overall height.
    *   **Analogy:** Think of the exterior pictures you see in real estate brochures – those are essentially elevations. They show you how the building looks from the street.
    *   **Reference:** The National Building Code of India (NBC) and local building rules (Kerala Panchayat Building Rules, Kerala Municipality Building Rules) often dictate specific requirements for how external features are represented in elevations, particularly regarding setbacks, floor heights, and architectural features. IS 962:1989 also provides guidelines for architectural drawings, including elevations.

*   **Sections:** These views show the building as if it were cut by a vertical plane. They reveal the internal structure, the relationship between floors, the thickness of walls and slabs, and the construction of elements like the roof.
    *   **Analogy:** Imagine cutting a sandwich in half. The section shows you all the layers inside – the bread, the filling, the cheese – in their relative positions.
    *   **From our textbooks:** Shah, M.G., Kale, C. M. and Patki, S.Y.'s "Building Drawing With An Integrated Approach to Built Environment" is particularly good at explaining how sectional views complement plans and elevations to provide a complete understanding of the building's construction and internal arrangement.

### 3. Materials and Tools: Your 3D Toolkit

For this project, we'll be using common and readily available materials:

*   **Cardboard:** This is our primary building material. You’ll want to use reasonably stiff cardboard. Corrugated cardboard (like from shipping boxes) is excellent for walls and structural elements. Thinner cardstock or Bristol board can be useful for finer details like window panes or roof shingles.
*   **Drawing Paper/Tracing Paper:** For accurately transferring dimensions from your drawings to the cardboard.
*   **Cutting Tools:**
    *   **Craft Knife/X-Acto Knife:** Essential for precise cutting. Always use a cutting mat to protect your surface and the blade. *Safety first! Always cut away from yourself.*
    *   **Scissors:** Useful for larger, straighter cuts, but a knife offers more control for intricate work.
*   **Adhesives:**
    *   **White Glue/PVA Glue:** Great for general bonding. Apply thinly to avoid warping the cardboard.
    *   **Hot Glue Gun:** Provides a quick, strong bond, but be cautious as it can melt thinner cardstock and requires care to avoid burns.
*   **Measuring Tools:**
    *   **Ruler/Scale:** Absolutely vital for transferring dimensions accurately.
*   **Marking Tools:**
    *   **Pencil:** For marking cut lines and assembly points.
    *   **Pen:** For finer detail lines on the model if desired.
*   **Optional but Recommended:**
    *   **Cutting Mat:** Protects your work surface and your knife blade.
    *   **Metal Ruler:** Provides a straight edge for guiding your craft knife.
    *   **Tweezers:** For handling small pieces.

### 4. The Process: From 2D Lines to 3D Form

This isn't a race to finish; it's about understanding the translation. Remember, this is *not expected to be completed in lab hours*. The lab session will focus on understanding the process and perhaps starting the initial steps.

Here's a general approach, which you’ll adapt based on your specific drawings:

#### Step 1: Deconstruct the Drawings and Plan Your Cuts

*   **Scale it Down:** Your drawings are usually to scale (e.g., 1:50, 1:100). You’ll need to decide on a scale for your model. A common scale for cardboard models is 1:50 or even 1:25 if you want more detail. Ensure your chosen scale is consistent across all components.
*   **Identify Components:** Break down the building into its constituent parts:
    *   Foundation (optional for a simple cardboard model, but good to consider the base)
    *   Walls (internal and external)
    *   Doors and Windows (including frames and openings)
    *   Roof (structure, covering)
    *   Floor slabs/Ground floor
*   **Layout Your Cuts:**
    *   **Walls:** You’ll likely cut out individual wall sections from cardboard. Think about how the plan dictates the length and height of each wall. From the elevation, you get the height. The plan gives you the length. You'll need to combine this information.
        *   **Example:** Let's say your plan shows an external wall that is 10 meters long, and the front elevation shows the wall height to the eaves is 3 meters. If your model scale is 1:50, then on your cardboard, this wall would be 10m / 50 = 0.20 meters (or 20 cm) long, and 3m / 50 = 0.06 meters (or 6 cm) high. You'd then cut out a rectangle of 20 cm x 6 cm.
    *   **Doors and Windows:** These are usually cut-outs in the walls. You'll need to measure their exact dimensions from the elevations or plan and transfer these to the wall pieces *before* assembly.
        *   **Remember this:** The sill height (height of the bottom of the window from the floor) is crucial and is usually indicated in the elevation. You'll need to measure up from the base of your wall piece to mark where the window starts.
    *   **Roof:** The roof can be the trickiest part. You’ll need to visualize its shape from the elevations and sections. Often, you’ll construct the roof in pieces – for a simple gable roof, two sloping planes meeting at a ridge.
        *   **Textbook Reference:** "Building Drawing and Detailing" by Dr. Balagopal T.S. Prabhu often has detailed diagrams of various roof types, which will be invaluable for understanding how to construct them from flat pieces.

#### Step 2: Transferring Dimensions and Cutting

*   **Accurate Transfer:** Lay your drawing paper over the cardboard (or trace from your drawing onto paper, then onto cardboard). Carefully measure and mark all your cut lines, door/window openings, and any other details using your ruler and pencil.
*   **Precision is Key:** Use your craft knife with a metal ruler as a guide for all straight cuts. Take your time. Jagged edges will make assembly difficult and the final model look messy.
    *   **Exam Tip:** Poor cutting precision is a common pitfall. It not only affects the appearance but also the accuracy of assembly. Think of it as a drafting exercise – clean lines and accurate dimensions are paramount.

#### Step 3: Assembling the Components

*   **Start with the Base:** Often, it's best to have a base (e.g., a piece of cardboard representing the ground floor slab) to which you can attach the walls.
*   **Build Up:**
    *   **Walls:** Glue the wall pieces together at the corners. You might need to create small tabs or use internal corner supports (small strips of cardboard glued inside) for added strength.
    *   **Doors and Windows:** Once the walls are dry, you can add simplified door frames and window frames from thin strips of cardboard. The openings you cut earlier will allow these to be fitted.
    *   **Roof:** Assemble the roof structure separately and then attach it to the top of the walls.

#### Step 4: Adding Details and Finishing Touches

*   **Details:** Add any finer details if time and skill permit – window panes (using tracing paper or thin plastic), door handles (small paper shapes), roof textures, etc.
*   **Labeling:** You might even want to label different rooms or components, referencing the original drawings.

### 5. Important Considerations & Pitfalls to Avoid

*   **Scale Consistency:** As mentioned, maintaining the chosen scale throughout is non-negotiable. A 1:50 scale for walls and a 1:20 scale for a window will result in a distorted and incorrect model.
*   **Wall Thickness:** Decide if your model will represent the actual wall thickness or if it will be a simplified representation with thin walls. For cardboard models, often thin walls are used, but the *internal dimensions* and *opening placements* must still reflect the drawing's intent. If the drawing shows a wall of 300mm thickness, and your model walls are only 2mm thick cardboard, you need to be consistent about whether that 2mm represents the center-line, the outer face, or is just a simplified representation.
*   **Openings Alignment:** Ensure that door and window openings align correctly between adjacent walls. This is where studying the plan and elevations together becomes critical.
*   **Roof Pitch and Overhangs:** Pay close attention to the roof slope (pitch) and any overhangs shown in the elevations and sections. These define the building's silhouette.
*   **Structural Integrity:** While it's a model, it should hold together. Use glue sparingly but effectively. Reinforce corners or weak points if necessary.
*   **Time Management:** This is a project that requires patience and time. Don't rush the cutting or gluing.
