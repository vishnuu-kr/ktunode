# Engineering Graphics and Computer-Aided Drawing: Module 3 - Sections of Solids

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into a crucial aspect of engineering graphics: **Sections of Solids**. This module is all about understanding how to reveal the internal structure of 3D objects by cutting them with imaginary planes. Think of it like slicing a cake to see what's inside – that’s precisely what we do in technical drawings, but with much more precision and purpose. This skill is fundamental for visualizing, designing, and manufacturing complex parts, and it directly ties into our Course Outcome 3 (CO3): "Plot sectional views and develop surfaces of a given object."

## 1. Introduction to Sections of Solids

Why do we need sections? Imagine you have a solid block of metal, say a gear or a housing for an electronic component. To understand its internal features – like holes, cavities, or how different parts fit together – simply looking at the outside isn't enough. Orthographic projections (front, top, side views) give us external information. Sectional views, however, cut through the object to expose the hidden details. This is vital for manufacturing because it tells machinists exactly where to cut or drill.

**What is a Sectional View?**
A sectional view is a view of an object as seen after it has been cut by a **section plane**. The part of the object between the observer and the section plane is removed. The cut surface is then shown as a filled area, typically with **section lines** or **hatching lines**. These lines indicate the material that has been cut.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Section Plane:** An imaginary plane that cuts through the solid. The **position and inclination** of this plane are critical.
*   **Cutting Plane Line:** In orthographic projections, the section plane is represented by a **thick line with short dashes and a gap**, with arrows at the ends indicating the direction of view. We'll use this to show *where* the cut is made.
*   **Section Line (Hatching Line):** Thin lines drawn at an angle (usually 45 degrees) on the cut surface. They help to distinguish the cut surface from other visible surfaces. The spacing and direction of these lines are important. For different adjacent parts, the direction of hatching might be reversed.
*   **True Shape of the Section:** The actual shape of the surface revealed by the section plane. We often need to project this shape in its true size and form.

Remember the textbooks like Varghese (VIP Publishers) and Benjamin (Pentex Publishers)? They emphasize that understanding the relationship between the section plane and the object's geometry is paramount.

## 2. Types of Section Planes and Their Effects

The nature of the section depends entirely on the orientation of the section plane relative to the object. For this module, we'll focus on solids with their axes in the vertical position, which simplifies things considerably. The common types of section planes we'll encounter are:

*   **Perpendicular to the Axis:**
    *   **Horizontal Plane:** Cuts the solid parallel to the base. This is very common for prisms, pyramids, cones, and cylinders.
    *   **Vertical Plane (Perpendicular to Horizontal Plane):** Cuts the solid vertically. This can be parallel to a face or diagonal.
*   **Inclined to the Axis:** The section plane makes an angle with the axis of the solid.
*   **Perpendicular to the Axis:** The section plane is at right angles to the axis of the solid.

Let's explore how these apply to specific solids. This is where we directly address CO3, as we're plotting sectional views.

## 3. Sections of Specific Solids (Axis in Vertical Position)

We will systematically look at how different section planes interact with the basic solids: Tetrahedron, Prisms, Pyramids, Cone, and Cylinder.

### 3.1 Sections of a Tetrahedron

A tetrahedron is the simplest polyhedron, having four triangular faces, six edges, and four vertices. Imagine a pyramid with a triangular base.

**Scenario:** A tetrahedron resting on its base in the HP.

*   **Horizontal Section Plane (Parallel to the Base):** If you slice a tetrahedron parallel to its base, what shape do you expect to see? It will be a triangle, similar in shape to the base, but smaller. This is a direct application of geometric similarity. Think of slicing a pointed party hat parallel to its base – you get a smaller circle. For a tetrahedron, you get a smaller triangle.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Perpendicular to the Base):** A vertical plane cutting through a tetrahedron can produce different shapes depending on its position. If it passes through an edge of the base and the opposite vertex (the apex), it will cut the tetrahedron into two halves, revealing a triangular section. If it passes through the apex and cuts through the base, the section will be a triangle. If it cuts through two opposite edges, the section shape would be a rectangle or trapezoid.
*   **Inclined Section Plane:** This is where things get a bit more complex. An inclined plane will typically produce a section that is a triangle, quadrilateral, or pentagon, depending on how many faces and edges it intersects.

**Exam Tip:** For simple cases like a horizontal cut parallel to the base, finding the true shape is straightforward – it's the same as the projection of the cut on the HP. For inclined planes, you might need to project points onto an auxiliary plane to find the true shape.

### 3.2 Sections of Prisms

Prisms have two identical bases (polygons) connected by rectangular faces. Think of a Toblerone box (triangular prism) or a standard building block (rectangular prism).

**Scenario:** A prism resting on its base in the HP.

*   **Horizontal Section Plane (Perpendicular to the Axis):** If you cut a prism with a plane parallel to its base (and thus perpendicular to its vertical axis), the section revealed will be identical in shape and size to its base. Whether it's a triangular prism, square prism, or hexagonal prism, a horizontal cut will yield a triangle, square, or hexagon, respectively. This is a fundamental concept that relates to the definition of a prism itself.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Parallel to a Side Face):** A vertical plane parallel to one of the rectangular side faces will produce a rectangular section. The dimensions of this rectangle will depend on the height of the prism and the distance of the plane from the side face.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Through Opposite Edges/Vertices):** A vertical plane passing through opposite vertical edges or vertices of the bases will reveal a rectangular section (if the base is a rectangle) or a section that's a rectangle or trapezoid depending on the base shape.
*   **Inclined Section Plane:** An inclined plane will result in a section that is a quadrilateral (trapezoid or parallelogram) or even a pentagon or hexagon, depending on how many edges and faces it cuts.

**Relating to CO3:** Plotting these sections involves projecting points where the cutting plane intersects the edges of the prism. The resulting sectional view will show the true shape of the cut surface.

### 3.3 Sections of Pyramids

Pyramids have a base (a polygon) and triangular faces that meet at a single point called the apex. Think of the pyramids of Giza.

**Scenario:** A pyramid resting on its base in the HP.

*   **Horizontal Section Plane (Perpendicular to the Axis):** This is a classic. When you cut a pyramid with a plane parallel to its base, the section shape is similar to the base, but it shrinks as you move up towards the apex. So, a horizontal cut through a square pyramid will produce a smaller square. A horizontal cut through a triangular pyramid will produce a smaller triangle. The size of the section depends on its distance from the apex. The further up you cut, the smaller the section. This is a direct illustration of the principle of similar figures.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Through the Apex):** If a vertical plane passes through the apex and cuts the base, the section revealed will be a triangle. This triangle will be isosceles if the plane passes through the midpoint of opposite base sides.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Not through the Apex):** A vertical plane not passing through the apex but cutting through the pyramid will produce a trapezoidal or triangular section, depending on whether it cuts through opposite base edges or just one base edge and the pyramid's faces.
*   **Inclined Section Plane:** Similar to prisms, inclined planes can produce a variety of shapes: triangles, quadrilaterals, or pentagons.

**Everyday Analogy:** Imagine slicing a carrot. If you slice it parallel to the base (the flat end), you get a smaller circle. If you slice it through the pointy tip and down through the base, you get a triangle. The shape changes as the carrot tapers.

### 3.4 Sections of a Cone

A cone has a circular base and a curved surface that tapers to an apex. Think of an ice-cream cone or a traffic cone.

**Scenario:** A cone resting on its circular base in the HP.

*   **Horizontal Section Plane (Perpendicular to the Axis):** This is the most common and important case for cones. A plane parallel to the base cuts the cone to reveal a **circle**. The size of the circle decreases as the plane moves closer to the apex. This is a fundamental property of cones.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Through the Apex):** A vertical plane passing through the apex of the cone will cut the cone along two opposite generator lines. The section revealed is a **triangle**. This triangle will be isosceles.
*   **Inclined Section Plane:** This is where we get the famous **conic sections**:
    *   If the plane is inclined and cuts through *both* the base and the curved surface, but not parallel to the base, and not through the apex, it results in an **ellipse**.
    *   If the plane is inclined and cuts through the curved surface and is parallel to *one* generator line, it produces a **parabola**.
    *   If the plane is inclined and cuts through the curved surface, making a steeper angle than a generator line, it results in a **hyperbola**. (Though often, we just deal with ellipses and parabolas in introductory engineering graphics).

**Connection to CO3:** Understanding how these different plane orientations produce circles, triangles, ellipses, and parabolas is a direct application of plotting sectional views. The true shape of these conic sections needs to be projected, often using auxiliary planes or by determining the intersection points on the cone's generators.

### 3.5 Sections of a Cylinder

A cylinder has two identical circular bases connected by a curved surface. Think of a can of soup or a pipe.

**Scenario:** A cylinder resting on its base in the HP.

*   **Horizontal Section Plane (Perpendicular to the Axis):** A plane parallel to the base cuts the cylinder to reveal a **circle**, identical in size to the base. This is because a cylinder has a constant radius along its length.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Parallel to the Axis):** A vertical plane parallel to the axis (and thus parallel to the curved surface) will cut the cylinder to reveal a **rectangle**. The height of the rectangle will be the height of the cylinder, and its width will depend on how far the plane is from the axis.
*   **Vertical Section Plane (Through the Axis):** A vertical plane passing through the axis of the cylinder will reveal the largest possible rectangular section, with the width equal to the diameter of the cylinder's base.
*   **Inclined Section Plane:** An inclined plane will cut the cylinder to produce an **ellipse**. The shape of the ellipse depends on the angle of inclination of the plane. If the plane is inclined but still perpendicular to the front view's plane (meaning it's inclined in the top view), it creates an elliptical section.

**Relating to CO3:** The ability to draw these sections – circles, rectangles, and ellipses – by accurately projecting the intersection points is a core skill.

## 4. Determining the True Shape of the Section

Often, the sectional view shown on the primary projection plane (like the front view or top view) is foreshortened. To get the **true shape** of the section, we need to project the cutting points onto a plane that is parallel to the section plane. This auxiliary plane is placed in a convenient position.

**General Procedure to find the True Shape:**

1.  **Identify the Section Plane:** Determine its orientation (horizontal, vertical, inclined).
2.  **Draw the Orthographic Projections:** Show the solid in its Front View (FV) and Top View (TV).
3.  **Draw the Cutting Plane Line:** Represent the section plane on the relevant view (usually TV for horizontal cuts, FV for vertical cuts). Add arrows to indicate the direction of view.
4.  **Locate Intersection Points:** Mark the points where the cutting plane line intersects the visible and hidden edges/generators of the solid in the orthographic views.
5.  **Draw the Sectional View:** In the view where the cutting plane is seen as an edge (e.g., FV for horizontal cuts), draw the section by connecting the projected intersection points. Hatch the area.
6.  **Determine True Shape:**
    *   If the section plane is **parallel to a principal plane** (e.g., horizontal plane cutting a vertical axis solid), the section shown in the corresponding orthographic view (e.g., top view) is already the true shape.
    *   If the section plane is **inclined**, you need to construct an **auxiliary view**. Draw an auxiliary plane parallel to the inclined section plane. Project the intersection points onto this auxiliary plane. The resulting projection will be the true shape of the section.

**Example:** Imagine cutting a cone with a plane inclined to the base but not parallel to any generator. In the Top View, the cutting plane is a line. In the Front View, this line appears foreshortened. To get the true shape of the resulting ellipse, you would draw an auxiliary plane parallel to the inclined cutting plane, projecting points from the FV onto this auxiliary plane.

## 5. Hatching (Section Lines)

The way we hatch the sectioned area is also important.

*   **General Hatching:** For most common sections, thin lines drawn at 45 degrees to the outline of the section are used. The spacing is uniform and usually around 1-2 mm.
*   **Adjacent Parts:** If the drawing shows a complex object with multiple parts assembled, adjacent parts are hatched in opposite directions. This clearly distinguishes the parts.
*   **Thin Sections:** For very thin sections (like sheet metal parts), solid black hatching might be used.
*   **Large Sections:** If the sectioned area is very large, hatching might be done along the edges only.

**Textbook References:** Books like Kulkarni, Rastogi, and Sarkar (Engineering Graphics with AutoCAD) and Venugopal (Engineering Drawing & Graphics) provide excellent examples of correct hatching techniques and their application in AutoCAD. This links to CO5 – using CAD tools to create these precise drawings.

## 7. Exam Focus and Common Pitfalls

*   **True Shape is Key:** Most exam problems will ask for the *true shape* of the section, not just the section as seen in the front or top view. So, mastering auxiliary views is crucial.
*   **Accurate Point Projection:** The accuracy of your sectional view depends on accurately locating the intersection points where the cutting plane crosses the solid's edges.
*   **Understanding Plane Orientation:** Be very clear about the relationship between the section plane and the solid's axis. A slight misinterpretation can lead to the wrong shape.
*   **Hatching Convention:** Always use correct hatching for clarity and to meet drawing standards.
*   **Solids with Hidden Lines:** When drawing the sectional view, the parts behind the section plane are removed. However, edges or outlines of the solid that are still visible *beyond* the section plane (in the direction of the arrow) are drawn as continuous thin lines. Hidden lines are generally not shown in sectional views unless they are essential for clarity.

**Remember this:** Sectioning is about revealing what's inside. The cutting plane defines the boundary of what you see, and the resulting shape tells a story about the solid's geometry.

## Sample Questions with Answers

**Q1: A square pyramid is cut by a plane parallel to its base. What is the shape of the section?**

**Answer:** The shape of the section will be a square, similar to the base but smaller in size. This is because the pyramid tapers uniformly towards the apex, and a plane parallel to the base preserves the base's shape proportionally. This relates to geometric similarity.

**Q2: When a cone is cut by a plane passing through its apex and perpendicular to the base, what section is obtained?**

**Answer:** A triangle. Specifically, an isosceles triangle, as the plane passes through the apex and cuts through the curved surface and the base along two opposite generator lines. This demonstrates how a plane through the apex of a tapering solid produces a triangular section.

**Q3: What is the shape of the section obtained when a cylinder is cut by a plane inclined to the axis and intersecting the curved surface?**

**Answer:** An ellipse. This is a classic example of a conic section. The degree of elongation of the ellipse depends on the angle of inclination of the plane. If the plane were parallel to the axis, it would be a rectangle. If it were perpendicular to the axis (parallel to the base), it would be a circle. The inclined cut creates the elliptical shape. This directly tests the understanding of different plane orientations on a cylinder.

**Q4: A square prism is cut by a plane parallel to its base. State the shape of the section and explain how its true shape is determined.**

**Answer:**
*   **Shape of the section:** A square, identical in shape and size to the base of the prism.
*   **Determination of true shape:** Since the cutting plane is parallel to the base, and the prism's axis is vertical, the section as viewed in the Top View (where the base is projected) is already the true shape of the section. No auxiliary view is needed. The sectional view would be drawn in the Front View or Side View, showing the square cut face filled with hatching.

**Q5: Describe the process to find the true shape of a section when a cone is cut by a plane inclined to its axis and cutting the cone in a parabolic shape.**

**Answer:**
To obtain a parabolic section from a cone with a plane inclined to the axis, the plane must be parallel to one of the cone's generators.
1.  **Orthographic Views:** Draw the Front View (FV) and Top View (TV) of the cone resting on its base.
2.  **Cutting Plane:** In the TV, draw the cutting plane as a line parallel to one of the slant edges (generators) of the cone. In the FV, this cutting plane line will appear foreshortened.
3.  **Locate Points:** Identify the points where this cutting plane line intersects the cone's boundary in the TV and FV. For the parabolic section, these points lie on the generators in the TV.
4.  **Auxiliary View:** Construct an auxiliary plane parallel to the inclined cutting plane. The position of this auxiliary plane is often represented by an XY line parallel to the cutting plane line in the TV.
5.  **Project Points:** Project the intersection points from the FV onto this auxiliary plane. The distance of each point from the respective XY line in the FV is transferred perpendicular to the new auxiliary XY line. For a parabolic section, you'd typically pick points along the generators in the TV and project them.
6.  **Draw the Section:** Connect the projected points on the auxiliary plane to form the true shape of the section, which will be a parabola. Hatch this area.

This question tests the ability to visualize the 3D cut and apply the auxiliary projection method accurately, which is a critical exam component for CO3.
