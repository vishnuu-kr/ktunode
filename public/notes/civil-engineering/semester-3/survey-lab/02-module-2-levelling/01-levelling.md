# Survey Lab: Module 2 - Levelling

## Introduction to Levelling

Levelling is the process of determining the difference in elevation between two or more points. It is a fundamental surveying technique used to establish vertical control and create topographic maps.

### Key Concepts:

*   **Elevation:** The vertical distance of a point above or below a reference datum.
*   **Datum:** A surface of zero elevation, commonly the mean sea level.
*   **Vertical Control:** A network of points with known elevations.
*   **Difference in Elevation:** The vertical distance between two points.

---

## 2.1 Levelling Instruments

### 2.1.1 Levelling Instruments and Accessories

Levelling instruments are designed to establish a horizontal line of sight, allowing for the measurement of vertical distances.

#### **Types of Levelling Instruments:**

*   **Dumpy Level:** A traditional levelling instrument where the telescope is fixed rigidly to the vertical spindle. Adjustment for levelness is done by levelling screws.
    *   **Description:** Simple to use, robust, but requires careful levelling.
    *   **Components:** Telescope, leveling bubble, leveling screws, vertical axis.
*   **Tilting Level:** The telescope can be tilted slightly for fine adjustments, allowing for quicker setups.
    *   **Description:** Faster to use than dumpy levels, offers greater precision in some applications.
    *   **Components:** Telescope, leveling bubble, leveling screws, fine tilting screw.
*   **Automatic (Compensator) Level:** Features an internal compensator that automatically keeps the line of sight horizontal within a certain range, even if the instrument is not perfectly leveled.
    *   **Description:** Most common and convenient type. Requires minimal user intervention for levelling.
    *   **Components:** Telescope, internal compensator, leveling screws.
*   **Digital Level:** Incorporates electronic sensors to read digital levelling staffs.
    *   **Description:** Highly efficient, reduces reading errors, provides data in digital format.
    *   **Components:** Telescope, digital sensor, onboard data storage.

#### **Accessories:**

*   **Levelling Staff (Rods):** Graduated rods used to measure vertical distances.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Invar Staffs:** Made of Invar metal, which has a very low coefficient of thermal expansion, making them ideal for precise levelling.
        *   **Wooden or Aluminium Staffs:** Standard staffs for general levelling work.
        *   **Folding Staffs:** Collapsible staffs for portability.
        *   **Guitars:** Used for automatic levels to provide a coded reading.
    *   **Markings:** Typically marked in metres, decimetres, and centimetres. Zero point is at the bottom.
*   **Levelling Tripod:** A sturdy tripod used to support the levelling instrument, ensuring stability.
*   **Plumb Bob:** Used for centering the instrument over a station point. (Less common with modern automatic levels where the instrument is placed on the tripod).
*   **Ranging Rods:** Used to indicate turning points or stations when sighting distances are long.

### 2.1.2 Setting Up and Using a Levelling Instrument

The process of setting up and using a levelling instrument involves placing it on a stable tripod and ensuring the line of sight is perfectly horizontal.

#### **Steps for Setting Up and Using (General for most levels):**

1.  **Select a Location:** Choose a stable and suitable location for the instrument, considering the points to be observed.
2.  **Mount on Tripod:** Securely attach the levelling instrument to the tripod head.
3.  **Center Over Station:** If using a dumpy or tilting level, use the plumb bob (or optical plummet in the telescope) to center the instrument directly over the station point.
4.  **Rough Levelling:** Extend the tripod legs to roughly level the instrument.
5.  **Fine Levelling (Dumpy/Tilting Levels):**
    *   Adjust the levelling screws to bring the bubble in the circular level to the center.
    *   Rotate the instrument 90 degrees. Adjust levelling screws again to centre the bubble.
    *   Rotate 180 degrees. The bubble should remain centered.
    *   Rotate another 90 degrees. The bubble should remain centered.
    *   Repeat until the bubble remains centered in all directions.
6.  **Fine Levelling (Automatic Levels):**
    *   Adjust the levelling screws until the bubble in the circular level is within the marked circle. The compensator will then automatically make the line of sight horizontal.
7.  **Focus the Telescope:** Focus on the levelling staff.
8.  **Take Readings:** Read the staff where the horizontal crosshair intersects it.

## 2.2 Levelling Procedures

### 2.2.1 Different Methods of Levelling

Several methods are employed in levelling, each suited for different scenarios and precision requirements.

#### **Methods of Levelling:**

*   **Differential Levelling:**
    *   **Description:** The most common method. Used to determine the difference in elevation between two points by taking fore-sight and back-sight readings from one or more instrument setups.
    *   **Applications:** Establishing ground profiles, setting out levels for construction.
*   **Profile Levelling:**
    *   **Description:** Used to determine the elevations of a series of points along a straight line or curved path. Typically involves taking readings at regular intervals along the path.
    *   **Applications:** Road design, pipeline routes, canal alignments.
*   **Cross-Section Levelling:**
    *   **Description:** Used to determine the elevations of points across a line, usually at regular intervals along a profile line.
    *   **Applications:** Earthwork calculations for road construction, embankment design.
*   **Trigonometric Levelling:**
    *   **Description:** Determines the difference in elevation between two points using vertical angles and horizontal distances. Requires a theodolite or total station.
    *   **Applications:** When direct levelling is impractical due to large distances or obstructions.
*   **Contouring:**
    *   **Description:** The process of mapping lines that connect points of equal elevation on the ground surface.
    *   **Applications:** Creating topographic maps, understanding terrain features.

### 2.2.2 Balancing Sight Lengths

Balancing sight lengths is a crucial technique in differential levelling to minimize errors introduced by instrument imperfections and atmospheric refraction.

#### **Concept:**

*   **Backsight (BS):** A reading taken on a levelling staff held on a point of known or previously established elevation. It is taken *back* towards the instrument from a new station.
*   **Foresight (FS):** A reading taken on a levelling staff held on a point whose elevation is to be determined. It is taken *forward* from the instrument.
*   **Turning Point (TP):** A point where the instrument is moved. A backsight is taken from the previous setup, and a foresight is taken to the new setup from the same turning point.
*   **Sight Length:** The distance between the instrument and the levelling staff.

#### **Procedure:**

*   The length of the backsight and foresight from any single instrument setup should be kept as nearly equal as possible.
*   **Reasoning:**
    *   **Instrument Error (Collimation Error):** If the line of sight is not perfectly horizontal, the error introduced is proportional to the sight length. Equalizing sight lengths cancels out this error.
    *   **Earth Curvature and Refraction:** Earth's curvature causes the true horizontal line to curve downwards, making staff readings slightly lower than they should be. Atmospheric refraction bends light rays upwards, making staff readings slightly higher. These effects are also dependent on distance, and balancing sights minimizes their combined impact.

#### **Example Scenario:**

Imagine setting up the instrument between Point A (known elevation) and Point B (unknown elevation).

1.  **Setup 1:**
    *   Place instrument between A and B.
    *   Hold staff on A (Backsight).
    *   Hold staff on a temporary point (Turning Point 1) (Foresight).
    *   *Aim to make the distance from the instrument to A roughly equal to the distance from the instrument to TP1.*
2.  **Setup 2:**
    *   Move instrument to a new location.
    *   Hold staff on TP1 (Backsight).
    *   Hold staff on B (Foresight).
    *   *Aim to make the distance from the instrument to TP1 roughly equal to the distance from the instrument to B.*

**Important Note:** While balancing sight lengths is ideal, sometimes it's not perfectly achievable due to site constraints. In such cases, it's more critical to minimize the *difference* between BS and FS lengths.

### 2.2.3 Recording and Calculating Levelling Data

Accurate recording and calculation are essential for obtaining reliable elevation differences.

#### **Levelling Field Book (Example Format):**

| Station | BS     | IS     | FS     | Rise   | Fall   | Elevation | Remarks        |
| :------ | :----- | :----- | :----- | :----- | :----- | :-------- | :------------- |
| A       | 1.520  |        |        |        |        | 100.000   | Starting BM    |
| TP1     | 1.200  | 2.450  | 1.800  | 0.650  |        | 100.650   | Turn Point 1   |
| B       |        | 1.950  | 2.100  |        | 0.150  | 100.500   | End Point      |
|         |        |        |        |        |        |           |                |
| **SUM** | **2.720** |        | **3.900** | **0.650** | **0.150** |           |                |
| **Check** |        |        |        |           |        |           | **Sum BS - Sum FS = Sum Rise - Sum Fall** |

**Explanation of Columns:**

*   **Station:** The name or number of the point where the staff is held.
*   **BS (Backsight):** Reading taken on the staff at the beginning of a sight (station of known elevation or a turning point).
*   **IS (Intermediate Sight):** Readings taken on the staff between the backsight and foresight, used for intermediate points or when the staff is not moved between instrument setups.
*   **FS (Foresight):** Reading taken on the staff at the end of a sight (station of unknown elevation or a turning point).
*   **Rise:** The difference between a BS/FS and the previous reading on the same setup, where the BS/FS is *less* than the previous reading (staff reading decreased, elevation increased). Calculated as `BS - FS` or `FS - BS` (if the latter is smaller).
*   **Fall:** The difference between a BS/FS and the previous reading on the same setup, where the BS/FS is *greater* than the previous reading (staff reading increased, elevation decreased). Calculated as `BS - FS` or `FS - BS` (if the former is smaller).
*   **Elevation:** The calculated elevation of the station.
*   **Remarks:** Notes about the station (e.g., Bench Mark, Turning Point, change of ground).

#### **Calculations:**

*   **Rise/Fall Method:**
    *   For each setup, calculate the difference between consecutive staff readings.
    *   If the second reading is *less* than the first, the difference is "Rise".
    *   If the second reading is *greater* than the first, the difference is "Fall".
    *   **Elevation of next point = Elevation of previous point + Rise - Fall**
*   **Height of Instrument (HI) Method:**
    *   **HI = Elevation of Station + BS** (for each setup)
    *   **Elevation of next point = HI - FS** (or HI - IS)

#### **Checks:**

*   **Sum of BS - Sum of FS = Sum of Rise - Sum of Fall:** This is a fundamental arithmetic check for differential levelling.
*   **Sum of Elevations Calculated (Rise/Fall Method) = Sum of Elevations Calculated (HI Method):** This further verifies the calculations.
*   **Check on Turning Points:** Ensure that the calculated elevation of a turning point from one setup matches its calculated elevation from the next setup.

## 2.3 Levelling Calculations and Adjustments

### 2.3.1 Calculation of Elevations

This section focuses on the practical application of the methods learned for calculating elevations.

#### **Rise and Fall Method Calculation Example:**

Let's continue with the previous example:

*   **Setup 1:**
    *   Station A: Elevation = 100.000 m
    *   BS on A = 1.520 m
    *   FS on TP1 = 1.800 m
    *   Reading on A (1.520) < Reading on TP1 (1.800) => **Fall**
    *   Fall = 1.800 - 1.520 = 0.280 m (This is incorrect in the previous table; the fall should be calculated between consecutive readings at the same setup, not BS and FS directly unless there's only one reading after BS)

    Let's re-do the example for clarity:

    | Station | BS     | IS     | FS     | Rise   | Fall   | Elevation | Remarks        |
    | :------ | :----- | :----- | :----- | :----- | :----- | :-------- | :------------- |
    | A       | 1.520  |        |        |        |        | 100.000   | Starting BM    |
    | TP1     |        | 2.450  | 1.800  | 0.650  |        | 100.650   | Turn Point 1   |
    | B       | 1.200  | 1.950  | 2.100  |        | 0.150  | 100.500   | End Point      |
    | **SUM** | **2.720** |        | **3.900** | **0.650** | **0.150** |           |                |
    | **Check** |        |        |        |           |        |           | **2.720 - 3.900 = -1.180; 0.650 - 0.150 = 0.500. There is an error in the table provided in the previous section. Let's correct the calculation and explanation.** |

    **Corrected Rise/Fall Calculation Logic:**

    *   **From Station A to TP1 (Setup 1):**
        *   Reading on A (BS) = 1.520
        *   Reading on TP1 (FS) = 1.800
        *   Since the second reading (1.800) is *greater* than the first (1.520), there is a **Fall**.
        *   Fall = 1.800 - 1.520 = 0.280 m
        *   Elevation of TP1 = Elevation of A - Fall = 100.000 - 0.280 = 99.720 m

    *   **From TP1 to B (Setup 2):**
        *   Reading on TP1 (BS) = 1.200
        *   Reading on B (FS) = 2.100
        *   Since the second reading (2.100) is *greater* than the first (1.200), there is a **Fall**.
        *   Fall = 2.100 - 1.200 = 0.900 m
        *   Elevation of B = Elevation of TP1 - Fall = 99.720 - 0.900 = 98.820 m

    **Let's present this in a corrected table format for the Rise/Fall method:**

    | Station | BS     | IS     | FS     | Rise   | Fall   | Elevation | Remarks        |
    | :------ | :----- | :----- | :----- | :----- | :----- | :-------- | :------------- |
    | A       | 1.520  |        |        |        |        | 100.000   | Starting BM    |
    | TP1     |        |        | 1.800  |        | 0.280  | 99.720    | Turn Point 1   |
    | TP1     | 1.200  |        |        |        |        | 99.720    | BS on TP1      |
    | B       |        |        | 2.100  |        | 0.900  | 98.820    | End Point      |
    | **SUM** | **2.720** |        | **3.900** | **0.000** | **1.180** |           |                |
    | **Check** |        |        |        |           |        |           | **Sum BS - Sum FS = 2.720 - 3.900 = -1.180. Sum Rise - Sum Fall = 0.000 - 1.180 = -1.180. The check passes.** |

#### **HI Method Calculation Example:**

Using the same data:

*   **Setup 1:**
    *   Station A: Elevation = 100.000 m
    *   BS on A = 1.520 m
    *   HI = Elevation of A + BS on A = 100.000 + 1.520 = 101.520 m
    *   FS on TP1 = 1.800 m
    *   Elevation of TP1 = HI - FS on TP1 = 101.520 - 1.800 = 99.720 m

*   **Setup 2:**
    *   Station TP1: Elevation = 99.720 m (from previous calculation)
    *   BS on TP1 = 1.200 m
    *   HI = Elevation of TP1 + BS on TP1 = 99.720 + 1.200 = 100.920 m
    *   FS on B = 2.100 m
    *   Elevation of B = HI - FS on B = 100.920 - 2.100 = 98.820 m

**HI Method Field Book Format:**

| Station | BS     | IS     | FS     | HI      | Elevation | Remarks        |
| :------ | :----- | :----- | :----- | :------ | :-------- | :------------- |
| A       | 1.520  |        |        | 101.520 | 100.000   | Starting BM    |
| TP1     |        |        | 1.800  |         | 99.720    | Turn Point 1   |
| TP1     | 1.200  |        |        | 100.920 | 99.720    | BS on TP1      |
| B       |        |        | 2.100  |         | 98.820    | End Point      |

### 2.3.2 Error Sources and Adjustments in Levelling

Understanding potential errors and how to mitigate them is crucial for accurate levelling.

#### **Common Error Sources:**

*   **Instrumental Errors:**
    *   **Collimation Error:** The line of sight is not perfectly horizontal. (Minimized by balancing sights).
    *   **Improper Levelling:** Instrument not truly leveled. (Careful setup minimizes this).
    *   **Faulty Levelling Bubble:** Bubble not calibrated.
*   **Natural Errors:**
    *   **Earth Curvature:** Affects readings over longer distances.
    *   **Atmospheric Refraction:** Bending of light rays in the atmosphere. (Both effects are generally minimized by balancing sight lengths).
    *   **Temperature Variations:** Affect instrument calibration and staff markings (especially for Invar staffs).
*   **Personal Errors:**
    *   **Improper Staff Holding:** Staff not held vertically.
    *   **Parallax:** Improper focusing of the telescope.
    *   **Reading Error:** Incorrectly reading the staff graduations.
    *   **Poor Turning Point Stability:** Staff base not firm.

#### **Types of Adjustments:**

*   **Field Adjustments:**
    *   **Balancing Sight Lengths:** As discussed, crucial for minimizing instrumental and natural errors.
    *   **Proper Staff Holding:** Using a plumb bob with the staff or ensuring it's held vertically.
    *   **Stable Setup:** Ensuring the tripod is firm and the instrument is well-leveled.
*   **Office Adjustments (Least Squares Method):**
    *   **Purpose:** When levelling loops are closed (e.g., starting and ending at the same benchmark, or closing a traverse), discrepancies (errors) are inevitable. Least squares adjustment distributes these errors proportionally to minimize their impact.
    *   **Process:**
        1.  **Identify the Discrepancy:** `Discrepancy = Sum of FS - Sum of BS` (or vice-versa depending on convention). This should ideally be zero.
        2.  **Calculate the Correction:** The correction for each reading is usually applied to the total difference in elevation (or to the HI).
        3.  **Distribute Corrections:**
            *   For **equal sight lengths**, corrections can be applied equally to all readings.
            *   For **unequal sight lengths**, corrections are often applied proportionally to the distance of each sight from the instrument.

#### **Example of Correction Application (Simplified):**

Suppose a loop levelling resulted in a total calculated difference of +0.050 m (meaning the final elevation is 0.050 m higher than it should be based on the starting benchmark).

*   **Correction per station:** If there were 10 stations, the correction per station would be `-0.050 m / 10 stations = -0.005 m/station`.
*   **Applying the correction:** Every calculated elevation would have -0.005 m added to it to close the loop accurately.

**More precisely, corrections are applied to the intermediate or final calculated elevations based on the cumulative length of sights.**

## 2.4 Levelling Applications

### 2.4.1 Establishing Benchmarks and Vertical Control

Benchmarks are fundamental to establishing a network of known elevations.

#### **Benchmarks (BMs):**

*   **Definition:** A relatively permanent point whose elevation is known with high accuracy.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Permanent Benchmarks:** Often carved into concrete structures, bridge abutments, or stone markers.
    *   **Temporary Benchmarks:** Established for the duration of a specific project.
*   **Hierarchy:** Benchmarks are often established in a hierarchical manner, with primary benchmarks having the highest accuracy, from which secondary and tertiary benchmarks are derived.
*   **Purpose:** To provide reliable starting points for levelling surveys.

#### **Establishing Vertical Control:**

*   **Process:** Involves a network of levelling runs connecting known benchmarks or establishing new ones.
*   **Levelling Loops:** Running levelling surveys that start and end at the same benchmark or connect two known benchmarks. This allows for the detection and adjustment of errors.
*   **Trig Levelling:** Using trigonometric principles to establish elevations when direct levelling is not feasible.
*   **Importance:** Vertical control is essential for:
    *   **Topographic Mapping:** Creating contours.
    *   **Engineering Design:** Setting out road gradients, drainage levels, building foundations.
    *   **Construction:** Ensuring structures are built to the correct elevations.

### 2.4.2 Levelling for Construction and Engineering Projects

Levelling plays a critical role throughout the lifecycle of construction and engineering projects.

#### **Applications:**

*   **Setting Out Gradients:** For roads, railways, drainage channels, and pipelines to ensure proper flow.
*   **Foundation Levels:** Establishing the correct depth and elevation for building foundations.
*   **Volume Calculations:** Determining earthwork quantities (cut and fill) for excavation and embankment construction.
*   **Monitoring Structures:** Checking for settlement or heave in bridges, buildings, or dams over time.
*   **Water Supply and Drainage Systems:** Ensuring correct elevations for pipe slopes and water flow.
*   **Earthwork Measurement:** Calculating the volume of material to be moved. This involves taking levels before and after excavation/embankment.

#### **Example: Earthwork Volume Calculation**

1.  **Pre-construction Levelling:** Take a series of levels across the site (profile and cross-sections) to establish the existing ground surface elevations.
2.  **Design Levelling:** Determine the desired finished surface elevations based on the project design.
3.  **Calculate Average Depth of Cut/Fill:** For a given area, the difference between the finished elevation and the existing ground elevation at various points is calculated. These differences can be averaged or processed using methods like grid squaring or numerical integration to determine the volume of material to be cut or filled.
    *   **Volume of Cut = Area × Average Depth of Cut**
    *   **Volume of Fill = Area × Average Depth of Fill**

## Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
What is the primary purpose of balancing sight lengths in differential levelling?

**Answer:**
The primary purpose of balancing sight lengths is to minimize errors caused by instrument collimation error, earth curvature, and atmospheric refraction. By making the backsight and foresight distances roughly equal from any instrument setup, these errors, which are proportional to distance, tend to cancel each other out.

**Question 2:**
You are performing differential levelling. The reading on the levelling staff held at a Bench Mark (BM) of elevation 150.000 m is 1.250 m. The reading on the staff at the next point (TP1) is 1.850 m. Calculate the elevation of TP1 using the HI method.

**Answer:**
*   **Given:**
    *   Elevation of BM = 150.000 m
    *   Backsight (BS) on BM = 1.250 m
    *   Foresight (FS) on TP1 = 1.850 m
*   **Calculation:**
    *   Height of Instrument (HI) = Elevation of BM + BS on BM
    *   HI = 150.000 m + 1.250 m = 151.250 m
    *   Elevation of TP1 = HI - FS on TP1
    *   Elevation of TP1 = 151.250 m - 1.850 m = 149.400 m

**Question 4:**
List three sources of natural errors in levelling and how they can be mitigated.

**Answer:**
Three sources of natural errors in levelling are:

1.  **Earth Curvature:** The Earth's surface is curved, meaning the true horizontal line of sight deviates from the tangent at the instrument.
    *   **Mitigation:** Balancing sight lengths. The effect is proportional to the square of the distance, so keeping BS and FS lengths equal minimizes the differential effect.
2.  **Atmospheric Refraction:** The bending of light rays as they pass through layers of air with different densities.
    *   **Mitigation:** Balancing sight lengths. Similar to earth curvature, this effect is distance-dependent. Also, avoiding levelling during periods of extreme temperature gradients (e.g., midday sun).
3.  **Temperature Variations:** Affecting the expansion or contraction of the levelling instrument's components and the levelling staff.
    *   **Mitigation:** Using instruments and staffs made of materials with low thermal expansion (like Invar for staffs). Allowing instruments to acclimatize to ambient temperature. Regularly calibrating equipment.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Levelling is about vertical measurements.**
*   **Always use a stable tripod and ensure the instrument is well-leveled.**
*   **Balance sight lengths (BS and FS) to minimize errors.**
*   **The zero of the levelling staff is at the bottom.**
*   **Backsight (BS) is taken on a point of known elevation.**
*   **Foresight (FS) is taken on a point of unknown elevation.**
*   **Intermediate Sights (IS) are taken between BS and FS.**
*   **Check your arithmetic religiously!** The `Sum BS - Sum FS = Sum Rise - Sum Fall` check is vital.
*   **Understand the difference between the Rise/Fall method and the HI method.** Both should yield the same results.
*   **Benchmarks (BMs) are your reference points.**
*   **Precision in levelling is crucial for engineering and construction.**
