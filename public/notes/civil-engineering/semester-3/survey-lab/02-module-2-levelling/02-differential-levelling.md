### **Introduction to Differential Levelling**

Differential levelling is a fundamental surveying technique used to determine the **difference in elevation** between two or more points. It is a precise method for establishing vertical control and is crucial for a wide range of surveying applications, including:

*   **Construction:** Setting out building foundations, roads, bridges, and other infrastructure.
*   **Topographic Mapping:** Creating contour maps and understanding the terrain.
*   **Water Management:** Designing drainage systems, canals, and reservoirs.
*   **Geodetic Control:** Establishing precise height networks.

The core principle of differential levelling is to use a **level instrument** and a **levelling staff** to measure the vertical distances from a known horizontal line of sight.

### **Key Concepts and Definitions**

#### **1. Levelling Instrument (Level)**

*   **Definition:** An optical instrument that provides a perfectly horizontal line of sight when properly adjusted.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Dumpy Level:** A simpler type where the telescope is fixed perpendicular to the vertical axis.
    *   **Tilting Level:** Allows the telescope to be tilted slightly to achieve a precise horizontal line of sight.
    *   **Automatic/Self-Leveling Level:** Uses a compensator (pendulum or prism system) to automatically maintain a horizontal line of sight, reducing the need for manual leveling.
*   **Components:**
    *   **Telescope:** For viewing the levelling staff.
    *   **Leveling Screws:** Used to make the instrument bubble horizontal.
    *   **Vertical Axis:** The axis around which the instrument rotates horizontally.
    *   **Line of Collimation (Line of Sight):** The imaginary line passing through the optical center of the objective lens and the intersection of the stadia hairs.

#### **2. Levelling Staff (Rod)**

*   **Definition:** A graduated rod, typically made of wood or fiberglass, with markings representing vertical distances.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Solid Staff:** A single piece, usually for shorter distances.
    *   **Folding Staff:** Collapsible for portability.
    *   **Invar Staff:** Made of Invar alloy, which has a very low coefficient of thermal expansion, used for high-precision levelling.
*   **Markings:** Usually in meters, decimeters, and centimeters. Red and white or black and white markings are used for easy reading.

#### **3. Bench Mark (BM)**

*   **Definition:** A fixed point of known elevation, used as a reference for subsequent levelling operations.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Permanent BM:** Established by government agencies or surveying authorities with high precision.
    *   **Temporary BM (Turning Point):** A point established during a levelling survey that becomes a BM for subsequent setups.

#### **4. Back Sight (BS)**

*   **Definition:** A staff reading taken on a point of **known** elevation, with the levelling instrument positioned between the known point and the point to be determined.
*   **Purpose:** To determine the Height of Instrument (HI).

#### **5. Fore Sight (FS)**

*   **Definition:** A staff reading taken on a point of **unknown** elevation, with the levelling instrument positioned between the point of known elevation and the point to be determined.
*   **Purpose:** To determine the reduced level of the new point.

#### **6. Intermediate Sight (IS)**

*   **Definition:** A staff reading taken on a point whose elevation is to be determined, but which is **not** the final point in a levelling run. It's an intermediate observation before moving the instrument.

#### **7. Height of Instrument (HI)**

*   **Definition:** The elevation of the horizontal line of sight of the levelling instrument above a reference datum (usually mean sea level).
*   **Calculation:** $\text{HI} = \text{Reduced Level of BS point} + \text{BS reading}$

#### **8. Reduced Level (RL) / Elevation**

*   **Definition:** The vertical distance of a point above or below a reference datum.

#### **9. Turning Point (TP)**

*   **Definition:** A point where the levelling instrument is moved. A backsight is taken from the previous setup to the turning point, and a foresight is taken from the new setup to the same turning point. This ensures a check on the work.

#### **10. Datum**

*   **Definition:** An arbitrary horizontal plane of reference used for the determination of elevations. Mean Sea Level (MSL) is commonly used as a datum.

### **Differential Levelling Procedures**

#### **1. Simple Levelling**

*   **Description:** Used when the two points are close enough and there are no obstructions, allowing the levelling instrument to be set up only once between the points.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Set up the levelling instrument at a convenient location.
    2.  Place the staff on the starting point of known RL (e.g., a BM).
    3.  Take a Back Sight (BS) reading on the staff.
    4.  Calculate the HI: $\text{HI} = \text{RL of starting point} + \text{BS reading}$.
    5.  Place the staff on the point of unknown RL.
    6.  Take a Fore Sight (FS) reading on the staff.
    7.  Calculate the RL of the new point: $\text{RL of new point} = \text{HI} - \text{FS reading}$.

*   **Example:**
    *   Point A (BM) has an RL of 100.000 m.
    *   Instrument is set up between A and Point B.
    *   BS on staff at A = 1.500 m.
    *   FS on staff at B = 2.100 m.

    *   **Calculation:**
        *   HI = RL of A + BS = 100.000 m + 1.500 m = 101.500 m
        *   RL of B = HI - FS = 101.500 m - 2.100 m = 99.400 m

#### **2. Compound Levelling (or Reciprocal Levelling)**

*   **Description:** Used when the distance between points is too great, or there are obstructions, requiring the levelling instrument to be set up multiple times. This involves using **Turning Points (TPs)**.

*   **Procedure:**
    1.  **First Setup:**
        *   Set up the level instrument at a convenient location (Setup 1).
        *   Take a Back Sight (BS1) on the starting point of known RL (e.g., BM).
        *   Calculate HI1: $\text{HI1} = \text{RL of BM} + \text{BS1}$.
        *   Take a Fore Sight (FS1) on a suitable point (TP1) in the direction of levelling.
        *   Calculate RL of TP1: $\text{RL of TP1} = \text{HI1} - \text{FS1}$.

    2.  **Second Setup:**
        *   Move the instrument to a new location (Setup 2).
        *   Place the staff on TP1.
        *   Take a Back Sight (BS2) on TP1.
        *   Calculate HI2: $\text{HI2} = \text{RL of TP1} + \text{BS2}$.
        *   Take a Fore Sight (FS2) on the next point or another turning point (TP2).
        *   Calculate RL of TP2: $\text{RL of TP2} = \text{HI2} - \text{FS2}$.

    3.  **Continue:** Repeat the process, taking BS readings on the previous TP and FS readings on the next TP or the final point.

    4.  **Final Point:** Take a Fore Sight (FS) on the final point of interest.
        *   Calculate RL of the final point: $\text{RL of final point} = \text{HI of last setup} - \text{FS on final point}$.

    5.  **Check:** The most crucial step is to check the accuracy of the calculated RL of the last TP. From the new setup, take a Back Sight on the last TP. This BS reading should match the previous FS reading on that TP if the RL of the TP is correct. Alternatively, establish a known BM at the end and compare its calculated RL with its known RL.

*   **Field Book Recording (Traditional Method):**

    | Station | BS      | IS      | FS      | HI      | RL      | Remarks         |
    | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :-------------- |
    | A (BM)  | 1.250   |         |         | 101.250 | 100.000 | Start point     |
    | TP1     | 1.800   | 2.500   | 0.900   | 102.150 | 100.350 | Turn point 1    |
    | TP2     | 1.050   | 3.100   | 1.650   | 101.550 | 100.500 | Turn point 2    |
    | B       |         |         | 1.400   |         | 100.150 | End point       |

    *   **Calculations:**
        *   **Setup 1:**
            *   HI1 = RL of A + BS1 = 100.000 + 1.250 = 101.250 m
            *   RL of TP1 = HI1 - FS1 = 101.250 - 0.900 = 100.350 m
        *   **Setup 2:**
            *   HI2 = RL of TP1 + BS2 = 100.350 + 1.800 = 102.150 m
            *   RL of TP2 = HI2 - FS2 = 102.150 - 1.650 = 100.500 m
        *   **Setup 3:**
            *   HI3 = RL of TP2 + BS3 = 100.500 + 1.050 = 101.550 m
            *   RL of B = HI3 - FS3 = 101.550 - 1.400 = 100.150 m

    *   **Check:** The RL of TP2 calculated from Setup 2 (100.500 m) should ideally match the RL calculated if a BS were taken from Setup 3 onto TP2. (In this example, BS3 on TP2 is 1.050, and RL of TP2 is 100.500, so HI3 = 101.550. If we had taken FS on TP2 from Setup 2, that FS should be 1.650. The check is essentially verifying that RL of TP2 + BS3 = HI3).

#### **3. Fly Levelling**

*   **Description:** Levelling performed between two points of known elevation (often BMs) without returning to the starting point. This is often used for checking or transferring elevations.

#### **4. Profile Levelling**

*   **Description:** Levelling taken at regular intervals along a line or at specific points of interest to determine the ground profile. Intermediate sights (IS) are frequently used.

#### **5. Block Levelling**

*   **Description:** Levelling carried out over an area to determine the elevation of multiple points, often for setting out buildings or earthwork calculations.

### **Calculations in Differential Levelling**

*   **Description:** This method directly calculates whether a point is higher (Rise) or lower (Fall) than the previous point.

#### **2. Height of Instrument (HI) Method**

*   **Description:** This is the most common and straightforward method. It calculates the elevation of the line of sight for each instrument setup.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  For each setup, calculate the HI: $\text{HI} = \text{RL of BS point} + \text{BS reading}$.
    2.  For each FS or IS reading, calculate the RL of the point: $\text{RL of point} = \text{HI} - \text{FS or IS reading}$.
*   **Field Book Recording (using the same data as above):**

    | Station | BS      | IS      | FS      | HI      | RL      | Remarks        |
    | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------------- |
    | A (BM)  | 1.250   |         |         | 101.250 | 100.000 | Starting BM    |
    | TP1     | 1.800   | 2.500   | 0.900   | 102.150 | 100.350 | Turning Point 1 |
    | TP2     | 1.050   | 3.100   | 1.650   | 101.550 | 100.500 | Turning Point 2 |
    | B       |         |         | 1.400   |         | 100.150 | Ending Point   |

    *   **Calculations:**
        *   **Setup 1:**
            *   HI1 = RL(A) + BS1 = 100.000 + 1.250 = 101.250 m
            *   RL(TP1) = HI1 - FS1 = 101.250 - 0.900 = 100.350 m
            *   RL(Point with IS) = HI1 - IS = 101.250 - 2.500 = 108.750 m (This was not the intention of IS, IS is usually taken on the same points as FS from the previous setup or on a new point before the next setup)
            *   **Correction:** IS readings are on points between the BS and FS points.
                *   RL of point with IS (assuming it's a new point before TP1) = HI1 - IS = 101.250 - 2.500 = 98.750 m.
                *   **Revised Understanding:** IS readings are taken on points whose RL is needed but are not turning points.
                *   Let's assume the IS is taken on the same point as the FS from the previous setup for a check. If IS was taken on TP1, then RL(TP1) = HI1 - IS = 101.250 - 2.500 = 98.750. This contradicts the RL of TP1 calculated as 100.350.
                *   **Standard practice:** IS readings are taken on points whose RL is required *before* the next turning point. So, we can have a BS, then multiple ISs, then an FS at a turning point, or BS, IS, FS.

        *   **Setup 2:**
            *   HI2 = RL(TP1) + BS2 = 100.350 + 1.800 = 102.150 m
            *   RL of point with IS = HI2 - IS = 102.150 - 2.500 (Let's assume the previous IS was on a point named P1) = 108.750 m. (This interpretation is not typical for standard field books)
            *   **Correct interpretation of the sample field book:** The IS reading of 2.500 is on TP1, but from the first setup.
            *   RL(TP1) = HI1 - IS1 = 101.250 - 2.500 = 98.750. This is still a contradiction.

        *   **Let's assume the IS readings are intermediate sights on points with unknown RLs.**
            *   Setup 1: HI1 = 101.250.
                *   BS on A = 1.250 (RL = 100.000)
                *   IS on P1 = 2.500 -> RL(P1) = 101.250 - 2.500 = 98.750
                *   FS on TP1 = 0.900 -> RL(TP1) = 101.250 - 0.900 = 100.350

            *   Setup 2: HI2 = RL(TP1) + BS2 = 100.350 + 1.800 = 102.150
                *   BS on TP1 = 1.800
                *   IS on P2 = 2.500 (This is the same reading as before, likely a mistake in the example) -> RL(P2) = 102.150 - 2.500 = 99.650
                *   FS on TP2 = 1.650 -> RL(TP2) = 102.150 - 1.650 = 100.500

            *   Setup 3: HI3 = RL(TP2) + BS3 = 100.500 + 1.050 = 101.550
                *   BS on TP2 = 1.050
                *   FS on B = 1.400 -> RL(B) = 101.550 - 1.400 = 100.150

    *   **Checking the HI Method:** The primary check is to ensure the RL of a turning point calculated from two different setups is consistent.
        *   RL of TP1 from Setup 1: 100.350
        *   From Setup 2, BS on TP1 = 1.800. HI2 = 102.150. If we had taken an FS on TP1 from Setup 2, that FS would be HI2 - RL(TP1) = 102.150 - 100.350 = 1.800. This is exactly the BS reading from Setup 2, which is the expected behaviour for a correctly executed levelling traverse.

*   **Note:** The HI method is generally preferred for its simplicity and reduced arithmetic errors.

### **Field Checks and Error Control**

#### **1. Field Checks**

*   **Double Meridian Distance (DMD) / Traverse Closure:** Not applicable to levelling.
*   **Arithmetic Checks:**
    *   **HI Method:** The RL of a turning point must be consistent when calculated from two different setups. $\text{HI1} - \text{FS1} = \text{HI2} - \text{BS2}$ (where FS1 and BS2 are on the same TP).
    *   **Rise and Fall Method:**
        *   Sum of BS - Sum of FS = Last RL - First RL
        *   Sum of Rise - Sum of Fall = Last RL - First RL
        *   Sum of BS + Sum of IS = Sum of FS + Sum of Rise - Sum of Fall (This check is often misinterpreted, the previous checks are more robust).
*   **Re-observation:** If an error is detected, re-observe the readings on the affected stations.

#### **2. Tolerable Error**

*   The acceptable error depends on the precision required for the project.
*   A common rule of thumb for ordinary levelling is:
    $\text{Allowable Error} = \pm \sqrt{D} \times \text{Constant}$
    where $D$ is the distance levelled in km, and the Constant varies (e.g., 5 mm for ordinary, 3 mm for precise).
*   **Example:** For 1 km of ordinary levelling, the allowable error is approximately $\pm \sqrt{1} \times 5 \text{ mm} = \pm 5 \text{ mm}$.

### **Sources of Error and Their Minimization**

#### **1. Instrumental Errors**

*   **Collimation Error (Line of Sight not perpendicular to the vertical axis):**
    *   **Minimization:** Regular calibration and adjustment of the level instrument. Ensure the instrument is properly levelled.
*   **Wavy Levelling Staff:**
    *   **Minimization:** Use a straight and rigid staff. Check for damage.
*   **Uneven Expansion/Contraction of Staff:**
    *   **Minimization:** Use Invar staffs for high-precision work. Avoid prolonged exposure to direct sunlight.
*   **Staff not Held Vertical:**
    *   **Minimization:** The staff holder must hold the staff perfectly vertical. Use a staff bubble to check.

#### **2. Field Procedure Errors**

*   **Improper Levelling of Instrument:**
    *   **Minimization:** Ensure the instrument is accurately levelled using the leveling screws. Automatic levels significantly reduce this error.
*   **Reading the Staff Incorrectly:**
    *   **Minimization:** Proper training of the observer. Use clear sighting techniques.
*   **Parallax Error:**
    *   **Minimization:** Focus the telescope clearly. Move your eye slightly up and down behind the eyepiece; if the crosshairs appear to move relative to the staff, parallax is present.
*   **Unequal Balancing of Sight Lengths:**
    *   **Description:** The distance from the instrument to the BS staff is significantly different from the distance to the FS staff. This is a major source of error.
    *   **Minimization:** Keep the BS and FS sight lengths as equal as possible. If this is not possible, increase the accuracy of the instrument or reduce the distance.
*   **Earth Curvature and Refraction:**
    *   **Description:** Earth's curvature causes the line of sight to deviate from a true horizontal line. Atmospheric refraction bends the light rays.
    *   **Correction:** These effects tend to cancel each other out when BS and FS distances are equal. For long sights, they become significant. Standard tables or formulas can be used for corrections, but for most ordinary levelling, equal sight lengths are sufficient.
        *   Curvature effect is negative (lowers the observed point).
        *   Refraction effect is positive (raises the observed point).
        *   Combined effect $\approx 0.067 \times (d^2 / R)$, where $d$ is distance in km and $R$ is Earth's radius.

#### **3. Personal Errors**

*   **Inexperience of Observer/Staff Holder:**
    *   **Minimization:** Proper training and supervision.

### **Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**

A levelling instrument is set up at Station 1. A backsight of 1.200 m is taken on a benchmark (BM) of RL 50.000 m. A foresight of 0.800 m is taken on point B. Calculate the RL of point B and the Height of Instrument (HI) at Station 1.

**Answer 1:**

*   HI = RL of BM + BS = 50.000 m + 1.200 m = **51.200 m**
*   RL of Point B = HI - FS = 51.200 m - 0.800 m = **50.400 m**

**Question 2:**

Using the HI method, complete the following levelling field book.

| Station | BS      | IS      | FS      | HI      | RL      | Remarks |
| :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ |
| A (BM)  | 1.500   |         |         |         | 120.000 | Start   |
| TP1     | 2.000   | 2.800   | 1.200   |         |         | TP1     |
| B       |         |         | 1.800   |         |         | End     |

**Answer 2:**

*   **Setup 1:**
    *   HI1 = RL(A) + BS1 = 120.000 + 1.500 = 121.500 m
    *   RL(TP1) = HI1 - FS1 = 121.500 - 1.200 = 120.300 m
    *   RL(Point with IS) = HI1 - IS1 = 121.500 - 2.800 = 118.700 m

*   **Setup 2:**
    *   HI2 = RL(TP1) + BS2 = 120.300 + 2.000 = 122.300 m
    *   RL(B) = HI2 - FS2 = 122.300 - 1.800 = 120.500 m

| :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ |

| TP1     | 2.000   | 2.800   | 1.200   | 122.300 | 120.300 | TP1     |

**Question 3:**

Perform a check on the Rise and Fall method for the following field book data.

| Station | BS      | IS      | FS      | Rise    | Fall    | RL      | Remarks |
| :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ |
| X (BM)  | 1.100   |         |         |         |         | 85.000  | Start   |
| TP1     | 1.500   | 1.800   | 0.900   | 0.200   |         | 85.200  | TP1     |
| Y       |         |         | 1.000   |         | 0.300   | 84.900  | End     |

**Answer 3:**

*   **Given Data:**
    *   First RL = 85.000 m
    *   Last RL = 84.900 m
    *   Sum of BS = 1.100 + 1.500 = 2.600 m
    *   Sum of FS = 0.900 + 1.000 = 1.900 m
    *   Sum of Rise = 0.200 m
    *   Sum of Fall = 0.300 m

*   **Checks:**
    *   **Check 1:** Sum of BS - Sum of FS = Last RL - First RL
        *   2.600 - 1.900 = +0.700 m
        *   84.900 - 85.000 = -0.100 m
        *   **This check fails! There is an error in the field book data provided.**

    *   **Let's assume the RL of TP1 is correct (85.200m) and re-calculate the fall from TP1 to Y.**
        *   If RL(TP1) = 85.200, and FS on TP1 from the first setup is 0.900, then HI1 = 85.000 + 1.100 = 86.100.
        *   RL(TP1) = HI1 - FS1 = 86.100 - 0.900 = 85.200. This is consistent.
        *   From TP1 to Y: BS2 = 1.500 on TP1. HI2 = RL(TP1) + BS2 = 85.200 + 1.500 = 86.700.
        *   FS2 on Y = 1.000. RL(Y) = HI2 - FS2 = 86.700 - 1.000 = 85.700.
        *   The provided RL for Y is 84.900, which is a significant difference.

    *   **Let's assume the RL of Y is correct (84.900m) and check the fall.**
        *   If RL(Y) = 84.900, and FS2 on Y = 1.000, then HI2 = 84.900 + 1.000 = 85.900.
        *   From Setup 1, BS1 on X = 1.100, HI1 = 85.000 + 1.100 = 86.100.
        *   FS1 on TP1 = 0.900. RL(TP1) = 86.100 - 0.900 = 85.200.
        *   From Setup 2, BS2 on TP1 = 1.500. HI2 = RL(TP1) + BS2 = 85.200 + 1.500 = 86.700.
        *   This HI2 (86.700) does not match the HI2 calculated from the RL of Y (85.900).

    *   **Conclusion:** The provided Rise and Fall values are inconsistent with the BS/FS readings and the given RLs, indicating an error in the raw data or the derived Rise/Fall values.

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **Accuracy is paramount:** Always double-check your readings and calculations.
*   **Balance sight lengths:** This is the most critical factor in minimizing errors due to earth curvature and refraction.
*   **Two-peg test:** Periodically perform the two-peg test to ensure the instrument's line of sight is perpendicular to the vertical axis.
*   **Bookkeeping:** Keep your field book neat and organized. Any corrections should be neatly crossed out, not erased.
*   **Turning points:** Ensure a clear turning point is established and that the BS and FS readings on the turning point from consecutive setups are consistent.
*   **Datum:** Always be aware of the datum being used for elevations.
*   **Types of Levelling:** Choose the appropriate method based on the terrain, distance, and required accuracy.
*   **Error Sources:** Understand the potential sources of error and actively work to minimize them.
