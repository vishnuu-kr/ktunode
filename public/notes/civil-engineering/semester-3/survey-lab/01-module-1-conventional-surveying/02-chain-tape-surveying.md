# SURVEY LAB - Module 1: Conventional Surveying

## Topic: Chain/Tape Surveying

### Introduction to Chain/Tape Surveying

Chain surveying is one of the most fundamental methods of surveying used to determine the relative positions of various points on the ground by measuring distances. It's a process of dividing a large area into smaller, manageable triangles and then measuring the lengths of the sides of these triangles. This method is primarily used for small areas and relatively flat terrain.

### 1. Principles of Chain/Tape Surveying

*   **Triangulation:** The fundamental principle is to divide the area to be surveyed into a network of well-conditioned triangles.
    *   **Well-conditioned triangles:** Triangles where no angle is less than 30° or greater than 120°. This ensures accuracy in plotting and calculation.
    *   **Why triangles?** Triangles are the strongest geometric shapes. If the lengths of all three sides are known, the shape and area of the triangle are fixed.
*   **Measurement of Distances:** The primary operation is the direct measurement of the lengths of the sides of these triangles.
*   **Accuracy:** Relies heavily on the accurate measurement of horizontal distances. Vertical distances (heights) are generally not directly measured in basic chain surveying.
*   **Framework:** A network of lines is laid out on the ground, forming a framework of triangles.
    *   **Main Stations:** Points marking the important corners of the area or the boundaries.
    *   **Traverse Stations:** Points along the boundaries or within the area to form the triangles.
    *   **Lines:** The measured distances between stations.
        *   **Main Lines (or Base Lines):** The longest lines of the survey, typically run along the main boundaries or across the area to establish control.
        *   **Tie Lines:** Lines connecting a station on a main line to an object or another station not on the main line, used to locate details.
        *   **Check Lines (or Proof Lines):** Lines measured to check the accuracy of the measurements of the sides of the triangles. They should be measured independently.

### 2. Instruments Used in Chain/Tape Surveying

#### 2.1. Chains

Chains are a series of interconnected links, typically made of steel or G.I. wire. They are used for measuring distances.

*   **Gunter's Chain:**
    *   **Length:** 66 feet (22 yards or 4 poles).
    *   **Number of links:** 100 links.
    *   **Length of each link:** 0.66 feet (7.92 inches).
    *   **Advantages:** Convenient for land area calculations, as 10 square chains = 1 acre.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Heavy, prone to kinking, less accurate for precise work.
*   **Engineer's Chain:**
    *   **Length:** 100 feet.
    *   **Number of links:** 100 links.
    *   **Length of each link:** 1 foot.
    *   **Advantages:** More convenient for metric system if decimal divisions are used.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Less convenient for acre calculations.
*   **Revenue Chain:**
    *   **Length:** 33 feet.
    *   **Number of links:** 16 links.
    *   **Length of each link:** 2.0625 feet.
    *   **Uses:** Used in some areas for specific land revenue surveys.

**Important Components of a Chain:**

*   **Links:** Small rings of steel or brass.
*   **Handles:** Brass handles at each end for carrying and pulling.
*   **Tally Markers (or Wisps):** Brass tallies are attached at every 10 links (e.g., at 10, 20, 30, etc., links from the handle).
    *   1 tally: 10, 30, 50, 70, 90 links.
    *   2 tallies: 20, 40, 60, 80 links.
    *   The 100-link mark is the handle.

#### 2.2. Tapes

Tapes are generally preferred over chains for their accuracy and ease of handling.

*   **Cloth Tape:**
    *   **Composition:** Strong linen or synthetic fibers, often with metallic threads woven in for greater accuracy and less stretching.
    *   **Length:** Commonly 30m, 50m, 100ft.
    *   **Advantages:** Lightweight, flexible, easy to fold.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Prone to stretching and shrinkage due to humidity and temperature changes, less durable. Not suitable for precise work or rough terrain.
*   **Metallic Tape:**
    *   **Composition:** Cloth tape with fine brass or copper wires woven in.
    *   **Length:** Commonly 30m, 50m, 100ft.
    *   **Advantages:** Less stretching than plain cloth tapes, more durable.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Still susceptible to temperature variations.
*   **Steel Tape:**
    *   **Composition:** A thin ribbon of steel.
    *   **Length:** Commonly 30m, 50m, 100ft, 200ft, 300ft.
    *   **Advantages:** Very accurate, less affected by humidity, durable.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can kink easily if mishandled, heavier, susceptible to temperature changes.
*   **Invar Tape:**
    *   **Composition:** An alloy of nickel (approx. 36%) and steel (approx. 64%).
    *   **Length:** Typically 30m or 100ft.
    *   **Advantages:** Very low coefficient of thermal expansion, making it highly accurate for precise measurements, especially over long distances or with significant temperature variations.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Expensive, delicate, requires careful handling.

**Important Components of a Tape:**

*   **Rings:** Brass rings at intervals (e.g., every meter or foot) for holding and pulling.
*   **Hook/Handle:** A hook or handle at the zero end for attachment to ranging rods or stakes.
*   **Markings:** Clearly marked graduations (feet, inches, meters, centimeters).

#### 2.3. Other Essential Accessories

*   **Ranging Rods:** Used for sighting and marking stations, especially when the distance between stations is too large to see directly.
    *   **Length:** Usually 2 meters or 3 meters.
    *   **Appearance:** Painted in alternate bands of black and white (or red and white) for visibility.
    *   **Capped:** Usually fitted with a steel shoe at the bottom for driving into the ground and a ring at the top.
*   **Arrows (or Pins):** Thin steel rods, about 40 cm long, used to mark the end of each tape length or a specific point on the ground.
    *   **Quantity:** Typically a dozen are carried.
    *   **Appearance:** Often have a loop of wire at the top for easy picking up.
*   **Pegs/Stakes:** Wooden or steel stakes used to mark stations firmly on the ground.
*   **Hammer/Mallet:** For driving pegs and arrows.
*   **Plumb Bob:** Used to transfer a point from the tape to the ground when the tape is held above the ground.
*   **Wedges/Chocks:** Used to prevent wheels from rolling on slopes.
*   **Line Dumpy Level (or Auto Level) / Spirit Level:** Though not strictly for chain surveying, it might be used to check for horizontal distances on slopes.

### 3. The Process of Chaining and Taping

This involves measuring the distance between two points on the ground.

#### 3.1. Reconnaissance and Planning

*   **Purpose:** To examine the area to be surveyed, identify obstacles, choose suitable stations, and plan the lines of survey to form well-conditioned triangles.
*   **Steps:**
    1.  **Walk over the area:** Get a feel for the terrain, identify existing features, and potential hazards.
    2.  **Identify main stations:** Mark the significant corners of the survey area.
    3.  **Plan the framework:** Sketch a preliminary layout of lines (main lines, tie lines, check lines) that divide the area into triangles.
    4.  **Consider visibility:** Ensure lines of sight are clear between stations.
    5.  **Minimize obstacles:** Choose lines that avoid dense vegetation, buildings, or steep slopes as much as possible.
    6.  **Ensure accuracy:** Plan to include check lines to verify measurements.

#### 3.2. Setting Out Stations

*   **Stations:** Points marking the extremities of survey lines.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  **Locate the station:** Determine the exact position on the ground.
    2.  **Drive a peg:** Securely drive a peg into the ground at that location.
    3.  **Mark the center:** Use a nail or mark the exact center of the peg for precise alignment.
    4.  **Temporary marking:** If immediate precise marking is not needed, a ranging rod or a stone can be used temporarily.

#### 3.3. Ranging

Ranging is the process of establishing a straight line between two points (stations) on the ground. This is essential when the distance between stations is too great to be seen directly.

*   **Direct Ranging:**
    *   **When used:** When the two stations are mutually visible.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  **Place ranging rods:** Place a ranging rod vertically at each station (A and B).
        2.  **Helper (Chainman):** A helper (chainman) stands at station A with the tape handle and a ranging rod. The surveyor (leader) goes towards station B with the other end of the tape and a ranging rod.
        3.  **Alignment:** The surveyor at B aligns the chainman at A by looking along the line AB and giving signals. The chainman at A moves left or right until the ranging rod at A is hidden behind the ranging rod at B (from the perspective of the surveyor at B).
        4.  **Intermediate points:** If the distance is very long, intermediate ranging rods are placed along the line. The surveyor at B signals the chainman to move forward. When the chainman reaches a point where they can no longer see the ranging rod at A, they place an intermediate ranging rod vertically at that spot. This process is repeated, ensuring all ranging rods are in a straight line.
        5.  **Signals:** Standard hand signals are used for left, right, forward, stop, etc.

*   **Indirect Ranging:**
    *   **When used:** When the line of sight between the two stations is obstructed by obstacles like hills, buildings, or trees.
    *   **Methods:**
        1.  **Ranging by Obstacles (e.g., hills):**
            *   Set up ranging rods at A and B.
            *   The surveyor at A sights B and directs a chainman to move to a point C, such that A, C, and B are visible.
            *   From C, sight A and B again. The surveyor at A directs the chainman to move to a point D, such that C, D, and B are visible and D is on the line CB.
            *   Repeat until the chainman is at a point E such that A, E, and B are mutually visible. The line AE is the required straight line.
        2.  **Optical Square or Prism Square:** This instrument uses prisms to achieve a 90-degree angle. It can be used for ranging when direct sighting is difficult, or for setting out perpendicular lines.
        3.  **Artificial Horizon:** Used when both stations are obscured, e.g., by a building.
            *   Set up a ranging rod at station A.
            *   Walk parallel to the line AB, keeping the ranging rod at A in front.
            *   When the obstacle is reached, set up a second ranging rod at B (if visible) or use a third point C.
            *   From the new position, establish a parallel line.

#### 3.4. Measuring Distances (Chaining/Taping)

This is the core operation.

*   **Procedure:**
    1.  **Preparation:**
        *   Ensure the chain/tape is in good condition, with no kinks or broken links.
        *   Select the appropriate chain/tape for the required accuracy.
        *   Check for temperature correction factors and chain length correction factors if high precision is needed.
    2.  **Positioning:**
        *   The surveyor and two chainmen are typically involved.
        *   The first chainman holds the zero end of the chain/tape at the starting station (e.g., station A).
        *   The second chainman (leader) walks forward along the line of survey, carrying the other end of the chain/tape and a ranging rod.
    3.  **Ranging:** The leader moves forward until the surveyor (trailer) signals that the chain/tape is straight and aligned with the next station or ranging rod.
    4.  **Tension:** The chain/tape should be pulled taut to remove slack and any sag due to its own weight.
    5.  **Marking:**
        *   When the leader reaches the desired length (e.g., 100 links of a Gunter's chain), they place an arrow vertically into the ground at the end of the chain/tape.
        *   The surveyor (trailer) then moves to the arrow, picks it up, and holds the zero end of the chain/tape there.
        *   The surveyor counts the number of full chain lengths measured by the arrows collected. Tally markers on the chain help in counting.
    6.  **Counting:**
        *   The first chainman collects the arrows. The surveyor counts the arrows collected by the first chainman.
        *   Example: If the distance is 350 links, the first chainman walks 300 links (collecting 3 arrows). The surveyor is at the 300-link mark. The first chainman then walks another 50 links and places an arrow. The surveyor moves to that arrow, collecting the 4th arrow. Total distance is 3 full chains (300 links) + 50 links = 350 links.
    7.  **Booking:** All measurements are recorded in the field book.
    8.  **Final Measurement:** For the last segment of the distance, the tape is extended to the exact point, and the fractional part is read directly from the tape markings.

#### 3.5. Taking Offsets

Offsets are the perpendicular distances measured from a surveyed line (chain line) to objects or features that are not on the chain line. They are used to locate details and features of the survey area.

*   **Types of Offsets:**
    *   **Perpendicular Offset:** Measured at a right angle from the chain line to the object. This is the most common type.
        *   **Method:**
            1.  **Identify the chain line:** This is the main line being measured.
            2.  **Locate the object:** Find the feature you want to locate (e.g., a wall, a tree, a stream).
            3.  **Estimate the perpendicular:** Visually estimate a perpendicular line from the chain line to the object.
            4.  **Using a square:** For accuracy, an **optical square** or a **line ranger** (a simple prismatic instrument) is used to establish a 90-degree angle from the chain line to the object.
            5.  **Measure the offset:** Measure the distance from the point on the chain line (where the perpendicular line meets it) to the object. This distance is the offset.
            6.  **Record:** Record the offset distance and the distance along the chain line where the offset was taken.
    *   **Oblique Offset:** Measured at an angle other than 90 degrees from the chain line to the object.
        *   **When used:** When it's difficult or impossible to measure a perpendicular offset, often due to the nature of the terrain or the position of the object.
        *   **Method:** Measure the distance from the nearest station or a point on the chain line to the object at any convenient angle. The angle is also recorded.
        *   **Disadvantage:** More complex to plot.

*   **Booking Offsets:**
    *   A common method is to draw a sketch of the chain line and show the objects and their offset distances.
    *   Alternatively, in a tabular format, record:
        *   **Chainage:** The distance along the chain line from the starting station where the offset is measured.
        *   **Offset:** The perpendicular distance to the object.
        *   **Description:** The feature being located.

### 4. Errors in Chain/Tape Surveying and Their Correction

Errors can be systematic or accidental.

#### 4.1. Systematic Errors (Correctible Errors)

These errors occur due to inaccuracies in the instruments, physical conditions, or the method of operation. They tend to be constant in magnitude and sign for a given set of conditions.

*   **Error due to Incorrect Chain/Tape Length:**
    *   **Cause:** The actual length of the chain or tape is not exactly its nominal length (e.g., due to wear, damage, or manufacturing defect).
    *   **Correction:** `Correction = (Lm - Ln) * L / Ln`
        *   `Lm`: Measured length.
        *   `Ln`: Nominal (correct) length of the chain/tape.
        *   `L`: Measured length.
    *   **Example:** A 30m tape is found to be 0.05m too short. If a distance is measured as 150.00m using this tape, the true length is `(150 - 0.05) * 150 / 30 = 149.925m`. No, the formula should be `Correction = (Actual Length - Standard Length) * Number of standard lengths`. Or more simply, `True Length = Measured Length * (Actual Tape Length / Standard Tape Length)`.
        *   Correct formula: `True Length = Measured Length * (Correct Tape Length / Nominal Tape Length)`
        *   If tape is short (e.g., 29.95m actual, 30m nominal): `True Length = 150.00m * (29.95m / 30.00m) = 149.75m`.
        *   If tape is long (e.g., 30.05m actual, 30m nominal): `True Length = 150.00m * (30.05m / 30.00m) = 150.25m`.
    *   **Correction Factor:** Can be applied per tape length measurement.

*   **Error due to Temperature:**
    *   **Cause:** Metals expand when heated and contract when cooled. Tapes are calibrated at a standard temperature (e.g., 20°C).
    *   **Correction:** `Correction = α * L * (T - Ts)`
        *   `α`: Coefficient of thermal expansion of the tape material (e.g., for steel, ~12 x 10^-6 /°C).
        *   `L`: Measured length.
        *   `T`: Actual temperature during measurement.
        *   `Ts`: Standard temperature at which the tape was calibrated.
    *   **Sign:** Positive correction if `T > Ts` (tape is longer). Negative correction if `T < Ts` (tape is shorter).

*   **Error due to Tension:**
    *   **Cause:** The applied tension differs from the standard tension used during calibration. If the applied tension is greater than standard, the tape stretches, making the measured distance shorter than the true distance. If less, the tape sags more.
    *   **Correction:** `Correction = (P - Ps) * L / (A * E)`
        *   `P`: Actual pull applied.
        *   `Ps`: Standard pull used for calibration.
        *   `L`: Measured length.
        *   `A`: Cross-sectional area of the tape.
        *   `E`: Young's modulus of elasticity of the tape material.
    *   **Sign:** Positive correction if `P > Ps` (tape is shorter than measured). Negative correction if `P < Ps`.

*   **Error due to Sag:**
    *   **Cause:** When a tape is supported only at its ends and pulled taut, its own weight causes it to sag between the supports. This makes the distance measured shorter than the true horizontal distance. This error is significant for steel tapes over longer distances.
    *   **Correction:** `Correction = w² * L³ / (24 * P²) ` (for each span)
        *   `w`: Weight per unit length of the tape.
        *   `L`: Length of the span (distance between supports).
        *   `P`: Tension applied.
    *   **Note:** This correction is often negligible for short distances or when the tape is supported throughout its length (e.g., by chainmen holding it at intervals).

*   **Error due to Slope:**
    *   **Cause:** Measuring a distance along a slope instead of horizontally. The slope distance is always longer than the horizontal distance.
    *   **Correction:** `Correction = h² / (2 * Ls)` where `Ls` is the slope distance.
        *   Alternatively, using trigonometry: `Horizontal Distance = Slope Distance * cos(θ)` where `θ` is the angle of the slope.
        *   `Correction = Ls - Horizontal Distance = Ls - Ls * cos(θ) = Ls * (1 - cos(θ))`
    *   **When to use:** If the slope is significant, it's often better to measure the slope distance and the angle of the slope, then calculate the horizontal distance. Or, use the "break tape" method on slopes.

#### 4.2. Accidental Errors (Random Errors)

These errors occur due to a combination of factors beyond the surveyor's control or due to human limitations. They are generally small and tend to compensate each other (some positive, some negative).

*   **Causes:** Variations in sighting, positioning of arrows, slight wobbles in tape, uneven ground, wind.
*   **Correction:** Accidental errors cannot be corrected by formulas. They are minimized by:
    *   Careful fieldwork.
    *   Repeating measurements.
    *   Using appropriate instruments.
    *   Applying compensating techniques (e.g., measuring in both directions and averaging).
    *   Statistical analysis of multiple measurements.

#### 4.3. Correction for Cumulative Errors

If systematic errors are not corrected for each measurement, they can accumulate. Total systematic error is the sum of individual systematic errors.

*   **Total Correction:** Sum of individual corrections for temperature, tension, sag, and slope, applied to the total measured distance.

#### 4.4. Checking Measurements

*   **Check Lines:** Measure key lines in the network independently and compare them with the calculated lengths from the survey.
*   **Remeasurement:** If discrepancies are found, remeasure the affected lines.

### 5. Principles of Marking Stations and Ranging (Revisited for Clarity)

*   **Stations:**
    *   **Permanent:** For important corners, use concrete pillars or large, well-driven stakes.
    *   **Temporary:** For intermediate points, use arrows, small pegs, or stones.
    *   **Marking:** The exact point of a station must be clearly marked (e.g., with a nail, a center punch mark on a peg).

*   **Ranging:**
    *   **Purpose:** To ensure that measurements are taken along straight lines.
    *   **Direct Ranging:** Visual alignment of ranging rods.
    *   **Indirect Ranging:** Used when direct sighting is impossible. Requires more complex techniques like reciprocal ranging or using alignment devices.
    *   **Chainage:** The process of measuring along the survey line and marking each full chain length with an arrow, and the final fractional length.

### 6. Booking Field Measurements

Accurate and systematic recording of all measurements in a field book is crucial.

*   **Field Book:** A notebook used by surveyors.
*   **Layout:** Typically has two columns for distances and a central area for sketches and notes.
    *   **Left Column:** Used for measurements taken with the left hand (e.g., left offsets, left ranging rod readings).
    *   **Right Column:** Used for measurements taken with the right hand (e.g., right offsets, right ranging rod readings).
    *   **Center:** Used for sketches of the survey area, showing the chain lines, stations, and features.
*   **What to Record:**
    *   **Date and Time:**
    *   **Weather Conditions:** (Affects tape behavior)
    *   **Survey Area/Project Name:**
    *   **Names of Surveyors:**
    *   **Instrument Used:** (Chain/Tape type, length, calibration details if relevant)
    *   **Chain Lines:** Clearly label main lines, tie lines, and check lines (e.g., AB, BC, CD, AC, BD).
    *   **Chainage:** Distance along the chain line from the starting station.
    *   **Offset Distances:** Perpendicular or oblique distances to features.
    *   **Object Descriptions:** Clear identification of the features being located by offsets.
    *   **Sketches:** Illustrate the layout and relative positions of features.
    *   **Corrections Applied:** If any corrections are calculated and applied in the field.
    *   **Tally Markers:** Note the count of tally markers passed.

### 7. Advantages and Disadvantages of Chain/Tape Surveying

#### 7.1. Advantages

*   **Simplicity:** The method is conceptually simple and easy to understand.
*   **Low Cost:** Requires relatively inexpensive instruments.
*   **Suitable for Small Areas:** Effective for surveying small, open, and relatively flat areas.
*   **Accuracy for Short Distances:** Can be very accurate for short, well-controlled measurements.
*   **Fundamental Skill:** Provides a foundational understanding of distance measurement in surveying.
*   **No Specialized Knowledge:** Doesn't require complex mathematical calculations or understanding of angles initially.

#### 7.2. Disadvantages

*   **Laborious and Time-Consuming:** Measuring long distances manually is slow and requires significant effort.
*   **Inaccurate for Large Areas:** Becomes impractical and prone to significant cumulative errors over large or undulating terrain.
*   **Affected by Obstacles:** Lines of sight can be blocked by buildings, trees, or steep slopes, requiring indirect ranging.
*   **Accuracy Dependent on Conditions:** Susceptible to errors from temperature, tension, sag, and slope.
*   **Not Suitable for Rough Terrain:** Difficult and slow to measure distances over broken or steep ground.
*   **Limited in Scope:** Cannot measure angles directly, making it insufficient for creating a complete map of features unless combined with other techniques.

### 8. Application to Simple Field Scenarios

*   **Measuring the area of a rectangular field:**
    1.  Measure the length and width using a tape.
    2.  Area = Length × Width.
*   **Measuring the area of a triangular field:**
    1.  Measure the lengths of all three sides (a, b, c) using a tape.
    2.  Use Heron's formula to calculate the area:
        *   `s = (a + b + c) / 2`
        *   `Area = sqrt(s * (s - a) * (s - b) * (s - c))`
    3.  Alternatively, measure a base and the perpendicular height to the opposite vertex. Area = 0.5 * base * height.
*   **Establishing boundaries of a plot:**
    1.  Set out corner stations.
    2.  Measure the distances between stations along the boundary lines using a tape.
    3.  Use tie lines to locate features near the boundary.
*   **Setting out a building foundation:**
    1.  Mark the corners of the foundation using pegs.
    2.  Measure the distances between the corners and check diagonals to ensure they are equal (for a rectangle/square).
    3.  Use offsets to locate wall lines accurately.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

1.  **Question:** A Gunter's chain is 66 feet long and consists of 100 links. What is the length of one link in feet?
    **Answer:** 66 feet / 100 links = 0.66 feet per link.

2.  **Question:** You are measuring a distance with a 30m steel tape. The tape was calibrated at 20°C. During measurement, the temperature is 28°C. The coefficient of thermal expansion for steel is 1.2 x 10⁻⁵ /°C. If you measure a distance of 120m, what is the correction for temperature?
    **Answer:**
    *   Temperature difference (T - Ts) = 28°C - 20°C = 8°C
    *   Correction = α * L * (T - Ts)
    *   Correction = (1.2 x 10⁻⁵ /°C) * 120m * 8°C
    *   Correction = 0.01152 m.
    *   Since the temperature is higher than standard, the tape has expanded, so the correction is **positive** (the measured length is shorter than the true length).

3.  **Question:** What are the main principles of chain surveying?
    **Answer:** The main principles are triangulation (dividing the area into well-conditioned triangles) and the direct measurement of the lengths of the sides of these triangles.

4.  **Question:** When is indirect ranging necessary, and what is one method used to achieve it?
    **Answer:** Indirect ranging is necessary when the line of sight between two stations is obstructed by an obstacle (e.g., building, hill). One method is ranging by obstacles, where intermediate points are established by moving forward and establishing new visible points.

5.  **Question:** Describe the difference between a perpendicular offset and an oblique offset.
    **Answer:** A perpendicular offset is measured at a 90-degree angle from the chain line to an object, while an oblique offset is measured at any convenient angle from the chain line to the object.

6.  **Question:** A 30m tape is found to be 5cm long (0.05m) when compared to a standard meter. If you measure a length of 150m using this tape, what is the true length?
    **Answer:**
    *   The tape is 30.05m actual length for a nominal 30m length.
    *   True Length = Measured Length * (Actual Tape Length / Nominal Tape Length)
    *   True Length = 150.00m * (30.05m / 30.00m)
    *   True Length = 150.00m * 1.001666...
    *   True Length = 150.25m

7.  **Question:** List three types of chains used in surveying and their typical lengths.
    **Answer:**
    *   Gunter's Chain: 66 feet
    *   Engineer's Chain: 100 feet
    *   Revenue Chain: 33 feet

8.  **Question:** What are tally markers on a chain and what is their purpose?
    **Answer:** Tally markers are brass rings attached to the chain at every 10-link interval (or other specific intervals depending on the chain type). Their purpose is to help the chainman quickly count the number of full chain lengths measured, making the process of recording distances more efficient and less prone to counting errors.

9.  **Question:** You measured a distance of 200m on a slope. If the average slope angle was 3 degrees, what is the horizontal distance? (Use cos(3°) ≈ 0.9986)
    **Answer:**
    *   Horizontal Distance = Slope Distance * cos(θ)
    *   Horizontal Distance = 200m * cos(3°)
    *   Horizontal Distance = 200m * 0.9986
    *   Horizontal Distance = 199.72m

10. **Question:** What is the primary geometric shape used in chain surveying, and why is it preferred?
    **Answer:** The primary geometric shape is the **triangle**. It is preferred because it is a rigid shape; if the lengths of all three sides are known, the shape and area of the triangle are fixed and cannot be altered. This ensures accuracy in plotting and calculations.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Accuracy is paramount:** Always strive for accurate measurements.
*   **Well-conditioned triangles:** Ensure all triangles in the framework have angles between 30° and 120°.
*   **Check lines are essential:** Always include check lines to verify the accuracy of your measurements.
*   **Ranging:** Ensure lines are straight by proper ranging techniques.
*   **Tension and Sag:** Be mindful of the tension applied to the tape and the effect of sag, especially over longer distances.
*   **Temperature:** Account for temperature variations when high accuracy is required.
*   **Booking:** Record all measurements clearly, concisely, and systematically in the field book.
*   **Reconnaissance:** Proper planning and reconnaissance before fieldwork can save time and improve accuracy.
*   **Instrument Care:** Handle chains and tapes with care to prevent damage and maintain accuracy.
*   **Understand Errors:** Be aware of the potential sources of error and how to minimize or correct them.
