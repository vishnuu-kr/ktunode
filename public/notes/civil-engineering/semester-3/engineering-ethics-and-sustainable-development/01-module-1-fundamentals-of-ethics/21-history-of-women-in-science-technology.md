# Engineering Ethics and Sustainable Development

## Module 1: Fundamentals of Ethics

### Topic: History of Women in Science & Technology

---

### **Learning Outcomes:**

*   **LO1:** Understand the historical exclusion of women from STEM fields and the systemic barriers they faced.
*   **LO2:** Identify key women who made significant contributions to science and technology throughout history, often in the face of adversity.
*   **LO3:** Recognize the evolution of societal attitudes towards women in STEM and the ongoing challenges and opportunities for gender equality.
*   **LO4:** Analyze the ethical implications of historical and contemporary gender disparities in STEM, particularly concerning fairness, equity, and the advancement of knowledge for societal benefit.

### **1. Introduction: The Historical Context of Exclusion**

*   **Key Concept:** **Patriarchy** - A social system in which men hold primary power and predominate in roles of political leadership, moral authority, social privilege, and control of property. This has historically influenced access to education and professions.
*   **Systemic Barriers:**
    *   **Limited Educational Opportunities:** Formal education, especially in higher sciences and mathematics, was largely inaccessible to women for centuries.
    *   **Societal Expectations:** Women were primarily confined to domestic roles, discouraged from pursuing intellectual or professional careers.
    *   **Legal and Social Restrictions:** Many legal systems and social norms prevented women from owning property, entering professions, or even publishing under their own names.
    *   **Bias in Recognition:** Even when women contributed significantly, their work was often attributed to male colleagues or overlooked entirely.
    *   **"Gatekeeping" in Academia:** Male-dominated institutions often acted as gatekeepers, limiting women's entry and advancement.

### **2. Pioneering Women in Science & Technology**

This section highlights a selection of women whose contributions often went unrecognized or were actively suppressed.

#### **2.1 Ancient and Medieval Periods**

*   **Hypatia of Alexandria (c. 350-415 AD):**
    *   **Field:** Mathematics, Astronomy, Philosophy.
    *   **Contributions:** Renowned teacher and scholar at the Neoplatonic school in Alexandria. Known for her work on conic sections (building on Apollonius) and astronomical instruments (astrolabe, hydrometer).
    *   **Challenges:** Faced significant opposition and was eventually murdered by a mob, illustrating the dangers of intellectual freedom for women in that era.
    *   **Key Point:** One of the earliest documented female scientists and mathematicians, demonstrating intellectual capabilities existed despite societal constraints.

*   **Trotula of Salerno (11th Century):**
    *   **Field:** Medicine, Gynecology.
    *   **Contributions:** Associated with a prominent school of medicine in Salerno, Italy. Her writings, particularly "On the Diseases of Women," were highly influential in medieval medicine, providing practical advice on women's health.
    *   **Challenges:** The authorship and precise identity of "Trotula" have been debated, a common issue for women writers in history where their contributions were questioned or attributed to a collective.
    *   **Key Point:** Advanced the understanding of women's health and demonstrated women's participation in a critical professional field.

#### **2.2 The Renaissance and Enlightenment**

*   **Maria Sibylla Merian (1647-1717):**
    *   **Field:** Entomology, Scientific Illustration.
    *   **Contributions:** Pioneer in entomology. Conducted meticulous research on the life cycles of insects, publishing "Metamorphosis insectorum Surinamensium," a groundbreaking work with detailed illustrations of insect metamorphosis and plant interactions.
    *   **Challenges:** Undertook an arduous expedition to Suriname with her daughter, a highly unusual undertaking for women of her time. Her scientific rigor was remarkable for an era when insects were often viewed with disgust.
    *   **Key Point:** Combined artistic skill with scientific observation, contributing significantly to the understanding of biological transformations.

*   **Émilie du Châtelet (1706-1749):**
    *   **Field:** Mathematics, Physics, Philosophy.
    *   **Contributions:** Best known for her French translation and commentary on Isaac Newton's *Principia Mathematica*, which remains the standard French translation. She also contributed to understanding kinetic energy, proposing the concept of energy being proportional to the square of velocity ($E=mv^2$), a precursor to Einstein's more complete formulation.
    *   **Challenges:** Her intellectual partnership with Voltaire brought her some recognition, but her contributions to physics were often overshadowed by his more popular writings.
    *   **Key Point:** A brilliant mathematician and physicist who bridged Newtonian mechanics and Enlightenment philosophy.

*   **Caroline Herschel (1750-1848):**
    *   **Field:** Astronomy.
    *   **Contributions:** Assisted her brother, William Herschel, in his astronomical observations. She independently discovered several comets, nebulae, and star clusters, and cataloged them. She was the first woman to receive a salary as a scientific researcher.
    *   **Challenges:** Worked largely in the shadow of her famous brother, though her contributions were substantial.
    *   **Key Point:** A prolific discoverer in her own right, paving the way for women in professional astronomy.

#### **2.3 The 19th and Early 20th Centuries**

*   **Ada Lovelace (1815-1852):**
    *   **Field:** Mathematics, Computing.
    *   **Contributions:** Considered the first computer programmer. She wrote an algorithm intended to be processed by Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, a mechanical general-purpose computer. Her notes contained what is recognized as the first algorithm intended for implementation on a machine.
    *   **Challenges:** Her mother fostered her mathematical education to steer her away from her father's poetic inclinations, but Ada's intellectual brilliance shone through.
    *   **Key Point:** Foresaw the potential of computers beyond mere calculation, envisioning them for music and art.

*   **Marie Curie (1867-1934):**
    *   **Field:** Physics, Chemistry.
    *   **Contributions:** Pioneer in radioactivity research. Discovered two new elements, polonium and radium. Developed techniques for isolating radioactive isotopes. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the first person and only woman to win the Nobel Prize twice, and the only person to win the Nobel Prize in two different scientific fields (Physics and Chemistry).
    *   **Challenges:** Faced immense sexism and xenophobia in France, struggling for funding and recognition despite groundbreaking discoveries. Her work with radiation also had severe health consequences.
    *   **Key Point:** A monumental figure whose relentless pursuit of knowledge revolutionized physics and chemistry and whose commitment to scientific discovery for humanitarian purposes (e.g., mobile X-ray units during WWI) is legendary.

*   **Nettie Stevens (1861-1912):**
    *   **Field:** Genetics, Cytology.
    *   **Contributions:** Discovered the role of chromosomes in sex determination (the X and Y chromosomes) in insects, independently of Edmund Beecher Wilson, who published his findings first. Her work provided crucial evidence for chromosomal inheritance.
    *   **Challenges:** Her discovery was largely overshadowed by Wilson's publication, a common issue of women's work being marginalized.
    *   **Key Point:** Her foundational work in genetics was critical for understanding biological sex determination.

*   **Lise Meitner (1878-1968):**
    *   **Field:** Physics.
    *   **Contributions:** Key physicist in the discovery of nuclear fission. She and Otto Hahn discovered the element protactinium. During her exile from Nazi Germany, she collaborated with her nephew Otto Frisch, and together they provided the first physical explanation of nuclear fission, theorizing that uranium split into lighter elements.
    *   **Challenges:** As a Jewish woman, she was forced to flee Nazi Germany. Despite her crucial theoretical work on nuclear fission, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this discovery was awarded solely to Otto Hahn.
    *   **Key Point:** A brilliant physicist whose theoretical insights were essential to understanding nuclear fission, yet she was denied the Nobel Prize recognition she deserved.

*   **Grace Hopper (1906-1992):**
    *   **Field:** Computer Science, Mathematics.
    *   **Contributions:** Pioneering computer scientist. Developed the first compiler for a computer programming language. Her work led to the development of COBOL, one of the first high-level programming languages. Credited with popularizing the term "debugging" after finding an actual moth causing a computer malfunction.
    *   **Challenges:** Served in the U.S. Navy during WWII and later as a reservist, breaking barriers in a male-dominated military and technical field.
    *   **Key Point:** Her contributions laid the groundwork for modern software development and made computers more accessible.

#### **2.4 Mid-20th Century to Present**

*   **Rosalind Franklin (1920-1958):**
    *   **Field:** Chemistry, X-ray Crystallography.
    *   **Contributions:** Her X-ray diffraction images of DNA, particularly "Photo 51," provided critical evidence for Watson and Crick's DNA double helix model. She also conducted groundbreaking research on the molecular structure of viruses.
    *   **Challenges:** Her data was shown to Watson and Crick without her full knowledge or consent, and her contributions were largely unacknowledged during her lifetime and in the initial Nobel Prize awarded to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins. She died at a young age from ovarian cancer, possibly linked to her work with radiation.
    *   **Key Point:** Her scientific contributions were instrumental to one of the most significant discoveries of the 20th century, yet she faced significant professional and ethical challenges regarding the recognition of her work.

*   **Katherine Johnson (1918-2020), Dorothy Vaughan (1910-2008), Mary Jackson (1921-2005):**
    *   **Field:** Mathematics, Aerospace Engineering.
    *   **Contributions:** Known as the "human computers" at NASA. Their complex mathematical calculations were critical to the success of early U.S. space flights, including Project Mercury and the Apollo program. Katherine Johnson calculated the trajectory for Alan Shepard's first mission and the launch window for Apollo 11. Mary Jackson was NASA's first Black female engineer. Dorothy Vaughan was a supervisor who became an expert in early computer programming.
    *   **Challenges:** Worked in segregated facilities within NASA. They faced racial and gender discrimination, but their brilliance and dedication were essential to NASA's achievements.
    *   **Key Point:** Their story, highlighted in the book and film "Hidden Figures," showcases the immense talent and resilience of Black women in STEM who overcame significant obstacles.

*   **Chien-Shiung Wu (1912-1997):**
    *   **Field:** Experimental Physics.
    *   **Contributions:** Conducted the "Wu experiment," which provided crucial experimental evidence for the violation of parity conservation in weak nuclear interactions, a fundamental discovery in particle physics.
    *   **Challenges:** Despite her pivotal role in confirming the theory of parity violation, the Nobel Prize for this discovery was awarded to her male colleagues, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang.
    *   **Key Point:** Her experimental prowess was essential for confirming a major theoretical advancement, yet her recognition was withheld.

### **3. Evolution of Attitudes and Ongoing Challenges**

*   **Increased Access to Education:** Over the 20th century, opportunities for women in STEM education gradually increased, though often with significant resistance.
*   **"Leaky Pipeline":** Despite more women entering STEM fields, there's a significant drop-off at various career stages (e.g., graduate school, early career, leadership positions).
*   **Persistent Biases:**
    *   **Unconscious Bias:** Subtle biases in hiring, promotion, and peer review can disadvantage women.
    *   **Stereotyping:** The persistent stereotype of scientists and engineers as male continues to affect perceptions and opportunities.
    *   **Workplace Culture:** Some STEM environments can be unwelcoming or exclusionary due to a lack of diversity and inclusion.
*   **The "Matilda Effect":** The tendency for the work of female scientists to be ignored or misattributed to their male colleagues.
*   **Intersectionality:** Women of color, LGBTQ+ women, and women with disabilities often face compounded challenges due to intersecting forms of discrimination.
*   **Current Trends:**
    *   Growing awareness and efforts towards gender equality in STEM.
    *   Mentorship programs, diversity initiatives, and advocacy groups.
    *   Increased representation in some fields, but significant gaps remain, especially in leadership.

### **4. Ethical Implications of Gender Disparities in STEM**

*   **Fairness and Justice:**
    *   Is it fair to deny opportunities or recognition based on gender?
    *   Does exclusion violate principles of equal opportunity?
*   **Advancement of Knowledge:**
    *   Excluding half the population from scientific inquiry limits the diversity of perspectives, ideas, and problem-solving approaches.
    *   Societal progress is hindered when a significant portion of intellectual talent is underutilized.
*   **Societal Benefit:**
    *   Technological and scientific advancements should ideally benefit all of society. If the creators are not representative of society, the innovations may not fully address diverse needs.
    *   Ethical engineering requires considering the impact of technology on all populations.
*   **Role Models and Inspiration:**
    *   Visible women in STEM inspire future generations. The historical lack of role models contributed to lower participation rates.
*   **The Ethics of Recognition:**
    *   It is an ethical imperative to acknowledge and credit all contributors to scientific and technological advancements fairly. The historical erasure of women's contributions is an ethical failing.

---

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Persistence of Bias:** While progress has been made, systemic barriers and biases against women in STEM persist.
*   **The "Matilda Effect" is Real:** Be aware of the historical tendency to overlook or misattribute women's contributions.
*   **Intersectionality Matters:** The challenges faced by women in STEM are often amplified by other aspects of their identity.
*   **Diversity Drives Innovation:** Inclusive STEM fields are more likely to produce innovative solutions that benefit society broadly.
*   **Ethical Responsibility:** As future engineers, understanding this history is crucial for building equitable and inclusive practices in your own careers and institutions.

### **Practice Questions and Exercises:**

1.  **Question:** Identify and describe at least two systemic barriers that historically prevented women from participating fully in science and technology.
    *   **Answer:** Two key barriers were limited educational opportunities (formal schooling in advanced sciences was often barred to women) and restrictive societal expectations (women were primarily expected to fulfill domestic roles, not pursue intellectual careers). Legal and social restrictions, such as the inability to own property or publish under their own names, also played a significant role.

2.  **Question:** Choose one woman from the provided list (e.g., Ada Lovelace, Marie Curie, Rosalind Franklin) and explain her significant contribution to science or technology, and the challenges she faced.
    *   **Answer (Example for Rosalind Franklin):** Rosalind Franklin was a chemist and X-ray crystallographer whose X-ray diffraction images of DNA, particularly "Photo 51," were crucial evidence for the structure of the DNA double helix discovered by Watson and Crick. She faced significant challenges including sexism in the scientific community, her data being used without her full consent, and her contributions being largely unacknowledged during her lifetime, even when her male colleagues received a Nobel Prize for work that heavily relied on her data.

3.  **Question:** What is the "Matilda Effect," and why is it ethically significant in the context of engineering and science?
    *   **Answer:** The "Matilda Effect" is the tendency for the work of female scientists to be systematically ignored, denied, or attributed to their male colleagues. It is ethically significant because it represents a failure of fairness and justice, denying recognition and credit where it is due. This undermines the principles of meritocracy, discourages women from pursuing STEM careers, and ultimately hinders the collective progress of science and technology by failing to fully utilize and acknowledge all available talent.

4.  **Question:** How does the historical exclusion of women from STEM fields relate to the principles of engineering ethics, particularly fairness and societal benefit?
    *   **Answer:** The historical exclusion of women from STEM is a direct violation of fairness and equal opportunity. Ethically, engineers are expected to act with integrity and uphold justice. Denying opportunities based on gender is inherently unfair. Furthermore, it impacts societal benefit because it limits the pool of talent and diversity of thought that drives innovation. When technological solutions are developed by a homogenous group, they may not adequately address the needs or perspectives of the entire society, leading to inequitable outcomes or overlooked societal challenges.

5.  **Exercise:** Research a contemporary woman working in a STEM field that interests you. Identify her current work and any challenges she might be facing or advocating against. Discuss how her work contributes to the field and potentially to sustainable development. (This exercise requires external research.)
