# Structural Analysis - I: Module 4 - Three Hinged Arches

## 1. Introduction to Arches and their Action

### 1.1 What is an Arch?

An arch is a structural element that spans a space and supports a load by transferring it outwards to abutments at either side. Unlike beams which primarily resist loads through bending, arches primarily resist loads through axial compression.

### 1.2 Action of an Arch

The key to the action of an arch lies in its curved shape. When a load is applied to an arch, it creates an internal force system that includes:

*   **Axial Thrust (Compression):** This is the dominant force. The curved shape directs the vertical loads and the bending moments generated by these loads into an inward horizontal thrust at the supports. This thrust is primarily compressive.
*   **Shear Force:** Present, but generally smaller than in a beam of the same span and loading due to the axial thrust.
*   **Bending Moment:** While arches are designed to minimize bending, some bending moment is still present, especially in non-uniformly loaded arches or arches with hinges.

**Key Concept:** The outward and downward forces acting on an arch are resolved into an inward horizontal thrust and an upward vertical reaction at the supports. This inward thrust allows the arch to carry loads efficiently with minimal bending stress, unlike a simply supported beam which experiences significant bending.

### 1.3 Advantages of Arches

*   **Efficient Load Carrying:** Primarily resist loads through compression, leading to smaller cross-sections and material savings.
*   **Aesthetically Pleasing:** Their curved form is often considered visually appealing.
*   **Reduced Bending Moments:** Compared to beams, arches generally have lower bending moments for similar loading conditions.
*   **Long Spans:** Suitable for spanning large distances.

## 2. Three Hinged Arches

### 2.1 Definition and Characteristics

A three-hinged arch is a statically determinate structure that contains three internal hinges. Typically, these hinges are located at:

1.  **Both supports (springings):** Allowing for vertical reactions and horizontal thrust.
2.  **The crown (apex):** The highest point of the arch, allowing for the arch to adjust to differential settlement of supports and non-uniform loading.

### 2.2 Degree of Indeterminacy

For a parabolic or circular arch with supports at the same level, the degree of indeterminacy is zero (statically determinate).

*   **Reactions:** 3 (horizontal thrust H, vertical reactions at each support V_A, V_B)
*   **Internal Forces:** 2 (shear force and bending moment are zero at the hinges).
*   **Equilibrium Equations:** 3 (ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0)

Number of unknowns (reactions) = 3. Number of equilibrium equations = 3. Thus, the structure is statically determinate.

### 2.3 Advantages of Three Hinged Arches

*   **Statically Determinate:** Easy to analyze using basic equilibrium equations.
*   **No Secondary Stresses:** Insensitive to temperature variations and settlement of supports because the hinges allow for rotation.
*   **Simplified Design:** Analysis is straightforward.

## 3. Eddy's Theorem

Eddy's Theorem is a fundamental principle for understanding the behavior of arches, particularly when considering the shape of the Bending Moment Diagram (BMD).

### 3.1 Statement of Eddy's Theorem

"The bending moment at any point in an arch rib is equal to the bending moment of a simply supported beam carrying the same load, minus the product of the horizontal thrust and the vertical distance of the point from the line of thrust."

Mathematically:
$M_{arch}(x) = M_{beam}(x) - H \cdot y$

Where:
*   $M_{arch}(x)$ is the bending moment at point $x$ in the arch rib.
*   $M_{beam}(x)$ is the bending moment at point $x$ in a simply supported beam with the same span and subjected to the same loads.
*   $H$ is the horizontal thrust of the arch.
*   $y$ is the vertical distance of the point $x$ from the line of thrust (which is the horizontal thrust line).

### 3.2 Implication of Eddy's Theorem

Eddy's Theorem can be re-written as:
$M_{arch}(x) + H \cdot y = M_{beam}(x)$

Or, rearranging:
$M_{beam}(x) - M_{arch}(x) = H \cdot y$

This implies that the difference between the bending moment of a simply supported beam and the bending moment of an arch is equal to the bending moment caused by the horizontal thrust acting at a distance $y$ from the arch axis.

**Key Insight:** For a parabolic arch supporting a uniformly distributed load (UDL) or a concentrated load at the crown, the bending moment diagram of the arch is a straight line (zero bending moment at the crown, which is a hinge). Eddy's theorem helps visualize how this happens by relating it to the BMD of a simply supported beam.

## 4. Types of Three Hinged Arches (with supports at the same level)

### 4.1 Parabolic Arches

A parabolic arch is often preferred because its shape naturally resists a uniformly distributed vertical load with only axial compression, resulting in zero bending moment in the arch rib itself.

**Equation of a Parabola:**
For an arch with supports at $(-L/2, 0)$ and $(L/2, 0)$ and apex at $(0, h)$, the equation is:
$y = h \left(1 - \frac{4x^2}{L^2}\right)$
or
$y = -\frac{4h}{L^2} x^2 + h$

Where:
*   $y$ is the vertical rise at a horizontal distance $x$ from the center.
*   $h$ is the rise of the arch (sagitta).
*   $L$ is the span of the arch.

### 4.2 Circular Arches

A circular arch has a constant radius of curvature. While aesthetically pleasing, it does not naturally resist a UDL with zero bending.

**Equation of a Circle:**
For a circular arch with supports at $(-L/2, 0)$ and $(L/2, 0)$ and apex at $(0, h)$, the center of the circle will be at $(0, k)$. The radius $R$ can be determined.
The equation of the circle is:
$x^2 + (y - k)^2 = R^2$

Since the supports are at $(L/2, 0)$, $(L/2)^2 + (0 - k)^2 = R^2 \implies L^2/4 + k^2 = R^2$.
Since the apex is at $(0, h)$, $0^2 + (h - k)^2 = R^2 \implies (h - k)^2 = R^2$.
So, $L^2/4 + k^2 = (h - k)^2$
$L^2/4 + k^2 = h^2 - 2hk + k^2$
$L^2/4 = h^2 - 2hk$
$2hk = h^2 - L^2/4$
$k = \frac{h^2 - L^2/4}{2h}$

And the radius $R = h - k = h - \frac{h^2 - L^2/4}{2h} = \frac{2h^2 - h^2 + L^2/4}{2h} = \frac{h^2 + L^2/4}{2h}$.

## 5. Determination of Forces in Three Hinged Arches

The analysis of a three-hinged arch involves determining the reactions and then cutting the arch at any point to find the internal forces (bending moment, normal thrust, radial shear).

### 5.1 Determining Reactions

1.  **Vertical Reactions ($V_A$ and $V_B$):** These can be determined by considering the arch as a simply supported beam under the applied loads.
    *   $\Sigma F_y = 0 \implies V_A + V_B - \text{Sum of vertical loads} = 0$
    *   $\Sigma M_{\text{at A}} = 0 \implies V_B \cdot L - \text{Sum of moments of loads about A} = 0$

2.  **Horizontal Thrust ($H$):** This is the crucial reaction for arches. Since the bending moment at the crown hinge is zero, we can utilize this condition.

    *   **Method 1: Using Eddy's Theorem (conceptually)**
        The bending moment in the arch at the crown is zero. If we consider the arch as a simply supported beam with span $L$ and the same vertical loads, the bending moment at the crown (mid-span) will be $M_{beam, crown}$.
        From Eddy's Theorem: $M_{arch, crown} = M_{beam, crown} - H \cdot y_{crown}$
        Since $M_{arch, crown} = 0$ and $y_{crown} = h$ (the rise at the crown):
        $0 = M_{beam, crown} - H \cdot h$
        Therefore, $H = \frac{M_{beam, crown}}{h}$

    *   **Method 2: Equilibrium of the Crown Hinge**
        Consider the left half of the arch (from support A to the crown hinge). The forces acting on this segment are $V_A$, $H$ (acting towards the right), and the applied loads on that segment. At the crown hinge, the bending moment is zero.
        Take moments about the crown hinge (at a height $h$ from the supports).
        $\Sigma M_{\text{at crown}} = 0$
        This equation will involve $V_A$, $H$, and the moments of the applied loads on the left half about the crown. Solving this equation gives the value of $H$.

    **Example for $H$ determination (UDL $w$ per unit length over the entire span $L$):**
    *   $V_A = V_B = wL/2$
    *   Consider the left half (span $L/2$). Loads: $w$ (UDL), $V_A = wL/2$ (upwards at A), $H$ (rightwards at A), and reactions from the crown hinge (vertical $R_v$ upwards, horizontal $H$ leftwards).
    *   Bending moment at the crown hinge from the left half:
        Consider the left half as a cantilever fixed at the crown, with an upward reaction $V_A$ at the support and a downward UDL $w$. This is incorrect.

    Let's use the correct approach by considering the left half as a simply supported beam from A to the crown, subject to loads and the horizontal thrust at A.
    Span of the half-arch = $L/2$. Rise at the crown = $h$.
    Vertical reaction at A, $V_A = wL/2$.
    Horizontal thrust at A, $H$.
    The equation of the parabola is $y = h(1 - 4x^2/L^2)$. At $x=L/2$, $y=0$. At $x=0$, $y=h$.
    Consider the left half of the arch. Forces are $V_A$ at support, $H$ at support, UDL $w$, and forces at the crown hinge.
    The resultant moment of forces on the left half about the crown hinge must be zero.
    The moment of $V_A$ about the crown is $V_A \cdot (L/2)$.
    The moment of $H$ about the crown is $H \cdot h$.
    The moment of the UDL $w$ over the span $L/2$ about the crown. The resultant load is $w \cdot (L/2)$ acting at $L/4$ from A. The distance of its centroid from the crown is $L/2 - L/4 = L/4$. So, the moment is $(wL/2) \cdot (L/4)$.

    $\Sigma M_{\text{crown}} = 0$ (taking counter-clockwise as positive)
    $V_A \cdot (L/2) - H \cdot h - (wL/2) \cdot (L/4) = 0$
    $(wL/2) \cdot (L/2) - H \cdot h - wL^2/8 = 0$
    $wL^2/4 - H \cdot h - wL^2/8 = 0$
    $wL^2/8 = H \cdot h$
    $H = \frac{wL^2}{8h}$

    This result is consistent with Eddy's theorem, as the maximum bending moment in a simply supported beam with UDL $w$ over span $L$ is at mid-span and is $(wL^2/8)$. The rise at the crown is $h$. So, $H = M_{beam, crown} / h$.

### 5.2 Determining Bending Moment ($M(x)$)

The bending moment at any section $x$ of the arch can be found by cutting the arch at that section.

1.  **Consider the arch as a simply supported beam:** Calculate $M_{beam}(x)$ for the applied loads.
2.  **Calculate $H$**: As determined above.
3.  **Calculate the lever arm $y$**: This is the vertical distance of the point $x$ from the line of thrust (the horizontal line at the level of supports, or where H acts). If the arch axis is defined by $y(x)$, then $y$ at section $x$ is simply $y(x)$.
4.  **Apply Eddy's Theorem:** $M_{arch}(x) = M_{beam}(x) - H \cdot y(x)$.

    *   **For a parabolic arch with UDL $w$:**
        $M_{beam}(x)$ at a distance $x$ from support A (span $L$): $M_{beam}(x) = \frac{wL}{2}x - \frac{wx^2}{2}$
        Equation of parabola: $y(x) = h \left(1 - \frac{4x^2}{L^2}\right)$ (Let's use origin at support A for easier calculation of $M_{beam}$)
        $y(x) = \frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$
        $H = \frac{wL^2}{8h}$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \left(\frac{wL}{2}x - \frac{wx^2}{2}\right) - \left(\frac{wL^2}{8h}\right) \left(\frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)\right)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{wLx}{2} - \frac{wx^2}{2} - \frac{wL^2}{8h} \cdot \frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{wLx}{2} - \frac{wx^2}{2} - \frac{w}{2}(Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{wLx}{2} - \frac{wx^2}{2} - \frac{wLx}{2} + \frac{wx^2}{2} = 0$
        This confirms that for a parabolic arch with UDL, the bending moment is zero everywhere.

    *   **For a parabolic arch with a concentrated load $P$ at the crown:**
        $V_A = V_B = P/2$
        $M_{beam, crown} = (P/2) \cdot (L/2) = PL/4$
        $H = \frac{M_{beam, crown}}{h} = \frac{PL/4}{h} = \frac{PL}{4h}$
        For any section $x$ from support A:
        $M_{beam}(x) = \frac{P}{2}x$ for $x \le L/2$
        $y(x) = \frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = M_{beam}(x) - H \cdot y(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - \left(\frac{PL}{4h}\right) \left(\frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)\right)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - \frac{PL}{4h} \cdot \frac{4h}{L^2} (Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - \frac{P}{L} (Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - Px + \frac{Px^2}{L} = -\frac{Px}{2} + \frac{Px^2}{L}$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} \left( \frac{x}{L/2} - 1 \right)$ which is incorrect. Let's re-check calculation.

        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - \frac{P}{L} (Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - Px + \frac{Px^2}{L}$
        $M_{arch}(x) = -\frac{Px}{2} + \frac{Px^2}{L}$

        Let's use the parabolic shape $y = h - \frac{4h}{L^2}x^2$ with origin at crown. Support at $(-L/2, -h)$ and $(L/2, -h)$.
        $y = h (1 - \frac{4x^2}{L^2})$ with origin at mid-span, supports at $(L/2, 0)$ and $(-L/2, 0)$, apex at $(0, h)$.
        $M_{beam}(x)$ for load $P$ at crown, origin at support A ($x=0$ to $L$):
        $V_A = P/2$. $M_{beam}(x) = (P/2)x$ for $0 \le x \le L/2$.
        $y(x) = \frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$.
        $H = \frac{PL}{4h}$.
        $M_{arch}(x) = M_{beam}(x) - H \cdot y(x)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - \left(\frac{PL}{4h}\right) \left(\frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)\right)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - \frac{PL}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - \frac{P}{L}(Lx - x^2)$
        $M_{arch}(x) = \frac{Px}{2} - Px + \frac{Px^2}{L}$
        $M_{arch}(x) = -\frac{Px}{2} + \frac{Px^2}{L}$
        Let's check at the crown ($x = L/2$):
        $M_{arch}(L/2) = -\frac{P(L/2)}{2} + \frac{P(L/2)^2}{L} = -\frac{PL}{4} + \frac{PL^2/4}{L} = -\frac{PL}{4} + \frac{PL}{4} = 0$. This is correct.

### 5.3 Determining Normal Thrust ($N_T$)

The normal thrust at any section is the component of the resultant internal force perpendicular to the cross-section. It is primarily the compressive force.

1.  **Calculate $V_x$ and $H$**: These are the vertical and horizontal components of the reaction at the support.
2.  **Calculate Shear Force $V_{shear}(x)$**: Consider the segment of the arch up to section $x$. $V_{shear}(x)$ is the algebraic sum of vertical forces acting on that segment.
3.  **Calculate the slope of the arch at section $x$, $\theta(x)$**:
    For $y = \frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{4h}{L^2}(L - 2x)$.
    The slope $\theta = \tan^{-1} \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)$.
    So, $\tan \theta = \frac{dy}{dx}$.

4.  **Calculate Normal Thrust ($N_T$)**:
    $N_T = H \cos \theta + V_{shear}(x) \sin \theta$

    Alternatively, if we consider the resultant internal force $R = \sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear}^2}$, the normal thrust is the component of this resultant along the tangent to the arch axis.

    Let's re-examine the forces acting on a cut section:
    *   Horizontal Thrust: $H$ (acting leftwards on the right segment, rightwards on the left segment).
    *   Vertical Shear: $V_{shear}$ (upwards on the left segment, downwards on the right segment).
    *   Bending Moment: $M$
    *   Normal Thrust: $N_T$

    Consider a small element of the arch. The resultant of internal forces at a section is $F = H + iV_{shear}$ in complex plane.
    The tangent to the arch is at angle $\theta$ to the horizontal.
    The normal force $N_T$ is the component of the resultant force along the tangent.

    Let's consider the forces acting on the left segment of the arch up to section $x$:
    *   Horizontal force at support A: $H$
    *   Vertical force at support A: $V_A$
    *   Vertical shear force at section $x$: $V_{shear}(x)$ (This is the net vertical force on the left segment, excluding reactions at A)
    *   Internal axial force at section $x$: $N_T$
    *   Internal shear force at section $x$: $V_{shear\_internal}(x)$

    The relationship is:
    $N_T = H \cos \theta + V_{shear\_internal}(x) \sin \theta$
    $V_{shear\_internal}(x) = V_A - \text{sum of vertical loads up to x}$

    **Example for Normal Thrust (UDL $w$ on parabolic arch):**
    $H = wL^2 / (8h)$
    $V_A = wL/2$
    $V_{shear\_internal}(x) = V_A - wx = wL/2 - wx$
    $\tan \theta = \frac{4h}{L^2}(L - 2x)$
    We need $\cos \theta$ and $\sin \theta$.
    $\sec^2 \theta = 1 + \tan^2 \theta$
    $\cos^2 \theta = \frac{1}{1 + \tan^2 \theta}$
    $\cos \theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta}}$
    $\sin \theta = \tan \theta \cos \theta = \frac{\tan \theta}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta}}$

    $\tan^2 \theta = \left(\frac{4h}{L^2}(L - 2x)\right)^2 = \frac{16h^2}{L^4}(L - 2x)^2$
    $1 + \tan^2 \theta = 1 + \frac{16h^2}{L^4}(L - 2x)^2$

    This becomes algebraically very intensive.

    **Simpler Approach for Normal Thrust:**
    The resultant of $H$ and $V_{shear\_internal}$ is the total force. The normal thrust is the component of this resultant along the tangent.
    Consider the resultant force acting at the section. Its horizontal component is $H$ and its vertical component is $V_{shear\_internal}$. The resultant force magnitude is $\sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2}$.
    The tangent at the section is at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. The normal force $N_T$ is the component of the resultant along the tangent.

    Let's use the property that the resultant of horizontal thrust ($H$) and the shear force ($V_{shear\_internal}$) acting at the section is inclined at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. The normal thrust is the component of this resultant along the tangent.

    A more direct method is to consider the force polygon of the segment. The internal forces at the section are $N_T$, $V_{shear\_internal}$, and $M$.
    Consider the equilibrium of the segment from support A to section $x$.
    Forces are $V_A$ (up), $H$ (right), applied loads, $N_T$ (along tangent), $V_{shear\_internal}$ (perpendicular to tangent), $M$ (moment).
    The resultant of $H$ and $V_{shear\_internal}$ is the total reactive force. The normal thrust is the component of this resultant along the tangent.

    Let the tangent at $x$ make an angle $\theta$ with the horizontal.
    The resultant of $H$ and $V_{shear\_internal}$ has a magnitude $R = \sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2}$.
    The direction of this resultant is $\alpha$ such that $\tan \alpha = V_{shear\_internal} / H$.
    The normal thrust $N_T$ is the component of this resultant along the tangent.
    The angle between the resultant and the tangent is $|\alpha - \theta|$.
    $N_T = R \cos(|\alpha - \theta|) = \sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2} \cos(|\alpha - \theta|)$

    This is still complicated. Let's use the components method.
    $N_T = H \cos \theta + V_{shear\_internal} \sin \theta$. (Incorrect, this is if $V_{shear\_internal}$ is acting along the shear axis and $H$ along the horizontal)

    Correct approach:
    Consider the internal forces at the cut section: Normal force ($N_T$), Shear force ($V_{shear}$), and Bending Moment ($M$).
    $N_T$ is the force acting perpendicular to the cross-section.
    $V_{shear}$ is the force acting parallel to the cross-section.

    Let's look at the resultant force acting on the segment from the support to the section.
    The resultant of $H$ and $V_{shear\_internal}$ is directed at an angle $\phi$ from the horizontal where $\tan \phi = V_{shear\_internal} / H$.
    The tangent to the arch axis is at an angle $\theta$ from the horizontal.
    The normal thrust $N_T$ is the component of the resultant force along the tangent.
    $N_T = \sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2} \cos(\phi - \theta)$
    $= \sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2} (\cos \phi \cos \theta + \sin \phi \sin \theta)$
    $= \sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2} (\frac{H}{\sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2}} \cos \theta + \frac{V_{shear\_internal}}{\sqrt{H^2 + V_{shear\_internal}^2}} \sin \theta)$
    $N_T = H \cos \theta + V_{shear\_internal} \sin \theta$. This is correct if $\theta$ is the angle of the tangent to the horizontal.

    Let's use the parabolic arch example with UDL $w$:
    $H = wL^2 / (8h)$
    $V_{shear\_internal}(x) = wL/2 - wx$
    $\tan \theta = \frac{4h}{L^2}(L - 2x)$
    $\cos \theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{16h^2}{L^4}(L-2x)^2}} = \frac{L^2}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}$
    $\sin \theta = \tan \theta \cos \theta = \frac{4h(L-2x)}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}$

    $N_T = \left(\frac{wL^2}{8h}\right) \left(\frac{L^2}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}\right) + \left(\frac{wL}{2} - wx\right) \left(\frac{4h(L-2x)}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}\right)$
    $N_T = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \frac{L^2}{\sqrt{...}} + w\left(\frac{L}{2}-x\right) \frac{4h(L-2x)}{\sqrt{...}}$
    $N_T = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \frac{L^2}{\sqrt{...}} + w\frac{(L-2x)}{2} \frac{4h(L-2x)}{\sqrt{...}}$
    $N_T = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \frac{L^2}{\sqrt{...}} + \frac{2wh(L-2x)^2}{\sqrt{...}}$

    This is becoming extremely cumbersome. Let's look for a simpler property.
    For a parabolic arch carrying a UDL, the normal thrust at any section $x$ is given by:
    $N_T(x) = H \sec \theta = H \sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta}$
    $N_T(x) = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{4h}{L^2}(L - 2x)\right)^2}$
    $N_T(x) = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \sqrt{1 + \frac{16h^2}{L^4}(L - 2x)^2}$

    **If the arch is parabolic and carries a UDL, the normal thrust is minimum at the crown ($x=0$ or $x=L$, where $\tan \theta = 0$, $\sec \theta = 1$) and maximum at the supports ($x=L/2$, where $\tan \theta$ is max).**
    At the crown ($x=L/2$): $N_T = H = wL^2/(8h)$.
    At the supports ($x=0$ or $x=L$): $\tan \theta = \frac{4h}{L^2}(L) = 4h/L$.
    $N_T(\text{support}) = H \sec \theta = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \sqrt{1 + (4h/L)^2} = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \sqrt{1 + \frac{16h^2}{L^2}} = \frac{wL^2}{8h} \frac{\sqrt{L^2+16h^2}}{L} = \frac{wL}{8h}\sqrt{L^2+16h^2}$.

### 5.4 Determining Radial Shear ($Q_R$)

Radial shear is the component of the internal force acting perpendicular to the arch rib axis (i.e., along the radial direction).

1.  **Calculate $H$ and $V_{shear\_internal}(x)$**: As determined previously.
2.  **Calculate the slope of the arch at section $x$, $\theta(x)$**: $\tan \theta = dy/dx$.
3.  **Calculate Radial Shear ($Q_R$)**:
    $Q_R = V_{shear\_internal}(x) \cos \theta - H \sin \theta$

    Let's use the parabolic arch example with UDL $w$:
    $H = wL^2 / (8h)$
    $V_{shear\_internal}(x) = wL/2 - wx = w(L/2 - x)$
    $\tan \theta = \frac{4h}{L^2}(L - 2x)$
    $\cos \theta = \frac{L^2}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}$
    $\sin \theta = \frac{4h(L-2x)}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}$

    $Q_R = w\left(\frac{L}{2} - x\right) \left(\frac{L^2}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}\right) - \left(\frac{wL^2}{8h}\right) \left(\frac{4h(L-2x)}{\sqrt{L^4 + 16h^2(L-2x)^2}}\right)$
    $Q_R = \frac{wL^2(L/2 - x)}{\sqrt{...}} - \frac{wL^2 h(L-2x)}{2h\sqrt{...}}$
    $Q_R = \frac{wL^2(L/2 - x)}{\sqrt{...}} - \frac{wL^2(L-2x)}{2\sqrt{...}}$
    $Q_R = \frac{wL^2(L/2 - x)}{\sqrt{...}} - \frac{wL^2(L/2-x)}{\sqrt{...}}$
    $Q_R = 0$

    **This means for a parabolic arch carrying a UDL, the radial shear is zero.** This is a very important property. The radial shear being zero means the resultant internal force is purely axial, along the tangent to the arch axis.

## 6. Worked Examples

### Example 1: Parabolic Arch with UDL

A three-hinged parabolic arch has a span of 40 m and a central rise of 10 m. It is subjected to a uniformly distributed load of 20 kN/m over its entire span. Determine the horizontal thrust, bending moment, normal thrust, and radial shear at a section 10 m from the left support.

**Given:**
*   Span, $L = 40$ m
*   Rise, $h = 10$ m
*   UDL, $w = 20$ kN/m
*   Section, $x = 10$ m from the left support.

**Solution:**

**1. Vertical Reactions ($V_A, V_B$):**
Since the load is symmetrical, $V_A = V_B = wL/2 = (20 \times 40)/2 = 400$ kN.

**2. Horizontal Thrust ($H$):**
For a parabolic arch with UDL, $H = \frac{wL^2}{8h}$
$H = \frac{20 \times 40^2}{8 \times 10} = \frac{20 \times 1600}{80} = \frac{32000}{80} = 400$ kN.

**3. Bending Moment ($M_{arch}(x)$):**
For a parabolic arch carrying a UDL, the bending moment is zero at all sections.
$M_{arch}(10 \text{ m}) = 0$ kNm.

**4. Normal Thrust ($N_T$):**
We use the formula $N_T(x) = H \sec \theta$, where $\tan \theta = \frac{dy}{dx}$.
The equation of the parabola (origin at support A): $y(x) = \frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$
$y(x) = \frac{4 \times 10}{40^2}(40x - x^2) = \frac{40}{1600}(40x - x^2) = \frac{1}{40}(40x - x^2)$
$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{40}(40 - 2x)$
At $x = 10$ m:
$\tan \theta = \frac{1}{40}(40 - 2 \times 10) = \frac{1}{40}(40 - 20) = \frac{20}{40} = 0.5$
$\sec \theta = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta} = \sqrt{1 + (0.5)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 0.25} = \sqrt{1.25} \approx 1.118$
$N_T = H \sec \theta = 400 \times 1.118 = 447.2$ kN.

**5. Radial Shear ($Q_R$):**
For a parabolic arch carrying a UDL, the radial shear is zero.
$Q_R = 0$ kN.

**Answers:**
*   Horizontal Thrust, $H = 400$ kN
*   Bending Moment, $M_{arch}(10 \text{ m}) = 0$ kNm
*   Normal Thrust, $N_T = 447.2$ kN
*   Radial Shear, $Q_R = 0$ kN

### Example 2: Parabolic Arch with Concentrated Load

A three-hinged parabolic arch of span 30 m and rise 5 m carries a concentrated load of 100 kN at the crown. Determine the horizontal thrust, bending moment, normal thrust, and radial shear at a section 5 m from the left support.

**Given:**
*   Span, $L = 30$ m
*   Rise, $h = 5$ m
*   Concentrated load, $P = 100$ kN at the crown.
*   Section, $x = 5$ m from the left support.

**Solution:**

**1. Vertical Reactions ($V_A, V_B$):**
Due to symmetry, $V_A = V_B = P/2 = 100/2 = 50$ kN.

**2. Horizontal Thrust ($H$):**
The bending moment in a simply supported beam of span $L$ with a concentrated load $P$ at the crown is $M_{beam, crown} = (P/2)(L/2) = PL/4$.
$H = \frac{M_{beam, crown}}{h} = \frac{PL/4}{h} = \frac{100 \times 30 / 4}{5} = \frac{750}{5} = 150$ kN.

**3. Bending Moment ($M_{arch}(x)$):**
$M_{arch}(x) = M_{beam}(x) - H \cdot y(x)$
$M_{beam}(x) = V_A \cdot x = 50x$ (for $x \le L/2$)
Equation of parabola (origin at support A): $y(x) = \frac{4h}{L^2}(Lx - x^2)$
$y(x) = \frac{4 \times 5}{30^2}(30x - x^2) = \frac{20}{900}(30x - x^2) = \frac{1}{45}(30x - x^2)$
At $x = 5$ m:
$M_{beam}(5) = 50 \times 5 = 250$ kNm.
$y(5) = \frac{1}{45}(30 \times 5 - 5^2) = \frac{1}{45}(150 - 25) = \frac{125}{45} = \frac{25}{9} \approx 2.78$ m.
$M_{arch}(5) = M_{beam}(5) - H \cdot y(5) = 250 - 150 \times \frac{25}{9} = 250 - \frac{3750}{9} = 250 - 416.67 = -166.67$ kNm.
(The negative sign indicates bending in the opposite direction to the convention if we assume positive moment is hogging for beam analysis. In arches, this would mean the outer fiber is in tension, or inner fiber is in compression.)

**4. Normal Thrust ($N_T$):**
$V_{shear\_internal}(x) = V_A - \text{loads up to x}$
For a concentrated load at the crown, up to $x = 5$ m, there are no loads. So, $V_{shear\_internal}(5) = V_A = 50$ kN.
$\tan \theta = \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{45}(30 - 2x)$
At $x = 5$ m:
$\tan \theta = \frac{1}{45}(30 - 2 \times 5) = \frac{1}{45}(30 - 10) = \frac{20}{45} = \frac{4}{9} \approx 0.444$
$\sec \theta = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta} = \sqrt{1 + (4/9)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 16/81} = \sqrt{97/81} = \frac{\sqrt{97}}{9} \approx \frac{9.85}{9} \approx 1.094$
$N_T = H \cos \theta + V_{shear\_internal} \sin \theta$
$\cos \theta = \frac{1}{\sec \theta} = \frac{9}{\sqrt{97}} \approx 0.914$
$\sin \theta = \tan \theta \cos \theta = \frac{4}{9} \times \frac{9}{\sqrt{97}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{97}} \approx 0.405$
$N_T = 150 \times 0.914 + 50 \times 0.405 = 137.1 + 20.25 = 157.35$ kN.

**5. Radial Shear ($Q_R$):**
$Q_R = V_{shear\_internal} \cos \theta - H \sin \theta$
$Q_R = 50 \times 0.914 - 150 \times 0.405 = 45.7 - 60.75 = -15.05$ kN.

**Answers:**
*   Horizontal Thrust, $H = 150$ kN
*   Bending Moment, $M_{arch}(5 \text{ m}) = -166.67$ kNm
*   Normal Thrust, $N_T = 157.35$ kN
*   Radial Shear, $Q_R = -15.05$ kN

## 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Three Hinged Arches are Statically Determinate.**
*   **Hinges eliminate secondary stresses** due to temperature changes and differential support settlement.
*   **Horizontal Thrust ($H$) is crucial** and is the dominant force in an arch.
*   **Eddy's Theorem is a powerful tool** relating arch bending moments to beam bending moments and horizontal thrust: $M_{arch}(x) = M_{beam}(x) - H \cdot y(x)$.
*   **Parabolic arches are ideal for UDLs** as they result in zero bending moment in the arch rib.
*   For parabolic arches with UDL:
    *   $H = wL^2 / (8h)$
    *   $M_{arch}(x) = 0$
    *   $Q_R = 0$
*   For parabolic arches with concentrated load at the crown:
    *   $H = PL / (4h)$
    *   Bending moment is not zero and depends on $x$.
*   Normal thrust ($N_T$) is the axial compressive force along the arch rib.
*   Radial shear ($Q_R$) is the force acting perpendicular to the arch rib axis.
