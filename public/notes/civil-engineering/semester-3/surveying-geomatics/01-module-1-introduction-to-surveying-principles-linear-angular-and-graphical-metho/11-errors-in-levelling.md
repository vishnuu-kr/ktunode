### 1. Introduction to Errors in Levelling

Levelling is the process of determining the relative vertical positions of points. It is a fundamental surveying technique, but like all measurement processes, it is subject to errors. Understanding these errors, their sources, and how to mitigate them is crucial for achieving accurate and reliable levelling results.

**Key Concept:** No measurement is perfect. Errors are inherent in the process and must be accounted for.

---

### 2. Sources of Errors in Levelling

Errors in levelling can originate from various sources, broadly categorized as follows:

*   **Instrumental Errors:** Errors arising from the equipment used.
*   **Personal Errors:** Errors caused by the surveyor's actions or limitations.
*   **Natural Errors:** Errors due to environmental factors.

---

### 3. Classification of Errors

Errors are further classified based on their behaviour and predictability:

*   **Systematic Errors (Consistent Errors):**
    *   Occur in a predictable manner and follow a definite mathematical law.
    *   Tend to be cumulative (increase or decrease the measurement consistently).
    *   Can often be determined and corrected for.
*   **Random Errors (Accidental Errors):**
    *   Occur irregularly and unpredictably.
    *   Cannot be predicted or determined by any known law.
    *   Tend to compensate for each other (some are positive, some are negative).
    *   Can be controlled by statistical methods and careful observation.
*   **Gross Errors (Blunders):**
    *   Result from mistakes, carelessness, or faulty equipment.
    *   Are usually large in magnitude and easily detectable.
    *   Require the measurement to be repeated.

### 4. Types of Errors in Levelling and Their Causes

Let's delve deeper into the specific types of errors and their contributing factors:

#### 4.1. Instrumental Errors

These arise from imperfections in the levelling instrument or its setup.

*   **Collimation Error (Line of Sight Not Horizontal):**
    *   **Cause:** The line of sight of the telescope is not perfectly parallel to the bubble line of the level. This can be due to improper adjustment of the telescope's optical elements.
    *   **Effect:** Causes a constant difference between the observed reading and the true reading on the staff. This difference is proportional to the distance from the instrument to the staff.
    *   **Mitigation:** Regular adjustment of the level (two-peg test). Balancing the distances between the instrument and the forward/backward staffs.

*   **Spindle Error (Vertical Axis Not Perpendicular to the Trunnion Axis):**
    *   **Cause:** Imperfect manufacturing or wear of the instrument's bearings.
    *   **Effect:** The level bubble will not remain centered when the instrument is rotated horizontally, leading to a tilted line of sight.
    *   **Mitigation:** Proper instrument maintenance and calibration.

*   **Graduation Error of Levelling Staff:**
    *   **Cause:** Inaccurate markings or wear on the levelling staff.
    *   **Effect:** Readings taken on the staff will be incorrect.
    *   **Mitigation:** Using calibrated and well-maintained staffs. Checking staffs against a known standard if suspicion arises.

#### 4.2. Personal Errors

These are introduced by the actions or limitations of the surveyor.

*   **Improper Instrument Setting:**
    *   **Cause:** The instrument not being properly levelled or centred over the tribrach/tripod.
    *   **Effect:** Leads to tilting of the instrument and consequently a non-horizontal line of sight.
    *   **Mitigation:** Careful levelling of the instrument using the plate bubbles.

*   **Faulty Staff Handling:**
    *   **Cause:** The levelling staff not being held vertically, especially by the staff holder.
    *   **Effect:** Causes a reading that is smaller than the true reading. This error is greater when the staff is tilted away from the instrument.
    *   **Mitigation:** Staff holder should ensure the staff is plumb. Using a staff bubble (level attached to the staff).

*   **Reading the Staff Incorrectly:**
    *   **Cause:** Parallax error (eye not perpendicular to the scale), misinterpreting the staff graduations, or estimating the fractional part of the reading.
    *   **Effect:** Leads to incorrect staff readings.
    *   **Mitigation:** Proper sighting technique (ensuring the eye is in line with the horizontal crosshair and the staff division). Using an invar staff with precise markings.

*   **Booking Errors:**
    *   **Cause:** Incorrectly recording readings in the field book or during transcription.
    *   **Effect:** Leads to erroneous calculations and reduced accuracy.
    *   **Mitigation:** Careful and systematic recording of readings. Double-checking bookings.

#### 4.3. Natural Errors

These are caused by external environmental factors.

*   **Atmospheric Refraction:**
    *   **Cause:** Light rays bending as they pass through layers of air with different densities, due to variations in temperature and pressure. The bending is generally downwards.
    *   **Effect:** Makes the staff appear higher than it actually is, leading to a lower reading. This effect is more pronounced for long sight distances and significant temperature gradients.
    *   **Mitigation:** Balancing sight lengths. Avoiding levelling during extreme temperature fluctuations.

*   **Curvature of the Earth:**
    *   **Cause:** The Earth's surface is curved, not flat.
    *   **Effect:** Makes the apparent horizontal line of sight appear above the true horizontal level. This causes the staff reading to be lower than the true value. The effect is cumulative with distance.
    *   **Mitigation:** Balancing sight lengths.

*   **Combined Effect of Curvature and Refraction:**
    *   **Combined Effect:** Refraction causes a reading to be lower, while curvature causes a reading to be lower. Therefore, the combined effect is that the observed reading is generally lower than the true reading. The combined effect is approximately 0.067 * D^2 (where D is the distance in kilometers).
    *   **Mitigation:** Balancing sight lengths is the primary method to counteract both effects.

*   **Wind:**
    *   **Cause:** Strong winds can cause the instrument or staff to vibrate.
    *   **Effect:** Leads to unstable readings and difficulty in precise observation.
    *   **Mitigation:** Avoiding levelling during strong winds. Using a stable tripod and ensuring the staff is held firmly. Shielding the instrument if necessary.

*   **Temperature Variations:**
    *   **Cause:** Expansion or contraction of instrument parts due to temperature changes.
    *   **Effect:** Can alter the calibration of the instrument and affect the accuracy of readings.
    *   **Mitigation:** Allowing instruments to acclimatize to ambient temperature. Regular calibration.

### 5. Methods to Minimize and Correct Errors

*   **Balancing Sight Lengths:** This is the most crucial technique to eliminate the combined effect of curvature and refraction.
    *   **Principle:** If the distance to the backsight (BS) is equal to the distance to the foresight (FS), the errors due to curvature and refraction will be equal and opposite, thus cancelling each other out.
    *   **Procedure:** When setting up the level, adjust the position of the instrument so that the distance from the instrument to the BS staff is approximately the same as the distance from the instrument to the FS staff.

*   **Two-Peg Test (Collimation Adjustment):**
    *   **Purpose:** To check and adjust the collimation error of the level.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Set up the level at a midpoint between two pegs (A and B), approximately equidistant.
        2.  Take readings on both pegs (e.g., BS on A, FS on B).
        3.  Move the instrument to a new location closer to peg B, ensuring it's still within a reasonable distance of both pegs.
        4.  Take readings on both pegs again.
        5.  Compare the differences in readings. If the collimation is perfect, the difference between BS and FS readings on the first setup should be equal to the difference between BS and FS readings on the second setup. If there's a discrepancy, adjustments are made to the instrument.

*   **Using a Level Bubble on the Staff:**
    *   **Purpose:** To ensure the staff is held plumb.
    *   **Procedure:** The staff holder visually checks the staff bubble and adjusts the staff's verticality to keep the bubble centred.

*   **Proper Instrument Handling and Maintenance:**
    *   **Procedure:** Regularly clean, calibrate, and service levelling instruments. Protect them from damage and extreme environmental conditions.

*   **Careful Reading and Booking:**
    *   **Procedure:** Maintain focus on the crosshairs, avoid parallax, ensure accurate estimation of the smallest division, and record readings systematically and clearly in the field book.

### 6. Error Propagation in Levelling

Error propagation refers to how errors in individual measurements combine to affect the overall accuracy of a levelling traverse or network.

*   **Cumulative Errors:**
    *   Systematic errors tend to accumulate. For example, a constant collimation error will increase with each setup.
    *   **Formula:** For a series of *n* equal sights of length *l*, if the error per sight is *e*, the total systematic error is *n* * e*. If the total distance is *L = n*l*, the error per unit length is *e/l*. The total error can be expressed as *e/l * L*.

*   **Random Errors and Statistical Analysis:**
    *   Random errors tend to cancel out. Their effect is reduced by increasing the number of observations.
    *   **Probable Error (PE):** A statistical measure indicating the likelihood of the true value falling within a certain range around the measured value. It's the error that has a 50% chance of being exceeded.
    *   **Calculating Probable Error in Levelling:**
        *   Calculate the difference between readings for repeated measurements of the same quantity.
        *   Calculate the probable error of a single observation.
        *   The probable error of the mean of *n* observations is PE_mean = PE_single / sqrt(n).
        *   In levelling, the probable error of the entire levelling operation is often calculated based on the differences in reduced levels at turning points or closure of loops.
        *   **Formula for probable error of a levelling operation (simplified for a series of sights):**
            PE = ± 0.6745 * sqrt(Σv^2 / (n-1))
            Where:
            *   Σv^2 is the sum of the squares of the deviations of individual readings from the mean.
            *   n is the number of readings.
        *   **More practical formula for probable error per unit distance:**
            PE per km = ± A * sqrt(K)
            Where:
            *   A is the probable error per instrument setup.
            *   K is the total distance in km.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
List three main categories of errors encountered in levelling and provide one example for each.

**Answer 1:**
*   **Instrumental Errors:** Collimation error.
*   **Personal Errors:** Staff not held plumb.
*   **Natural Errors:** Atmospheric refraction.

**Question 2:**
What is the primary method to eliminate the combined effects of Earth's curvature and atmospheric refraction in levelling? Explain its principle.

**Answer 2:**
The primary method is **balancing sight lengths**. The principle is that if the distance to the backsight (BS) is equal to the distance to the foresight (FS), the errors due to curvature and refraction will be equal and opposite, thus cancelling each other out.

**Question 3:**
A surveyor is levelling a line and encounters the following:
*   Setup 1: BS = 1.500m, FS = 2.500m (Distance to BS = 50m, Distance to FS = 50m)
*   Setup 2: BS = 1.200m, FS = 2.200m (Distance to BS = 60m, Distance to FS = 60m)

If the collimation error in the instrument is +0.001m per meter of distance from the instrument, what is the collimation error at each setup?

**Answer 3:**
*   **Setup 1:**
    *   Distance to BS = 50m
    *   Distance to FS = 50m
    *   Collimation error at Setup 1 = (0.001 m/m) * 50m = +0.050m (on the BS reading) and +0.050m (on the FS reading).

*   **Setup 2:**
    *   Distance to BS = 60m
    *   Distance to FS = 60m
    *   Collimation error at Setup 2 = (0.001 m/m) * 60m = +0.060m (on the BS reading) and +0.060m (on the FS reading).

*(Note: In a real scenario, balancing sight lengths for collimation error correction involves comparing the difference between BS and FS readings at different distances. The question as phrased provides a direct way to calculate the error per meter.)*

**Question 4:**
What is a "two-peg test" used for in levelling?

**Answer 4:**
A "two-peg test" is used to check and adjust the **collimation error** of a dumpy level or automatic level. It verifies if the line of sight is truly horizontal when the instrument is properly levelled.

**Question 5:**
If the probable error of a levelling traverse is ±5 mm per km, what is the probable error for a traverse of 3 km?

**Answer 5:**
Probable error for 3 km = (Probable error per km) * (Total distance in km)
Probable error for 3 km = ± 5 mm/km * 3 km = ± 15 mm.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Accuracy vs. Precision:** While precision refers to the repeatability of measurements, accuracy refers to how close the measurements are to the true value. Both are important in levelling.
*   **Systematic errors can be corrected or eliminated.** This is usually done through proper calibration, adjustments, and adhering to specific procedures like balancing sight lengths.
*   **Random errors cannot be eliminated but can be minimized.** This is achieved through careful observation, using better equipment, and employing statistical analysis to determine the reliability of results.
*   **Gross errors must be eliminated by repeating the measurement.** Identify and rectify the mistake that caused the blunder.
*   **Balancing sight lengths is critical for reducing curvature and refraction.**
*   **The two-peg test is essential for ensuring instrument accuracy.**
*   **Proper field procedures, including careful reading and booking, are paramount.**
