# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts - Sunk Cost

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Define sunk cost and explain its fundamental nature.
*   Differentiate sunk costs from relevant costs in decision-making.
*   Understand why sunk costs should be ignored in rational decision-making.
*   Identify and analyze sunk costs in various engineering and business scenarios.
*   Apply the concept of sunk costs to make informed economic decisions.

---

## 1. Introduction to Sunk Costs

### 1.1 What is a Sunk Cost?

A **sunk cost** is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of future decisions. It is a historical cost that has already been "sunk" into a project or activity.

### 1.2 Key Characteristics of Sunk Costs:

*   **Already Incurred:** The money has already been spent.
*   **Irrecoverable:** The cost cannot be recouped, no matter what happens in the future.
*   **Past-Oriented:** Sunk costs relate to past actions and expenditures.

---

## 2. Sunk Costs vs. Relevant Costs

The critical distinction for engineers when making decisions is between sunk costs and **relevant costs**.

### 2.1 Relevant Costs

**Relevant costs** are future costs that differ among the alternatives being considered. These are the costs that *matter* when making a decision because they will be affected by the choice you make.

### 2.2 Why the Distinction Matters

*   **Rational Decision-Making:** Rational decision-making in economics and engineering focuses on future outcomes and costs that can be influenced by the decision.
*   **Avoiding the Sunk Cost Fallacy:** Ignoring sunk costs prevents the **sunk cost fallacy** (also known as "throwing good money after bad"), where past investments influence current decisions even when those past investments are irrecoverable.

---

## 3. The Sunk Cost Fallacy (Escalation of Commitment)

### 3.1 The Fallacy Explained

The sunk cost fallacy occurs when individuals or organizations continue a behavior or endeavor as a result of previously invested resources (time, money, or effort), even when it is clear that continuing is not the best decision. It's the tendency to stick with a losing proposition because of what has already been put into it.

### 3.2 Psychological Underpinnings

*   **Desire for Justification:** People want to justify their past decisions. Admitting a mistake and abandoning a project can feel like admitting failure.
*   **Loss Aversion:** The pain of losing already invested resources can be greater than the prospect of future gains, leading to continued investment.
*   **Commitment and Consistency:** Once a commitment is made, there's a psychological drive to remain consistent with that commitment.

---

## 4. Identifying and Analyzing Sunk Costs in Engineering

Engineers encounter sunk costs in numerous situations. The key is to recognize them and consciously exclude them from the decision-making process.

### 4.1 Common Examples of Sunk Costs for Engineers:

*   **Research and Development (R&D) Costs:** Money spent on developing a new technology or product before production begins.
*   **Feasibility Study Costs:** Expenses incurred to determine if a project is viable.
*   **Initial Setup Costs:** Costs for acquiring land, building factories, or purchasing specialized machinery for a project that may be terminated.
*   **Training Costs:** Expenses spent on training personnel for a specific project that is later cancelled.
*   **Marketing and Advertising for a Failed Product:** Money spent promoting a product that ultimately does not sell.
*   **Costs of a Previous Attempt:** If a project failed, the costs of that previous attempt are sunk.

### 4.2 How to Analyze Sunk Costs:

When faced with a decision involving past expenditures:

1.  **Identify all costs associated with each option.**
2.  **Categorize each cost:** Is it a sunk cost or a future, relevant cost?
3.  **Focus solely on the relevant costs and expected future benefits.**
4.  **Compare the net present value (NPV) or other appropriate metrics of future cash flows for each alternative, ignoring sunk costs.**

## 5. Decision-Making Under Uncertainty and Sunk Costs

### 5.1 The Rational Approach: Future Focus

Rational economic decision-making dictates that only future costs and benefits should be considered. The question should always be: "What is the best course of action *from this point forward*?"

### 5.2 Scenario: Continuing a Project

Imagine a company has spent $1 million developing a new software. It needs another $500,000 to complete and launch it. Market research now indicates that the software will only generate $300,000 in revenue.

*   **Sunk Cost:** The initial $1 million spent on development. This money is gone regardless of whether they finish the software or not.
*   **Relevant Costs:** The future $500,000 needed for completion.
*   **Future Benefit:** The expected $300,000 in revenue.

**Decision:** If the company proceeds, they will spend $500,000 and gain $300,000, resulting in a future net loss of $200,000. If they abandon the project, they avoid the future $500,000 expenditure and the $300,000 revenue. The rational decision is to abandon the project, even though it means "losing" the initial $1 million (which is already lost). Continuing would only exacerbate the loss by an additional $200,000.

## 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

A civil engineering firm has spent $100,000 on preliminary design and site surveys for a new bridge project. The project is estimated to cost an additional $5 million for construction. However, a recent geological report reveals significant unforeseen soil instability, increasing the estimated construction cost to $7 million. The projected revenue from tolls remains the same at $6 million. Should the firm continue with the bridge project?

**Answer 1:**

*   **Sunk Cost:** The $100,000 spent on preliminary design and site surveys. This cost cannot be recovered.
*   **Relevant Costs:**
    *   If they proceed: Additional $7 million for construction.
    *   If they abandon: $0 additional cost.
*   **Future Benefit:** $6 million in toll revenue.

**Analysis:**

*   If they proceed: Cost = $7 million, Revenue = $6 million. Net outcome = -$1 million.
*   If they abandon: Cost = $0, Revenue = $0. Net outcome = $0.

**Conclusion:** The firm should abandon the bridge project. Continuing would result in a further loss of $1 million, in addition to the sunk $100,000. The decision should be based on the future costs and benefits, not on the money already spent.

---

**Question 2:**

A software development team has spent six months and $200,000 developing a new application. The project is currently behind schedule, and the projected completion cost has risen from $150,000 to $250,000. The estimated market value if completed successfully is now $300,000. What is the sunk cost in this scenario? Should the team continue development?

**Answer 2:**

*   **Sunk Cost:** The $200,000 already spent on development. This amount is irrecoverable.
*   **Relevant Costs (if proceeding):** The additional $250,000 required to complete the project.
*   **Future Benefit:** The estimated market value of $300,000.

**Analysis:**

*   If they proceed: Additional Cost = $250,000, Future Benefit = $300,000. Net future outcome = $300,000 - $250,000 = $50,000 profit.
*   If they abandon: Additional Cost = $0, Future Benefit = $0. Net future outcome = $0.

**Conclusion:** Yes, the team should continue development. Although the initial $200,000 is a sunk cost, the future costs ($250,000) are less than the future benefits ($300,000), yielding a positive net outcome of $50,000 from this point forward. The decision is based on the future profitability, not the past investment.

---

**Question 3:**

You bought a non-refundable ticket for a concert for $100. On the night of the concert, you are feeling unwell, and a friend invites you to a movie that you would enjoy more and would cost $50. You also have an important work deadline that you need to finish by tomorrow morning.

Which of the following is a sunk cost in your decision-making process?

a) The $100 for the concert ticket.
b) The $50 for the movie ticket.
c) The potential salary loss if you miss the work deadline.
d) The cost of the medicine you need for your illness.

**Answer 3:**

a) The $100 for the concert ticket.

**Explanation:** The $100 for the concert ticket has already been spent and cannot be recovered, regardless of whether you go to the concert, the movie, or work. This makes it a sunk cost. The $50 for the movie and the potential salary loss are future, relevant costs that will be affected by your decision. The cost of medicine is a relevant cost if it impacts your ability to attend either event or work, but the ticket itself is sunk.

---

## 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Sunk costs are history.** They should not influence your decisions about the future.
*   **Focus on future, incremental costs and benefits.** These are the only factors that should drive your choices.
*   **Beware of the sunk cost fallacy.** It's a common cognitive bias that can lead to irrational decisions.
*   **Always ask: "What happens from this point forward?"** This mindset helps you focus on relevant information.
*   **For engineers, understanding sunk costs is crucial for efficient resource allocation and project management.** It helps in deciding whether to continue or abandon projects based on their future viability, not past investments.
