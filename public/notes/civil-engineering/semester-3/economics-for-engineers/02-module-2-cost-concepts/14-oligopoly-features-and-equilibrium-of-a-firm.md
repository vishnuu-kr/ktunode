### 1. Introduction to Oligopoly

Oligopoly is a market structure characterized by a small number of large firms that dominate the market. These firms are interdependent, meaning that the actions of one firm directly affect the others. This interdependence leads to strategic behavior, where firms anticipate and react to the decisions of their rivals.

---

### 2. Key Features of Oligopoly

*   **Few Sellers:** The most defining characteristic is the presence of a small number of firms, typically between 2 and 10, that control a significant majority of the market share.
    *   **Example:** The automotive industry, the airline industry, and the smartphone operating system market (iOS and Android) are classic examples of oligopolies.
*   **Homogeneous or Differentiated Products:**
    *   **Homogeneous Oligopoly:** Products are identical (e.g., raw materials like steel, aluminum, or crude oil). The primary competition is on price.
    *   **Differentiated Oligopoly:** Products have slight differences in quality, branding, design, or features (e.g., cars, soft drinks, or smartphones). Competition can be on price, advertising, and product innovation.
*   **High Barriers to Entry:** Significant obstacles prevent new firms from entering the market easily. These can include:
    *   **Economies of Scale:** Existing large firms can produce at lower per-unit costs due to their scale of operations, making it difficult for new, smaller firms to compete on price.
    *   **Brand Loyalty:** Established firms often have strong brand recognition and customer loyalty, which new entrants must overcome.
    *   **Patents and Technology:** Control over key technologies or patents can restrict entry.
    *   **Government Regulations:** Licensing requirements or other regulations can limit the number of firms.
    *   **Control over Raw Materials:** Exclusive access to essential inputs can be a barrier.
    *   **High Start-up Costs:** Industries like aerospace or telecommunications require massive initial investments.
*   **Interdependence and Strategic Behavior:** This is the hallmark of oligopoly. Firms must consider the potential reactions of their competitors when making decisions about pricing, output, advertising, or product development.
    *   **"What will my rivals do if I lower my price?"** This question drives strategic decision-making.
*   **Non-Price Competition:** Firms often compete through advertising, product differentiation, customer service, and innovation to attract customers rather than solely relying on price cuts, which could lead to destructive price wars.
*   **Potential for Collusion:** Because of the small number of firms, there's an incentive for them to cooperate and act like a monopoly, fixing prices or dividing markets. However, collusion is often illegal and unstable due to the incentive for individual firms to cheat.

### 3. Models of Oligopoly Equilibrium

The lack of a single, universally accepted model for oligopoly is due to the complex nature of strategic interdependence. Here are some prominent models:

#### 3.1. The Cournot Model (Quantity Competition)

*   **Concept:** Firms compete by choosing their output levels simultaneously, assuming the output of their rivals is fixed.
*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Two firms (duopoly) producing a homogeneous product.
    *   Firms choose their output levels simultaneously.
    *   Each firm assumes its rival's output will remain constant regardless of its own output decision.
    *   Market demand is known.
    *   Each firm aims to maximize its profit.
*   **Key Idea:** Each firm has a **reaction function** (or best response function) that shows its profit-maximizing output level for each possible output level of its rival. The equilibrium occurs where these reaction functions intersect – the **Cournot-Nash Equilibrium**.
*   **Equilibrium Characteristics:**
    *   The total output is less than perfect competition but more than monopoly.
    *   The price is higher than perfect competition but lower than monopoly.
    *   Each firm produces less than it would in a perfectly competitive market but more than it would in a monopoly.
*   **Example:** Imagine two firms producing identical water bottles. If Firm A decides to produce 100 bottles, Firm B will determine its profit-maximizing output based on that assumption, and vice-versa. The Cournot model finds a stable point where neither firm has an incentive to change its output, given the other's output.

#### 3.2. The Bertrand Model (Price Competition)

*   **Concept:** Firms compete by setting their prices simultaneously, assuming the prices of their rivals are fixed.
*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Two or more firms producing a homogeneous product.
    *   Firms choose their prices simultaneously.
    *   Consumers buy from the firm with the lowest price.
    *   If prices are equal, consumers are indifferent and split their purchases (often equally).
*   **Key Idea:** In a Bertrand model with homogeneous products, the equilibrium price is driven down to the marginal cost of production.
*   **Equilibrium Characteristics:**
    *   Price = Marginal Cost (P = MC).
    *   This results in zero economic profit for all firms.
    *   This outcome is identical to perfect competition, even with only two firms.
*   **Important Note:** The Bertrand model highlights the power of price competition. If products are differentiated, the equilibrium price will be above marginal cost.
*   **Example:** Imagine two identical gas stations across the street from each other. If one raises its price, all customers go to the other. If one lowers its price slightly, it captures the entire market. This pressure forces prices down to the cost of producing the gas.

#### 3.3. The Stackelberg Model (Sequential Quantity Competition)

*   **Concept:** One firm (the leader) chooses its output first, and then the other firm(s) (the follower(s)) observe the leader's output and choose their own output levels.
*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Two firms (duopoly) producing a homogeneous product.
    *   One firm (leader) makes its output decision first.
    *   The other firm (follower) observes the leader's output and then makes its own output decision.
    *   Both firms are rational and seek to maximize profit.
*   **Key Idea:** The leader firm, by moving first, can exploit the follower's reaction function to its advantage, often leading to a higher profit for the leader and a lower profit for the follower compared to the Cournot model.
*   **Equilibrium Characteristics:**
    *   The leader produces more output than in the Cournot model.
    *   The follower produces less output than in the Cournot model.
    *   The market price is lower than in Cournot.
    *   The leader generally achieves higher profits.
*   **Example:** Think of a dominant firm in an industry that has a significant cost advantage or market presence. It might announce its production plans first, forcing smaller competitors to adjust.

#### 3.4. Collusion and Cartels

*   **Concept:** Firms in an oligopoly may attempt to cooperate to reduce competition and increase joint profits, acting as a single monopolist.
*   **Cartel:** A formal agreement among firms to coordinate their actions, such as setting prices, limiting output, or dividing markets.
    *   **Example:** OPEC (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries) is a famous example of a cartel where oil-producing nations coordinate production to influence global oil prices.
*   **Incentives for Collusion:**
    *   Higher profits than competitive behavior.
    *   Price stability.
    *   Reduced uncertainty.
*   **Incentives to Cheat:**
    *   Each member firm has an incentive to secretly increase its output or lower its price to capture a larger market share and thus higher profits, as long as the others maintain the agreed-upon terms.
*   **Instability of Cartels:** The temptation to cheat makes cartels inherently unstable. If one firm cheats and gets caught, or if enough firms cheat, the cartel can collapse.
*   **Collusion can be explicit (illegal) or tacit (informal understanding).**

#### 3.5. Game Theory and Oligopoly

*   **Concept:** Game theory provides a framework for analyzing strategic interactions between rational decision-makers. Oligopoly is a natural application.
*   **Key Elements of a Game:**
    *   **Players:** The firms in the oligopoly.
    *   **Strategies:** The actions each firm can take (e.g., price, output, advertising).
    *   **Payoffs:** The profits each firm receives based on the combination of strategies chosen by all players.
*   **Prisoner's Dilemma:** A classic game theory scenario illustrating why rational individuals might not cooperate, even if it's in their best interest to do so. In oligopoly, the "defect" strategy might be to cut prices or increase output, even if cooperation (collusion) would yield higher joint profits.
*   **Example:** Consider two firms deciding whether to advertise. If both advertise, they incur costs, and market shares might not change much. If neither advertises, they save money and keep their profits. However, if one advertises and the other doesn't, the advertising firm gains market share, while the non-advertising firm loses out. This dilemma can lead to both firms advertising even though they would be better off not doing so.

### 4. Equilibrium of a Firm in Oligopoly

Unlike perfect competition where firms are price takers and reach equilibrium at P=MC, or monopoly where the firm is a price maker with a single demand curve, the equilibrium of a firm in oligopoly is complex and depends on the specific model and the behavior of rivals.

*   **No Single Equilibrium Point:** There isn't a simple rule like P=MC or MR=MC that universally defines the oligopoly firm's equilibrium across all scenarios.
*   **Dependence on Expectations:** A firm's optimal decision (price, output) depends heavily on its expectations about its rivals' actions.
*   **Reaction Functions:** In models like Cournot, equilibrium is achieved when each firm's output is the best response to its rival's output, and neither has an incentive to deviate.
*   **Kinked Demand Curve Model (Sweezy Model):**
    *   **Concept:** Assumes that if a firm raises its price, its rivals will not follow, leading to a significant loss of customers (elastic demand for price increases). If it lowers its price, rivals will also lower their prices, leading to only a small increase in customers or market share (inelastic demand for price decreases).
    *   **Implication:** This creates a "kink" in the firm's demand curve, resulting in a discontinuous marginal revenue curve. As long as marginal cost intersects the vertical segment of the marginal revenue curve, the firm's price and output remain stable, even if marginal cost changes within that range.
    *   **Critique:** This model explains price rigidity but doesn't explain *how* the initial price was determined.
*   **Profit Maximization:** Despite the complexities, the fundamental goal of an oligopolistic firm remains profit maximization. It will produce where its marginal revenue (which is influenced by rivals' actions) equals its marginal cost, taking into account the strategic implications of its output decision.

### 5. Factors Affecting Collusion and Competition

*   **Number of Firms:** Fewer firms make collusion easier to achieve and maintain.
*   **Product Homogeneity:** Homogeneous products make price collusion simpler.
*   **Market Growth:** Slow or declining markets can increase incentives to cheat to maintain market share. Fast-growing markets can accommodate more firms without intense rivalry.
*   **Transparency of Pricing:** If prices are easily observable, it's easier to detect cheating.
*   **Barriers to Entry:** High barriers protect existing firms from new entrants, making collusion more sustainable.
*   **Government Policies:** Antitrust laws aim to prevent collusion and promote competition.
*   **Product Differentiation:** Strong differentiation can reduce the incentive to engage in price wars, as firms compete on non-price factors.
*   **Past Behavior and Trust:** A history of cooperation or conflict influences current strategic decisions.

---

### 6. Efficiency and Welfare Implications

*   **Compared to Perfect Competition:** Oligopolies generally result in:
    *   **Higher prices:** Firms have market power and can charge prices above marginal cost.
    *   **Lower output:** Total output is less than in perfect competition.
    *   **Deadweight Loss:** A loss of economic efficiency occurs because the market produces less than the socially optimal amount.
*   **Compared to Monopoly:** Oligopolies are generally more efficient than monopolies, as there is some competition, leading to lower prices and higher output than a single monopolist would achieve.
*   **Non-Price Competition:** While beneficial for consumers (more choice, better quality), extensive advertising and product differentiation can be costly and may not always lead to genuine improvements in welfare. Some argue it leads to "wasteful advertising."
*   **Innovation:** Oligopolies can have incentives to innovate due to the potential for above-normal profits and the need to differentiate from rivals. However, the threat of rivals quickly imitating successful innovations can dampen this incentive.
*   **Potential for Collusion:** If successful, collusion leads to outcomes similar to monopoly, with significant welfare losses.

### 7. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** Which of the following is NOT a typical characteristic of an oligopoly?
    a) Few dominant firms
    b) Significant barriers to entry
    c) Price takers
    d) Interdependence among firms

**Answer:** c) Price takers. Firms in an oligopoly have market power and are not price takers; they are price makers to some extent, but their decisions are interdependent.

---

**Question 2:** In the Cournot model of duopoly, what do firms compete on?
    a) Price
    b) Output
    c) Advertising
    d) Product quality

**Answer:** b) Output. Firms simultaneously choose their output levels, assuming their rival's output is fixed.

---

**Question 3:** The Bertrand model of duopoly, with homogeneous products, predicts that the equilibrium price will be equal to:
    a) Monopoly price
    b) Average total cost
    c) Marginal cost
    d) Marginal revenue

**Answer:** c) Marginal cost. This is due to the intense price competition driving prices down to the cost of production.

---

**Question 4:** Explain why cartels tend to be unstable.

**Answer:** Cartels tend to be unstable because each member firm has a strong incentive to cheat on the agreement by secretly increasing its output or lowering its price. By doing so, it can capture a larger market share and increase its individual profits, even if it harms the cartel as a whole in the long run. The risk of detection and punishment from other members is often outweighed by the short-term gain.

---

**Question 5:** Describe the "kinked demand curve" model and its implication for price stability in an oligopoly.

**Answer:** The kinked demand curve model suggests that an oligopolistic firm faces a demand curve that is more elastic for price increases (because rivals won't follow) and less elastic for price decreases (because rivals will match the price cut). This kink in the demand curve leads to a discontinuous marginal revenue curve. If the firm's marginal cost curve intersects the vertical portion of the marginal revenue curve, the firm will not change its price even if its marginal cost fluctuates within that range, leading to price stability.

---

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Interdependence is Key:** The defining feature of oligopoly is the strategic interaction between a small number of firms.
*   **No Single Model:** Due to interdependence, there isn't one definitive model for oligopoly; Cournot, Bertrand, and Stackelberg offer different perspectives.
*   **Barriers to Entry Matter:** High barriers protect existing firms and make oligopolies more likely to persist.
*   **Collusion vs. Competition:** Firms face a constant tension between the benefits of cooperating (collusion) and the potential gains from competing aggressively.
*   **Welfare Implications:** Oligopolies generally lead to less efficient outcomes than perfect competition but are often more efficient than pure monopolies.
*   **Strategic Thinking:** Understanding oligopoly requires thinking about how your actions affect your rivals and how their anticipated reactions will affect you.
