# Environmental Law and Policy: Module 4 - Hazardous Substances and Activities
## Topic: Law of Treaties

This module explores the legal framework governing hazardous substances and activities, with a particular focus on the role of the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the rules it promulgates. This specific topic delves into how international treaties shape and inform domestic environmental law, especially concerning hazardous substances.

### 1. Fundamental Principles of International Treaty Law

Treaties are the cornerstone of international law, serving as formal agreements between sovereign states. They create rights and obligations for the parties involved.

*   **Definition of a Treaty:** A treaty is a legally binding agreement between two or more states, or between a state and an international organization, governed by international law. It can be called a treaty, convention, agreement, protocol, covenant, charter, or exchange of notes.

*   **Pacta Sunt Servanda (Agreements Must Be Kept):** This is the fundamental principle of treaty law, meaning that treaties are binding on the parties and must be performed in good faith. (Article 26 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties - VCLT).

*   **Sovereignty:** While treaties bind states, they generally do not create obligations or rights for third states without their consent. (Article 34 VCLT).

*   **Reservations:** States can, in certain circumstances, make reservations to a treaty, excluding or modifying the legal effect of certain provisions in their application to that state. However, reservations cannot be contrary to the object and purpose of the treaty. (Article 19 VCLT).

*   **Interpretation:** Treaties must be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose. (Article 31 VCLT).

### 2. Key Stages in the Life Cycle of a Treaty

Treaties, like any legal instrument, go through a process from creation to termination.

*   **Negotiation and Adoption:** States negotiate the text of a treaty, often within international forums (e.g., United Nations). Once a text is agreed upon, it is "adopted" by the participating states.

*   **Authentication:** The adopted text is authenticated, confirming it is the final and definitive version.

*   **Expression of Consent to be Bound:** States must formally agree to be bound by the treaty. This can be done through:
    *   **Signature:** For some treaties, signature alone can express consent.
    *   **Ratification:** A more formal process where a state's competent authority (e.g., legislature) approves the treaty.
    *   **Acceptance, Approval, or Accession:** Similar to ratification, these are formal acts expressing consent.

*   **Entry into Force:** A treaty enters into force when the required number of states have expressed their consent to be bound. This is often specified in the treaty itself.

*   **Registration and Publication:** Treaties are registered with the Secretariat of the United Nations and published. This promotes transparency. (Article 102 of the UN Charter).

*   **Application and Implementation:** States are expected to apply the treaty provisions. This is where domestic law plays a crucial role.

*   **Amendment and Modification:** Treaties can be amended or modified by agreement between the parties.

*   **Termination or Withdrawal:** A treaty can be terminated or a state can withdraw from it under specific conditions, such as mutual consent, material breach, supervening impossibility of performance, or fundamental change of circumstances. (Articles 54-62 VCLT).

### 3. Relationship Between International Treaties and Domestic Law (Focus on US Law & EPA)

The effectiveness of international environmental agreements often depends on how they are integrated into national legal systems. The US has a specific approach to this.

*   **Monism vs. Dualism:**
    *   **Monist Systems:** International law is automatically part of domestic law.
    *   **Dualist Systems:** International law and domestic law are separate systems. International law must be "transformed" or "incorporated" into domestic law to be effective.
    *   **The US System:** The US generally operates on a dualist approach, though with some nuances.

*   **Self-Executing Treaties:**
    *   **Definition:** A self-executing treaty is one that becomes domestic law automatically upon ratification, without the need for further legislative action. Its provisions are considered sufficiently clear and precise to be applied by domestic courts.
    *   **Example:** Treaties that grant individuals private rights or impose obligations directly enforceable in domestic courts are often considered self-executing. However, the determination is complex and depends on the intent of the parties and the language of the treaty.

*   **Non-Self-Executing Treaties:**
    *   **Definition:** A non-self-executing treaty requires implementing legislation by Congress before its provisions become domestic law and enforceable by domestic courts.
    *   **Example:** Many environmental treaties, which require states to take certain actions or achieve certain goals, are often non-self-executing. They set international standards that the US then translates into domestic regulations.

*   **The Role of the President and Congress:**
    *   **President:** Negotiates and signs treaties.
    *   **Senate:** Provides advice and consent for ratification (requires a two-thirds vote).
    *   **Congress:** May pass implementing legislation for non-self-executing treaties.

*   **The EPA's Role:**
    *   The EPA is a federal agency tasked with implementing and enforcing environmental laws.
    *   When the US becomes a party to an environmental treaty (whether self-executing or not), the EPA is often responsible for developing and enforcing the domestic regulations necessary to meet the treaty's obligations.
    *   This can involve creating new rules under existing statutes (like the Clean Air Act or Clean Water Act) or advocating for new legislation.

### 4. How International Treaties Influence EPA Regulations on Hazardous Substances

International agreements are crucial drivers for domestic environmental policy, particularly concerning hazardous substances that transcend borders.

*   **Setting International Standards:** Treaties often establish international norms, standards, and best practices for the management of hazardous substances and activities. The EPA then uses these as benchmarks for its regulatory actions.

*   **Addressing Transboundary Pollution:** Many hazardous substances can travel across national borders through air, water, or trade. Treaties are essential for coordinating responses and preventing states from offloading environmental burdens onto their neighbors.

*   **Examples of Influential Treaties:**

    *   **Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal:**
        *   **Purpose:** To reduce the movement of hazardous waste from developed to less developed countries and to ensure that wastes are managed in an environmentally sound manner.
        *   **EPA Influence:** The EPA implements the Basel Convention through the **Hazardous Waste Management International Shipments (HWMI) Regulations** under the **Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA)**. These regulations control the import and export of hazardous waste.

    *   **Rotterdam Convention on the Prior Informed Consent Procedure for Certain Hazardous Chemicals and Pesticides in International Trade:**
        *   **Purpose:** To promote shared responsibility and cooperative efforts among parties in the international trade of certain hazardous chemicals in order to protect human health and the environment from potential harm. It establishes a prior informed consent (PIC) procedure.
        *   **EPA Influence:** The EPA, along with other US agencies, plays a role in designating chemicals for the PIC procedure and communicating PIC decisions to other countries.

    *   **Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs):**
        *   **Purpose:** To eliminate or restrict the production, use, and release of POPs, which are chemicals that remain intact in the environment for long periods, become widely distributed geographically, accumulate in the fatty tissue of living organisms, and are toxic to humans and wildlife.
        *   **EPA Influence:** The EPA implements the Stockholm Convention through various domestic regulations, including those under the **Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA)** and RCRA, to control the production, use, and disposal of POPs.

    *   **Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer:**
        *   **Purpose:** To phase out the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances (ODS) like chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs).
        *   **EPA Influence:** The EPA implements the Montreal Protocol through the **Clean Air Act**, specifically **Title VI – Stratospheric Ozone Protection**. This involves regulations on ODS production, import, export, and use, as well as leak prevention and recovery.

*   **Treaty Obligations as Driving Force for Regulation:** The US's commitment to these treaties often compels the EPA to develop or strengthen domestic regulations to ensure compliance, even if domestic pressures for such regulation were less intense.

### 5. Importance of Treaties in Addressing Transboundary Environmental Issues

Hazardous substances and activities rarely respect national borders. International cooperation, formalized through treaties, is essential for effective environmental protection.

*   **Global Commons:** Issues like climate change, ozone depletion, and the spread of hazardous waste affect the entire planet or large regions. No single nation can solve these problems alone.

*   **Level Playing Field:** Treaties can create a more level playing field for businesses by establishing common environmental standards, preventing "pollution havens" where industries relocate to countries with weaker regulations.

*   **Information Sharing and Cooperation:** Treaties facilitate the exchange of scientific data, technological expertise, and best practices between countries, which is vital for understanding and managing complex environmental risks.

*   **Dispute Resolution:** Treaties often include mechanisms for resolving disputes that may arise between parties regarding treaty obligations.

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Pacta Sunt Servanda:** Treaties are binding and must be performed in good faith.
*   **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT):** The primary international agreement codifying treaty law.
*   **Self-Executing vs. Non-Self-Executing:** Crucial distinction for how treaties become domestic law in the US.
*   **Implementing Legislation:** Congress often enacts laws to bring non-self-executing treaties into effect.
*   **EPA's Role:** The EPA is the primary agency responsible for implementing international environmental treaty obligations through domestic regulations.
*   **Transboundary Nature:** Many hazardous substance issues require international cooperation, making treaties essential.
*   **Examples of Key Treaties:** Basel, Rotterdam, Stockholm, and Montreal Protocols are critical for understanding the EPA's international environmental law responsibilities.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define the principle of *pacta sunt servanda* and explain its significance in international treaty law.

**Answer 1:**
*Pacta sunt servanda* is a fundamental principle of international law that means "agreements must be kept." It signifies that treaties are legally binding on the parties that have consented to them and must be performed in good faith. Its significance lies in establishing the foundational obligation for states to adhere to their treaty commitments, without which international law and cooperation would be unworkable.

**Question 2:**
Distinguish between self-executing and non-self-executing treaties in the U.S. legal system. Provide an example of a type of environmental treaty that is likely to be non-self-executing.

**Answer 2:**
A **self-executing treaty** is one whose provisions automatically become domestic law upon ratification, without the need for further legislative action. Its terms are typically precise enough to be applied directly by domestic courts.

A **non-self-executing treaty** requires implementing legislation passed by Congress to become domestic law and enforceable in U.S. courts.

An example of a type of environmental treaty likely to be non-self-executing is one that requires a state to achieve specific environmental goals or adopt certain regulatory approaches, as these often necessitate detailed domestic implementation plans and legislative action. For instance, a treaty requiring a reduction in a certain pollutant by a specific date might require Congress to pass legislation authorizing the EPA to set specific national emission standards.

**Question 3:**
Explain how the Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal influences EPA regulations.

**Answer 3:**
The Basel Convention aims to control the transboundary movement of hazardous wastes. The EPA implements this convention in the U.S. through the **Hazardous Waste Management International Shipments (HWMI) Regulations** under the **Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA)**. These regulations govern the import and export of hazardous waste, ensuring that such movements are conducted in an environmentally sound manner and with prior informed consent from importing countries, aligning U.S. practices with the Convention's objectives.

**Question 4:**
Why are international treaties particularly important for addressing issues related to hazardous substances and activities?

**Answer 4:**
International treaties are crucial for addressing hazardous substances and activities because these issues often transcend national borders. Hazardous substances can spread through air, water, or trade, affecting multiple countries. Treaties enable:
*   **Global Cooperation:** To coordinate responses and prevent transboundary pollution.
*   **Standard Setting:** To establish common environmental norms and prevent a "race to the bottom" in environmental regulations.
*   **Information Sharing:** To facilitate the exchange of scientific data and best practices.
*   **Collective Action:** To address global environmental problems like the depletion of the ozone layer or the spread of persistent organic pollutants.
