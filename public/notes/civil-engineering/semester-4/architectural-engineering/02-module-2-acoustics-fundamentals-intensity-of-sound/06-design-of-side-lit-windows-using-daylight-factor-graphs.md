### 1. Introduction to Daylight Factor (DF)

#### Key Concept: Daylight Factor (DF)

**Definition:** Daylight Factor (DF) is the ratio of the illuminance at a point on a surface inside a building to the illuminance on a horizontal plane in the free atmosphere (unobstructed by the building), measured at the same instant. It is expressed as a percentage.

$$DF = \frac{\text{Illuminance inside}}{\text{Outdoor Horizontal Illuminance}} \times 100\%$$

#### Significance of DF:

*   **Quantifies daylight availability:** Provides a standardized metric to assess how much daylight reaches interior spaces.
*   **Design tool:** Enables architects and engineers to predict and control interior daylight levels.
*   **Energy efficiency:** Optimizing daylight reduces reliance on artificial lighting, leading to energy savings.
*   **Occupant comfort and well-being:** Adequate daylight improves visual comfort, reduces eye strain, and enhances mood.
*   **Building performance assessment:** Used in sustainability certifications (e.g., LEED, BREEAM).

#### Types of Illuminance Contributing to DF:

*   **Sky Component (SC):** The illuminance directly from the visible sky.
*   **Externally Reflected Component (ERC):** The illuminance reflected by external surfaces (e.g., adjacent buildings, ground) onto the point inside.
*   **Internally Reflected Component (IRC):** The illuminance that has been reflected from internal surfaces (e.g., walls, ceilings, floors) before reaching the point.

$$DF = SC + ERC + IRC$$

### 2. Factors Influencing Daylight Factor

Several factors significantly impact the Daylight Factor within a room:

*   **Window Size and Shape:** Larger windows generally allow more daylight. The shape and aspect ratio also play a role.
*   **Window Position and Orientation:**
    *   **Height:** Higher windows capture more direct sky.
    *   **Placement:** Centered windows can provide more uniform illumination than off-center ones.
    *   **Orientation:** North-facing windows (in the Northern Hemisphere) provide relatively constant and diffuse daylight, ideal for avoiding glare. East and West facing windows receive direct sunlight, which can cause glare and overheating. South-facing windows receive direct sunlight for a significant portion of the day, requiring shading strategies.
*   **Room Geometry:**
    *   **Depth and Width:** Deeper rooms generally have lower DF values further from the window.
    *   **Ceiling Height:** Higher ceilings can help distribute daylight more effectively.
    *   **Room Aspect Ratio:** The ratio of length to width influences how light spreads.
*   **Surface Reflectances:**
    *   **Internal Surfaces:** Lighter and more reflective surfaces (walls, ceiling, floor) increase the Internally Reflected Component (IRC), improving light distribution and DF at greater depths. Darker surfaces absorb more light.
    *   **External Surfaces:** Reflective external surfaces can contribute to the Externally Reflected Component (ERC).
*   **External Obstructions:** Adjacent buildings, trees, and other structures can block direct sky and reflected light, significantly reducing DF.
*   **Glazing Properties:**
    *   **Transmittance:** The amount of visible light that passes through the glass.
    *   **Shading Devices:** Internal or external shading (blinds, louvres, overhangs) can reduce daylight penetration, especially direct sunlight.
*   **Sky Type:** The DF is dependent on the type of sky (e.g., overcast, clear, partly cloudy). Most DF calculations are based on a standard **uniform overcast sky**.

### 3. Understanding Daylight Factor Graphs and Tables

Daylight Factor is often presented visually through graphs and numerically in tables.

#### 2.1. Daylight Factor Graphs (Contour Maps)

*   **Representation:** These are plan views of a room showing lines of constant DF (isofootcandles or isollux lines for daylight).
*   **Interpretation:**
    *   **Closer contour lines:** Indicate areas where the DF changes rapidly.
    *   **Spaced contour lines:** Indicate areas with more uniform DF.
    *   **Numerical values on contours:** Represent the percentage of DF at that location.
*   **Purpose:** Help visualize the distribution of daylight across the floor plan and identify areas that might be too dark or too bright.

#### 2.2. Daylight Factor Tables

*   **Representation:** Typically show DF values at specific points within a room, often at standard working plane heights (e.g., 0.85m above the floor).
*   **Commonly Tabulated Points:**
    *   **Center of the room**
    *   **Mid-point between window and back wall**
    *   **Points at various depths from the window**
    *   **Points at different lateral positions across the room width**
*   **Purpose:** Provide quantitative data for assessing the illuminance levels at critical locations.

### 4. Design of Side-Lit Windows using Daylight Factor

Side-lit windows, a common approach in architectural design, involve windows placed on one or more of the room's walls.

#### Design Process:

1.  **Define Target DF:** Determine the required DF for the space based on its function and desired illuminance levels. General guidelines exist:
    *   **General Offices:** 2-5%
    *   **Classrooms:** 3-5%
    *   **Reading Rooms:** 4-6%
    *   **Corridors:** 1-2%

2.  **Model the Room:** Create a simplified representation of the room, including its dimensions, window size and placement, and internal surface reflectances.

3.  **Utilize DF Calculation Tools/Software:**
    *   **Manual Calculations:** Can be complex and time-consuming, often involving simplified formulas or nomograms.
    *   **Software:** Specialized lighting design software (e.g., DIALux, Relux, Radiance) can perform detailed DF calculations and generate contour plots.
    *   **Online Calculators/Spreadsheets:** For simpler cases, online tools or pre-built spreadsheets can provide DF estimates.

4.  **Analyze DF Graphs/Tables:**
    *   **Uniformity:** Check the ratio of the minimum DF to the maximum DF in the room to ensure a comfortable visual environment. A common target is a uniformity ratio of at least 0.4 (minimum DF is 40% of the maximum DF).
    *   **Depth Penetration:** Ensure adequate DF levels are achieved at points furthest from the window.
    *   **Potential for Glare:** While DF itself doesn't directly measure glare, very high DF values near the window, especially with direct sunlight penetration, can indicate a glare risk.

5.  **Iterate and Refine Window Design:** Based on the analysis, adjust:
    *   **Window Size:** Increase or decrease to achieve target DF values.
    *   **Window Height:** Optimize for daylight penetration and glare control.
    *   **Window Placement:** Adjust laterally for better light distribution.
    *   **Surface Reflectances:** Consider painting walls and ceilings lighter colors.
    *   **Shading Strategies:** If glare is an issue, consider internal blinds or external louvres.

#### Example Scenario: Designing a Classroom Window

**Objective:** Design a side-lit window for a classroom to achieve a minimum average DF of 4% on the working plane.

**Room Dimensions:**
*   Width: 8m
*   Depth: 10m
*   Height: 3m

**Initial Assumptions:**
*   Working plane height: 0.85m
*   Wall reflectance: 70%
*   Ceiling reflectance: 80%
*   Floor reflectance: 30%
*   Window on one of the 8m walls.

**Step 1: Window Size Estimation**

A common rule of thumb for side-lit spaces is that the window-to-floor area ratio influences DF. For classrooms, a ratio of **1:10** is often a starting point.

*   Floor area = 8m * 10m = 80 m²
*   Target window area = 80 m² / 10 = 8 m²

If the window is placed on the 8m wall, a window height of 1m and a width of 8m would achieve this area. However, this is unrealistic. Let's consider a more practical window size:

*   Window width: 4m
*   Window height: 2m
*   Window area = 4m * 2m = 8 m²

**Step 2: Placement on the Wall**

*   **Lateral Placement:** Center the 4m wide window on the 8m wall. This would place it from 2m to 6m along the wall.
*   **Vertical Placement:** Position the bottom of the window at 0.85m (working plane height) for maximum penetration, but this would be too low and cause glare. A more practical starting point is to place the bottom of the window at 0.85m to 1m above the floor, and the top as high as possible (e.g., 0.5m from the ceiling). Let's assume the window spans from 1m to 3m height on the 8m wall.

**Step 3: DF Analysis (Using simplified DF calculation principles/software simulation)**

Using a DF calculation tool or approximation:

*   **Scenario A: Window (4m wide, 2m high) placed centrally on the 8m wall, bottom at 1m.**
    *   **DF at desk closest to window (1m from wall):** ~8-12% (depending on exact placement and sky)
    *   **DF at mid-point (5m from wall):** ~3-5%
    *   **DF at back wall (10m from wall):** ~1-2%

**Evaluation:** The average DF might be around the target of 4%, but the DF at the back wall is quite low. Uniformity might also be an issue.

**Step 4: Refinement**

*   **Increase Window Size:** If DF is consistently too low, increase the window size. For instance, a 5m wide, 2.5m high window.
*   **Improve Internal Reflectances:** Ensure walls and ceiling are light-colored.
*   **Consider Ceiling Slot/Light Shelf:** A light shelf (an external or internal horizontal projection that reflects daylight onto the ceiling) can bounce light deeper into the room and improve uniformity.
*   **Add a second window:** If one window doesn't suffice, consider adding a second, smaller window or extending the window length.
*   **Adjust Window Height:** Placing the window higher (e.g., bottom at 1.2m, top at 2.8m) can improve penetration, but might increase glare if not managed.

**Iteration Example:** Let's say the analysis shows the DF at the back wall is too low (1%). We might consider:

*   **Increasing window width to 5m and height to 2.5m.** This will increase the DF throughout the room.
*   **Optimizing internal reflectances.** Ensure walls are at least 70% and ceiling 80%.

By simulating these changes, we can then re-evaluate the DF values and uniformity.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define Daylight Factor and list the three components that contribute to it.

**Answer 1:**
Daylight Factor (DF) is the ratio of the illuminance inside a building to the outdoor horizontal illuminance, expressed as a percentage. The three components are:
1.  **Sky Component (SC):** Direct illuminance from the visible sky.
2.  **Externally Reflected Component (ERC):** Illuminance reflected from external surfaces.
3.  **Internally Reflected Component (IRC):** Illuminance reflected from internal surfaces.

---

**Question 2:**
A classroom has a target average DF of 5%. If the DF at the front of the room near the window is 15% and the DF at the back of the room is 2%, what is the uniformity ratio? Is this considered good uniformity for a classroom?

**Answer 2:**
Uniformity Ratio = Minimum DF / Maximum DF = 2% / 15% = 0.133 (or 13.3%)
This uniformity ratio is very low. A generally accepted minimum for good uniformity is 0.4 (40%). This classroom has poor uniformity, with a significant drop-off in daylight from the front to the back.

---

**Question 3:**
List four architectural design features that can improve the Daylight Factor at the rear of a side-lit room.

**Answer 3:**
Four features that can improve DF at the rear of a side-lit room:
1.  **Increasing window size:** Larger windows allow more light penetration.
2.  **Increasing window height:** Higher windows can direct light deeper into the room.
3.  **Using lighter/higher reflectance internal surfaces:** Particularly on walls and ceilings, to increase the IRC.
4.  **Implementing a light shelf:** A light shelf bounces daylight onto the ceiling, helping it penetrate further into the space.
5.  **Reducing room depth relative to width:** A shallower room will naturally have better DF at the rear.
6.  **Optimizing window placement:** Ensure the window is positioned to maximize sky view and minimize obstructions.

**Question 4 (Design Exercise):**
You are designing an office space that is 6m wide, 8m deep, and 3m high. You are using a side-lit window on one of the 6m walls. You want to achieve a minimum DF of 2% at the furthest point from the window (8m away).
Consider two window design options:

*   **Option A:** A window 3m wide and 2m high, with its sill at 1m from the floor.
*   **Option B:** A window 4m wide and 2m high, with its sill at 1.2m from the floor.

Without specific software, discuss which option is more likely to meet your minimum DF requirement at the rear of the room and why, considering the factors influencing DF.

**Answer 4:**
**Analysis:**

*   **Room dimensions:** 6m (width) x 8m (depth) x 3m (height)
*   **Target at rear (8m depth):** Minimum DF of 2%
*   **Window Wall:** 6m wide
*   **Internal Surface Reflectances:** Assume standard values (e.g., walls 70%, ceiling 80%, floor 30%)

**Option A:** Window 3m wide x 2m high, sill at 1m.
*   **Window Area:** 6 m²
*   **Window-to-Floor Ratio:** 6 m² / (6m * 8m) = 6 m² / 48 m² = 1:8

**Option B:** Window 4m wide x 2m high, sill at 1.2m.
*   **Window Area:** 8 m²
*   **Window-to-Floor Ratio:** 8 m² / 48 m² = 1:6

**Discussion:**

*   **Window Area/Ratio:** Option B has a larger window area and a more favorable window-to-floor ratio (1:6 vs 1:8). A larger window generally leads to higher DF values throughout the room, including at the rear.
*   **Window Height/Sill Level:** Option B has a slightly higher sill (1.2m vs 1m). While a higher sill might seem to reduce penetration, the increased window width in Option B is likely to compensate. A higher sill can also help prevent low-angle glare. However, the total window height is the same.
*   **Lateral Placement:** Assuming both windows are centered on the 6m wall, Option B (4m wide) will be closer to the side walls than Option A (3m wide). This can have a minor impact on lateral light distribution but is less critical for the depth penetration.

**Conclusion:**

**Option B is more likely to meet the minimum DF requirement of 2% at the furthest point (8m depth).** This is primarily due to its larger window area and more favourable window-to-floor ratio, which will contribute to a higher Sky Component and Externally Reflected Component reaching deeper into the room. The slightly higher sill in Option B is a trade-off that might be acceptable if it helps control glare.

To confirm, a precise DF calculation for both options would be necessary. If Option B still falls short, further adjustments like increasing window height or improving internal reflectances would be needed.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Daylight Factor is a static measure:** It assumes a uniform overcast sky and doesn't account for direct sunlight or the sun's movement.
*   **DF is a tool, not the sole criterion:** Always consider glare, visual comfort, and thermal performance alongside DF.
*   **Uniformity is crucial:** Aim for a relatively consistent DF across the usable area of the room.
*   **Light colours matter:** High reflectance values for internal surfaces significantly boost the Internally Reflected Component (IRC).
*   **Context is key:** The required DF varies significantly based on the room's intended use.
*   **Software is your friend:** For accurate and detailed analysis, use lighting design software.
*   **Iteration is normal:** Window design is an iterative process; be prepared to adjust parameters based on performance analysis.
