# DESIGN OF CONCRETE STRUCTURES - Module 1: Properties of Concrete and Steel

## Topic: Properties of Concrete and Steel

This module introduces the fundamental materials used in concrete structures: concrete and steel. Understanding their individual properties is crucial for designing safe, durable, and economical structures.

2.  **Explain the key properties of concrete relevant to structural design.**

5.  **Explain the key properties of steel relevant to structural design.**

### 1. Constituents of Concrete and Their Roles

Concrete is a composite material made from cement, aggregates (fine and coarse), water, and often admixtures.

*   **Cement:**
    *   **Definition:** A binder that hardens and binds other materials together. Typically Portland cement.
    *   **Role:** Reacts with water (hydration) to form a paste that coats aggregates and binds them, creating a solid mass. It is the "glue" of concrete.
    *   **Key Types (for context):**
        *   **Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC):** Most common type.
        *   **Portland Pozzolana Cement (PPC):** Contains pozzolanic materials, offering improved durability and resistance to aggressive environments.
*   **Aggregates:**
    *   **Definition:** Inert granular materials that form the bulk of concrete.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Fine Aggregates (Sand):** Typically natural or crushed rock with particle sizes generally below 4.75 mm.
            *   **Role:** Fills the voids between coarse aggregates, improves workability, and contributes to the overall density.
        *   **Coarse Aggregates (Gravel or Crushed Stone):** Typically crushed rock or gravel with particle sizes generally above 4.75 mm.
            *   **Role:** Provides strength, stiffness, and reduces the shrinkage of concrete. They are the primary load-bearing components.
    *   **Importance of Quality:** Aggregates must be clean, hard, durable, and free from organic matter and deleterious substances that can affect the properties of concrete.
*   **Water:**
    *   **Definition:** Essential for the chemical reaction with cement.
    *   **Role:**
        *   Initiates the hydration process, forming a gel-like structure that binds the aggregates.
        *   Provides workability to the fresh concrete, allowing it to be mixed, transported, and placed.
    *   **Quality:** Water should be clean and free from impurities like salts, acids, alkalis, sugars, and organic matter, as these can adversely affect the strength and durability of concrete.
*   **Admixtures (Optional):**
    *   **Definition:** Materials added to concrete before or during mixing, other than cement, water, and aggregates.
    *   **Purpose:** To modify the properties of fresh or hardened concrete.
    *   **Common Types:**
        *   **Plasticizers/Superplasticizers:** Improve workability without adding more water (water reducers). This leads to higher strength.
        *   **Retarders:** Slow down the setting time of concrete, useful in hot weather or for long-haul transportation.
        *   **Accelerators:** Speed up the setting and hardening time, useful in cold weather or for early strength gain.
        *   **Air-entraining agents:** Introduce microscopic air bubbles, improving resistance to freeze-thaw cycles and de-icing salts.
        *   **Pozzolans (e.g., fly ash, silica fume):** Enhance durability, reduce permeability, and improve strength at later ages.

### 2. Key Properties of Concrete Relevant to Structural Design

The performance of concrete in a structure is determined by its various properties.

*   **Strength:**
    *   **Compressive Strength ($f_{ck}$ or $f'_{c}$):** The most important property. It's the maximum stress concrete can withstand before failure under compression.
        *   **Definition:** Measured by crushing standard concrete cylinders or cubes after 28 days of curing.
        *   **Characteristic Compressive Strength ($f_{ck}$):** The strength of concrete below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall. This is the design value.
        *   **Cube vs. Cylinder Strength:** Cube strength is generally higher than cylinder strength for the same concrete mix. Design codes typically specify which to use.
    *   **Tensile Strength:** Concrete is weak in tension. Its tensile strength is about 8-15% of its compressive strength.
        *   **Importance:** Due to its low tensile strength, concrete cannot effectively resist tensile stresses. This is why steel reinforcement is embedded within concrete.
    *   **Flexural Strength (Modulus of Rupture):** The tensile stress in the outermost fibers of a concrete beam or slab at failure.
        *   **Importance:** Relevant for designing elements subjected to bending, like beams and slabs.
    *   **Shear Strength:** The ability to resist shear forces.
*   **Workability:**
    *   **Definition:** The ease with which fresh concrete can be mixed, transported, placed, compacted, and finished without segregation.
    *   **Measurement:** Commonly measured using the **slump test**. A higher slump indicates greater workability (more fluid concrete).
    *   **Importance:** Proper workability ensures that concrete can be placed around reinforcement and into formwork, achieving a dense and homogeneous structure.
*   **Durability:**
    *   **Definition:** The ability of concrete to resist weathering action, chemical attack, abrasion, and other degradative processes throughout its service life.
    *   **Factors Affecting Durability:**
        *   **Permeability:** Low permeability concrete resists the ingress of aggressive substances (water, chlorides, sulfates). Achieved through low water-cement ratio and proper curing.
        *   **Resistance to Chemical Attack:** Depends on cement type and presence of protective measures against sulfates, acids, etc.
        *   **Resistance to Freeze-Thaw Cycles:** Air-entrained concrete is essential in cold climates.
        *   **Abrasion Resistance:** Important for surfaces like floors and pavements.
*   **Shrinkage:**
    *   **Definition:** A reduction in volume of concrete due to loss of moisture.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Plastic Shrinkage:** Occurs within hours of placing, before setting, due to rapid surface drying.
        *   **Drying Shrinkage:** Occurs over a longer period as moisture evaporates from the hardened concrete.
    *   **Effects:** Can lead to cracking if not managed through proper curing and jointing.
*   **Creep:**
    *   **Definition:** The gradual increase in strain in concrete under a sustained load over time, even at constant stress.
    *   **Effects:** Can lead to long-term deflections in structural members and redistribution of stresses. It is more pronounced in concrete with higher water content and at higher temperatures.
*   **Thermal Properties:**
    *   **Coefficient of Thermal Expansion:** Concrete expands and contracts with temperature changes. The coefficient is similar to that of steel, which is beneficial for reinforced concrete.
    *   **Thermal Conductivity:** Affects heat transfer through concrete elements, relevant for fire resistance and thermal insulation.
*   **Modulus of Elasticity (Young's Modulus, $E_c$):**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region. It represents the stiffness of concrete.
    *   **Importance:** Affects the deflection and cracking of concrete members under load. A higher modulus means a stiffer material. It depends on the strength and type of aggregates.

### 3. Factors Influencing Concrete Properties

Several factors can significantly alter the performance of concrete.

*   **Mix Proportions (Water-Cement Ratio, Aggregate Content, Cement Content):**
    *   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):** The most critical factor. Lower w/c ratio leads to higher strength, lower permeability, and improved durability but reduces workability.
    *   **Aggregate Properties:** Size, shape, texture, and grading of aggregates affect workability, strength, and shrinkage.
    *   **Cement Content:** Higher cement content generally increases strength and heat of hydration.
*   **Curing:**
    *   **Definition:** Maintaining adequate moisture content and temperature in concrete for a specific period after placing.
    *   **Importance:** Essential for proper hydration of cement and development of strength and durability. Inadequate curing can lead to reduced strength, increased permeability, and surface cracking.
    *   **Methods:** Water curing (ponding, spraying), membrane curing.
*   **Admixtures:** As discussed earlier, admixtures are used to tailor specific properties.
*   **Age of Concrete:** Strength and stiffness of concrete increase with age, especially during the early stages of hydration.
*   **Environmental Conditions:** Temperature, humidity, and exposure to aggressive agents during mixing, placing, curing, and service life.
*   **Compaction:** Proper compaction removes entrapped air, leading to a denser and stronger concrete.
*   **Mixing:** Thorough and consistent mixing ensures a homogeneous concrete mix.

### 4. Types of Steel Used in Reinforced Concrete Construction

Steel is used as reinforcement to carry tensile stresses that concrete cannot resist.

*   **Steel Reinforcing Bars (Rebars):**
    *   **Definition:** Steel bars of various diameters, typically with deformed surfaces to enhance bond with concrete.
    *   **Types (based on strength and form):**
        *   **Mild Steel Bars (e.g., Fe 250):** Historically used, characterized by a yielding stress of 250 MPa. Low carbon content, ductile.
        *   **High Yield Strength Deformed (HYSD) Bars (e.g., Fe 415, Fe 500, Fe 550):** The most common type today. Higher yield strength (415 MPa, 500 MPa, 550 MPa respectively) and possess ribs or deformations for better bond. These are preferred for efficiency in design.
        *   **Stainless Steel Bars:** Used in corrosive environments (e.g., marine structures) for enhanced durability, though more expensive.
*   **Steel Wires and Strands:**
    *   **Definition:** High-strength steel wires, often used in pre-stressed and post-tensioned concrete.
    *   **Application:** Used for bridges, long-span beams, and other structures requiring very high tensile capacity.
*   **Welded Wire Fabric (WWF) / Steel Fabric:**
    *   **Definition:** A mesh of steel wires welded together at their intersections.
    *   **Application:** Commonly used in concrete slabs, pavements, and walls for crack control and as secondary reinforcement.

### 5. Key Properties of Steel Relevant to Structural Design

Understanding steel's mechanical properties is critical for its role in reinforced concrete.

*   **Yield Strength ($f_y$):**
    *   **Definition:** The stress at which steel begins to deform plastically (permanently).
    *   **Importance:** This is the primary design parameter for steel reinforcement. It dictates the load-carrying capacity of the steel.
    *   **Characteristic Yield Strength ($f_y$):** The yield strength below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall.
*   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($f_u$):**
    *   **Definition:** The maximum stress steel can withstand before fracture.
    *   **Importance:** Indicates the ductility and toughness of the steel. The ratio of ultimate to yield strength ($f_u/f_y$) is an indicator of ductility.
*   **Modulus of Elasticity (Young's Modulus, $E_s$):**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region. For steel, it is approximately 200 GPa, regardless of the grade.
    *   **Importance:** Affects the stiffness of reinforced concrete members and the amount of strain steel can undergo before yielding.
*   **Ductility:**
    *   **Definition:** The ability of steel to undergo significant plastic deformation before fracture.
    *   **Importance:** Ductility is crucial for reinforced concrete structures to provide warning signs (e.g., large deflections, visible cracking) before catastrophic failure. This allows for evacuation and timely repairs. It also allows the steel to yield and redistribute stresses.
*   **Bond Strength:**
    *   **Definition:** The force per unit area required to pull a reinforcing bar out of the concrete.
    *   **Importance:** Good bond is essential for the composite action between concrete and steel. Deformed bars provide a much better bond than plain bars due to mechanical interlock.
*   **Weldability:**
    *   **Definition:** The ability of steel to be joined by welding.
    *   **Importance:** For fabricating complex reinforcement cages or splicing bars.
*   **Corrosion Resistance:**
    *   **Definition:** The ability of steel to resist rust or degradation due to chemical reactions.
    *   **Importance:** Standard steel reinforcement is susceptible to corrosion, especially in the presence of chlorides and moisture, which can lead to expansion and cracking of concrete. Stainless steel or epoxy-coated rebar are used in aggressive environments.

### 6. Interaction Between Concrete and Steel in Reinforced Concrete

The success of reinforced concrete as a structural material relies on the effective interaction between concrete and steel.

*   **Composite Action:**
    *   **Definition:** Concrete and steel act together as a single unit to resist loads.
    *   **Mechanism:** The strong bond between concrete and steel, facilitated by the deformed surfaces of the rebar, allows stresses to be transferred between the two materials.
    *   **Benefits:** Concrete resists compression, while steel resists tension, utilizing the best properties of both materials.
*   **Bond Stress:**
    *   **Definition:** The shear stress developed at the interface between the steel reinforcement and the surrounding concrete.
    *   **Transfer of Force:** Bond stress is the mechanism by which tensile forces are transferred from the concrete to the steel in tension zones and compressive forces are transferred from the concrete to the steel in compression zones.
    *   **Factors Affecting Bond:** Bar diameter, surface deformation (ribs), concrete strength, and cover to reinforcement.
*   **Co-efficient of Thermal Expansion:**
    *   **Similarity:** The coefficient of thermal expansion for steel is very close to that of concrete (approximately $12 \times 10^{-6} / ^\circ C$).
    *   **Advantage:** This similarity minimizes the development of internal stresses due to temperature variations, preventing cracking at the interface.
*   **Cracking of Concrete:**
    *   **Cause:** As concrete is stressed in tension, it cracks.
    *   **Steel's Role:** Steel reinforcement is placed to control the width of these cracks. When concrete cracks, the tensile stress is transferred to the steel, which can then deform elastically (or plastically) to accommodate the tension.
    *   **Crack Width Control:** Depends on the yield strength of the steel, the spacing of the bars, and the concrete cover. High-yield strength bars can lead to wider cracks if not spaced appropriately.
*   **Stress Distribution:**
    *   **Compression:** Concrete primarily carries compressive stresses.
    *   **Tension:** Steel reinforcement carries tensile stresses.
    *   **Shear:** Both concrete and steel contribute to shear resistance, with stirrups (shear reinforcement) often made of steel.

---

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **w/c Ratio is King:** The water-cement ratio is the most critical factor influencing concrete strength and durability.
*   **Concrete is Weak in Tension:** Steel reinforcement is essential to carry tensile forces.
*   **Ductility of Steel is Crucial:** It provides warning signs before failure.
*   **Bond is Key to Composite Action:** The bond between steel and concrete ensures they work together.
*   **Curing is Non-Negotiable:** Proper curing is vital for concrete to achieve its potential strength and durability.
*   **Steel's Thermal Expansion Matches Concrete's:** This is a fundamental advantage for reinforced concrete.

### Answers to Practice Questions

**Multiple Choice Questions:**

1.  **c) Compressive strength**
2.  **b) React with water to bind aggregates**
3.  **c) Higher strength and higher durability**
4.  **b) 200 GPa**
5.  **c) Allows for plastic deformation before failure, providing warning**

**Short Answer Questions:**

1.  **Cement:** Binder, reacts with water to form a paste. **Aggregates (fine & coarse):** Inert filler, provide bulk and strength. **Water:** Hydration agent, provides workability. **Admixtures (optional):** Modify properties.
2.  **Workability** is the ease of handling fresh concrete. Measured by the **slump test**.
3.  **Creep** is time-dependent deformation under sustained load, affecting deflections. **Shrinkage** is volume reduction due to moisture loss, potentially causing cracking.
4.  Steel reinforcement is necessary because concrete has very low tensile strength and cannot resist tensile stresses effectively. Steel takes up these tensile forces.
5.  Deformed bars have ribs/patterns that interlock with concrete, providing superior **bond strength** compared to plain bars, which rely solely on adhesion and friction.
6.  Similar thermal expansion coefficients ensure that both materials expand and contract at nearly the same rate with temperature changes. This minimizes internal stresses and prevents delamination or cracking at the concrete-steel interface.

**Problem Solving/Conceptual Question:**

Reducing the water-cement ratio from 0.5 to 0.4 will likely cause the following changes:

a)  **Compressive Strength:** Likely to **increase**. A lower w/c ratio results in a denser cement paste and fewer capillary pores, leading to higher strength.
b)  **Workability:** Likely to **decrease**. Less water means a stiffer, less fluid mix, making it more difficult to handle.
c)  **Durability (permeability):** Likely to **increase** (meaning permeability decreases). A lower w/c ratio creates a less permeable concrete, reducing the ingress of aggressive substances.
d)  **Drying Shrinkage:** Likely to **increase**. While a lower w/c ratio generally reduces porosity, the reduced water content itself can lead to more significant moisture loss and thus potentially higher drying shrinkage, though the effect of reduced pore volume can sometimes counteract this. The net effect can be complex and dependent on other factors.
