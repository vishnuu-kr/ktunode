# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts

## Topic: Sunk Cost

---

### 1. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Define** what a sunk cost is.
*   **Distinguish** between sunk costs and relevant costs in decision-making.
*   **Explain** why sunk costs should be ignored when making future decisions.
*   **Apply** the concept of sunk cost to various engineering and business scenarios.
*   **Analyze** how the sunk cost fallacy can lead to poor decisions.

---

### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

#### 2.1 What is a Sunk Cost?

A **sunk cost** is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. It is a past expenditure that is irrelevant to future decisions.

*   **Irrecoverable:** Once spent, the money is gone and cannot be recouped, regardless of any future actions taken.
*   **Past Expenditure:** It relates to a decision or action that has already happened.
*   **Irrelevant to Future Decisions:** Because sunk costs cannot be changed by any future decision, they should not influence the choice between alternative courses of action.

#### 2.2 Sunk Costs vs. Relevant Costs

It's crucial to differentiate sunk costs from costs that *are* relevant to future decisions.

*   **Relevant Costs:** These are future costs that differ between the available alternatives. They are the costs that a decision-maker can influence.
    *   **Opportunity Costs:** The value of the next best alternative forgone.
    *   **Incremental Costs (or Differential Costs):** The additional cost incurred by choosing one alternative over another.
    *   **Avoidable Costs:** Costs that can be eliminated by choosing a particular course of action.

**Key Distinction:** Sunk costs are **past** and **unavoidable**, while relevant costs are **future** and **avoidable** (or at least differ between choices).

---

### 3. Why Sunk Costs Should Be Ignored in Decision-Making

The fundamental principle of rational decision-making dictates that only future costs and benefits should be considered. Sunk costs, being past and unrecoverable, do not meet this criterion.

*   **Focus on the Future:** Decisions should be based on what will happen from this point forward, not on what has already happened.
*   **Maximizing Future Value:** Ignoring sunk costs helps decision-makers focus on choices that will yield the greatest future benefit or minimize future loss.
*   **Avoiding the "Sunk Cost Fallacy":** Continuing to invest in a failing project simply because significant resources have already been committed is a common cognitive bias known as the sunk cost fallacy. Rationality demands cutting losses when future prospects are dim, irrespective of past investments.

### 4. Examples

#### 4.1 Engineering Project Example

**Scenario:** A company has spent $500,000 on research and development for a new product. The product is not performing as well as expected in early testing, and market analysis now suggests it will not be profitable. The company has two options:

1.  **Continue Development:** Spend an additional $200,000 to finish development and launch the product, with an expected return of $150,000.
2.  **Abandon Project:** Stop all work and write off the $500,000 already spent.

**Analysis:**

*   **Sunk Cost:** $500,000 (already spent on R&D). This amount cannot be recovered.
*   **Relevant Costs:**
    *   Option 1: $200,000 (additional cost)
    *   Option 2: $0 (no further expenditure)
*   **Future Benefits:**
    *   Option 1: $150,000
    *   Option 2: $0

**Decision:**

*   **Option 1:** Net future outcome = $150,000 (benefit) - $200,000 (cost) = -$50,000
*   **Option 2:** Net future outcome = $0 (benefit) - $0 (cost) = $0

**Rational Decision:** Abandon the project. The $500,000 sunk cost should be ignored. Choosing to continue development will result in an additional loss of $50,000, whereas abandoning the project results in a net future outcome of $0.

#### 4.2 Software Development Example

**Scenario:** A software company spent $1 million developing a new accounting software package. After a year of development, a competitor launches a similar, superior product at a lower price. The company estimates that if they complete their software, they can sell it for a total revenue of $800,000, but it will cost another $300,000 to finish and market it.

**Analysis:**

*   **Sunk Cost:** $1 million (already spent).
*   **Relevant Costs:**
    *   Complete Software: $300,000
    *   Abandon Software: $0
*   **Future Benefits:**
    *   Complete Software: $800,000
    *   Abandon Software: $0

**Decision:**

*   **Complete Software:** Net future outcome = $800,000 (revenue) - $300,000 (cost) = $500,000
*   **Abandon Software:** Net future outcome = $0

**Rational Decision:** Complete the software. Even though $1 million was spent, the future prospect of earning $500,000 (revenue minus future costs) is better than earning nothing. The $1 million is a sunk cost and should not deter them from making a profitable future decision.

#### 4.3 Personal Decision Example

**Scenario:** You bought a non-refundable ticket for a concert for $100. On the day of the concert, you feel unwell, and the weather is terrible. You have an option to stay home and rest or go to the concert.

**Analysis:**

*   **Sunk Cost:** $100 (cost of the ticket, which cannot be refunded).
*   **Relevant Factors (Future Considerations):**
    *   **Going to the concert:** Cost = potential worsening of health, travel inconvenience. Benefit = enjoyment of the concert (if any).
    *   **Staying home:** Cost = missing the concert, feeling regret about the ticket. Benefit = rest and recovery.

**Decision:** The decision should be based on which option provides the greatest *overall* benefit or least *overall* disutility from this point forward. The $100 is gone regardless. If you feel too sick to enjoy the concert, staying home is the more rational choice, even though you "lose" the ticket price.

### 5. The Sunk Cost Fallacy

*   **Definition:** The sunk cost fallacy, also known as the "Concorde fallacy," is the tendency to continue an endeavor as a result of previously invested resources (time, money, or effort), even when it is evident that continuing is not the best decision.
*   **Why it happens:**
    *   **Desire to Avoid Waste:** People dislike admitting that a past investment was a mistake.
    *   **Escalation of Commitment:** A psychological tendency to stick with a course of action when faced with mounting evidence that it is a poor choice.
    *   **Emotional Attachment:** Sometimes, individuals become emotionally invested in a project.

**Consequences:** Pursuing failing projects, wasting further resources, missing out on more profitable opportunities.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define sunk cost in your own words.

**Answer 1:**
A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered, no matter what future decision is made. It's money spent in the past.

**Question 2:**
Identify the sunk cost in the following scenario:
A construction company has spent $1 million on materials and labor for a bridge project. Due to unexpected geological issues, the estimated cost to complete the bridge has doubled, and the projected future revenue from tolls is now insufficient to cover the increased costs. The company can either abandon the project or spend an additional $1.5 million to complete it.

**Answer 2:**
The sunk cost is the $1 million already spent on materials and labor.

**Question 3:**
Explain why a rational decision-maker should ignore sunk costs when evaluating the bridge project in Question 2.

**Answer 3:**
A rational decision-maker should ignore the $1 million sunk cost because it has already been spent and cannot be recovered. The decision to proceed or abandon should be based solely on the *future* costs and benefits. If the additional $1.5 million cost to complete the bridge still results in a net loss compared to the future revenue, it would be irrational to proceed, even though $1 million has already been invested. The focus should be on minimizing future losses.

**Question 4:**
A software company spent $50,000 on developing a mobile app. To launch it, they need to spend another $20,000. Market research indicates that the app will generate $60,000 in revenue. Should the company launch the app? Justify your answer.

**Answer 4:**
Yes, the company should launch the app.
*   **Sunk Cost:** $50,000 (already spent).
*   **Relevant Cost:** $20,000 (to launch).
*   **Future Benefit:** $60,000 (revenue).
*   **Net Future Outcome:** $60,000 (revenue) - $20,000 (cost) = $40,000.
Since the future benefits ($60,000) exceed the future costs ($20,000), resulting in a positive net future outcome of $40,000, it is rational to proceed. The $50,000 already spent is irrelevant to this decision.

**Question 5:**
What is the sunk cost fallacy, and provide an example of how an engineer might fall victim to it.

**Answer 5:**
The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to continue investing in a project or endeavor simply because significant resources have already been committed, even when evidence suggests it's no longer a good idea.
**Example:** An engineer leads a team developing a new automated manufacturing system. After two years and spending $500,000, it becomes clear that a new, more efficient technology has emerged, making their system outdated before it's even fully operational. However, the engineer, feeling responsible for the initial investment, pushes the team to continue with the original plan, spending an additional $200,000, rather than switching to the new technology, even though the new technology offers better long-term returns.

---

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Sunk costs are gone forever.** They cannot be recovered.
*   **Only future costs and benefits matter** for rational decision-making.
*   **Sunk costs are irrelevant** when choosing between alternatives.
*   **Be aware of the sunk cost fallacy** and avoid letting past investments dictate future choices.
*   **Focus on incremental analysis:** Compare the additional costs and benefits of each alternative.
*   **In engineering, this applies to R&D, capital projects, training, and any situation with prior expenditure.**
