# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS

## Module 1: Basic Economics Concepts

## Topic: Measurement of Elasticity and Its Applications

---

### **1. Introduction to Elasticity**

*   **Definition:** Elasticity is a measure of the responsiveness of one economic variable to a change in another. In simpler terms, it tells us how much one thing changes when something else changes.
*   **Importance for Engineers:** Understanding elasticity is crucial for engineers involved in:
    *   **Product Development & Pricing:** How will demand for a new product change with price?
    *   **Resource Allocation:** How will the demand for raw materials or labor respond to price fluctuations?
    *   **Market Analysis:** Understanding the sensitivity of consumers to price changes for their engineered products.
    *   **Forecasting:** Predicting the impact of economic changes on demand for engineering services or products.

---

### **2. Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)**

*   **Definition:** Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good or service to a change in its price.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \text{PED} = \frac{\text{Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\text{Percentage Change in Price}} $$
*   **Calculation:**
    *   Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded = $ \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{(Q_1 + Q_2)/2} \times 100 $
    *   Percentage Change in Price = $ \frac{P_2 - P_1}{(P_1 + P_2)/2} \times 100 $
    *   **Midpoint Formula (preferred for accuracy):** This formula uses the average of the initial and final quantities and prices to avoid the "initial price/quantity" bias.

    $$ \text{PED} = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1) / ((Q_1 + Q_2)/2)}{(P_2 - P_1) / ((P_1 + P_2)/2)} $$

*   **Interpretation of PED Values:**
    *   **Elastic Demand (PED > 1):** A small change in price leads to a larger percentage change in quantity demanded. Demand is very responsive.
        *   *Example:* Luxury goods, goods with many substitutes.
    *   **Inelastic Demand (PED < 1):** A change in price leads to a smaller percentage change in quantity demanded. Demand is not very responsive.
        *   *Example:* Necessities, goods with few substitutes (e.g., essential medications).
    *   **Unit Elastic Demand (PED = 1):** The percentage change in quantity demanded is exactly equal to the percentage change in price.
    *   **Perfectly Inelastic Demand (PED = 0):** Quantity demanded does not change at all, regardless of price changes.
        *   *Example:* Life-saving drugs with no alternatives. (Theoretical concept).
    *   **Perfectly Elastic Demand (PED = ∞):** Any increase in price leads to zero quantity demanded, and at a specific price, any quantity can be demanded.
        *   *Example:* A perfectly competitive market with homogeneous products. (Theoretical concept).

*   **Factors Affecting PED:**
    *   **Availability of Substitutes:** More substitutes lead to higher elasticity.
    *   **Necessity vs. Luxury:** Necessities tend to be inelastic; luxuries tend to be elastic.
    *   **Proportion of Income Spent:** Goods that represent a large portion of income tend to be more elastic.
    *   **Time Horizon:** Demand tends to become more elastic over longer periods as consumers have more time to adjust.
    *   **Definition of the Market:** A broader market definition (e.g., "food") is usually more inelastic than a narrow one (e.g., "apples").

*   **Application for Engineers:**
    *   **Pricing Strategies:** If demand for an engineered product is elastic, a price decrease might significantly increase sales and total revenue. If inelastic, a price increase might boost revenue.
    *   **Forecasting Demand:** Predicting how changes in the price of components or finished goods will affect sales volume.
    *   **New Product Introduction:** Estimating the price sensitivity of potential customers.

### **3. Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED)**

*   **Definition:** Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED) measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of one good to a change in the price of another good.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \text{XED} = \frac{\text{Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded of Good A}}{\text{Percentage Change in Price of Good B}} $$
*   **Interpretation of XED Values:**
    *   **Substitute Goods (XED > 0):** An increase in the price of Good B leads to an increase in the quantity demanded of Good A.
        *   *Example:* If the price of Coca-Cola increases, the demand for Pepsi might increase.
    *   **Complementary Goods (XED < 0):** An increase in the price of Good B leads to a decrease in the quantity demanded of Good A.
        *   *Example:* If the price of gasoline increases, the demand for large SUVs might decrease.
    *   **Unrelated Goods (XED ≈ 0):** Changes in the price of Good B have little to no effect on the quantity demanded of Good A.
        *   *Example:* The price of bananas and the demand for car tires.

*   **Application for Engineers:**
    *   **Competitive Analysis:** Understanding how price changes of competitor products affect the demand for your engineered products.
    *   **Bundling Strategies:** Assessing the impact of pricing one product on the demand for another product in a bundle.
    *   **Component Sourcing:** If a key component's price increases significantly, how will it affect the demand for the final engineered product that uses it?

---

### **4. Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)**

*   **Definition:** Income Elasticity of Demand (YED) measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in consumer income.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \text{YED} = \frac{\text{Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\text{Percentage Change in Income}} $$
*   **Interpretation of YED Values:**
    *   **Normal Goods (YED > 0):** As income increases, the quantity demanded increases.
        *   **Necessity Goods (0 < YED < 1):** Demand increases with income, but at a slower rate than income.
            *   *Example:* Food, basic clothing.
        *   **Luxury Goods (YED > 1):** Demand increases with income at a faster rate than income.
            *   *Example:* Sports cars, designer watches, high-end electronics.
    *   **Inferior Goods (YED < 0):** As income increases, the quantity demanded decreases. Consumers switch to better alternatives.
        *   *Example:* Public transportation (if people can afford cars), generic brands.

*   **Application for Engineers:**
    *   **Market Forecasting:** Predicting demand for engineered products based on economic growth and changes in disposable income.
    *   **Product Planning:** Identifying which product segments are likely to grow or shrink with changes in the economic climate. For example, during economic downturns, demand for luxury engineered goods might fall, while demand for basic necessities remains stable or even increases.

### **5. Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)**

*   **Definition:** Price Elasticity of Supply (PES) measures the responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a good or service to a change in its price.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \text{PES} = \frac{\text{Percentage Change in Quantity Supplied}}{\text{Percentage Change in Price}} $$
*   **Interpretation of PES Values:**
    *   **Elastic Supply (PES > 1):** A change in price leads to a larger percentage change in quantity supplied. Producers can easily adjust output.
    *   **Inelastic Supply (PES < 1):** A change in price leads to a smaller percentage change in quantity supplied. Producers find it difficult to adjust output quickly.
    *   **Unit Elastic Supply (PES = 1):** The percentage change in quantity supplied is equal to the percentage change in price.
    *   **Perfectly Inelastic Supply (PES = 0):** Quantity supplied remains fixed, regardless of price changes.
        *   *Example:* Unique works of art, land in a fixed location.
    *   **Perfectly Elastic Supply (PES = ∞):** Producers will supply any amount at a specific price, but nothing above or below.
        *   *Example:* A perfectly competitive market where firms are price takers.

*   **Factors Affecting PES:**
    *   **Availability of Inputs:** If inputs are readily available and easy to acquire, supply is more elastic.
    *   **Time Horizon:** Supply tends to be more elastic in the long run than in the short run, as producers have more time to adjust production levels.
    *   **Mobility of Factors of Production:** If factors can easily switch between producing different goods, supply is more elastic.
    *   **Inventory Levels:** Firms with high inventory can respond to price changes more quickly.
    *   **Capacity Utilization:** Firms operating below capacity can increase output more easily.

*   **Application for Engineers:**
    *   **Production Planning:** Understanding how sensitive the supply of manufactured goods is to price changes.
    *   **Raw Material Sourcing:** Assessing the impact of price fluctuations on the availability of essential materials.
    *   **Capacity Expansion Decisions:** If the market anticipates higher prices and supply is elastic, it might justify investment in expanding production capacity.

---

### **6. Applications of Elasticity in Engineering Economics**

*   **Pricing Decisions for Engineered Products:**
    *   If a product has elastic demand, increasing price might reduce total revenue. If inelastic, increasing price can increase total revenue.
    *   Understanding PED helps in setting competitive prices.
*   **Demand Forecasting:**
    *   YED helps predict demand for high-tech or luxury engineered goods based on economic growth.
    *   XED helps predict how demand for your product might be affected by price changes of related goods (substitutes or complements).
*   **Impact of Government Policies:**
    *   **Taxes:** If demand is inelastic, consumers will bear a larger portion of a tax burden (e.g., taxes on fuel). If supply is inelastic, producers will bear more.
    *   **Subsidies:** Subsidies are more effective in increasing quantity when demand or supply is elastic.
*   **Investment Decisions:**
    *   Understanding the elasticity of demand for your company's products or services informs decisions about investing in new technologies or expanding production.
    *   If demand for a new technology is highly elastic, initial pricing needs to be carefully considered.
*   **Resource Management:**
    *   If the supply of a critical raw material is inelastic, price volatility can be a significant risk. Engineers might need to seek alternative materials or invest in securing stable supply chains.

### **7. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**
A company manufacturing specialized industrial robots observes that when they decrease the price of their robots from $500,000 to $400,000, the quantity demanded increases from 50 units per year to 70 units per year.
a) Calculate the Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) for these robots using the midpoint formula.
b) Based on your calculation, is the demand for these robots elastic or inelastic?
c) If the company wants to increase its total revenue, should it consider further price decreases or increases? Explain.

**Question 2:**
The demand for a new type of electric vehicle (EV) is considered highly elastic due to many competing models. If the price of a key battery component (essential for EVs) increases by 15%, how might this affect the demand for EVs? Explain using the concept of cross-price elasticity.

**Question 3:**
Consider a company that produces high-endurance engineering software. If consumer income is expected to rise by 5% next year, and the income elasticity of demand for this software is 1.8, what is the expected percentage change in the quantity demanded for the software? Is this software considered a normal good or an inferior good?

**Question 4:**
A manufacturer of custom-built CNC machines finds that if they increase the price of their machines by 10%, the quantity they are willing and able to supply decreases by only 3%.
a) Calculate the Price Elasticity of Supply (PES).
b) Is the supply of these machines elastic or inelastic?
c) What factors might explain this elasticity?

### **8. Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**
a) **Midpoint Formula Calculation:**
   *   $Q_1 = 50$, $Q_2 = 70$
   *   $P_1 = \$500,000$, $P_2 = \$400,000$
   *   Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded = $ \frac{(70 - 50) / ((50 + 70)/2)}{} \times 100 = \frac{20 / 60}{} \times 100 = 33.33\% $
   *   Percentage Change in Price = $ \frac{(\$400,000 - \$500,000) / ((\$500,000 + \$400,000)/2)}{} \times 100 = \frac{-\$100,000 / \$450,000}{} \times 100 = -22.22\% $
   *   $ \text{PED} = \frac{33.33\%}{-22.22\%} = -1.5 $
   *   (Ignoring the negative sign as it only indicates the inverse relationship) **PED = 1.5**

b) Since PED (1.5) > 1, the demand for these robots is **elastic**.

c) Because demand is elastic, a price decrease leads to a proportionally larger increase in quantity demanded. This means that the revenue effect from selling more units outweighs the revenue loss from the lower price per unit. Therefore, to increase total revenue, the company should consider **further price decreases**.

**Answer 2:**
This scenario involves **cross-price elasticity of demand**. Since EVs and battery components are complementary goods (EVs require batteries), an increase in the price of battery components (Good B) would lead to a **decrease** in the demand for EVs (Good A). This is because the higher cost of a key input will likely lead EV manufacturers to increase their prices, or reduce their output, thus reducing the quantity of EVs demanded at any given price. The XED would be negative.

**Answer 3:**
*   Percentage Change in Income = +5%
*   Income Elasticity of Demand (YED) = 1.8
*   Expected Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded = YED × Percentage Change in Income
*   Expected Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded = 1.8 × 5% = **9%**

Since the YED (1.8) is positive and greater than 1, the engineering software is considered a **luxury good**.

**Answer 4:**
a) **PES Calculation:**
   *   Percentage Change in Quantity Supplied = -3%
   *   Percentage Change in Price = +10%
   *   $ \text{PES} = \frac{-3\%}{10\%} = -0.3 $
   *   (Ignoring the negative sign) **PES = 0.3**

b) Since PES (0.3) < 1, the supply of these custom-built CNC machines is **inelastic**.

c) Factors that might explain inelastic supply include:
    *   **Long production lead times:** Custom-built machines require significant design and manufacturing time.
    *   **Specialized inputs and skilled labor:** The availability of specific materials or highly skilled engineers might be limited, making it difficult to ramp up production quickly.
    *   **High fixed costs:** Significant investment in specialized machinery or facilities might mean that output cannot be easily or quickly changed in response to price signals.

### **8. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Elasticity is about responsiveness:** It quantifies how much one variable changes in response to a change in another.
*   **PED, XED, and YED are crucial for demand analysis:** They help understand consumer behavior and market dynamics.
*   **PES is vital for supply-side analysis:** It informs production decisions and market equilibrium.
*   **The sign of XED and YED is important for interpretation:** Positive for substitutes and normal goods, negative for complements and inferior goods.
*   **The magnitude of elasticity dictates the degree of responsiveness:** Values greater than 1 indicate elastic; less than 1 indicate inelastic.
*   **Engineers must consider elasticity for:** pricing, forecasting, investment, and policy analysis.
*   **The midpoint formula is generally preferred for PED and PES calculations** to avoid bias.
*   **Factors affecting elasticity are key to analysis.** Always consider why a good might be elastic or inelastic.
