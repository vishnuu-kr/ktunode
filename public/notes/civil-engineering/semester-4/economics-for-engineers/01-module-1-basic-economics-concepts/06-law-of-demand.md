# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS: Module 1: Basic Economics Concepts - Law of Demand

## 1. Introduction to the Law of Demand

The Law of Demand is a fundamental principle in microeconomics that describes the relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity consumers are willing and able to purchase at that price, assuming all other factors remain constant (ceteris paribus).

### 1.1. Key Concepts & Definitions

*   **Demand:** The **willingness** and **ability** of consumers to purchase a particular good or service at various prices during a given period. It's not just wanting something; it's having the desire and the means to buy it.
*   **Quantity Demanded (Qd):** The specific amount of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at a **specific price** during a given period.
*   **Law of Demand:** States that, *ceteris paribus*, as the **price** of a good or service **increases**, the **quantity demanded** will **decrease**, and conversely, as the **price** **decreases**, the **quantity demanded** will **increase**.
*   **Ceteris Paribus:** A Latin phrase meaning "all other things being equal" or "all other things held constant." This assumption is crucial for isolating the effect of price on quantity demanded.

### 1.2. The Inverse Relationship

The Law of Demand describes an **inverse** or **negative** relationship between price and quantity demanded. This means they move in opposite directions.

*   **High Price → Low Quantity Demanded**
*   **Low Price → High Quantity Demanded**

## 2. Why Does the Law of Demand Hold True?

Several underlying reasons explain the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded:

### 2.1. Income Effect

*   **Definition:** When the price of a good falls, consumers have more **real income** (purchasing power) left over. This increased purchasing power allows them to buy more of that good, or other goods.
*   **Example:** If the price of your favorite coffee drops from $4 to $3, you have an extra dollar. You might use that dollar to buy another cup of coffee, increasing your quantity demanded.

### 2.2. Substitution Effect

*   **Definition:** When the price of a good falls, it becomes relatively **cheaper** compared to other substitute goods. Consumers will switch from more expensive substitutes to the now cheaper good, increasing its quantity demanded.
*   **Example:** If the price of beef increases, consumers might buy more chicken (a substitute) instead. Conversely, if the price of beef falls, it becomes more attractive than chicken, and consumers may buy more beef.

### 2.3. Diminishing Marginal Utility

*   **Definition:** As a consumer consumes more units of a good, the additional satisfaction (utility) they get from each successive unit tends to **decrease**. To entice consumers to buy more, the price must fall to compensate for this diminishing additional satisfaction.
*   **Example:** The first slice of pizza you eat might be incredibly satisfying. The second slice is still good, but perhaps not as satisfying as the first. By the fifth slice, you might not want any more, regardless of the price. For the seller to sell you that fifth slice, the price would likely need to be very low.

## 3. The Demand Curve

The Law of Demand is visually represented by the **demand curve**.

### 3.1. Key Concepts & Definitions

*   **Demand Curve:** A graphical representation of the relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity demanded.
*   **Price (P):** Typically plotted on the vertical (Y) axis.
*   **Quantity Demanded (Qd):** Typically plotted on the horizontal (X) axis.

### 3.2. Characteristics of the Demand Curve

*   **Downward Sloping:** The demand curve slopes downwards from left to right, illustrating the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.
*   **Derived from a Demand Schedule:** A demand schedule is a table that lists the quantities demanded at various prices.

### 3.3. Example Demand Schedule and Curve

**Demand Schedule for Widgets:**

| Price ($) | Quantity Demanded (Widgets) |
| :-------- | :-------------------------- |
| 10        | 100                         |
| 8         | 150                         |
| 6         | 200                         |
| 4         | 250                         |
| 2         | 300                         |

**Demand Curve for Widgets:**

```
      ^ Price (P)
      |
10 ---*-------
      |       .
 8 ---|-----*---
      |     .   .
 6 ---|---*-----
      |   .     .
 4 ---|-*-------
      | .       .
 2 ---*---------
      |
      +-------------------> Quantity Demanded (Qd)
        100 150 200 250 300
```

*   Each point on the curve represents a specific price and the corresponding quantity demanded.
*   As price falls from $10 to $2, the quantity demanded increases from 100 to 300.

## 4. Movements Along the Demand Curve vs. Shifts in the Demand Curve

It is crucial to distinguish between a **movement along the demand curve** and a **shift in the demand curve**.

### 4.1. Movement Along the Demand Curve (Change in Quantity Demanded)

*   **Cause:** A change in the **price** of the good itself.
*   **Effect:** Results in a movement from one point to another *on the same demand curve*.
*   **Term:** Called a "change in quantity demanded."

    *   **Example:** If the price of widgets falls from $8 to $6, we move down along the existing demand curve for widgets, and the quantity demanded increases from 150 to 200.

### 4.2. Shift in the Demand Curve (Change in Demand)

*   **Cause:** A change in any of the **non-price determinants of demand** (factors other than the price of the good itself).
*   **Effect:** Causes the *entire demand curve* to shift to the right (increase in demand) or to the left (decrease in demand).
*   **Term:** Called a "change in demand."

    *   **Increase in Demand:** The demand curve shifts to the **right**. Consumers are willing and able to buy more at every price.
    *   **Decrease in Demand:** The demand curve shifts to the **left**. Consumers are willing and able to buy less at every price.

## 5. Determinants of Demand (Factors that Shift the Demand Curve)

These are the "other things" that are held constant under the *ceteris paribus* assumption. When these change, the demand curve shifts.

### 5.1. Income

*   **Normal Goods:** Goods for which demand **increases** as consumer income **increases**.
    *   **Example:** Luxury cars, fine dining, vacations. If your salary increases, you're likely to buy more of these.
*   **Inferior Goods:** Goods for which demand **decreases** as consumer income **increases**.
    *   **Example:** Instant noodles, generic brand clothing, public transport (if driving a car becomes affordable). As you earn more, you might switch to higher-quality alternatives.

### 5.2. Prices of Related Goods

*   **Substitute Goods:** Goods that can be used in place of each other.
    *   **Effect:** If the price of a substitute **increases**, the demand for the good in question **increases** (shifts right). If the price of a substitute **decreases**, the demand for the good in question **decreases** (shifts left).
    *   **Example:** Tea and Coffee. If the price of coffee increases, demand for tea will likely increase.
*   **Complementary Goods:** Goods that are used together.
    *   **Effect:** If the price of a complement **increases**, the demand for the good in question **decreases** (shifts left). If the price of a complement **decreases**, the demand for the good in question **increases** (shifts right).
    *   **Example:** Cars and Gasoline. If the price of gasoline increases significantly, the demand for cars might decrease.
