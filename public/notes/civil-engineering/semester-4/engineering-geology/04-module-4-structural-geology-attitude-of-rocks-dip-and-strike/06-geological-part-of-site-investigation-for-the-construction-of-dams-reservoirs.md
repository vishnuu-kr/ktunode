# Engineering Geology: Structural Geology - Dip and Strike in Dam & Reservoir Site Investigation

## Module 4: Structural Geology

### Topic: Geological Part of Site Investigation for the Construction of Dams & Reservoirs

---

### 1. Introduction to Site Investigation for Dams & Reservoirs

A dam and reservoir project is a massive undertaking with significant geological implications. A thorough site investigation is crucial to ensure the dam's safety, stability, and the reservoir's containment. The geological part of this investigation focuses on understanding the subsurface conditions, particularly the bedrock and its structural features.

**Key Goals of Geological Site Investigation for Dams & Reservoirs:**

*   **Foundation Stability:** Ensuring the dam can withstand the immense hydrostatic pressure of the reservoir.
*   **Water Containment:** Preventing leakage from the reservoir through the foundation and abutments.
*   **Seismic Hazard Assessment:** Identifying potential earthquake risks and designing accordingly.
*   **Material Availability:** Locating suitable construction materials (e.g., fill, concrete aggregate).
*   **Reservoir Stability:** Preventing landslides or slope failures within the reservoir basin.

---

### 2. Understanding Rock Mass Behavior: The Importance of Structural Geology

The stability and behavior of rock masses under the load of a dam and the pressure of reservoir water are heavily influenced by their structural fabric. Structural geology provides the tools to understand these fabric elements and their impact on engineering design.

**Key Structural Fabric Elements:**

*   **Bedding Planes:** Layering in sedimentary rocks.
*   **Foliation:** Parallel alignment of minerals in metamorphic rocks.
*   **Joints:** Fractures without displacement.
*   **Faults:** Fractures with significant displacement.
*   **Shear Zones:** Zones of intense fracturing and deformation.
*   **Dykes and Sills:** Igneous intrusions.

**How these features affect dams and reservoirs:**

*   **Permeability:** Joints, faults, and shear zones can act as conduits for water flow, leading to leakage from the reservoir and potential uplift pressures on the dam.
*   **Shear Strength:** The presence of discontinuities (especially if they are favorably oriented) can significantly reduce the shear strength of the rock mass, compromising the dam's foundation stability.
*   **Deformation:** Weak or fractured rock layers can deform under load, leading to settlement and instability.

### 3. Attitude of Rocks: Dip and Strike

The **attitude** of a rock layer or planar structural feature refers to its orientation in space. This orientation is critical in understanding how these features might influence the dam and reservoir. The two primary parameters used to describe attitude are **dip** and **strike**.

#### 3.1. Strike

**Definition:** The **strike** of a planar geological feature (like a bedding plane, joint, or fault) is the direction of a horizontal line on that plane. It is the compass bearing of the line formed by the intersection of the geological plane with a horizontal plane.

**Key Characteristics:**

*   **Measured as a compass bearing:** Typically expressed as a three-figure bearing (e.g., 045°, 135°, 225°).
*   **Perpendicular to dip direction:** The strike direction is always 90 degrees away from the dip direction.
*   **Infinite possibilities:** Can have any compass bearing.
*   **Conventionally recorded:** Often recorded with two opposing bearings (e.g., N45°E or S45°W, often simplified as 045°/225°).

**Example:** If a bedding plane strikes in a northwest-southeast direction, its strike can be recorded as 135° (SE) or 315° (NW).

#### 3.2. Dip

**Definition:** The **dip** of a planar geological feature is the angle between the plane and a horizontal plane, measured in the direction perpendicular to the strike. It is also the direction in which the plane is inclined downwards.

**Key Characteristics:**

*   **Measured as an angle and a direction:** Consists of two components:
    *   **Dip Angle (or Dip Magnitude):** The angle of inclination, measured in degrees from the horizontal (0° to 90°). A vertical plane has a dip of 90°, and a horizontal plane has a dip of 0°.
    *   **Dip Direction:** The compass bearing pointing in the direction of maximum inclination (downwards) of the plane.
*   **Perpendicular to strike:** The dip direction is always 90 degrees away from the strike direction.
*   **"Strike and Dip":** Often recorded together as "Strike/Dip Angle Dip Direction" (e.g., 045°/30°SE, meaning strike is 045°, dipping at 30° towards the southeast).

**Example:** A fault might have a strike of 090° and dip 60° south (090°/60°S). This means the fault line on the surface runs east-west, and the fault plane plunges downwards towards the south at an angle of 60 degrees.

#### 3.3. Apparent Dip

**Definition:** The **apparent dip** is the dip of a plane measured in any direction other than the true dip direction. It is always less than or equal to the true dip.

**Importance:** When taking measurements in boreholes or on inclined surfaces, you might not always be able to measure the true dip. Understanding apparent dip helps in correctly interpreting geological data.

**Key Concept:** The true dip is the maximum angle of inclination. Any other angle of inclination measured on the same plane in a different direction is an apparent dip.

#### 3.4. Measuring Dip and Strike

**Methods:**

*   **Clinometer (Brunton Compass):** This is the most common field tool.
    1.  **Find the strike:** Orient the compass so the needle swings freely. Hold the compass on the rock surface and tilt it until the bubble in the circular level is centered. The edge of the compass (usually marked) is now aligned with the strike. Read the bearing of this line. Record the strike as a two-way bearing (e.g., N45°E or S45°W).
    2.  **Find the dip:** With the compass still on the rock surface and aligned with the strike, tilt the compass perpendicular to the strike until the bubble is centered. The dip angle is read from the inclinometer scale, and the dip direction is read from the compass.
*   **Geological Hammer and Compass:** Similar principles, but less precise.
*   **Geophysical Surveys (e.g., seismic, ground-penetrating radar):** Can infer subsurface structure.
*   **Boreholes:** Orientation of rock layers or discontinuities intersected by a borehole can be determined using specialized borehole cameras or core orientation devices.

### 4. Geological Site Investigation for Dams & Reservoirs: Application of Dip and Strike

Understanding the dip and strike of various geological features is paramount in selecting a suitable dam site and designing the reservoir.

#### 4.1. Dam Foundation Design

*   **Homoclinal Structures:** Rock layers dipping uniformly in one direction.
    *   **Dam across strike:** If the dam is built perpendicular to the strike of dipping beds, the foundation will be stable as long as the dip angle is not too shallow and the beds are competent.
    *   **Dam along strike:** If the dam is built parallel to the strike of dipping beds, leakage can be a major issue through permeable layers or along bedding planes.
*   **Anticlines and Synclines:** Upward and downward folds of rock layers.
    *   **Axis parallel to river:** A dam built across a syncline (downfold) might be stable, but leakage can occur along the dipping flanks. A dam across an anticline (upfold) might have more issues with water escaping upwards.
    *   **Axis perpendicular to river:** Can lead to complex drainage patterns and potential seepage issues on the flanks of the reservoir.
*   **Faults:**
    *   **Faults crossing the dam alignment:** Require careful evaluation. If the fault is active or highly permeable, it may necessitate shifting the dam alignment or extensive grout curtains and cut-off trenches.
    *   **Faults with low dip:** Can act as slip surfaces for landslides, particularly in the abutments.
*   **Joints and Foliation:**
    *   **Joints dipping downstream (especially in abutments):** Can lead to wedges of rock that might slide out, causing abutment failure.
    *   **Joints dipping towards the reservoir:** Can create seepage paths into the foundation.
    *   **Foliation dipping unfavorably:** Similar to bedding planes, can create zones of weakness and preferential seepage.

**Example:** Consider a dam site where a permeable sandstone layer dips at 30° towards the reservoir. If the dam is built across the strike of this layer, water could seep through the sandstone into the downstream foundation, potentially causing uplift pressures and reducing bearing capacity. A cut-off trench or grout curtain would be essential.

#### 4.2. Reservoir Rim Stability

*   **Slope Failures:** The stability of the reservoir slopes is influenced by the orientation of rock layers, joints, and faults relative to the reservoir water level.
*   **Landslides:** If bedding planes or fault planes dip steeply towards the reservoir, the reservoir water can saturate these discontinuities, reducing their shear strength and potentially triggering landslides.
*   **Reservoir Leakage:** Permeable strata or fractured zones with unfavorable dip can lead to significant water loss from the reservoir.

**Example:** If a dam is built across a river valley where shale beds dip at 45° into the valley wall (reservoir rim), and these shales are prone to swelling or softening upon wetting, the reservoir water could saturate them. This might lead to slope instability and potential landslides into the reservoir.

#### 4.3. Permeability and Seepage Control

*   **Strike and Dip of Permeable Layers:** Identifying the orientation of permeable rock layers, fractured zones, and faults is critical for designing effective seepage control measures.
*   **Grout Curtains:** To prevent leakage, grout curtains are often injected into permeable zones. The effectiveness of these curtains depends on understanding the extent and connectivity of these zones, which is informed by dip and strike data.
*   **Cut-off Trenches:** Excavating trenches and backfilling them with impermeable material (like clay or concrete) to block seepage paths. The depth and location of these trenches are guided by the attitude of geological features.

### 5. Practice Questions & Exercises

**Question 1:**
A geologist measures the attitude of a bedding plane. The compass reads 045° when the edge of the clinometer is aligned with the strike. When the clinometer is tilted perpendicular to the strike, the bubble is centered, and the dip angle reads 25°. The dip direction is towards the southeast. What is the strike and dip of the bedding plane?
    *   a) 045°/25°SE
    *   b) 135°/25°SE
    *   c) 045°/25°SW
    *   d) 225°/25°SE

**Question 2:**
Which of the following geological features, when dipping steeply downstream in the abutment of a dam, poses the greatest risk of instability?
    *   a) Joint parallel to the dam axis
    *   b) Bedding plane dipping upstream
    *   c) Joint dipping downstream, forming a wedge with the abutment surface
    *   d) Fault parallel to the river valley

**Question 3:**
Explain how the strike and dip of a fault can influence the design of a cut-off trench for a dam.

**Question 4:**
Describe a scenario where a dam built across a syncline could be more problematic than a dam built across an anticline, considering the attitude of the rock layers.

---

### 6. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
**a) 045°/25°SE**
*   The strike is the direction of the horizontal line on the plane, which is given as 045°.
*   The dip is the angle of inclination and its direction. The angle is 25°, and the direction is southeast.

**Answer 2:**
**c) Joint dipping downstream, forming a wedge with the abutment surface**
*   A joint dipping downstream, if it forms a wedge with the abutment surface, can lead to a block of rock detaching and sliding downstream due to the lateral pressure from the dam and the reservoir. The other options are generally less critical or even beneficial.

**Answer 3:**
The strike and dip of a fault significantly influence the design of a cut-off trench by:
*   **Determining the trench alignment:** The trench should ideally intercept the fault zone to seal it.
*   **Dictating the trench depth:** If the fault dips shallowly towards the reservoir, the trench may need to be deeper and extend further upstream to effectively cut off seepage along the fault plane. If the fault dips steeply away from the reservoir, the trench might not need to be as deep.
*   **Guiding grout injection:** Understanding the dip and strike helps in planning grout holes to effectively seal the fault zone. The strike indicates the lateral extent to be sealed, and the dip guides the angle and depth of injection.

**Answer 4:**
A dam built across a **syncline** (downward fold) where the limbs (sides) of the fold dip towards the reservoir could be problematic. If these dipping limbs are permeable or fractured, water from the reservoir can seep along them and escape into the ground on the flanks of the reservoir, leading to significant water loss. In contrast, a dam built across an **anticline** (upward fold) might have its crest of permeable layers exposed upstream, potentially allowing water to escape upwards if not properly sealed at the crest. However, if the crest of the anticline is impermeable, it can form a natural barrier. The specific lithology and the angle of dip of the limbs are critical in both cases, but a syncline with permeable, dipping flanks presents a clear pathway for reservoir water to escape laterally.

---

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Attitude (Dip and Strike) is Fundamental:** It dictates how geological features will behave under load and interact with reservoir water.
*   **Strike is Horizontal; Dip is Inclination:** Always remember their perpendicular relationship.
*   **Unfavorable Orientations:** Be particularly wary of discontinuities (joints, faults, bedding planes) dipping downstream or towards the reservoir, especially if they are permeable.
*   **Three-Dimensional Understanding:** Site investigation requires inferring the subsurface attitude of structures from surface observations and subsurface data.
*   **Safety First:** Unfavorable geological structures can lead to catastrophic failures. Thorough investigation and appropriate engineering solutions are non-negotiable.
*   **Seepage is a Major Concern:** The primary role of structural geology in dam and reservoir site selection is to identify and mitigate potential seepage pathways.
*   **Clinometer is Your Friend:** Learn to use it effectively in the field.
