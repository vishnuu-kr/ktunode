# Engineering Geology: Module 4 - Structural Geology: Attitude of Rocks (Dip and Strike)

---

## Introduction to Structural Geology

Structural geology is the study of the deformation of rocks and the resulting structures. Understanding these structures is crucial in engineering geology for several reasons:

*   **Site Investigation:** Identifying faults, folds, and joints helps assess geological hazards and stability.
*   **Foundation Design:** The orientation and nature of rock discontinuities affect foundation bearing capacity and settlement.
*   **Tunneling and Mining:** Knowledge of structural features guides excavation methods, support requirements, and potential groundwater ingress.
*   **Resource Exploration:** Structural controls often dictate the location of mineral deposits and hydrocarbon reservoirs.

This module focuses on the **attitude of rocks**, which refers to the spatial orientation of rock layers or surfaces. The primary parameters used to describe this attitude are **dip** and **strike**.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

### 1. Rock Layers and Planes

*   **Bedding Plane:** The surface that separates successive layers of sedimentary rocks. This is a fundamental plane whose attitude we describe.
*   **Other Planar Structures:** This also applies to other planar geological features like:
    *   **Joints:** Fractures along which there has been no significant movement.
    *   **Faults:** Fractures along which there has been significant displacement.
    *   **Cleavage:** A type of foliation in metamorphic rocks, often planar.

### 2. Strike

*   **Definition:** The compass direction of the line formed by the **intersection of a geological plane (e.g., bedding plane, fault) and a horizontal plane.**
*   **Measurement:** Strike is always measured as a **trend** using a compass.
*   **Convention:**
    *   It is usually expressed as a **trend**, e.g., North, East, South, West.
    *   More commonly, it's given as an **azimuth** (0-360°) or as a **quadrant bearing** (e.g., N30°E, S75°W).
    *   The strike direction is always **perpendicular** to the direction of the true dip.
    *   It is often expressed as a **pair of opposite directions**, e.g., N45°E - S45°W. This is because a line has two opposite directions.

*   **Example:** If a bedding plane intersects a horizontal surface along a line trending northeast, its strike is Northeast. This can be represented as N45°E or S45°W.

### 3. Dip

*   **Definition:** The angle between a geological plane and a horizontal plane, measured in a vertical plane perpendicular to the strike.
*   **Components of Dip:**
    *   **Dip Angle (or Dip Magnitude):** The angle of inclination of the plane below the horizontal. It ranges from 0° (horizontal) to 90° (vertical).
    *   **Dip Direction:** The compass direction in which the plane is inclined downwards.

*   **Measurement:**
    *   The **dip angle** is measured in degrees using a clinometer.
    *   The **dip direction** is measured in degrees using a compass.

*   **Relationship with Strike:** The dip direction is always **90 degrees away** from the strike direction, pointing in the direction of maximum inclination.

*   **Examples:**
    *   A bedding plane dipping at 30° towards the east would be recorded as **Dip 30°E**.
    *   A fault dipping at 60° towards the southwest would be recorded as **Dip 60°SW**.

### 4. Horizontal Plane

*   A horizontal plane has **no dip** and **no strike**. Its attitude is described as **horizontal**.

### 5. Vertical Plane

*   A vertical plane has a dip of **90°**. The dip direction can be any direction.
*   The strike of a vertical plane is **horizontal** and can be in any direction.

## Field Measurement of Dip and Strike

### Tools Used:

*   **Brunton Compass (or Geological Compass):** A specialized compass that includes:
    *   A sighting needle to align with the dip direction.
    *   A clinometer to measure the dip angle.
    *   A compass for measuring strike and dip directions.
    *   A leveling bubble.
*   **Clinometer:** Used to measure angles of inclination.

### Measurement Procedure:

1.  **Locate a planar geological feature:** Identify a clear bedding plane, joint, or fault.
2.  **Determine the Strike:**
    *   Place the Brunton compass on the planar surface so that the sighting needle and the edge of the compass base are aligned along the plane.
    *   Ensure the compass is level using the leveling bubble.
    *   Rotate the compass around the vertical axis until the needle is deflected to its maximum extent (this indicates it's not aligned with the dip direction).
    *   The compass needle will now align with the **strike line**. Read the bearing indicated by the needle. This is one of the strike directions.
    *   The opposite strike direction is 180° from the first.
    *   *Alternatively:* You can directly measure the strike by orienting the compass so that the edge of the compass base is along the plane, and then turning the compass until it's level. The needle will then point along the strike. Read the strike direction.
3.  **Determine the Dip Direction and Dip Angle:**
    *   Once the strike is identified, orient the compass so that its edge (or sighting needle) is perpendicular to the strike line and pointing downwards along the steepest inclination of the plane.
    *   Tilt the compass on its edge until the sighting needle is aligned with the dip direction.
    *   Use the clinometer to measure the angle of inclination. This is the **dip angle**.
    *   Read the compass to determine the **dip direction**.

## Relationship Between Strike, Dip, and Horizontal Plane

*   **Strike:** Lies on the horizontal plane.
*   **Dip:** Measured perpendicular to the strike, downwards from the horizontal.
*   Imagine a tilted book. The edge of the table the book is resting on (if the book is tilted) represents the strike. The angle the book's cover makes with the table surface is the dip angle, and the direction the book is tilted downwards is the dip direction.

## Apparent Dip

### Definition:

*   **Apparent Dip:** The dip of a plane measured in a direction that is **not perpendicular** to its true strike.
*   Apparent dip is always **less than the true dip**, unless measured in the direction of true dip (in which case it's equal to the true dip).

### Why is it important?

*   In real-world scenarios, we often encounter outcrops where the measured dip is not the true dip. Understanding apparent dip allows us to correct for these measurements.
*   On geological maps, sections are often drawn at different angles, leading to apparent dips in the cross-section.

### Calculation of Apparent Dip:

Let:
*   $\phi_{true}$ = True dip angle
*   $\alpha$ = Angle between the direction of true dip and the direction of apparent dip.
*   $\phi_{app}$ = Apparent dip angle

The relationship is given by:

$tan(\phi_{app}) = tan(\phi_{true}) * cos(\alpha)$

Alternatively, using strike and dip directions:

If the true dip direction is $D_{true}$ and the apparent dip direction is $D_{app}$, then $\alpha$ is the angle between $D_{true}$ and $D_{app}$.

### Determining True Dip from Apparent Dips:

If you have measured apparent dips in at least **two different directions**, you can calculate the true dip and strike. This is typically done using graphical methods (e.g., stereographic projection) or mathematical formulas.

**Graphical Method (Spearman's Rule - Simplified):**

1.  Draw a horizontal line representing the strike of a hypothetical plane.
2.  From a point on this line, draw a line downwards representing the true dip direction, making an angle $\phi_{true}$ with the horizontal.
3.  Choose two directions at angles $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ from the true dip direction.
4.  Measure the apparent dips $\phi_{app1}$ and $\phi_{app2}$ in these directions using $tan(\phi_{app}) = tan(\phi_{true}) * cos(\alpha)$.
5.  In the field, measure apparent dips in two different directions.
6.  Convert these apparent dips and the angles between their directions into the apparent dip formula. Solve for the true dip.

**Example Exercise:**

A bedding plane has a true dip of 40° towards the northeast (N45°E). What is the apparent dip measured in a direction directly north (0°)?

*   True dip direction ($D_{true}$) = N45°E
*   Apparent dip direction ($D_{app}$) = North (0°)
*   Angle between true dip direction and apparent dip direction ($\alpha$) = 45°
*   True dip angle ($\phi_{true}$) = 40°

$tan(\phi_{app}) = tan(40°) * cos(45°)$
$tan(\phi_{app}) = 0.8391 * 0.7071$
$tan(\phi_{app}) = 0.5933$
$\phi_{app} = arctan(0.5933) \approx 30.68°$

So, the apparent dip measured towards the north is approximately 30.7°.

## Geological Maps and Cross-Sections

### Geological Maps:

*   Geological maps depict the surface distribution of rock units.
*   **Strike and Dip Symbols:** These are crucial annotations on geological maps.
    *   A long bar indicates the **strike direction**.
    *   A shorter tick mark on the strike bar points in the **dip direction**.
    *   The number next to the symbol indicates the **dip angle**.

    *Example:* A symbol with a long bar pointing NE-SW, with a tick mark pointing SE, and the number 30 next to it, means the rock layer strikes NE-SW and dips 30° towards the SE.

### Interpretation of Maps:

*   **Uniform Strike and Dip:** Indicates a single, relatively undisturbed rock layer.
*   **Changing Strike and Dip:** Can indicate folding (anticlines and synclines) or faulting.
*   **Parallel Strike Lines:** Suggest relatively uniform dipping beds.
*   **Converging Strike Lines:** Suggest folding.
*   **Offsetting Strike Lines:** Suggest faulting.

### Geological Cross-Sections:

*   Geological cross-sections are vertical slices through the Earth's crust, showing the subsurface structure.
*   The attitude of rock layers (dip and strike) shown on the map is projected onto the cross-section.
*   **Apparent Dip in Cross-Sections:** If a cross-section is not oriented perpendicular to the strike of the dipping beds, the beds will appear to have an apparent dip in the cross-section. The angle of the cross-section relative to the strike determines this apparent dip.

## Applications in Engineering Geology

### Foundation Design:

*   **Steeply Dipping Beds:** Can lead to anisotropic bearing capacity. Foundations on the "down-dip" side of a slope might be more stable than those on the "up-dip" side.
*   **Bedding Planes as Failure Surfaces:** In slopes or excavations, dipping bedding planes can act as planes of weakness, leading to landslides or rock falls if their orientation is unfavorable (e.g., dipping out of a slope).
*   **Joints and Faults:** Can reduce the shear strength of rock masses, influencing the stability of foundations, dams, and tunnels.

### Tunneling and Excavations:

*   **Orientation of Tunnels:** Tunnels oriented parallel to the strike of dipping beds are generally more stable than those crossing steeply dipping features.
*   **Water Ingress:** Fractured and jointed rock masses, especially those with unfavorable dip, can allow significant groundwater flow into excavations, requiring dewatering and robust support.
*   **Rock Burst Potential:** Highly stressed rock masses with certain structural orientations can be prone to sudden failures (rock bursts).

### Slope Stability:

*   **Dip of Bedding/Joints:** If bedding planes or joint sets dip out of a slope at an angle similar to the slope face, it significantly increases the risk of planar failure.
*   **Wedge Failure:** Intersecting joint sets can create wedge-shaped blocks that can slide out of a slope if their intersection line plunges in a favorable direction.

**Question 3:** A rock layer is observed to dip at 45° towards the southeast. What is its strike?

**Question 5:** A bedding plane has a true dip of 50° towards the West. Calculate the apparent dip in a direction that is 60° North of West.

**Question 6:** How can the dip and strike of rock layers influence the stability of a highway cut?

## Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
*   **Strike:** The compass direction of the line formed by the intersection of a geological plane and a horizontal plane.
*   **Dip:** The angle between a geological plane and a horizontal plane, measured in a vertical plane perpendicular to the strike. The dip includes both the angle of inclination and the direction of inclination.
*   **Relationship:** The direction of true dip is always perpendicular (90°) to the strike.

**Answer 2:**
If the strike is N20°E, the true dip can be either 90° clockwise or 90° counter-clockwise from this direction.
*   90° clockwise from N20°E is S70°E (20° + 90° = 110°, which is S70°E).
*   90° counter-clockwise from N20°E is N70°W (360° - 20° = 340°, or 20° - 90° = -70°, which is N70°W).
So, the possible directions of true dip are S70°E and N70°W.

**Answer 3:**
If the dip is 45° towards the southeast (SE), the strike is perpendicular to SE.
*   SE is 135° azimuth.
*   Perpendicular directions are 135° - 90° = 45° (NE) and 135° + 90° = 225° (SW).
The strike is Northeast-Southwest, or N45°E - S45°W.

**Answer 4:**
The rock layer strikes North-South (N-S). The tick mark pointing East indicates that the layer dips towards the East. The number 25 indicates the dip angle is 25°.
Therefore, the attitude of the rock layer is **Strike N-S, Dip 25°E**.

**Answer 5:**
*   True dip direction ($D_{true}$) = West (270° azimuth)
*   Apparent dip direction ($D_{app}$) = 60° North of West. This means from West (270°), we move 60° towards North (0° or 360°). So, $D_{app}$ = 270° + 60° = 330° (or -30°).
*   Angle between true dip direction and apparent dip direction ($\alpha$) = 60°.
*   True dip angle ($\phi_{true}$) = 50°.

$tan(\phi_{app}) = tan(\phi_{true}) * cos(\alpha)$
$tan(\phi_{app}) = tan(50°) * cos(60°)$
$tan(\phi_{app}) = 1.1918 * 0.5$
$tan(\phi_{app}) = 0.5959$
$\phi_{app} = arctan(0.5959) \approx 30.8°$

The apparent dip measured in a direction 60° North of West is approximately **30.8°**.

**Answer 6:**
The dip and strike of rock layers significantly influence the stability of a highway cut:
*   **Favorable Orientation:** If the rock layers strike parallel to the highway cut and dip *away* from the cut face (at a shallow angle), the cut is generally stable.
*   **Unfavorable Orientation:** If the rock layers strike perpendicular to the highway cut and dip *into* the cut face (at a moderate to steep angle), there is a high risk of planar failure (sliding of rock blocks along the bedding planes).
*   **Joints:** Similar to bedding planes, intersecting joint sets can create unstable wedges that could fail from the cut face.
*   **Permeability:** The orientation of these discontinuities also dictates how water can flow through the rock mass, potentially reducing shear strength and contributing to instability.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Strike is a line on the horizontal plane.**
*   **Dip is measured perpendicular to the strike.**
*   **Dip direction points to the steepest downhill slope.**
*   **Apparent dip is always less than or equal to true dip.**
*   **Two apparent dips in different directions are needed to calculate true dip and strike.**
*   **Geological maps use specific symbols to represent strike and dip.**
*   **Understanding attitude is critical for slope stability, foundation design, and underground excavations.**
*   **Always be mindful of the convention for expressing strike (e.g., N45°E-S45°W) and dip (e.g., 30°SE).**
