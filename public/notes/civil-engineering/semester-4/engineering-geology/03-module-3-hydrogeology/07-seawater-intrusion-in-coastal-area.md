# Engineering Geology: Module 3 - Hydrogeology

## Topic: Seawater Intrusion in Coastal Areas

This module delves into the critical phenomenon of seawater intrusion in coastal regions, a significant challenge for groundwater management and coastal infrastructure.

---

### 1. Understanding the Phenomenon of Seawater Intrusion

#### 1.1 Definition of Seawater Intrusion

*   **Seawater Intrusion:** The movement of saline seawater into freshwater aquifers (underground layers of permeable rock or sediment that hold groundwater) in coastal areas. This process degrades the quality of freshwater resources, rendering them unsuitable for drinking, irrigation, and industrial use.

#### 1.2 Causes of Seawater Intrusion

*   **Over-pumping of Coastal Aquifers:** This is the primary driver. Excessive withdrawal of freshwater from coastal wells lowers the potentiometric surface (the imaginary surface representing the hydrostatic pressure of groundwater).
    *   **Mechanism:** In a healthy coastal aquifer, a freshwater lens floats on denser seawater due to density differences. Pumping freshwater creates a cone of depression in the water table, which reduces the hydraulic pressure of the freshwater. This pressure difference allows the denser seawater to advance inland and upward into the aquifer.
*   **Reduced Freshwater Recharge:**
    *   **Droughts and Climate Change:** Decreased rainfall leads to less infiltration and groundwater recharge, weakening the freshwater pressure that normally pushes seawater back.
    *   **Dam Construction Upstream:** Dams can reduce the flow of rivers that naturally recharge coastal aquifers, especially in estuarine environments.
    *   **Urbanization and Land-Use Changes:** Increased impervious surfaces (roads, buildings) reduce infiltration. Drainage systems can also remove surface water before it can recharge the groundwater.
*   **Rising Sea Levels:**
    *   **Mechanism:** As global sea levels rise (due to thermal expansion of water and melting glaciers), the static pressure exerted by seawater at the coast increases. This higher pressure pushes the saltwater interface further inland, even without significant pumping.
*   **Changes in River Flow:**
    *   **Reduced Freshwater Discharge:** Lower river flows in coastal areas (e.g., due to upstream water use) reduce the natural flushing action that keeps seawater out of estuaries and adjacent aquifers.
*   **Geological Factors:**
    *   **Permeability and Connectivity:** Highly permeable formations (e.g., sand and gravel) allow for faster and easier seawater movement. Fractured bedrock or highly connected aquifer systems can also facilitate intrusion.
    *   **Presence of Tidal Inlets and Channels:** These can act as direct pathways for seawater to enter coastal aquifers.

#### 1.3 The Freshwater-Seawater Interface (or Wedge)

*   **Concept:** Seawater intrusion is not an instantaneous invasion but rather a gradual movement of a saline water body into freshwater. The boundary between freshwater and seawater is called the freshwater-seawater interface or saltwater wedge.
*   **Density Difference:** Seawater is denser than freshwater (approximately 2.5% to 3% denser due to dissolved salts). This density difference is crucial in understanding the behavior of the interface.
*   **Ghyben-Herzberg Principle:**
    *   **Concept:** This principle describes the relationship between the depth of freshwater below sea level and the depth of the freshwater lens above sea level. It states that for every unit of freshwater height above sea level, there are approximately 40 units of freshwater depth below sea level.
    *   **Formula:** $z = 40h$
        *   $z$: Depth of the freshwater-seawater interface below sea level.
        *   $h$: Height of the freshwater table above sea level.
    *   **Assumptions:** Assumes static conditions, homogeneous and isotropic aquifer, and no flow. While an oversimplification, it provides a fundamental understanding of the relationship.
*   **Dynamic Interface:** In reality, the interface is dynamic and fluctuates with changes in groundwater levels, pumping, and tidal cycles.

#### 1.4 Indicators of Seawater Intrusion

*   **Increasing Salinity:** The most direct indicator. Measured as Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), electrical conductivity (EC), or chloride concentration in groundwater samples.
*   **Chloride-to-Bromide Ratio (Cl/Br):** A naturally occurring ratio in seawater. An increase in this ratio in groundwater can indicate seawater mixing.
*   **Changes in Groundwater Levels:** Declining freshwater levels, especially near the coast, can signal intrusion.
*   **Vegetation Changes:** Salt-tolerant vegetation may appear in areas that were previously freshwater-dominated.
*   **Saltwater Intrusion Modeling:** Sophisticated computer models are used to simulate groundwater flow and solute transport, predicting the extent and rate of intrusion.

### 2. Impacts of Seawater Intrusion

#### 2.1 Degradation of Water Resources

*   **Drinking Water:** Increased salinity makes water unsafe for human consumption, leading to health issues.
*   **Agricultural Water:** High salt content can damage crops, reduce yields, and degrade soil quality.
*   **Industrial Water:** Many industrial processes require freshwater; saline water can cause corrosion and operational problems.

#### 2.2 Environmental Impacts

*   **Ecological Changes:** Freshwater ecosystems in coastal wetlands and estuaries can be harmed by increased salinity.
*   **Soil Salinization:** Inland soils can become saline due to increased groundwater salinity and its subsequent impact on surface water and plant life.

#### 2.3 Infrastructure Impacts

*   **Corrosion of Infrastructure:** Saltwater can accelerate the corrosion of concrete, steel pipes, and foundations in coastal areas.
*   **Subsidence (indirectly):** In some cases, the depletion of freshwater can lead to increased reliance on desalinization or alternative water sources, which might have their own infrastructure demands.

### 3. Factors Influencing Seawater Intrusion

#### 3.1 Hydrogeological Factors

*   **Aquifer Properties:**
    *   **Hydraulic Conductivity (K):** Higher K allows for faster intrusion.
    *   **Transmissivity (T = K * b):** T is the product of hydraulic conductivity (K) and aquifer thickness (b). Higher transmissivity means greater groundwater flow potential, which can either aid or resist intrusion depending on the hydraulic gradient.
    *   **Storativity (S):** Affects how aquifer levels respond to pumping and recharge.
*   **Aquifer Geometry and Stratigraphy:**
    *   **Aquifer Thickness and Depth:** Thicker aquifers can hold larger freshwater lenses.
    *   **Presence of Aquitards/Aquitards:** Impermeable or semi-permeable layers can retard intrusion, creating compartmentalization. However, breaches or faults in these layers can act as conduits.
    *   **Connectivity:** Unconfined vs. confined aquifers, interconnectedness of aquifer units.
*   **Hydraulic Gradients:**
    *   **Freshwater Gradient:** The natural slope of the freshwater table. A steeper inland gradient helps push seawater back.
    *   **Seawater Gradient:** The slope of the saltwater interface.
    *   **Pumping-Induced Gradients:** Cones of depression create inward gradients towards wells, pulling seawater.

#### 3.2 Hydrologic Factors

*   **Groundwater Pumping Rates and Distribution:**
    *   **Rate:** Higher pumping rates create stronger inland hydraulic gradients, drawing seawater in.
    *   **Distribution:** Concentrated pumping in a few wells is more detrimental than dispersed pumping. Pumping near the coast is particularly risky.
*   **Recharge Rates:**
    *   **Natural Recharge:** Rainfall, infiltration from rivers, lakes, and wetlands.
    *   **Artificial Recharge:** Intentional injection of freshwater into aquifers to maintain hydrostatic pressure and push back seawater.
*   **Sea Level:**
    *   **Static Sea Level:** The baseline pressure of the ocean.
    *   **Tidal Fluctuations:** Can cause short-term, localized intrusion events, especially near tidal channels.
*   **River Inflow and Estuarine Dynamics:**
    *   **Freshwater Discharge:** Rivers help maintain freshwater pressure in coastal aquifers and flush saline water from estuaries.
    *   **Estuarine Salinity:** The mixing of fresh and saltwater in estuaries influences the pressure boundary at the coast.

#### 3.3 Anthropogenic Factors (Human Activities)

*   **Groundwater Extraction:** As discussed, the primary human driver.
*   **Wastewater Discharge:** Leaky sewer lines or septic systems can introduce saline or polluted water into aquifers.
*   **Canal Construction and Dredging:** Can create direct pathways for seawater to reach inland areas.
*   **Coastal Development:** Reducing natural recharge areas and increasing impervious surfaces.

### 4. Monitoring and Management Strategies

#### 4.1 Monitoring

*   **Groundwater Level Monitoring:** Establishing a network of observation wells to track water table fluctuations.
*   **Water Quality Sampling:** Regular monitoring of salinity parameters (EC, TDS, Cl⁻) at strategic locations.
*   **Piezometer Networks:** Installing nested piezometers to monitor salinity at different depths within an aquifer.
*   **Geophysical Surveys:**
    *   **Electrical Resistivity Methods:** Saline water is more conductive than freshwater, allowing for mapping of saltwater intrusion zones.
    *   **Electromagnetic (EM) Methods:** Similar principle to resistivity.
*   **Tracer Studies:** Using chemical or isotopic tracers to understand groundwater flow paths and mixing.

#### 4.2 Management Strategies

*   **Sustainable Groundwater Management:**
    *   **Regulation of Pumping:** Setting pumping limits, enforcing well spacing regulations, and requiring permits for new wells.
    *   **Managed Aquifer Recharge (MAR):**
        *   **Purpose:** To augment freshwater supplies and maintain hydrostatic pressure, pushing back the saltwater wedge.
        *   **Methods:** Spreading basins, injection wells, infiltration galleries.
        *   **Water Sources:** Treated wastewater, stormwater, surplus river water.
*   **Barrier Systems:**
    *   **Freshwater Injection Barriers:** Pumping freshwater into injection wells along the coast to create a hydraulic barrier that prevents seawater from advancing.
    *   **Dams or Levees:** Physical barriers, though often impractical for groundwater management alone.
*   **Desalination:**
    *   **Purpose:** To treat saline groundwater or seawater to produce freshwater.
    *   **Methods:** Reverse Osmosis (RO), Multi-Stage Flash Distillation (MSF).
    *   **Challenges:** High energy consumption, cost, and brine disposal.
*   **Aquifer Storage and Recovery (ASR):**
    *   **Concept:** Injecting surplus freshwater into an aquifer during times of plenty and recovering it during times of scarcity. Can be used in conjunction with MAR to push back seawater.
*   **Relocation of Wells:** Moving pumping wells further inland to stay within the freshwater zone.
*   **Source Water Protection:** Protecting recharge areas from contamination and pollution.
*   **Demand Management:** Promoting water conservation measures to reduce overall water demand.
*   **Integrated Water Resource Management (IWRM):** A holistic approach considering all water sources and demands in a basin.

### 5. Case Studies and Examples

*   **Miami-Dade County, Florida, USA:** A classic example of severe seawater intrusion due to extensive groundwater pumping for a growing population. Managed through injection wells (freshwater barriers) and strict pumping regulations.
*   **Gaza Strip:** Faces extreme challenges with widespread seawater intrusion into its sole freshwater aquifer due to over-pumping and limited alternative water sources.
*   **Coastal California, USA:** Many coastal communities have implemented barrier projects and strict water management to combat intrusion.
*   **Netherlands:** Extensive coastline and low-lying areas make it vulnerable. Managed through dune infiltration, artificial recharge, and stringent pumping controls.

### 8. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
c) Over-pumping of freshwater

**Answer 2:**
The Ghyben-Herzberg principle describes the relationship between the depth of freshwater above sea level and the depth of the freshwater-seawater interface below sea level. It states that for every unit of freshwater height above sea level, there are approximately 40 units of freshwater depth below sea level. The formula is: $z = 40h$, where $z$ is the depth of the interface below sea level and $h$ is the height of the freshwater table above sea level.
**Limitations:** This principle assumes static conditions (no flow), a homogeneous and isotropic aquifer, and a sharp, stable interface. In reality, groundwater flow, aquifer heterogeneity, dynamic pumping, and a diffuse interface all influence the actual saltwater wedge.

**Answer 3:**
Three significant impacts of seawater intrusion are:
1.  **Degradation of Drinking Water Quality:** Increased salinity makes water undrinkable and can lead to health problems.
2.  **Damage to Agricultural Lands:** Saline water can stunt crop growth, reduce yields, and degrade soil fertility.
3.  **Corrosion of Infrastructure:** Saltwater can accelerate the deterioration of concrete, steel, and other materials used in coastal structures.

**Answer 4:**
Reduced freshwater recharge weakens the natural hydraulic pressure that freshwater exerts, pushing back the denser seawater. With less freshwater entering the aquifer, the potentiometric surface of the freshwater declines. This allows the higher pressure exerted by the seawater to advance inland, increasing the extent of intrusion.
**Examples of factors reducing freshwater recharge:**
*   **Droughts and reduced rainfall:** Less water infiltrates the ground.
*   **Urbanization:** Increased impervious surfaces prevent rainwater from reaching the aquifer.
*   **Dam construction upstream:** Reduces river flow that might naturally recharge coastal aquifers.

**Answer 5:**
**Freshwater Injection Barriers vs. Desalination**

*   **Freshwater Injection Barriers:**
    *   **Description:** Wells are drilled along the coast and inject freshwater into the aquifer, creating a continuous ridge of freshwater that prevents the inland movement of saline water.
    *   **Advantages:** Can be effective in protecting existing freshwater sources, relatively established technology, can maintain aquifer hydraulic integrity.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires a reliable source of freshwater for injection (which can be scarce), high operational costs (pumping), potential for induced leakage or unintended impacts on surrounding aquifers, and can be land-intensive.

*   **Desalination:**
    *   **Description:** Processes like Reverse Osmosis or Multi-Stage Flash Distillation are used to remove salt from seawater or brackish groundwater.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides a direct source of potable water independent of aquifer conditions, can supply water to areas where intrusion is severe and other methods are insufficient.
    *   **Disadvantages:** High energy consumption and associated costs, production of highly concentrated brine (which requires careful disposal to avoid environmental damage), capital intensive, and often requires a reliable source of brackish water or seawater.

### 9. Important Points to Remember

*   **Over-pumping is the primary culprit:** Managing groundwater extraction is paramount.
*   **Density difference is key:** Seawater is denser, influencing its movement.
*   **Ghyben-Herzberg is a useful conceptual tool:** But real-world scenarios are more complex.
*   **Sea-level rise is a growing threat:** Adding to the pressure from the ocean.
*   **Monitoring is crucial:** To detect and track intrusion early.
*   **Integrated management is best:** Combining technical solutions with water conservation and policy.
*   **Prevention is better than cure:** Sustainable groundwater practices are essential for coastal resilience.
