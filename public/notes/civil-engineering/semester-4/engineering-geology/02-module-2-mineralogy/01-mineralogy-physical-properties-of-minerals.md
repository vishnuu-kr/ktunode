# Engineering Geology: Module 2 - Mineralogy

## Topic: Physical Properties of Minerals

### Introduction to Mineralogy

Mineralogy is the study of minerals, their formation, composition, structure, and properties. Understanding the physical properties of minerals is crucial in Engineering Geology for several reasons:

*   **Identification:** Physical properties are the primary tools for identifying unknown minerals.
*   **Engineering Behavior:** These properties directly influence how rocks and the materials derived from them behave under engineering stresses (e.g., strength, durability, permeability).
*   **Resource Exploration:** Recognizing specific mineral assemblages can indicate the presence of valuable resources.
*   **Predicting Geological Processes:** Certain mineral properties can provide clues about the conditions under which they formed.

### Learning Outcomes Covered:

This module will equip you to:

*   Describe and explain the fundamental physical properties of minerals used for identification and engineering assessment.
*   Relate observed physical properties to the underlying atomic structure and chemical composition of minerals.
*   Discuss the importance of physical properties of minerals in engineering geological applications.
*   Apply knowledge of physical properties to identify common rock-forming minerals and their significance in engineering.

---

### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Mineral:** A naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and a characteristic crystalline structure.
*   **Crystal Structure:** The orderly, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of atoms within a mineral. This arrangement dictates many physical properties.
*   **Chemical Composition:** The specific elements and their proportions present in a mineral (e.g., SiO₂ for quartz).

### Major Physical Properties of Minerals

We will explore the key physical properties used to identify and characterize minerals:

#### 1. Color

*   **Definition:** The visual appearance of a mineral to the eye.
*   **Cause:** Can be due to the intrinsic chemical composition (chromophores) or impurities within the crystal structure.
*   **Significance in Engineering:** While useful for initial identification, color can be misleading as impurities can cause significant variations.
    *   **Example:** Quartz can be clear (pure), smoky (due to irradiation), amethyst (manganese impurities), or rose quartz (trace elements).
*   **Important Note:** Color is often the least reliable property for definitive identification due to variations.

#### 2. Streak

*   **Definition:** The color of a mineral's powder when rubbed against an unglazed porcelain plate (streak plate).
*   **Cause:** Results from the physical breakdown of the mineral into its constituent particles.
*   **Significance in Engineering:** A more reliable indicator of color than the mineral's bulk color, as it's less affected by surface oxidation or impurities.
    *   **Example:** Hematite (an iron ore) can appear black or silvery in bulk, but always has a reddish-brown streak. Pyrite ("fool's gold") is brassy yellow but has a greenish-black streak.
*   **Important Note:** Most minerals with a hardness greater than 7 will produce no streak, as they will scratch the streak plate itself.

#### 3. Luster

*   **Definition:** The way light reflects off the surface of a mineral.
*   **Cause:** Dependent on the chemical composition and the quality of the crystal surface.
*   **Categories:**
    *   **Metallic:** Opaque, shiny, like polished metal. (e.g., Galena, Pyrite)
    *   **Non-metallic:**
        *   **Vitreous (Glassy):** Like glass. (e.g., Quartz, Feldspar)
        *   **Resinous:** Like resin or plastic. (e.g., Sulfur)
        *   **Pearly:** Iridescent sheen, like a pearl. (e.g., Talc, some Micas)
        *   **Greasy:** Appears as if coated with oil. (e.g., Nepheline)
        *   **Silky:** Fibrous appearance. (e.g., Asbestos, some Gypsum)
        *   **Adamantine:** Brilliant, diamond-like. (e.g., Diamond, Zircon)
        *   **Earthy/Dull:** No reflection, porous. (e.g., Kaolinite, some clays)
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Provides important clues about chemical bonding and crystal structure. Metallic luster often indicates opaque minerals which might be opaque due to heavy elements or high electron density.
    *   **Example:** The vitreous luster of quartz is a key identifier. The metallic luster of pyrite is often mistaken for gold (which has a brighter yellow metallic luster).

#### 4. Hardness

*   **Definition:** The resistance of a mineral to scratching or abrasion.
*   **Cause:** Directly related to the strength of the chemical bonds and the atomic structure. Stronger bonds and tighter packing result in higher hardness.
*   **Measurement:** Measured using Mohs Hardness Scale (a relative scale of 1 to 10).
    *   **Mohs Hardness Scale:**
        1.  Talc
        2.  Gypsum
        3.  Calcite
        4.  Fluorite
        5.  Apatite
        6.  Orthoclase Feldspar
        7.  Quartz
        8.  Topaz
        9.  Corundum
        10. Diamond
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Extremely important for assessing a mineral's durability and resistance to weathering and abrasion. Rocks composed of harder minerals tend to be more resistant to erosion.
    *   **Examples for Testing:**
        *   Fingernail (hardness 2.5)
        *   Copper coin (hardness 3.5)
        *   Knife blade or glass (hardness 5.5)
        *   Steel file (hardness 6.5)
    *   **Practical Application:** Quartz (hardness 7) is a very common and hard mineral, contributing to the durability of many building stones like granite. Talc (hardness 1) is soft and easily scratched.
*   **Important Note:** Hardness is determined by testing a mineral against materials of known hardness.

#### 5. Cleavage

*   **Definition:** The tendency of a mineral to break along smooth, flat surfaces that correspond to planes of weakness in its crystal structure.
*   **Cause:** Weak atomic bonds along specific crystallographic planes.
*   **Description:** Classified by the number of directions of cleavage and the angles between the cleavage planes.
    *   **Perfect:** Breaks readily along smooth planes. (e.g., Mica - 1 direction, book-like sheets)
    *   **Good:** Breaks along preferred directions but not as easily as perfect. (e.g., Calcite - 3 directions at oblique angles, producing rhombohedrons)
    *   **Fair:** Some tendency to break along planes.
    *   **Poor:** Little tendency to break along planes.
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Affects how rocks will fracture and break, influencing slope stability, excavation behavior, and the ease of quarrying. Minerals with prominent cleavage can lead to anisotropic behavior in rocks.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Micas (Muscovite, Biotite):** One direction of perfect cleavage, resulting in thin, flexible sheets. This makes mica-rich rocks (like schists) prone to splitting.
        *   **Feldspars:** Two directions of good cleavage, at or near 90 degrees.
        *   **Halite (Rock Salt):** Three directions of perfect cleavage, at 90 degrees, forming cubes.
*   **Important Note:** Cleavage is a property of the mineral itself, not the crystal shape.

#### 6. Fracture

*   **Definition:** The way a mineral breaks when it does not cleave.
*   **Cause:** Occurs when the atomic bonds are equally strong in all directions, or when the breaking planes are not planes of weakness.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Conchoidal:** Smooth, curved surfaces resembling the inside of a shell. (e.g., Quartz, Obsidian)
    *   **Fibrous/Splintery:** Breaks into fibers or splinters. (e.g., Asbestos)
    *   **Hackly:** Jagged, sharp edges. (e.g., Native metals like copper)
    *   **Irregular/Uneven:** Rough, irregular surfaces. (Most common type)
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Affects the fragmentation characteristics of rocks during blasting, crushing, and excavation. Conchoidal fracture in minerals like quartz can create sharp, abrasive particles.
    *   **Example:** The conchoidal fracture of flint and chert is responsible for their sharp edges when worked into tools.

#### 7. Crystal Form (Habit)

*   **Definition:** The characteristic external shape of an individual mineral crystal, assuming it grew unimpeded.
*   **Cause:** Reflects the underlying internal crystal structure and the conditions of crystal growth.
*   **Common Forms:**
    *   **Cubic:** Cube-shaped. (e.g., Halite, Galena)
    *   **Prismatic:** Elongated, rod-like. (e.g., Quartz - hexagonal prism with pyramidal terminations, Tourmaline)
    *   **Bladed:** Flattened, elongated. (e.g., Kyanite)
    *   **Acicular:** Needle-like. (e.g., Wavellite)
    *   **Dendritic:** Branching, tree-like. (e.g., Dendritic quartz, Manganese oxides)
    *   **Massive:** No distinct crystal form, occurring in irregular aggregates. (e.g., some chalcedony)
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Can provide visual cues for identification. The habit can also indicate growth environments and potential anisotropy in rock behavior.
    *   **Example:** The prismatic habit of quartz is a useful identifying feature. The bladed habit of kyanite can influence the directional strength of rocks.

#### 8. Specific Gravity (Density)

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the mineral's density to the density of water. It's a measure of how heavy a mineral is for its size.
*   **Cause:** Determined by the atomic weight of the elements present and how tightly they are packed in the crystal structure. Minerals with heavier atoms and compact structures have higher specific gravity.
*   **Calculation:** Specific Gravity = (Weight of mineral in air) / (Weight of mineral in air - Weight of mineral in water)
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Crucial for geotechnical engineering.
    *   **Material Properties:** Higher specific gravity can correlate with increased strength and stiffness.
    *   **Foundation Design:** Used to calculate loads and bearing capacities.
    *   **Aggregate Selection:** Influences the density and performance of concrete and asphalt.
    *   **Ore Identification:** High specific gravity is characteristic of many metallic ores (e.g., Galena, density 7.5).
    *   **Example:** Quartz has a specific gravity of 2.65. Galena (lead sulfide) has a specific gravity of around 7.5, making it noticeably heavier than quartz when comparing equal volumes.
*   **Important Note:** Many common rock-forming minerals have specific gravities between 2.5 and 3.0.

#### 9. Tenacity

*   **Definition:** A mineral's resistance to breaking, bending, or powdering.
*   **Cause:** Related to the strength of chemical bonds and the presence or absence of cleavage.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Brittle:** Breaks or powders easily. (e.g., Quartz, Feldspar, Calcite)
    *   **Malleable:** Can be hammered into thin sheets without breaking. (e.g., Gold, Copper)
    *   **Ductile:** Can be drawn into wires. (e.g., Gold, Copper)
    *   **Sectile:** Can be cut into thin shavings with a knife. (e.g., Gypsum)
    *   **Flexible:** Bends but does not break, and remains in its bent state. (e.g., Talc, Gypsum)
    *   **Elastic:** Bends but does not break, and returns to its original shape. (e.g., Micas)
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Primarily relevant for native elements (metals) and minerals used for specific applications. Micas' elasticity is important in their use as electrical insulators. Brittleness affects the ease of crushing.
    *   **Example:** Native gold is malleable and ductile. Mica's elasticity is a key property for its use in electronics.

#### 10. Magnetism

*   **Definition:** The ability of a mineral to attract a magnet or be attracted by a magnet.
*   **Cause:** Presence of iron or other magnetic elements in the mineral's composition and structure.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Paramagnetic:** Weakly attracted to a magnet.
    *   **Ferromagnetic:** Strongly attracted to a magnet, and can themselves become magnetized. (e.g., Magnetite)
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Useful for identifying minerals containing iron. Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) is strongly magnetic and can be easily identified with a magnet. This is important in magnetic surveys for mineral exploration.
    *   **Example:** Magnetite is a common accessory mineral in igneous and metamorphic rocks.

#### 11. Reaction to Dilute Hydrochloric Acid (HCl)

*   **Definition:** The effervescence (fizzing) that occurs when a mineral is brought into contact with dilute hydrochloric acid.
*   **Cause:** Acid reacts with carbonate minerals, releasing carbon dioxide gas (CO₂).
*   **Significance in Engineering:** A very important test for identifying carbonate minerals like calcite and dolomite. The strength of the reaction can help distinguish between them.
    *   **Example:**
        *   **Calcite (CaCO₃):** Reacts vigorously with cold dilute HCl, producing abundant effervescence.
        *   **Dolomite (CaMg(CO₃)₂):** Reacts weakly or not at all with cold dilute HCl. If powdered, it will effervesce readily.
        *   **Aragonite (CaCO₃ polymorph of calcite):** Also reacts vigorously.
*   **Important Note:** This test is usually performed with dilute (10%) HCl. A drop is placed on the mineral or a small piece of the mineral is placed in a drop of acid.

#### 12. Feel

*   **Definition:** The tactile sensation of a mineral when touched.
*   **Cause:** Related to hardness, luster, and presence of surface coatings or impurities.
*   **Description:**
    *   **Smooth:** (e.g., Talc, Soapstone)
    *   **Greasy/Soapy:** (e.g., Talc)
    *   **Gritty:** (e.g., Quartz)
    *   **Cold:** Due to high thermal conductivity. (e.g., Metallic minerals)
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Can be a secondary indicator for identification, especially for very distinctive minerals like talc.
    *   **Example:** Talc feels exceptionally smooth and "soapy" to the touch, a characteristic often exploited in its industrial uses.

#### 13. Optical Properties (Advanced - brief mention)

*   **Definition:** Properties related to how light passes through transparent or translucent minerals.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Transparency/Diaphaneity:** Whether light can pass through (transparent, translucent, opaque).
    *   **Refractive Index:** How much light bends as it enters the mineral.
    *   **Birefringence:** The splitting of light into two rays.
    *   **Pleochroism:** Different colors observed when viewed from different directions.
*   **Significance in Engineering:** Primarily used in microscopy for detailed mineral identification, but the transparency of aggregates can influence light transmission in construction materials.

### Relationship Between Properties and Atomic Structure/Composition

It is crucial to remember that all physical properties are a direct consequence of a mineral's:

1.  **Chemical Composition:** The types of atoms and their bonding.
    *   **Example:** Strong ionic or covalent bonds lead to high hardness and melting points. Metallic bonds lead to metallic luster and conductivity.
2.  **Crystal Structure:** The arrangement of atoms in a repeating lattice.
    *   **Example:** Planes of weakness in the crystal lattice lead to cleavage. The symmetry of the lattice dictates crystal form.

---

### Importance of Physical Properties in Engineering Geology

*   **Material Characterization:** Understanding the properties of minerals in a rock mass allows engineers to predict the rock's behavior.
*   **Construction Materials:** The properties of minerals determine their suitability as aggregate, cementitious materials, or dimensional stone.
    *   **Example:** Hardness and durability of aggregate minerals are essential for pavement and concrete. The chemical inertness of feldspar is beneficial in concrete.
*   **Foundation Engineering:** Mineral composition influences strength, compressibility, and permeability of soils and rocks.
    *   **Example:** Swelling clays (like montmorillonite) with specific mineral structures can cause significant foundation issues.
*   **Slope Stability:** Cleavage and fracture patterns of minerals contribute to the mechanical behavior of rock slopes.
*   **Tunneling and Excavation:** Mineral hardness, abrasiveness, and tendency to cleave or fracture affect drilling, blasting, and excavation methods.
*   **Durability and Weathering:** Mineral properties dictate how rocks will degrade under environmental conditions.
    *   **Example:** Pyrite weathering can produce sulfuric acid, leading to rock degradation and acid mine drainage.

### Identification of Common Rock-Forming Minerals (Examples)

*   **Quartz (SiO₂):**
    *   **Luster:** Vitreous
    *   **Hardness:** 7
    *   **Cleavage:** None (fractures conchoidally)
    *   **Color:** Variable (clear, white, grey, pink, purple, brown)
    *   **Streak:** White
    *   **Feel:** Gritty
    *   **Engineering Significance:** Abundant, hard, durable. Forms the bulk of many igneous (granite), metamorphic (quartzite), and sedimentary (sandstone) rocks. Contributes to the strength and abrasion resistance of construction materials.
*   **Feldspar (e.g., Orthoclase - KAlSi₃O₈, Plagioclase - (Na,Ca)Al(Si,Al)₃O₈):**
    *   **Luster:** Vitreous to pearly
    *   **Hardness:** 6
    *   **Cleavage:** Two directions, nearly at 90 degrees.
    *   **Color:** White, pink, grey, greenish
    *   **Streak:** White
    *   **Specific Gravity:** ~2.6-2.8
    *   **Engineering Significance:** Most abundant mineral group in Earth's crust. Forms the backbone of igneous rocks like granite and basalt. Weathering of feldspar can produce clay minerals, affecting soil properties.
*   **Mica (e.g., Muscovite - KAl₂(AlSi₃O₁₀)(OH)₂, Biotite - K(Mg,Fe)₃(AlSi₃O₁₀)(OH)₂):**
    *   **Luster:** Vitreous to pearly (Muscovite), vitreous to submetallic (Biotite)
    *   **Hardness:** 2-3
    *   **Cleavage:** One direction, perfect (splits into thin flexible sheets)
    *   **Color:** Muscovite - colorless to silver; Biotite - black to dark brown
    *   **Streak:** White (Muscovite), White to pale brown (Biotite)
    *   **Tenacity:** Elastic (Muscovite), Flexible but not elastic (Biotite)
    *   **Engineering Significance:** Causes rocks like schists and gneisses to be foliated and anisotropic, affecting their strength and stability in excavations and slopes. Elasticity of mica is useful in electrical insulation.
*   **Calcite (CaCO₃):**
    *   **Luster:** Vitreous to dull
    *   **Hardness:** 3
    *   **Cleavage:** Three directions, perfect, oblique angles (forms rhombohedrons)
    *   **Color:** Colorless, white, grey, yellow, blue, red, brown
    *   **Streak:** White
    *   **Reaction to HCl:** Vigorous effervescence with cold dilute HCl
    *   **Engineering Significance:** Main constituent of limestone and marble. Used in cement production. Its softness and reaction to acid make it susceptible to chemical weathering and erosion, impacting the durability of limestone structures and aggregate.
*   **Hematite (Fe₂O₃):**
    *   **Luster:** Metallic to submetallic, earthy
    *   **Hardness:** 5-6
    *   **Cleavage:** None (often fractures unevenly)
    *   **Color:** Black to reddish-brown in bulk, characteristic reddish-brown streak
    *   **Streak:** Reddish-brown
    *   **Engineering Significance:** Primary iron ore. Its presence can stain rocks reddish-brown. Weathering can lead to iron staining.

### Practice Questions

1.  A mineral sample feels greasy and can be easily scratched with a fingernail. What property is being tested by the "greasy" feel, and what does the ease of scratching suggest about its hardness?
2.  Describe the difference between cleavage and fracture. Give an example of a mineral that exhibits perfect cleavage in one direction and a mineral that exhibits conchoidal fracture.
3.  Why is the streak test often considered more reliable than the color of a mineral for identification? Provide an example of two minerals where this is true.
4.  A rock sample taken from a construction site is found to be easily crumbled and disintegrates in water. Which physical property of the dominant minerals within the rock might explain its poor performance?
5.  You are given two samples, A and B. Sample A is visibly much heavier than Sample B for the same volume. What physical property is primarily responsible for this difference, and what does it suggest about the mineral composition of A compared to B?
6.  Explain the significance of the Mohs hardness scale in engineering geology.
7.  A mineral vigorously fizzes when a drop of dilute hydrochloric acid is applied. What mineral group is it likely to belong to, and why is this test important for engineering applications?
8.  How would the elastic tenacity of mica affect the engineering behavior of a schist bedrock compared to a granite bedrock (which is composed of quartz and feldspar)?

### Answers to Practice Questions

1.  The "greasy" feel is a tactile property. The ease of scratching with a fingernail (hardness ~2.5) suggests the mineral has a low hardness on the Mohs scale, likely less than 3.
2.  **Cleavage** is the tendency to break along smooth, flat planes of weakness in the crystal structure, while **fracture** is how a mineral breaks when it does not cleave.
    *   **Cleavage Example:** Mica (e.g., Muscovite) exhibits perfect cleavage in one direction.
    *   **Fracture Example:** Quartz exhibits conchoidal fracture.
3.  The streak test is more reliable than color because the color of a mineral can be affected by trace impurities or surface oxidation, which do not necessarily alter the color of its powder.
    *   **Example:** Hematite can appear black or silvery in bulk but always has a reddish-brown streak. Pyrite is brassy yellow but has a greenish-black streak.
4.  The poor performance and disintegration suggest that the dominant minerals are likely soft, easily weathered, or susceptible to hydration and expansion. This could include clay minerals (like smectites), easily dissolved carbonates (like calcite), or minerals with poor cohesion.
5.  Sample A is visibly heavier than Sample B for the same volume due to a higher **specific gravity**. This suggests that Sample A is composed of minerals with denser atoms and/or a more compact crystal structure, such as metallic ores (e.g., galena, magnetite), compared to Sample B which might be composed of lighter silicate minerals (e.g., quartz, feldspar).
6.  The Mohs hardness scale provides a relative measure of a mineral's resistance to scratching. In engineering geology, this translates to the **durability and abrasion resistance** of construction materials. Rocks composed of minerals with high hardness (like quartz) are more resistant to weathering and wear, making them suitable for demanding applications like road surfaces and concrete aggregates. Conversely, softer minerals will wear away more easily.
7.  The mineral likely belongs to the **carbonate** group (e.g., calcite, dolomite). This test is important for engineering because carbonate rocks (limestones, marbles) are widely used in construction (cement, aggregate, dimension stone). Understanding their reactivity to acidic environments (like acid rain or polluted soils) is crucial for assessing the long-term durability of structures built with these materials.
8.  The elastic tenacity of mica means that schist, being foliated with mica, can flex and deform to some extent without permanent damage or fracture. This makes schist potentially more prone to bending or warping under stress compared to granite, which is generally brittle and will fracture more cleanly. This can influence the stability of slopes and excavations in schistose rocks, as the foliation planes can become zones of weakness and slip.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Interconnectedness:** Physical properties are not independent; they are all linked to the mineral's atomic structure and chemical composition.
*   **Relative vs. Absolute:** Properties like hardness are relative (Mohs scale), while others like specific gravity can be precisely measured.
*   **Practical Application:** Focus on how each property impacts engineering design, construction, and material selection.
*   **Identification Strategy:** Use a combination of properties for accurate mineral identification, not just one. Color is often the least reliable alone.
*   **Rock vs. Mineral:** Remember that rocks are aggregates of minerals. The properties of a rock are a summation of the properties of its constituent minerals and how they are intergrown.
