# CONCRETE TECHNOLOGY: Module 2 - Properties of Hardened Concrete

This module delves into the fundamental mechanical properties of hardened concrete and explores how the water-cement ratio significantly influences these characteristics. Understanding these properties is crucial for designing durable and functional concrete structures.

---

## 1. Properties of Hardened Concrete

Hardened concrete possesses various properties that dictate its performance and suitability for different applications. The key mechanical properties are:

### 1.1. Modulus of Elasticity (E)

The Modulus of Elasticity, also known as the Young's Modulus, is a measure of a material's stiffness. It represents the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region of deformation.

*   **Definition:** It quantifies how much a material will deform elastically under a given load. In concrete, it indicates the stiffness of the concrete mix.
*   **Significance:**
    *   Crucial for structural design, especially in calculating deflections and stresses under load.
    *   Influences the cracking behavior of concrete.
    *   Higher modulus means less deformation under stress.
*   **Factors Affecting Modulus of Elasticity:**
    *   **Compressive Strength:** Generally, higher compressive strength leads to a higher modulus of elasticity.
    *   **Aggregate Properties:** The modulus of elasticity of the aggregate plays a significant role. Stiffer aggregates lead to stiffer concrete.
    *   **Cement Content:** Higher cement content can slightly increase the modulus.
    *   **Age of Concrete:** Modulus increases with age as hydration continues.
    *   **Moisture Content:** The modulus of elasticity of concrete is lower when it is dry compared to when it is saturated.
*   **Typical Values:**
    *   Normal strength concrete (e.g., 20-30 MPa compressive strength): $E$ typically ranges from 20,000 to 30,000 MPa.
    *   High-strength concrete: Can have moduli exceeding 40,000 MPa.
*   **Calculation/Estimation:** Empirical formulas are often used, such as those provided in building codes (e.g., ACI, Eurocode). A common empirical relationship relates the modulus of elasticity ($E_c$) to the square root of the compressive strength ($f_{ck}$):
    $E_c \approx 22,000 \left( \frac{f_{ck}}{10} \right)^{0.3}$ (MPa)

---

### 1.2. Compressive Strength ($f_{ck}$)

Compressive strength is the most fundamental property of concrete and is the primary criterion for its classification and design.

*   **Definition:** The maximum compressive stress that concrete can withstand before failure.
*   **Significance:**
    *   Determines the load-carrying capacity of concrete members.
    *   Used for mix design and quality control.
    *   Correlated with other mechanical properties.
*   **Testing Method:** Typically determined by testing cylindrical or cubical specimens under axial compression until failure. The most common test is the compression test on cylinders (150mm diameter x 300mm height or 100mm diameter x 200mm height) or cubes (150mm side).
*   **Factors Affecting Compressive Strength:**
    *   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):** As discussed later, this is a primary factor. Lower w/c ratios lead to higher strengths.
    *   **Aggregate Properties:** Strength, shape, texture, and grading of aggregates influence compressive strength.
    *   **Cement Type and Content:** Different cement types have varying strength development rates. Higher cement content generally increases strength.
    *   **Admixtures:** Certain admixtures (e.g., superplasticizers, silica fume) can significantly improve compressive strength.
    *   **Curing Conditions:** Proper curing (temperature and moisture) is essential for strength development.
    *   **Age of Concrete:** Strength increases with age, primarily up to 28 days, but continues to develop over time.
    *   **Compaction:** Adequate compaction reduces voids and increases strength.
*   **Characteristic Compressive Strength ($f_{ck}$):** The strength of concrete below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall. This is the basis for structural design.

---

### 1.3. Split Tensile Strength ($f_{st}$)

Concrete has low tensile strength because of the inherent weakness of the cement paste-aggregate bond and the presence of micro-cracks. The split tensile strength test is an indirect method to measure this property.

*   **Definition:** The tensile strength of concrete determined by applying a compressive load along the diameter of a cylindrical specimen. The induced tensile stress perpendicular to the applied load causes splitting.
*   **Significance:**
    *   Important for understanding the resistance of concrete to cracking under tensile stresses (e.g., due to shrinkage, thermal expansion).
    *   Relevant for pavements, beams, and other structures subjected to bending.
*   **Testing Method:** The Brazilian Test (Split Cylinder Test) involves placing a cylindrical specimen horizontally between the loading platens of a compression testing machine and applying a line load along the top and bottom.
*   **Relationship with Compressive Strength:** Split tensile strength is typically about 8-15% of the compressive strength.
    $f_{st} \approx 0.6 \text{ to } 0.7 \times \sqrt{f_{ck}}$ (MPa)
*   **Factors Affecting Split Tensile Strength:** Similar factors as compressive strength, with particular sensitivity to the quality of the paste-aggregate bond.

---

### 1.4. Flexural Strength ($f_{f}$)

Flexural strength, also known as modulus of rupture (MOR), is the tensile stress developed at the extreme fiber of a beam or slab subjected to bending when it fails.

*   **Definition:** The maximum bending tensile stress that concrete can withstand before cracking or failure.
*   **Significance:**
    *   Crucial for the design of structural elements subjected to bending, such as pavements, bridge decks, and beams.
    *   More directly relevant than split tensile strength for bending applications.
*   **Testing Method:** Typically determined by the three-point or four-point bending test on a prismatic (beam) specimen. The load is applied at the center of a simply supported beam (three-point) or at two points along the beam (four-point).
*   **Relationship with Compressive Strength:** Flexural strength is generally higher than split tensile strength and is typically around 15-20% of the compressive strength.
    $f_{f} \approx 0.7 \text{ to } 1.0 \times \sqrt{f_{ck}}$ (MPa)
*   **Factors Affecting Flexural Strength:**
    *   **Water-Cement Ratio:** Lower w/c ratios improve flexural strength.
    *   **Aggregate Characteristics:** The size, shape, and strength of coarse aggregates significantly influence flexural strength, as failure often occurs at the aggregate-paste interface.
    *   **Curing Conditions:** Proper curing is essential for developing flexural strength.
    *   **Surface Condition:** The condition of the tension face of the specimen can influence the test results.

## 2. Effect of Water-Cement Ratio (w/c) on Properties of Concrete

The water-cement ratio is arguably the single most important factor determining the strength and durability of concrete. It is the mass of water divided by the mass of cement in the mix.

*   **Key Principle:** Water in concrete serves two primary purposes:
    1.  **Hydration:** Reacts chemically with cement to form hardened paste that binds aggregates.
    2.  **Workability:** Provides fluidity for mixing, placing, and compacting.
*   **The "Ideal" vs. "Actual" Water Content:**
    *   **Ideal Water Content:** Only a fraction of the water added is consumed by hydration (approximately 0.22-0.25 by weight of cement is required for complete hydration).
    *   **Actual Water Content:** More water is added than required for hydration to achieve adequate workability. The excess water creates capillary pores in the hardened cement paste.
*   **Impact of w/c Ratio on Properties:**

    | Property                    | Effect of Decreasing w/c Ratio | Explanation                                                                                                                                                                 |
    | :-------------------------- | :----------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
    | **Compressive Strength**    | **Increases**                  | Lower w/c ratio leads to less excess water, fewer capillary pores, denser cement paste, and stronger bonds between cement hydration products and aggregates. **(Abrams' Law)** |
    | **Tensile Strength**        | **Increases**                  | Similar reasoning as compressive strength; the paste quality improves.                                                                                                        |
    | **Flexural Strength**       | **Increases**                  | Improved paste quality and aggregate-paste bond contribute to higher flexural strength.                                                                                        |
    | **Modulus of Elasticity**   | **Increases**                  | Denser and stiffer cement paste, along with better aggregate-paste interaction, results in a higher modulus.                                                                |
    | **Durability**              | **Increases**                  | Lower w/c ratio results in a less permeable concrete, reducing the ingress of aggressive substances (e.g., sulfates, chlorides) and improving resistance to freeze-thaw cycles. |
    | **Shrinkage (Drying)**      | **Decreases**                  | Less free water available for evaporation means less volume change due to drying.                                                                                             |
    | **Creep**                   | **Decreases**                  | Reduced porosity and moisture content in the paste lead to lower creep deformation under sustained load.                                                                        |
    | **Permeability**            | **Decreases**                  | Fewer interconnected pores mean a denser matrix, significantly reducing the passage of fluids.                                                                                |
    | **Workability**             | **Decreases**                  | Less water means a stiffer, less fluid mix, making it harder to place and compact without admixtures.                                                                       |

*   **Abrams' Law:** A fundamental principle in concrete technology, often stated as: "The compressive strength of concrete is inversely proportional to the water-cement ratio, provided that the mix is workable." Mathematically, this can be represented as:
    $f_c \propto \frac{1}{w/c}$ or $f_c = \frac{A}{B^{w/c}}$ where A and B are constants.

*   **Practical Implications:**
    *   To achieve high strength and durability, a low w/c ratio is desirable.
    *   However, very low w/c ratios can lead to poor workability, making placement difficult and potentially introducing voids if not compacted properly.
    *   The use of admixtures, particularly superplasticizers, allows for the achievement of low w/c ratios while maintaining excellent workability.

**Question 2:**
Which property is considered the most fundamental for classifying and designing concrete, and how is it typically tested?

**Question 3:**
Explain the difference between split tensile strength and flexural strength. For which application is flexural strength more directly relevant?

According to Abrams' Law, how does the water-cement ratio affect the compressive strength of concrete?

**Question 5:**

    b) Workability
    c) Permeability

## 5. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
The Modulus of Elasticity of concrete is a measure of its stiffness, representing the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region of deformation. Three significant factors influencing it are:
1.  Compressive Strength of concrete.
2.  Properties of aggregates (specifically their modulus of elasticity).
3.  Water-cement ratio.

**Answer 2:**
Compressive strength is considered the most fundamental property for classifying and designing concrete. It is typically tested by applying axial compression to cylindrical or cubical specimens until failure.

**Answer 3:**
Split tensile strength is determined indirectly by applying compression to a cylinder, inducing tensile stress perpendicular to the load. Flexural strength (modulus of rupture) is the direct tensile stress at the extreme fiber of a beam when subjected to bending. Flexural strength is more directly relevant for applications involving bending, such as pavements and beams.

**Answer 4:**
According to Abrams' Law, the compressive strength of concrete is inversely proportional to the water-cement ratio. This means that as the water-cement ratio decreases, the compressive strength of the concrete increases, provided the mix remains workable.

**Answer 5:**
Reducing the water-cement ratio from 0.50 to 0.40 would have the following general impacts:
    a) **Compressive Strength:** It would **increase**.
    b) **Workability:** It would **decrease** (the mix would become stiffer).
    c) **Permeability:** It would **decrease** (the concrete would become less permeable).

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **w/c Ratio is King:** The water-cement ratio is the most critical factor controlling strength and durability. Lower w/c = Higher strength, higher durability.
*   **Strength vs. Workability Trade-off:** Achieving very low w/c ratios necessitates the use of admixtures (like superplasticizers) to maintain adequate workability.
*   **Indirect Tensile Strength:** Concrete is weak in tension. Split tensile strength and flexural strength are always lower than compressive strength.
*   **Modulus of Elasticity and Stiffness:** A higher Modulus of Elasticity indicates a stiffer material, meaning less deformation under load.
*   **Age Matters:** Concrete strength and modulus of elasticity generally increase with age due to continued hydration.
*   **Aggregate Influence:** The properties of the aggregates (strength, stiffness, shape) significantly impact the hardened properties of concrete.
*   **Durability Link:** Lower w/c ratios are directly linked to improved durability due to reduced permeability.
