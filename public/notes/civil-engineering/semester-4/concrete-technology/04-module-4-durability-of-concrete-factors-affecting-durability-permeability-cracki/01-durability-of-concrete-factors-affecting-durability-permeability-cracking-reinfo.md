# CONCRETE TECHNOLOGY - Module 4: Durability of Concrete

## Introduction to Durability

**Definition:** Durability is the ability of concrete to resist weathering action, chemical attack, abrasion, or any other process of deterioration so that it retains its original properties, quality, and serviceability when exposed to its service environment throughout its intended service life.

**Importance of Durability:**
*   Ensures the long-term performance and safety of structures.
*   Reduces maintenance and repair costs.
*   Minimizes the need for premature replacement of concrete elements.
*   Contributes to the sustainability of construction.

## Factors Affecting Durability

Durability is influenced by a complex interplay of factors related to:

### 1. Material Properties

*   **Cement Type and Content:**
    *   **CEM I (Ordinary Portland Cement):** Higher C3S content leads to faster strength gain but can be more susceptible to sulphate attack and ASR if not properly formulated.
    *   **CEM II, III, IV, V (Blended Cements):** Incorporating Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCMs) like fly ash, slag, and silica fume significantly improves durability by:
        *   Reducing heat of hydration (minimizing thermal cracking).
        *   Improving resistance to chemical attack (sulphates, chlorides).
        *   Reducing permeability.
        *   Mitigating Alkali-Silica Reaction (ASR).
    *   **Higher Cement Content:** Generally leads to denser and less permeable concrete, but can increase heat of hydration and risk of cracking.
*   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):**
    *   **Key Concept:** Lower w/c ratio is the most critical factor for durability.
    *   **Effect:** Reduces pore volume, leading to lower permeability, higher strength, and improved resistance to chemical ingress and freeze-thaw damage.
    *   **Example:** A w/c of 0.40 will produce significantly more durable concrete than a w/c of 0.60.
*   **Aggregate Properties:**
    *   **Cleanliness:** Aggregates should be free from deleterious materials like clay, silt, organic matter, and salts, which can affect bonding and introduce impurities.
    *   **Strength and Hardness:** Contributes to overall concrete strength and resistance to abrasion.
    *   **Gradation:** Well-graded aggregates create a denser concrete mix with less voids, reducing permeability.
    *   **Alkali-Silica Reactivity (ASR):** Certain aggregates containing reactive silica can react with alkalis from cement, forming expansive gels that cause cracking. (Discussed in detail later).
*   **Admixtures:**
    *   **Water Reducers/Superplasticizers:** Allow for lower w/c ratios while maintaining workability, thus improving durability.
    *   **Air-Entraining Admixtures:** Introduce microscopic air bubbles, improving resistance to frost damage.
    *   **Corrosion Inhibitors:** Can protect steel reinforcement from corrosion.
    *   **Pozzolans (SCMs):** As mentioned above, they improve durability.

### 2. Mix Design and Construction Practices

*   **Adequate Compaction:** Proper compaction removes entrapped air and voids, creating a denser, less permeable concrete. Poor compaction leads to honeycombing and increased permeability.
*   **Proper Curing:** Essential for hydration and development of concrete properties. Inadequate curing leads to poor strength, increased permeability, and surface defects.
    *   **Methods:** Water curing (ponding, spraying, wet coverings), membrane curing.
    *   **Importance:** Maintains moisture in the concrete for hydration to continue, especially at the surface.
*   **Sufficient Cover to Reinforcement:** Provides a barrier against ingress of aggressive substances and prevents premature corrosion.
*   **Crack Control:** Cracks provide pathways for aggressive substances to penetrate the concrete.

### 3. Exposure Conditions (Service Environment)

*   **Chemical Attack:**
    *   **Sulphates:** Present in soil and groundwater, can react with cement paste to cause expansion and disintegration.
    *   **Acids:** Found in industrial wastewater, acid rain, can dissolve the cement paste.
    *   **Chlorides:** From de-icing salts, seawater, can penetrate concrete and corrode steel reinforcement.
*   **Physical Attack:**
    *   **Freeze-Thaw Cycles:** Water in pores freezes and expands, causing internal stresses and cracking.
    *   **Abrasion/Erosion:** Wear and tear caused by contact with solid particles (e.g., traffic, flowing water).
    *   **Carbonation:** Reaction of atmospheric carbon dioxide with calcium hydroxide in cement paste, reducing pH and initiating reinforcement corrosion.
*   **Moisture Content:** The presence of moisture is essential for most aggressive chemical reactions and freeze-thaw damage.

## Key Durability Mechanisms and Deterioration Processes

### 1. Permeability

*   **Definition:** Permeability is a measure of the ease with which fluids (water, gases) can pass through a porous material like concrete. It is governed by the size, distribution, and interconnectedness of pores.
*   **Factors Affecting Permeability:**
    *   **w/c Ratio (Most significant):** Lower w/c = less permeable.
    *   **Cement Content:** Higher cement content (within limits) generally reduces permeability.
    *   **SCMs:** Improve particle packing and form denser microstructure, reducing permeability.
    *   **Compaction:** Proper compaction minimizes voids.
    *   **Curing:** Adequate curing leads to a more developed and less permeable pore structure.
    *   **Aggregate Gradation:** Well-graded aggregates lead to denser concrete.
*   **Importance:** Low permeability is crucial for preventing the ingress of aggressive substances like chlorides, sulphates, and carbon dioxide.
*   **Measurement:** Can be measured by methods like the water permeability test or gas permeability test.

### 2. Cracking

*   **Definition:** Cracks are fractures in the concrete that extend through its entire thickness or partially.
*   **Causes of Cracking:**
    *   **Plastic Shrinkage:** Occurs shortly after placing due to rapid surface drying while the concrete is still plastic.
    *   **Drying Shrinkage:** Volume reduction due to the loss of moisture as concrete hardens and dries.
    *   **Thermal Contraction:** Volume reduction due to temperature drops after hydration heat dissipates or due to environmental temperature changes.
    *   **Thermal Expansion/Contraction:** Differences in thermal expansion between concrete and reinforcement, or within the concrete itself due to temperature gradients.
    *   **Sulphate Attack:** Expansion due to chemical reactions.
    *   **ASR:** Expansion due to gel formation.
    *   **Overloading:** Exceeding the tensile strength of concrete.
    *   **Settlement:** Uneven foundation settlement causing differential stresses.
    *   **Reinforcement Corrosion:** Expansion of rust products.
*   **Impact on Durability:** Cracks provide direct pathways for aggressive agents to reach the steel reinforcement or to penetrate deeper into the concrete, accelerating deterioration.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Control w/c ratio.
    *   Use SCMs.
    *   Proper curing.
    *   Control placing and finishing operations.
    *   Use appropriate crack width control techniques (e.g., reinforcement, jointing).

### 3. Reinforcement Corrosion

*   **Definition:** Corrosion is an electrochemical process that leads to the degradation of steel reinforcement.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   **Anode:** Steel loses electrons (oxidizes).
    *   **Cathode:** Oxygen gains electrons.
    *   **Electrolyte:** Moisture and dissolved ions (e.g., chlorides) in concrete.
    *   **Process:** Rust (iron oxides and hydroxides) forms, which occupies a larger volume than the original steel.
*   **Conditions for Corrosion:**
    *   **Presence of Steel:** Embedded reinforcement.
    *   **Presence of Oxygen:** Available from the atmosphere.
    *   **Presence of Moisture:** Electrolyte.
    *   **Depassivation of Steel:** Removal of the protective passive layer.
*   **Factors Causing Depassivation:**
    *   **Carbonation:** Atmospheric CO2 reacts with calcium hydroxide in concrete, reducing the pH. The passive layer on steel is stable at pH > 9-10. Carbonation reduces pH to < 9, destroying the passive layer.
    *   **Chloride Ion Ingress:** Chloride ions penetrate concrete and reach the steel surface. Even at high pH, chlorides can break down the passive layer.
*   **Consequences of Corrosion:**
    *   **Loss of Reinforcement Area:** Reduced structural capacity.
    *   **Cracking and Spalling:** Rust expansion creates tensile stresses in concrete, leading to cracking and delamination (spalling) of the concrete cover.
    *   **Reduced Bond:** Corrosion can reduce the bond between steel and concrete.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Maintain sufficient cover to reinforcement.
    *   Use a low w/c ratio and dense concrete.
    *   Use corrosion-resistant reinforcement (e.g., stainless steel, galvanized steel).
    *   Use corrosion inhibitors.
    *   Protect concrete surfaces (coatings, sealers).
    *   Control the service environment (minimize exposure to chlorides).

### 4. Carbonation

*   **Definition:** The chemical reaction between carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere and the calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) present in the hardened cement paste.
*   **Chemical Reaction:**
    Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 (Calcium Carbonate) + H2O
*   **Process:** CO2 diffuses into the concrete through pores. The carbonation front moves inward from the exposed surface.
*   **Effect on Concrete:**
    *   **Reduces Alkalinity (pH):** The pH of sound concrete is around 12.5-13.5. Carbonation reduces this to around 8-9.
    *   **Forms Calcium Carbonate:** This is generally inert and can even fill some pores, slightly reducing permeability in the carbonated zone.
    *   **Primary Concern: Reinforcement Corrosion:** The reduction in pH destroys the protective passive layer on steel, making it susceptible to corrosion if oxygen and moisture are present.
*   **Factors Affecting Carbonation Rate:**
    *   **Permeability of Concrete:** More permeable concrete allows faster CO2 ingress.
    *   **CO2 Concentration:** Higher atmospheric CO2 leads to faster carbonation.
    *   **Moisture Content:** Carbonation requires moisture but excess water hinders CO2 diffusion. Optimal moisture content for carbonation is around 40-60% saturation.
    *   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures generally increase reaction rates.
    *   **Cover Depth:** Greater cover depth provides a longer path for CO2.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Low w/c ratio and good compaction.
    *   Adequate cover depth.
    *   Use of SCMs (which consume less Ca(OH)2 or react with CO2).
    *   Protective coatings.

### 5. Chloride Penetration

*   **Definition:** The movement of chloride ions (Cl-) into concrete from external sources.
*   **Sources of Chlorides:**
    *   **Seawater and Marine Environments:** Direct exposure to seawater or sea spray.
    *   **De-icing Salts:** Applied to roads and bridges in cold climates.
    *   **Admixtures:** Some set-accelerating admixtures (e.g., calcium chloride) if used in excess.
    *   **Contaminated Aggregates/Water:** Less common but possible.
*   **Mechanism:** Chlorides can penetrate concrete through diffusion and capillary absorption, especially through pores and cracks.
*   **Effect on Concrete:**
    *   **Destroys Passive Layer:** Chloride ions break down the protective passive film on steel reinforcement, even in high pH environments.
    *   **Initiates Corrosion:** Once the passive layer is breached, steel corrosion begins if oxygen and moisture are present.
    *   **Increased Corrosion Rate:** Chlorides can also accelerate the corrosion process once initiated.
*   **Factors Affecting Chloride Penetration:**
    *   **Permeability of Concrete:** Low permeability significantly reduces penetration.
    *   **Chloride Concentration and Exposure Duration:** Higher concentrations and longer exposure increase ingress.
    *   **Concrete Cover Depth:** A thicker cover provides a longer diffusion path.
    *   **Cracks and Discontinuities:** Provide direct pathways.
    *   **Moisture Content:** Essential for diffusion.
    *   **Presence of SCMs:** Can bind some chlorides, reducing their availability for corrosion.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Low w/c ratio.
    *   Use of SCMs (fly ash, slag, silica fume).
    *   Sufficient and dense concrete cover.
    *   Protective coatings and sealers.
    *   Use of corrosion-resistant reinforcement.
    *   Low-chloride admixtures.

### 6. Sulphate Attack

*   **Definition:** Deterioration of concrete caused by the chemical reaction between sulphate ions (SO4^2-) present in the environment (soil, groundwater, seawater) and the components of hydrated cement paste.
*   **Sources of Sulphates:**
    *   **Soil:** Soils containing soluble sulphates (gypsum).
    *   **Groundwater:** Groundwater contaminated with sulphates.
    *   **Seawater:** Contains sulphates.
    *   **Industrial Waste:** Effluents from certain industries.
*   **Mechanism:** Sulphates react with calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) and calcium aluminate hydrates (e.g., C3A hydration products) in the hardened cement paste.
    *   **Reaction with Ca(OH)2:** Forms gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O).
        Ca(OH)2 + SO4^2- + 2H2O → CaSO4·2H2O + 2OH-
    *   **Reaction with Calcium Aluminate Hydrates:** Forms ettringite (a calcium sulfoaluminate hydrate). This reaction is highly expansive.
        3CaO·Al2O3·xH2O + 3(CaSO4·2H2O) + Ca(OH)2 + ... → Ettringite
*   **Consequences of Sulphate Attack:**
    *   **Expansion and Cracking:** The formation of gypsum and particularly ettringite involves a significant increase in volume, leading to internal tensile stresses, cracking, and eventual disintegration of the concrete.
    *   **Loss of Strength and Mass:** The cement paste is degraded, leading to a reduction in concrete strength and an increase in permeability.
    *   **Softening of Surface:** The surface layers can become soft and chalky.
*   **Factors Affecting Sulphate Attack:**
    *   **Sulphate Concentration:** Higher concentration leads to more severe attack.
    *   **Sulphate Type:** Different sulphate types have varying reactivities.
    *   **Permeability of Concrete:** Permeable concrete allows faster ingress of sulphates.
    *   **Cement Type:** C3A content in cement is a critical factor. Cements with low C3A content (e.g., CEM I/SR, CEM III/B, CEM III/C) are more resistant.
    *   **Moisture Content:** Attack requires the presence of moisture.
    *   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures can accelerate the reactions.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Use sulphate-resisting cement (e.g., ASTM Type II, Type V, or European CEM classes with low C3A).
    *   Use blended cements with SCMs (fly ash, slag) which react with Ca(OH)2 and reduce available C3A, thus improving resistance.
    *   Low w/c ratio.
    *   Adequate curing.
    *   Protective coatings.

### 7. Acid Attack

*   **Definition:** Deterioration of concrete caused by contact with acidic substances.
*   **Sources of Acids:**
    *   **Industrial Wastewater:** From chemical plants, food processing, etc.
    *   **Acid Rain:** Atmospheric pollution.
    *   **Sewer Gases:** Hydrogen sulfide can oxidize to sulfuric acid.
    *   **Soil:** Some soils can have acidic properties.
*   **Mechanism:** Acids react with the alkaline components of the cement paste, primarily calcium hydroxide and calcium silicate hydrates (CSH gel), dissolving them.
    *   **Reaction with Ca(OH)2:** Neutralizes the alkalinity.
        Ca(OH)2 + 2H+ → Ca^2+ + 2H2O
    *   **Reaction with CSH Gel:** Attacks the binding material, causing loss of cohesion and strength.
*   **Consequences of Acid Attack:**
    *   **Loss of Cement Paste:** The binding material is leached away, leading to softening, erosion, and loss of surface integrity.
    *   **Loss of Strength and Mass:** The concrete becomes weaker and less dense.
    *   **Exposure of Aggregates:** As the paste dissolves, aggregates can become dislodged.
*   **Factors Affecting Acid Attack:**
    *   **Acid Concentration and Type:** Stronger acids and acids that form soluble products cause more rapid attack.
    *   **pH of the Environment:** Lower pH indicates more aggressive conditions.
    *   **Permeability of Concrete:** Higher permeability allows faster ingress of acids.
    *   **Cement Type:** While all Portland cements are susceptible to some degree, dense, low-permeability concrete offers better resistance.
    *   **Exposure Duration and Flow Rate:** Continuous exposure or flow of acidic water accelerates attack.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Use concrete with low permeability (low w/c ratio, SCMs).
    *   Use cement types with higher resistance (e.g., cements with pozzolanic or slag components).
    *   Apply protective coatings or linings (e.g., acid-resistant tiles, coatings).
    *   Control the pH of the surrounding environment where possible.

### 8. Fire Resistance

*   **Definition:** The ability of concrete to maintain its structural integrity and load-bearing capacity when exposed to fire for a specified period.
*   **Mechanism of Fire Damage:**
    *   **Dehydration:** Water within the cement paste evaporates.
    *   **Decomposition:** Calcium hydroxide decomposes at high temperatures (around 400-500°C), leading to loss of binding capacity.
    *   **Thermal Expansion:** Differential thermal expansion between aggregates, cement paste, and reinforcement can cause internal stresses and cracking.
    *   **Spalling:** Surface layers of concrete can break away due to rapid heating, thermal stresses, and pressure buildup from trapped moisture. Explosive spalling is a concern, particularly with dense concrete and certain aggregates.
*   **Consequences of Fire Exposure:**
    *   **Reduction in Strength and Stiffness:** Elevated temperatures degrade the cementitious matrix.
    *   **Cracking and Spalling:** Can expose reinforcement to further heat and damage.
    *   **Loss of Bond:** Bond between concrete and reinforcement can be reduced.
*   **Factors Affecting Fire Resistance:**
    *   **Concrete Properties:**
        *   **w/c Ratio:** Lower w/c ratio concrete tends to be more resistant, but dense concrete can be more prone to explosive spalling if not properly designed.
        *   **Aggregate Type:** Limestone and siliceous aggregates have different thermal expansion properties. Lightweight aggregates (e.g., expanded shale, clay) generally exhibit better fire resistance and less spalling.
        *   **Cement Type:** Some cements might perform slightly differently, but the aggregate type and presence of SCMs are more significant.
        *   **SCMs:** Can improve fire resistance by reducing the amount of free lime and producing a more stable microstructure.
    *   **Cover to Reinforcement:** A thicker cover provides better protection to the steel.
    *   **Structural Design:** The geometry of the element, the amount and placement of reinforcement.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Use appropriate lightweight aggregates.
    *   Ensure adequate cover to reinforcement.
    *   Consider the use of SCMs.
    *   Apply fire-resistant coatings or encasements.
    *   Proper structural design to account for fire loads and performance.

### 9. Frost Damage (Freeze-Thaw Resistance)

*   **Definition:** Deterioration of concrete caused by repeated cycles of freezing and thawing of absorbed water within the concrete.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   **Water Expansion:** When water freezes, it expands by about 9%.
    *   **Hydraulic Pressure:** As water freezes in pores, it pushes unfrozen water into adjacent pores, creating hydraulic pressure.
    *   **Osmotic Pressure:** If de-icing salts are present, they can increase the concentration of ions in the unfrozen water, leading to osmotic pressure build-up.
    *   **Cracking and Pitting:** These pressures cause internal stresses that can lead to cracking, scaling, and surface disintegration.
*   **Factors Affecting Frost Damage:**
    *   **Permeability of Concrete:** Highly permeable concrete allows more water to enter the pores, making it more vulnerable.
    *   **Saturation Level:** Concrete must be saturated for frost damage to occur. Dry concrete is unaffected.
    *   **w/c Ratio:** Low w/c ratio leads to denser concrete with less pore space and thus better frost resistance.
    *   **Air Entrainment:** The most effective measure. Entrained air provides microscopic voids (air bubbles) within the concrete. As water freezes, it can move into these air voids, relieving the pressure. The optimal size and spacing of these bubbles are crucial.
    *   **Aggregate Type:** Some aggregates can cause "pop-outs" if they are porous, freeze-expand, and cause localized surface damage.
    *   **Presence of De-icing Salts:** Salts significantly worsen frost damage by lowering the freezing point and increasing osmotic pressures.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   **Air Entrainment:** Use of air-entraining admixtures is paramount for frost resistance.
    *   Low w/c ratio.
    *   Proper curing.
    *   Use of SCMs (can improve resistance by reducing permeability).
    *   Avoid saturated conditions where possible.
    *   Use de-icing agents that are less aggressive if possible.

### 10. Alkali-Silica Reaction (ASR)

*   **Definition:** An expansive chemical reaction between the reactive silica (SiO2) found in some aggregates and the alkali hydroxides (primarily NaOH and KOH) present in the pore solution of concrete.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   **Reaction:** Reactive silica in aggregate + Alkali hydroxides + Water → Alkali-silica gel.
    *   **Gel Formation:** The gel absorbs moisture from the surrounding concrete and swells, exerting internal tensile pressure.
    *   **Cracking:** This swelling pressure can exceed the tensile strength of the concrete, leading to characteristic map-cracking or random cracking.
*   **Conditions for ASR:**
    *   **Presence of Reactive Aggregates:** Aggregates containing amorphous or poorly crystalline silica (e.g., chert, opal, strained quartz).
    *   **Presence of Alkalis:** Sufficient concentration of alkali hydroxides from the cement.
    *   **Presence of Moisture:** Required for the reaction and gel swelling.
*   **Consequences of ASR:**
    *   **Cracking:** Characteristic map-cracking, often starting from the aggregate.
    *   **Expansion:** Overall expansion of the concrete element.
    *   **Loss of Strength and Stiffness:** Cracking reduces the load-bearing capacity and increases deflection.
    *   **Reinforcement Corrosion:** Cracks can allow ingress of aggressive agents, leading to corrosion.
*   **Factors Affecting ASR:**
    *   **Aggregate Reactivity:** The specific mineralogy and form of silica in the aggregate.
    *   **Alkali Content of Cement:** Higher alkali content increases the risk.
    *   **Moisture Content:** Wet conditions promote ASR.
    *   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures accelerate the reaction.
    *   **Pore Solution Chemistry:** The concentration of hydroxyl ions.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   **Aggregate Selection:** Avoid reactive aggregates, or use them with caution. Conduct petrographic examination and laboratory tests (e.g., ASTM C1260, ASTM C1293) to assess reactivity.
    *   **Low-Alkali Cement:** Use cement with a low alkali content (e.g., < 0.60% equivalent Na2O).
    *   **Use of SCMs:** Supplementary Cementitious Materials like fly ash, slag, and silica fume are highly effective in mitigating ASR. They react with alkalis, reduce the availability of alkalis, and produce a denser microstructure that hinders gel movement.
    *   **Lithium-based Admixtures:** Lithium compounds can help suppress the expansive reaction.
    *   **Control of Moisture:** Keeping concrete dry reduces the risk.

## Summary of Key Concepts and Points to Remember

*   **Durability is Paramount:** A fundamental aspect of concrete performance.
*   **Low w/c Ratio is King:** The most important factor for achieving good durability.
*   **SCMs are Durability Enhancers:** Fly ash, slag, silica fume improve resistance to chemical attack, reduce permeability, and mitigate ASR.
*   **Concrete is Not Impermeable:** It has a pore structure that allows ingress of fluids and gases.
*   **Cracks are Pathways:** They accelerate deterioration by allowing aggressive substances to penetrate.
*   **Reinforcement Corrosion is Driven by Depassivation:** Carbonation and chlorides are the main culprits.
*   **Air Entrainment is Crucial for Frost Resistance:** Provides relief for freezing water pressure.
*   **Sulphate Attack and Acid Attack Degrade the Cement Paste:** Resulting in expansion, cracking, and loss of strength.
*   **ASR is Caused by Reactive Aggregates:** Managed through aggregate selection, low-alkali cement, and SCMs.
*   **Balanced Design:** Durability requires a holistic approach considering materials, mix design, construction practices, and service environment.

## Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
What is the single most important factor in achieving durable concrete? Explain why.

**Answer:**
The single most important factor is the **water-cement ratio (w/c)**. A lower w/c ratio leads to a denser concrete with a less interconnected pore structure. This reduces permeability, making it harder for aggressive substances like chlorides, sulphates, CO2, and water to penetrate the concrete. Lower permeability also leads to higher strength and improved resistance to freeze-thaw damage.

**Question 2:**
Describe the mechanism of reinforcement corrosion in concrete and list two primary factors that initiate this process.

**Answer:**
Reinforcement corrosion is an electrochemical process involving an anode (where steel oxidizes), a cathode (where oxygen is reduced), and an electrolyte (moist concrete pore solution). The protective passive layer on steel, which is stable at high pH, must be broken down for corrosion to begin.
The two primary factors that initiate this process are:
1.  **Carbonation:** Atmospheric CO2 reduces the pH of the concrete, destroying the passive layer.
2.  **Chloride Ion Ingress:** Chloride ions penetrate the concrete and locally break down the passive layer, even at high pH.

**Question 3:**
Explain how air-entraining admixtures improve the frost resistance of concrete.

**Answer:**
Air-entraining admixtures introduce microscopic, uniformly distributed air bubbles (entrained air) into the concrete. When water within the concrete pores freezes and expands, it can move into these air voids. This relieves the hydraulic pressure that would otherwise build up, preventing internal tensile stresses and subsequent cracking or scaling of the concrete surface.

**Question 4:**
Name three types of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) and briefly explain how they contribute to concrete durability.

**Answer:**
1.  **Fly Ash:** Improves resistance to sulphate attack and alkali-silica reaction (ASR), reduces permeability, and lowers the heat of hydration.
2.  **Ground Granulated Blast-Furnace Slag (GGBFS):** Improves resistance to sulphate attack and chloride ingress, reduces permeability, and mitigates ASR.
3.  **Silica Fume:** Significantly reduces permeability, increases resistance to chloride penetration and chemical attack, and is very effective in mitigating ASR.

**Question 5:**
What are the main sources of sulphates that can attack concrete? What are the consequences of sulphate attack?

**Answer:**
Main sources of sulphates:
*   Sulphate-rich soils
*   Groundwater contaminated with sulphates
*   Seawater
*   Industrial waste or effluents

Consequences of sulphate attack:
*   Expansion and cracking of concrete due to the formation of expansive products like ettringite.
*   Loss of strength and mass of the concrete.
*   Disintegration and softening of the cement paste.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Durability is Proactive:** It needs to be designed and built into the concrete from the start, not fixed later.
*   **Synergistic Effects:** Multiple durability issues can occur simultaneously and exacerbate each other.
*   **Service Life:** Always consider the intended service life and the expected environmental exposure conditions.
*   **Testing:** Various tests exist to evaluate the durability performance of concrete and its constituent materials.
*   **Codes and Standards:** Adherence to relevant building codes and material standards is crucial for ensuring durability.
