# Irrigation and Drainage Engineering: Module 1 - Surface Irrigation Methods

## Topic: Delivery of Water to Farms

This module focuses on surface irrigation methods, with a particular emphasis on border irrigation. We will explore how water is delivered to farms within this system, covering its classification, design parameters, evaluation techniques, and the concept of an ideal wetting pattern.

---

By the end of this topic, you will be able to:

*   Understand the general principles and classification of surface irrigation methods.
*   Describe the components and operation of border irrigation systems.
*   Identify and explain key design parameters for border irrigation.
*   Understand methods for evaluating the performance of border irrigation.
*   Describe the concept of an ideal wetting pattern in border irrigation and its significance.

### 1. Introduction to Surface Irrigation

Surface irrigation is the oldest and most widely used irrigation method globally. It involves applying water to the soil surface by gravity, allowing it to infiltrate and spread over the land.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Gravity Flow:** Water moves from a higher elevation to a lower elevation due to the force of gravity.
*   **Infiltration:** The process by which water enters the soil surface.
*   **Distribution Uniformity:** The degree to which water is applied evenly across the irrigated area.

**Classification of Surface Irrigation Methods:**

Surface irrigation methods can be broadly classified based on the way water is conveyed and applied to the field:

*   **Basin Irrigation:** The field is enclosed by levees (dikes), and water is applied to fill the basin.
    *   **Level Basin:** No slope. Water fills the basin.
    *   **Graded Basin:** Slight slope. Water flows across the basin.
*   **Border Irrigation:** The field is divided into long, narrow strips (borders) separated by levees. Water is applied to the upper end of each border and flows down the slope.
*   **Furrow Irrigation:** The field is laid out in parallel furrows, and water is applied to each furrow individually.

### 2. Border Irrigation: Delivery of Water

Border irrigation is a method where the land is divided into long, narrow rectangular strips called **borders**, separated by **levees** or **ridges**. Water is introduced at the high end of each border and flows down the length of the border, wetting the soil.

**Components of a Border Irrigation System:**

*   **Water Source:** River, canal, well, reservoir, etc.
*   **Conveyance Channels:** Canals or ditches that transport water from the source to the farm.
*   **Distribution System:**
    *   **Main Canal/Ditch:** Larger channel carrying water to the head of several borders.
    *   **Lateral Ditches/Channels:** Smaller channels that branch off the main channel and supply water to individual borders.
    *   **Turnouts/Gates:** Structures that regulate the flow of water from the main or lateral channels into the borders.
*   **Borders:** The irrigated strips of land.
*   **Levees/Ridges:** Earth embankments separating adjacent borders.
*   **Drainage System (Optional but Recommended):** To carry away excess water and prevent waterlogging.

**How Water is Delivered to Borders:**

1.  **Head Ditch/Main Channel:** Water is brought from the source to the head of the field via a main conveyance channel.
2.  **Lateral Ditches:** The main channel feeds into lateral ditches that run parallel to the borders, typically at the higher end of the field.
3.  **Turnouts/Sluice Gates:** At the entrance of each border, a turnout or sluice gate is installed. These are crucial for controlling the flow of water into each specific border.
4.  **Flow into Border:** When a turnout is opened, water from the lateral ditch flows into the upper end of the border.
5.  **Advance and Recession:** The water then flows down the length of the border due to the slope. The process of water spreading down the border is called **advance**. As the irrigation event progresses and the inflow stops, the water front recedes from the lower end.

**Ideal Flow into a Border:**

*   **Uniform Inflow:** Ideally, water should be supplied to the entire width of the border at the head end to ensure even distribution.
*   **Controlled Discharge:** The rate at which water enters the border should be controllable to match the border's capacity to absorb and convey water.

### 3. Border Irrigation: Design Parameters

Designing a border irrigation system involves selecting appropriate dimensions and operational characteristics to achieve efficient and uniform water application.

**Key Design Parameters:**

*   **Border Width:**
    *   **Definition:** The distance between the centers of adjacent levees, or the width of the irrigated strip.
    *   **Considerations:** Influenced by soil type, available land, topography, and equipment used for cultivation. Wider borders are generally more efficient in terms of labor but can be harder to irrigate uniformly.
    *   **Typical Range:** 10-20 meters, but can vary.
*   **Border Length:**
    *   **Definition:** The distance from the head of the border to the toe (tail) of the border.
    *   **Considerations:** Influenced by topography, soil infiltration rate, water application rate, and desired irrigation uniformity. Longer borders require higher discharges and longer advance times.
    *   **Typical Range:** 50-200 meters, but can be longer.
*   **Border Slope (Longitudinal Slope):**
    *   **Definition:** The gradual drop in elevation along the length of the border.
    *   **Considerations:** Crucial for water flow and uniform distribution. Too flat a slope leads to slow advance and poor wetting at the head. Too steep a slope can cause excessive runoff and erosion.
    *   **Ideal Range:** Typically 0.0005 to 0.005 (0.05% to 0.5%).
*   **Border Spacing/Levee Height and Base:**
    *   **Definition:** The distance between the centers of adjacent borders, and the dimensions of the earthen ridges that separate them.
    *   **Considerations:** Levees must be high enough to contain the design water depth and strong enough to withstand water pressure. Spacing is related to border width and land use.
*   **Water Application Rate (Discharge per Unit Width):**
    *   **Definition:** The flow rate of water entering the border divided by the border width (e.g., liters per second per meter width).
    *   **Considerations:** This is a critical parameter that dictates the speed of water advance and the time required for irrigation. It should be matched to the soil infiltration characteristics and the border dimensions.
    *   **Typical Range:** 1-5 liters per second per meter (lps/m).
*   **Required Runoff Ratio:**
    *   **Definition:** The percentage of water applied that does not infiltrate into the soil and is typically allowed to drain off the end of the border.
    *   **Considerations:** A small amount of runoff is often desirable to ensure water reaches the entire length of the border without excessive application at the head.
    *   **Typical Range:** 10-20%.
*   **Irrigation Time (Duration):**
    *   **Definition:** The total time water is applied to the border.
    *   **Considerations:** Determined by the soil water holding capacity, the desired depth of irrigation, and the infiltration rate.

**Factors Influencing Design Choices:**

*   **Soil Type:** Infiltration rates vary significantly with soil texture. Sandy soils have high infiltration rates, requiring higher application rates and shorter borders. Clayey soils have low infiltration rates, requiring lower application rates and longer borders.
*   **Topography:** The natural slope of the land influences the ease of establishing borders and the required channel gradients.
*   **Water Source Availability and Capacity:** The volume and reliability of water supply affect the size of borders that can be irrigated simultaneously.
*   **Crop Requirements:** Different crops have different water needs and tolerances to waterlogging.
*   **Management Capabilities:** The skill and availability of labor for operating gates and monitoring the irrigation process.

### 4. Border Irrigation: Evaluation

Evaluating the performance of a border irrigation system is essential to assess its efficiency and identify areas for improvement.

**Key Evaluation Metrics:**

*   **Water Distribution Uniformity (DU):**
    *   **Definition:** A measure of how evenly water is applied across the irrigated area.
    *   **Calculation:** Often calculated based on water depth measurements at various points in the field.
    *   **Importance:** High DU ensures that all parts of the field receive adequate water without over-irrigating some areas.
*   **Application Efficiency (AE):**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of the water beneficially used by the crop (stored in the root zone) to the total water applied to the field.
    *   **Formula:** $AE = (Volume of water stored in root zone / Volume of water applied) \times 100\%$
    *   **Importance:** Indicates how much of the applied water actually contributes to crop needs.
*   **Conjunctive Use of Parameters (Advance and Recession Rates):**
    *   **Advance Rate:** The speed at which the water front moves down the border.
    *   **Recession Rate:** The speed at which the water front recedes from the tail end after inflow stops.
    *   **Importance:** These rates are critical for determining the correct irrigation time and ensuring water reaches the tail end without excessive runoff or deep percolation at the head.

**Methods for Evaluation:**

*   **Advance Rate Measurement:**
    *   Observe and record the time taken for the water front to reach various marked points along the border.
*   **Recession Rate Measurement:**
    *   After stopping the inflow, observe and record the time taken for the water front to recede from various marked points.
*   **Soil Moisture Sampling:**
    *   Measure soil moisture content at different depths and locations in the field before and after irrigation. This helps determine the volume of water stored in the root zone.
*   **Water Depth Measurement:**
    *   Using flow meters at the inflow and measuring runoff at the outlet can help determine the volume of water applied and lost.
    *   Probes or depth gauges can be used to measure water depth at specific locations.
*   **Performance Indices (e.g., CU, REI):**
    *   **Christiansen's Uniformity (CU):** $CU = (1 - (\Sigma |x_i - \bar{x}|) / (\Sigma x_i)) \times 100\%$, where $x_i$ is the depth of water applied at location $i$ and $\bar{x}$ is the average depth.
    *   **Relative Efficiency of Irrigation (REI):** This index considers both uniformity and application efficiency.

**Common Problems Identified During Evaluation:**

*   **Uneven wetting:** Water not reaching the tail end, or excessive ponding at the head.
*   **Excessive runoff:** Too much water applied or too high an application rate.
*   **Deep percolation losses:** Water moving beyond the root zone due to over-irrigation or high infiltration rates.
*   **Waterlogging:** Poor drainage leading to saturation of the root zone.

### 5. Border Irrigation: Ideal Wetting Pattern

The **ideal wetting pattern** in border irrigation refers to the optimal distribution of water in the soil profile after an irrigation event.

**Characteristics of an Ideal Wetting Pattern:**

*   **Uniform Wetting Across the Width:** The soil should be uniformly moist across the entire width of the border, from one levee to the other.
*   **Uniform Wetting Along the Length:** The soil should be uniformly moist along the entire length of the border, from the head to the toe. There should be no excessively dry or saturated zones.
*   **Target Depth:** The wetting front should reach the desired depth within the root zone of the crop.
*   **No Runoff:** Ideally, all applied water should infiltrate into the soil, although a small, controlled runoff is often accepted.
*   **No Deep Percolation:** Water should not infiltrate beyond the plant's root zone, as this represents a loss.

**Visualizing the Ideal Wetting Pattern:**

Imagine a cross-section of the border after irrigation. The ideal pattern would look like a uniform block of moist soil within the root zone, extending from levee to levee and from the surface down to the target depth.

**Achieving the Ideal Wetting Pattern:**

Achieving a perfectly ideal wetting pattern is challenging in practice due to variations in soil properties, topography, and operational errors. However, careful design and management can approach it:

*   **Proper Border Design:** Correct border width, length, and slope are crucial.
*   **Appropriate Application Rate:** Matching the inflow rate to the infiltration capacity of the soil.
*   **Correct Irrigation Time:** Applying water for the duration needed to achieve the desired wetting depth without over-irrigation.
*   **Minimizing Levee Seepage:** Ensuring levees are well-compacted to prevent water loss.

**Consequences of Deviating from the Ideal:**

*   **Under-irrigation:** Dry spots occur, limiting crop growth and yield.
*   **Over-irrigation:** Waterlogging, increased incidence of root diseases, nutrient leaching, and wasted water.
*   **Non-uniform wetting:** Some parts of the field receive too much water (leading to deep percolation and runoff), while others receive too little. This results in reduced crop yield and efficiency.

**Question 1:**
What is the primary force that drives water flow in surface irrigation methods like border irrigation?
**(a) Capillary action**
**(b) Evaporation**
**(c) Gravity**
**(d) Transpiration**

**Question 2:**
List three key design parameters for border irrigation and briefly explain their significance.

**Question 3:**
Describe the difference between the "advance" and "recession" phases of water flow in a border irrigation system.

**Question 4:**
Why is water distribution uniformity (DU) an important evaluation metric for border irrigation?

**Question 5:**
A farmer is experiencing issues with water not reaching the tail end of their border strips. What could be the potential causes, and what adjustments might they consider?

---

### Answers to Practice Questions:

**Answer 1:**
The primary force is **(c) Gravity**.

**Answer 2:**
Three key design parameters and their significance:
1.  **Border Slope:** Ensures water flows down the length of the border. An appropriate slope prevents water from ponding or flowing too rapidly, contributing to uniform wetting.
2.  **Border Length:** Affects the time required for water to advance and the potential for deep percolation or runoff. Shorter borders are generally easier to irrigate uniformly but may require more levees.
3.  **Water Application Rate (Discharge per Unit Width):** Determines how quickly the border fills and the water front advances. It must be matched to the soil's infiltration rate to achieve uniform wetting and avoid excessive runoff or deep percolation.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Advance:** The period during which water is being applied to the border and the water front is moving from the head (inlet) towards the toe (outlet) of the border.
*   **Recession:** The period after the inflow to the border has been stopped. During this phase, the water front recedes from the tail end back towards the head end as water infiltrates into the soil and evaporates.

**Answer 4:**
Water distribution uniformity (DU) is important because it indicates how evenly water is applied across the entire irrigated area of the border. High DU means most of the border receives roughly the same amount of water. Poor DU leads to some areas being over-irrigated (causing waterlogging, nutrient leaching) and others being under-irrigated (leading to water stress and reduced crop yield).

**Answer 5:**
Potential causes for water not reaching the tail end of border strips:
*   **Insufficient irrigation time:** The water was not applied for long enough.
*   **Too low an application rate:** The inflow is too slow for the border length and soil type.
*   **Excessive infiltration rate:** The soil absorbs water too quickly, so it infiltrates before reaching the tail end.
*   **Long border length:** The border might be too long for the given topography and inflow rate.
*   **Insufficient border slope:** The slope is too flat, slowing down water movement.
*   **Obstructions:** Debris or unevenness along the border hindering flow.

Adjustments the farmer might consider:
*   **Increase irrigation time:** Apply water for a longer duration.
*   **Increase application rate:** Open the turnout to allow a higher flow of water into the border (if the water source allows and the border can handle it).
*   **Shorten the border length:** If feasible, divide the long border into shorter ones.
*   **Improve border slope:** Re-level or adjust the land to ensure a proper gradient.
*   **Check and clear obstructions:** Ensure the border surface is smooth and clear of debris.
*   **Improve levees:** Ensure levees are well-compacted to reduce infiltration losses along the sides.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Gravity is the driving force** in surface irrigation.
*   Border irrigation divides land into **long, narrow strips**.
*   **Design parameters are inter-related** and must be matched to soil, crop, and water availability.
*   **Uniformity and efficiency** are key goals of good border irrigation design and management.
*   **Advance and recession rates** are critical for determining irrigation duration.
*   An **ideal wetting pattern** ensures uniform moisture in the root zone without waste.
*   **Evaluation** is crucial for identifying and rectifying performance issues.
