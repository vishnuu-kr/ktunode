### **1. Introduction to Irrigation Frequency and Interval**

Irrigation scheduling is a critical aspect of irrigation management, aiming to supply water to crops at the right time and in the right amount. Two key terms associated with irrigation scheduling are:

*   **Irrigation Frequency (or Frequency of Irrigation):** This refers to the **number of times irrigation is applied to a crop over a specific period** (e.g., per week, per month, per growing season). It answers the question: "How often do I need to irrigate?"

*   **Irrigation Interval (or Irrigation Interval):** This refers to the **time elapsed between two consecutive irrigations**. It answers the question: "How many days are there between irrigations?"

**Relationship:** Irrigation frequency and interval are inversely related. A higher frequency implies a shorter interval, and vice versa.

**Importance:**
*   **Optimizes Crop Water Use:** Applying water at the correct frequency and interval ensures that the crop has access to sufficient moisture when it needs it, without over-saturating the soil.
*   **Maximizes Crop Yield and Quality:** Consistent availability of water reduces crop stress, leading to better growth, higher yields, and improved quality.
*   **Efficient Water Resource Management:** Proper scheduling prevents water wastage through deep percolation or excessive surface runoff, conserving precious water resources.
*   **Prevents Waterlogging and Drought Stress:** Too frequent irrigation can lead to waterlogging, while infrequent irrigation can cause drought stress.

### **2. Factors Influencing Irrigation Frequency and Interval**

The determination of irrigation frequency and interval is not a one-size-fits-all approach. It depends on a complex interplay of various factors:

#### **2.1. Crop Characteristics:**

*   **Crop Type:** Different crops have varying water requirements and tolerance to drought.
    *   **High Water Requirement Crops (e.g., Rice, Sugarcane):** Require more frequent irrigation.
    *   **Low Water Requirement Crops (e.g., Sorghum, Millet):** Can tolerate longer intervals.
*   **Growth Stage:** A crop's water needs change throughout its life cycle.
    *   **Critical Growth Stages (e.g., Flowering, Fruiting):** Require more consistent moisture and therefore shorter intervals.
    *   **Early Vegetative Stage:** May have lower water needs and tolerate longer intervals.
*   **Rooting Depth:** Deeper-rooted crops can extract moisture from a larger soil volume, potentially allowing for longer intervals. Shallow-rooted crops need more frequent watering.

#### **2.2. Soil Characteristics:**

*   **Soil Texture:**
    *   **Sandy Soils:** Have low water-holding capacity and drain rapidly. They require more frequent, lighter irrigations (shorter intervals).
    *   **Clay Soils:** Have high water-holding capacity but drain slowly. They can sustain crops for longer periods between irrigations (longer intervals), but care must be taken to avoid waterlogging.
    *   **Loam Soils:** Have intermediate properties.
*   **Soil Structure:** Good soil structure promotes aeration and root penetration, influencing water availability.
*   **Water Holding Capacity (WHC):** Soils with higher WHC can store more water, allowing for longer intervals.
*   **Infiltration Rate:** Affects how quickly water enters the soil. If infiltration is low, longer durations of application might be needed, indirectly influencing the interval.
*   **Drainage Characteristics:** Soils with poor drainage are prone to waterlogging, necessitating careful scheduling and potentially longer intervals.

#### **2.3. Climatic Conditions:**

*   **Evapotranspiration (ET) Rate:** This is the combined loss of water from the soil surface (evaporation) and plant transpiration. Higher ET rates deplete soil moisture faster, requiring more frequent irrigation.
    *   **Factors affecting ET:** Temperature, humidity, solar radiation, wind speed.
    *   **Hot, Dry, Windy Conditions:** Lead to high ET and shorter irrigation intervals.
    *   **Cool, Humid, Calm Conditions:** Lead to low ET and longer irrigation intervals.
*   **Rainfall:**
    *   **Amount and Distribution:** Rainfall can supplement irrigation, potentially extending the interval between irrigations.
    *   **Timing:** Rainfall occurring shortly before a scheduled irrigation might make the irrigation unnecessary.

#### **2.4. Irrigation System Characteristics:**

*   **Application Rate:** The rate at which water is applied by the irrigation system. For border irrigation, this is the flow rate per unit width of the border.
*   **Application Efficiency:** The efficiency of the system in delivering water to the root zone. Lower efficiency might necessitate more frequent applications to compensate for losses.

### **3. Relationship between Soil Moisture, Irrigation Interval, and Frequency**

The core principle behind irrigation scheduling is to maintain soil moisture within a range that is optimal for plant growth.

*   **Field Capacity (FC):** The maximum amount of water a soil can hold against gravity after excess water has drained away.
*   **Permanent Wilting Point (PWP):** The soil moisture level at which plants can no longer extract water and wilt permanently.
*   **Available Soil Moisture (ASM):** The difference between Field Capacity and Permanent Wilting Point (ASM = FC - PWP). This is the water available to plants.
*   **Readily Available Soil Moisture (RASM) or Management Allowed Depletion (MAD):** The portion of ASM that can be depleted before the crop suffers from water stress. This is typically expressed as a percentage of ASM.
    *   **Example:** If ASM is 150 mm and MAD is 50%, then RASM = 0.50 * 150 mm = 75 mm.

**The Cycle:**

1.  **Irrigation:** Water is applied, replenishing the soil moisture to Field Capacity.
2.  **Depletion:** As plants transpire and water evaporates from the soil, soil moisture content decreases.
3.  **Threshold Reached:** When the soil moisture depletion reaches the Management Allowed Depletion (MAD), it's time to irrigate again.
4.  **Re-irrigation:** Another irrigation is applied to bring the soil moisture back to Field Capacity.

**How this influences Frequency and Interval:**

*   **High ET / Low WHC Soil:** Soil moisture depletes rapidly. The threshold for MAD is reached sooner. This requires more frequent irrigations (high frequency) and shorter intervals between them.
*   **Low ET / High WHC Soil:** Soil moisture depletes slowly. The threshold for MAD is reached later. This allows for less frequent irrigations (low frequency) and longer intervals.

### **4. Estimating Irrigation Frequency and Interval**

A common method for estimating irrigation interval is based on the crop's consumptive use (evapotranspiration) and the soil's moisture holding capacity.

**Basic Formula:**

$$ \text{Irrigation Interval (days)} = \frac{\text{Depth of readily available soil moisture (mm)}}{\text{Daily consumptive use of crop (mm/day)}} $$

Where:

*   **Depth of readily available soil moisture (mm):** This is calculated as:
    $$ \text{Readily Available Soil Moisture} = \text{Available Soil Moisture} \times \text{Management Allowed Depletion (MAD)} $$
    And,
    $$ \text{Available Soil Moisture} = (\text{Field Capacity} - \text{Permanent Wilting Point}) \times \text{Root Zone Depth} $$
    Note: FC and PWP are often expressed as soil moisture content (e.g., % by weight or volume). These need to be converted to depth (mm) by multiplying with the root zone depth.

*   **Daily Consumptive Use of Crop (mm/day):** This is the average daily evapotranspiration (ETc) of the crop. ETc can be estimated using meteorological data and crop coefficients.

**Example Calculation:**

Let's consider a crop with the following characteristics:

*   Root zone depth = 600 mm
*   Field Capacity (FC) = 20% by volume
*   Permanent Wilting Point (PWP) = 8% by volume
*   Management Allowed Depletion (MAD) = 50%
*   Average daily crop evapotranspiration (ETc) = 6 mm/day

**Step 1: Calculate Available Soil Moisture (ASM) in the root zone.**
*   Convert FC and PWP to depth in mm:
    *   Depth at FC = 0.20 (volume fraction) * 600 mm (root zone depth) = 120 mm
    *   Depth at PWP = 0.08 (volume fraction) * 600 mm (root zone depth) = 48 mm
*   ASM = Depth at FC - Depth at PWP = 120 mm - 48 mm = 72 mm

**Step 2: Calculate the Depth of Readily Available Soil Moisture (RASM).**
*   RASM = ASM * MAD = 72 mm * 0.50 = 36 mm

**Step 3: Calculate the Irrigation Interval.**
*   Irrigation Interval = RASM / Daily ETc = 36 mm / 6 mm/day = 6 days

**Therefore, based on these assumptions, the irrigation interval would be approximately 6 days.**

**Important Considerations for this Calculation:**

*   **Average Values:** This calculation uses average daily ETc. Actual ETc can vary daily.
*   **Soil Moisture Depletion Pattern:** This assumes uniform depletion.
*   **Rainfall:** This calculation does not account for rainfall. If rainfall occurs, the interval might be extended.
*   **Irrigation Efficiency:** The amount of water to be applied in an irrigation event is also influenced by irrigation efficiency.

### **5. Implications of Inappropriate Irrigation Frequency and Interval**

#### **5.1. Too Frequent Irrigation (Short Intervals):**

*   **Waterlogging:** Soil pores become saturated with water, reducing oxygen availability to plant roots. This can lead to root rot, inhibited growth, and nutrient uptake problems.
*   **Increased Disease Incidence:** Fungal diseases thrive in wet conditions.
*   **Nutrient Leaching:** Excess water can wash away essential nutrients from the root zone.
*   **Reduced Root Development:** Roots may not penetrate deeply if they constantly have access to water near the surface.
*   **Wastage of Water:** Unnecessary irrigation wastes water resources.
*   **Increased Energy Costs:** For pumped irrigation systems.

#### **5.2. Infrequent Irrigation (Long Intervals):**

*   **Drought Stress:** Plants experience water deficit, leading to wilting, reduced photosynthesis, stunted growth, and yield reduction.
*   **Reduced Yield and Quality:** Even temporary stress during critical growth stages can have long-lasting negative impacts on yield and quality.
*   **Lower Water Use Efficiency:** Plants may not be able to utilize the available soil moisture effectively if stressed.
*   **Salinity Buildup:** In arid and semi-arid regions, infrequent irrigation can lead to the accumulation of salts in the root zone as water evaporates.

### **6. Border Irrigation Context**

While the general principles of frequency and interval apply to all irrigation methods, in border irrigation:

*   **Uniformity is Key:** The design of the border (width, length, slope, and flow rate) aims for uniform water advance and recession across the border. This uniformity is crucial for the soil moisture depletion to be relatively consistent across the field, making the estimation of interval more reliable.
*   **Application Efficiency:** Border irrigation can have varying efficiencies depending on design and management. Low efficiency might require more frequent irrigations to compensate for water lost to deep percolation at the head of the border or runoff at the tail.
*   **Water Advance Rate:** Understanding the time it takes for water to advance across the entire border is crucial for determining the run time of an irrigation event. This, combined with the infiltration rate, determines the total water applied.

---

### **Key Points to Remember:**

*   **Irrigation Frequency:** How often irrigation is applied.
*   **Irrigation Interval:** The time between irrigations.
*   **Key Influencing Factors:** Crop type, growth stage, soil texture, climate (especially ET), and rainfall.
*   **Soil Moisture Depletion:** The primary driver for irrigation scheduling.
*   **Management Allowed Depletion (MAD):** The key parameter that defines when to irrigate.
*   **Estimating Interval:** Requires knowing RASM and daily ETc.
*   **Balance is Crucial:** Avoid both waterlogging (too frequent) and drought stress (too infrequent).
*   **Border Irrigation:** Uniformity and application efficiency are critical considerations.

1.  **Define Irrigation Frequency and Irrigation Interval.** Explain their relationship.
    *   **Answer:** Irrigation frequency is the number of irrigations over a period, while the interval is the time between irrigations. They are inversely related; a higher frequency means a shorter interval.
2.  **List at least five factors that influence the determination of irrigation frequency and interval.**
    *   **Answer:** Crop type, crop growth stage, soil texture, evapotranspiration rate, rainfall, rooting depth.
3.  **A farmer has a sandy loam soil with a field capacity of 25% and a permanent wilting point of 10% by volume. The effective root zone depth for the crop is 750 mm. The management allowed depletion is 60%. If the average daily consumptive use of the crop is 5 mm/day, calculate the approximate irrigation interval.**
    *   **Solution:**
        *   Depth at FC = 0.25 * 750 mm = 187.5 mm
        *   Depth at PWP = 0.10 * 750 mm = 75 mm
        *   Available Soil Moisture (ASM) = 187.5 mm - 75 mm = 112.5 mm
        *   Readily Available Soil Moisture (RASM) = 112.5 mm * 0.60 = 67.5 mm
        *   Irrigation Interval = RASM / Daily ETc = 67.5 mm / 5 mm/day = 13.5 days
        *   **Answer:** Approximately 13.5 days.
4.  **What are the consequences of irrigating too frequently? Give two examples.**
    *   **Answer:** Waterlogging (reduced oxygen to roots, root rot) and nutrient leaching.
5.  **Explain how soil texture affects the irrigation interval.**
    *   **Answer:** Sandy soils have low water-holding capacity and drain quickly, requiring more frequent irrigation (shorter intervals). Clay soils have high water-holding capacity, allowing for longer intervals between irrigations.
