## 1. Field Drains and Ditch Drains

### 1.1. Field Drains

Field drains are the primary components of a drainage system that intercept and collect excess water from the soil profile, bringing it to a main drain or outlet. They are typically laid in a specific pattern across the field to ensure effective water removal.

#### Types of Field Drains:

*   **Open Ditches (Surface Drains):** These are excavated channels on the surface of the land that carry away excess surface water. They are the simplest and oldest form of drainage.
    *   **Advantages:**
        *   Low construction cost.
        *   Easy to construct and maintain with simple equipment.
        *   Can also serve as irrigation channels in some cases.
        *   Effective for rapid removal of surface runoff.
    *   **Disadvantages:**
        *   Require significant land area, reducing cultivable land.
        *   Can obstruct farm operations (plowing, harvesting).
        *   Prone to siltation and weed growth, requiring regular maintenance.
        *   Can be a source of mosquito breeding.
        *   Water losses due to evaporation and seepage.
    *   **Design Considerations of Ditch Drains:**
        *   **Velocity:** Sufficient velocity to prevent silt deposition but not so high as to cause erosion. Typically, velocities range from 0.3 to 1.0 m/s.
        *   **Side Slopes:** Stable side slopes are crucial to prevent bank erosion. The slope depends on the soil type (e.g., 1:1 for cohesive soils, flatter for sandy soils).
        *   **Lining:** May be lined with concrete, riprap, or vegetation to prevent erosion and reduce seepage, especially in areas with high velocities or erodible soils.
        *   **Depth and Width:** Determined by the drainage coefficient (required discharge) and the hydraulic properties of the channel.
        *   **Bed Slope:** Usually follows the natural topography, but may need to be adjusted to achieve adequate velocity.
        *   **Freeboard:** The vertical distance between the design water level and the top of the ditch bank to prevent overflow during peak flows.
        *   **Spacing:** Determined by the hydraulic conductivity of the soil and the desired water table depth.

*   **Closed Drains (Sub-surface Drains):** These are buried conduits that collect and transport excess soil water. They are more efficient in lowering the water table and are preferred in modern agricultural practices.
    *   **Types of Closed Drains:**
        *   **Pipe Drains:** Perforated pipes (clay, concrete, PVC, corrugated plastic) laid underground.
        *   **Mole Drains:** Unlined channels formed by pulling a mole plow through the soil at a specific depth.
        *   **Tile Drains:** Similar to pipe drains, historically made of unglazed clay tiles.
        *   **Ditch Drains (as a type of field drain):** While often thought of as surface features, field ditches can also be considered a type of field drain in the broader sense if they are specifically designed to intercept subsurface flow. However, in the context of distinguishing from closed drains, "ditch drains" usually refers to surface channels.

### 1.2. Design Considerations of Ditch Drains (Recap)

*   **Capacity:** Determined by the drainage requirements (e.g., drainage coefficient).
*   **Hydraulics:** Manning's equation is commonly used to determine flow velocity and capacity: $V = \frac{1}{n} R^{2/3} S^{1/2}$, where $V$ is velocity, $n$ is Manning's roughness coefficient, $R$ is hydraulic radius, and $S$ is the bed slope.
*   **Erosion Control:** Side slopes, velocity limits, and lining.
*   **Maintenance:** Access for cleaning and repair.

## 2. Mole Drains

Mole drains are a form of sub-surface drainage where an unlined channel is formed in the soil by a blunt torpedo-shaped implement called a mole plow. The mole creates a stable, circular channel below the surface.

### 2.1. Design Considerations

*   **Depth:** Typically 40-60 cm below the surface. The depth must be sufficient to intercept the rising water table but not so deep that it becomes difficult to form or maintain.
*   **Diameter:** Usually 5-8 cm. The diameter is influenced by the mole plow and the soil's cohesive properties.
*   **Spacing:** Depends on the soil's hydraulic conductivity and the desired rate of water table drawdown. Typical spacing ranges from 1 to 4 meters.
*   **Slope:** Similar to open ditches, needs to be sufficient for gravity flow (typically 0.5% to 2%).
*   **Entry and Exit:** Need to be carefully managed to prevent erosion and siltation.
*   **Soil Conditions:**
    *   **Cohesiveness:** Mole drains work best in heavy clay or clay loam soils with good cohesion. They are unsuitable for sandy or loose soils as the channels will collapse.
    *   **Moisture Content:** Soil should be moist but not saturated for effective mole channel formation.

### 2.2. Suitability

*   **Suitable for:**
    *   Heavy clay soils with good cohesion.
    *   Areas where subsurface drainage is needed but the cost of pipe drainage is prohibitive.
    *   Relatively flat to gently sloping land.
    *   Soils with naturally poor internal drainage.
*   **Unsuitable for:**
    *   Sandy soils, peat soils, or soils with low cohesion.
    *   Soils with a high water table that is consistently near the surface.
    *   Soils prone to rapid degradation or collapse.
    *   Areas with aggressive groundwater chemistry that could degrade the soil structure.

## 3. Sub-surface Drainage Systems: Pipe Drainage Systems

Sub-surface pipe drainage systems use buried perforated pipes to collect and remove excess groundwater, thereby lowering the water table and improving soil aeration and root zone conditions.

### 3.1. Design Considerations for Pipe Drainage Systems

The design of pipe drainage systems involves determining the spacing, depth, diameter, and slope of the drainage pipes.

#### **3.1.1. Design for Uniform Flow Conditions**

Uniform flow occurs when the flow velocity and cross-sectional area remain constant along the length of the pipe. This is the most common assumption for gravity-flow pipe drainage.

*   **Key Design Parameters:**
    *   **Drainage Coefficient (or Drainage Requirement):** The rate at which water needs to be removed from the soil, typically expressed in mm/day or m³/s/ha. This is influenced by rainfall, evapotranspiration, irrigation, and crop needs.
    *   **Required Depth of Drains:** The target depth to which the water table needs to be lowered.
    *   **Soil Hydraulic Conductivity (K):** The ease with which water moves through the soil. Higher K allows for wider drain spacing.
    *   **Drain Spacing (S):** The horizontal distance between parallel drain pipes. This is a critical design parameter, often determined using empirical formulas or hydraulic models.
    *   **Drain Depth (d):** The depth of installation of the drain pipes.
    *   **Pipe Diameter (D):** The internal diameter of the pipes, which determines the carrying capacity.
    *   **Pipe Material:** Clay, concrete, PVC, corrugated plastic.
    *   **Perforation Size and Spacing:** Affects the rate of water entry into the pipe.
    *   **Hydraulic Gradients:** The difference in water surface elevation between the water table and the drain pipe.

*   **Empirical Formulas for Drain Spacing (Uniform Flow):**
    *   **Donnan's Formula:** A widely used formula for spacing based on hydraulic conductivity, depth of impermeable layer, drain depth, and required water table drop.
        $S = 4 \times \frac{K(a^2 - d^2)}{Q}$, where:
        *   $S$ = Drain spacing (m)
        *   $K$ = Hydraulic conductivity of the soil layer between the impermeable layer and the drain (m/day)
        *   $a$ = Depth from the surface to the impermeable layer (m)
        *   $d$ = Depth of the drain from the surface (m)
        *   $Q$ = Required rate of water table lowering (m/day)

    *   **Van Schilfgaarde's Formula:** A more refined formula that considers the hydraulic resistance of the soil surrounding the pipe.
        $S = \frac{4 \times K \times D_{rw} \times \Delta h}{Q}$, where:
        *   $S$ = Drain spacing (m)
        *   $K$ = Hydraulic conductivity (m/day)
        *   $D_{rw}$ = Depth of the saturated region above the drain (m)
        *   $\Delta h$ = Hydraulic head loss per unit length of drain (m/m)
        *   $Q$ = Required discharge per unit area (m/day)

*   **Pipe Diameter Calculation:**
    *   Once the spacing and required discharge per unit length of drain are known, Manning's equation or the Chezy formula can be used to determine the required pipe diameter to carry the flow under gravity.
    *   **Manning's Equation:** $Q = \frac{1}{n} A R^{2/3} S^{1/2}$, where $Q$ is discharge, $n$ is Manning's roughness coefficient, $A$ is the cross-sectional area of flow, $R$ is the hydraulic radius, and $S$ is the slope of the pipe. For a full pipe, $A = \pi D^2/4$ and $R = D/4$.

#### **3.1.2. Design for Non-Uniform Flow Conditions**

Non-uniform flow can occur in systems with varying slopes, pipe diameters, or when drains intercept concentrated flows. While less common for primary field drain design, it's relevant for main drains and collector systems.

*   **Key Considerations:**
    *   **Varying Slopes:** If the slope of the main drain changes, the flow characteristics will change.
    *   **Branching:** Where multiple field drains join a main or collector drain, the discharge increases, requiring larger pipe sizes or adjustments in slope.
    *   **Backwater Effects:** Obstructions or changes in channel geometry can cause backwater, leading to non-uniform flow.
    *   **Transient Flow:** In situations where the water table is dynamically changing (e.g., after rainfall or irrigation), the flow within the pipes might not be steady.

*   **Design Approaches:**
    *   **Sectional Design:** Design each section of the pipe system independently based on the flow in that section.
    *   **Hydraulic Modeling:** Use specialized software for unsteady flow analysis (e.g., HEC-RAS, SWMM) to simulate flow dynamics more accurately.
    *   **Larger Pipe Diameters:** Generally, larger diameters are used in main collectors to accommodate increased flow and potential fluctuations.

#### **3.1.3. Transport and Dewatering Situations**

*   **Transport Situations:** This refers to the design of drainage systems for transporting excess water from a site to an outlet. This includes the design of main collectors and outfalls.
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Total Discharge:** Sum of flows from all contributing field drains.
        *   **Velocity:** Must be sufficient to transport any suspended sediment but not cause erosion.
        *   **Gravity Flow:** Design assumes gravity flow to the outlet.
        *   **Outlet Protection:** The outlet should be protected against erosion and potential backflow.

*   **Dewatering Situations:** This involves the temporary removal of groundwater to allow for construction activities (e.g., building foundations, tunnels).
    *   **Types of Dewatering Systems:**
        *   **Wellpoints:** Small-diameter perforated pipes installed close to the excavation, connected to a header pipe and a vacuum pump to lower the water table.
        *   **Deep Wells:** Larger diameter wells installed with submersible pumps to abstract groundwater.
        *   **Ditch and Sump Systems:** Open ditches and sumps at the lowest points where water collects and is pumped out.
    *   **Design Considerations:**
        *   **Required Pumping Rate:** Determined by the permeability of the soil, the dewatering depth, and the area to be dewatered.
        *   **Pump Capacity and Head:** Selection of appropriate pumps to achieve the required flow and overcome the total head.
        *   **Wellpoint/Well Spacing and Depth:** Optimized for effective dewatering.
        *   **Discharge Location:** Ensuring the discharged water does not cause flooding or erosion elsewhere.
        *   **Permeability of Soil:** Critical for determining the effectiveness of wellpoint or well systems.
        *   **Drawdown:** The reduction in water table level.

## 4. Patterns of Drainage System

The pattern of a drainage system refers to the geometric arrangement of the field drains within a field. The choice of pattern depends on the topography, soil type, crop requirements, and farm operations.

### 4.1. Common Drainage Patterns:

*   **Parallel or Grid Pattern:**
    *   **Description:** Field drains are laid parallel to each other at uniform spacings across the entire field. This is the most common and efficient pattern for relatively uniform topography.
    *   **Advantages:** Maximizes drainage effectiveness, facilitates farm operations.
    *   **Variations:**
        *   **Herringbone Pattern:** Laterals enter a main drain at an angle, resembling a fish skeleton. Useful where the main drain is along a central contour or ridge.
        *   **Double Herringbone Pattern:** Similar to herringbone but with laterals on both sides of the main drain.
        *   **Basket Weave Pattern:** Interconnecting laterals forming a grid.

*   **Random Pattern:**
    *   **Description:** Drains are installed only in areas that require drainage, following natural depressions, waterlogged spots, or areas with high water tables.
    *   **Advantages:** Economical when waterlogging is localized.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be less effective than a systematic pattern if waterlogging is widespread or moves.

*   **Concentric Pattern:**
    *   **Description:** Drains are laid out in circles or ovals around a central outlet or sump. This pattern is useful for drainage of depressions or isolated waterlogged areas.
    *   **Advantages:** Effective for specific localized areas.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be expensive and may interfere with some farm operations.

*   **Terrace Drainage:**
    *   **Description:** In hilly or sloping areas, drains are installed along the contours of terraces to intercept and remove water that flows along the terrace.

## 5. Drainage Criteria Formulation

Drainage criteria define the acceptable limits for soil water conditions, influencing the design and performance of drainage systems. They are often expressed in terms of water table depth, soil moisture content, or drainage rate.

### 5.1. Drainage Criteria Formulation:

The formulation of drainage criteria is crucial for designing a system that effectively manages soil water for optimal crop production and land use.

#### **5.1.1. Off-Season Drainage**

*   **Purpose:** To manage soil moisture conditions during periods when the land is not cropped, typically for fallow periods, to facilitate land preparation, sowing, and to prevent anaerobic conditions that can harm soil structure and beneficial organisms.
*   **Criteria:**
    *   **Water Table Depth:** The water table should be lowered to a sufficient depth (e.g., 1.0-1.5 meters) to allow for aeration and prevent waterlogging during the fallow period.
    *   **Drainage Rate:** The system should be capable of removing accumulated water from rainfall or other sources to maintain the desired water table depth.
    *   **Soil Moisture:** Prevent excessive soil moisture that can lead to compaction or anaerobic conditions.

#### **5.1.2. Crop Season Drainage**

*   **Purpose:** To manage soil moisture during the growing season to ensure optimal conditions for crop growth, considering crop water requirements and susceptibility to waterlogging.
*   **Criteria:**
    *   **Water Table Depth:** The water table should be maintained within a specific range that is beneficial for the crop. This range varies significantly by crop. For many crops, a depth of 0.5-1.0 meter is desirable.
    *   **Soil Aeration:** Ensure adequate oxygen supply to the root zone.
    *   **Drainage Rate:** The system must be able to cope with peak drainage loads, which occur after heavy rainfall or irrigation.
    *   **Moisture Deficit:** While drainage aims to remove excess water, it should not lead to excessive drying of the root zone, especially during dry spells.

#### **5.1.3. Salt Drainage (Salinity Control)**

*   **Purpose:** To manage soil salinity in irrigated areas by removing excess salts from the root zone. Drainage plays a crucial role in leaching salts.
*   **Criteria:**
    *   **Leaching Requirement (LR):** The fraction of irrigation water that must pass through the root zone to maintain soil salinity at a level tolerable for the crop.
    *   **Drainage Rate:** The drainage system must be able to carry away the leached water containing salts.
    *   **Water Table Depth:** The water table should be kept sufficiently deep to prevent capillary rise of saline groundwater into the root zone.
    *   **Drainage Water Quality:** While not a direct design criterion for the system layout, the quality of drainage water (salt concentration) is monitored.

### 5.2. Use of Steady State and Unsteady State Approaches in Formulation:

The choice between steady-state and unsteady-state approaches depends on the nature of the drainage problem and the desired accuracy.

*   **Steady State Approach:**
    *   **Assumption:** Assumes that the rate of water inflow into the soil is equal to the rate of water outflow through the drains, and that these rates remain constant over time.
    *   **Application:** Useful for long-term average conditions, calculating average drain spacing, and initial design estimates when inflow is relatively constant (e.g., continuous seepage or steady irrigation).
    *   **Formulation:** Typically uses empirical formulas (like Donnan's) or steady-state analytical solutions based on Darcy's Law.
    *   **Limitations:** Does not account for transient fluctuations in water table or drainage rates, which are common after rainfall or irrigation events.

*   **Unsteady State Approach:**
    *   **Assumption:** Accounts for the dynamic changes in water table levels and flow rates over time. It considers the storage capacity of the soil.
    *   **Application:** Essential for analyzing drainage during and after transient events like rainfall or irrigation, understanding the rate of water table drawdown, and designing systems for specific storm events or dewatering operations.
    *   **Formulation:** Uses the Boussinesq equation or numerical models that solve partial differential equations incorporating the unsaturated zone, soil moisture dynamics, and drain response.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides a more realistic simulation of drainage system performance, especially for peak flow conditions and rapid water table fluctuations.

## 6. Criteria for Irrigated Area

In irrigated areas, drainage systems are essential to prevent waterlogging, salinity, and other problems that can arise from the application of irrigation water.

### 6.1. Key Criteria for Irrigated Areas:

*   **Waterlogging Prevention:**
    *   **Water Table Depth:** Maintain the water table at a depth that prevents waterlogging of crop roots. This depth is crop-specific.
    *   **Drainage Coefficient:** Sufficient to remove excess irrigation water and rainfall within a specified time.
*   **Salinity Control:**
    *   **Leaching Requirement (LR):** The drainage system must be capable of removing the volume of water required to meet the LR to prevent salt accumulation in the root zone.
    *   **Drainage Water Removal:** Efficient removal of saline drainage water to prevent re-saturation of the soil profile.
*   **Aeration:**
    *   **Oxygen Availability:** Ensure adequate oxygen supply to the root zone by maintaining a sufficiently low water table.
*   **Efficiency of Irrigation:**
    *   **Minimizing Water Losses:** While drainage is crucial, the overall water management strategy should aim to minimize unnecessary water application to reduce drainage loads.
*   **Crop Specific Requirements:**
    *   **Tolerance to Waterlogging:** Different crops have varying tolerances to high water tables and anaerobic conditions. Drainage criteria must be tailored to the specific crop being grown.
*   **Soil Properties:**
    *   **Hydraulic Conductivity:** Influences the spacing and capacity of the drainage system.
    *   **Water Holding Capacity:** Affects the rate at which water is released to the drains.

### 6.2. Incorporation of Intentional and Unavoidable Losses:

Effective drainage design in irrigated areas must consider all sources of water entering the soil and needing removal.

*   **Intentional Losses (Controlled Additions):**
    *   **Irrigation Water Application:** The primary source of water in irrigated areas. Design must account for the total volume of irrigation water applied, including any excess beyond the crop's actual needs.
    *   **Leaching Requirements:** The water deliberately applied to leach salts is an "intentional loss" from the field perspective but is a necessary component of saline land management.

*   **Unavoidable Losses (Uncontrolled Additions):**
    *   **Rainfall:** Natural precipitation contributes to soil moisture and can exacerbate waterlogging, especially during the irrigation season.
    *   **Seepage from Canals and Reservoirs:** Water leaking from irrigation infrastructure can recharge groundwater and raise water tables, necessitating drainage.
    *   **Upslope Runoff:** Surface water from higher elevations entering the area.
    *   **Deep Percolation from Irrigated Areas (beyond leaching):** Water percolating below the root zone from over-irrigation or inefficient application methods.
    *   **Condensation and Dew:** Though often minor, can contribute to soil moisture.

**Drainage Design Consideration:** The drainage system must be sized to handle the sum of these water inputs (irrigation, rainfall, seepage, etc.) minus the water removed by evapotranspiration, while maintaining the desired soil moisture regime. Drainage coefficients are often determined by considering the worst-case scenario of combined rainfall and irrigation events.

## Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** What are the main advantages of using open ditches as field drains compared to closed drains?

**Answer:** The main advantages of open ditches are their low construction cost, ease of construction and maintenance with simple equipment, and their ability to rapidly remove surface runoff.

---

**Question 2:** In what type of soil conditions are mole drains most suitable? Explain why.

**Answer:** Mole drains are most suitable in heavy clay or clay loam soils with good cohesion. This is because the cohesive nature of these soils allows the mole plow to form a stable, unlined channel that can persist for a reasonable period. Sandy or loose soils lack this cohesion, and mole channels formed in them would quickly collapse.

---

**Question 3:** Briefly explain the concept of a "drainage coefficient" in pipe drainage design.

**Answer:** A drainage coefficient represents the depth of water to be removed from the soil surface per day, usually expressed in mm/day or cm/day. It is a measure of the required drainage capacity of the system and is determined by factors such as rainfall patterns, irrigation practices, crop water requirements, and acceptable water table depths.

---

**Question 4:** Differentiate between steady-state and unsteady-state approaches in drainage criteria formulation. When would you prefer to use the unsteady-state approach?

**Answer:**
*   **Steady-state:** Assumes constant inflow and outflow rates, useful for long-term averages and initial design.
*   **Unsteady-state:** Accounts for dynamic changes in water table and flow rates over time, considering soil moisture storage.

You would prefer the unsteady-state approach when analyzing drainage performance during and after transient events like heavy rainfall or irrigation, or when needing to understand rapid water table drawdown.

---

**Question 5:** In an irrigated area with a moderate risk of salinity, what is the primary role of a drainage system in relation to salt management?

**Answer:** The primary role of a drainage system in relation to salt management is to facilitate the leaching of salts from the root zone. By maintaining a sufficiently deep water table and providing an outlet for drainage water, the system allows for the removal of accumulated salts that are transported downwards by irrigation water (leaching).

---

**Question 6:** Calculate the spacing of field drains in a field where the soil has a hydraulic conductivity of 0.5 m/day. The impermeable layer is 3.0 m below the surface, the drains are to be installed at a depth of 1.0 m, and the required rate of water table lowering is 0.02 m/day. Use Donnan's Formula.

**Answer:**
Using Donnan's Formula: $S = \frac{4 \times K(a^2 - d^2)}{Q}$

Given:
*   $K = 0.5$ m/day
*   $a = 3.0$ m (depth to impermeable layer)
*   $d = 1.0$ m (depth of drain)
*   $Q = 0.02$ m/day (required rate of water table lowering)

$S = \frac{4 \times 0.5 \times (3.0^2 - 1.0^2)}{0.02}$
$S = \frac{2.0 \times (9.0 - 1.0)}{0.02}$
$S = \frac{2.0 \times 8.0}{0.02}$
$S = \frac{16.0}{0.02}$
$S = 800$ meters

Therefore, the spacing of field drains should be 800 meters.

## Important Points to Remember:

*   **Drainage Coefficient:** The cornerstone of drainage system capacity design.
*   **Hydraulic Conductivity (K):** A critical soil parameter determining drain spacing and system effectiveness.
*   **Soil Type:** Dictates the suitability of different drainage methods (e.g., mole drains require cohesive soils).
*   **Crop Requirements:** Drainage criteria are crop-specific, considering their tolerance to waterlogging and salinity.
*   **Topography:** Influences the pattern and slope of the drainage system.
*   **Maintenance:** Open ditches require more frequent maintenance than closed pipe systems.
*   **Unsteady-state analysis:** Provides a more realistic assessment of system performance during dynamic hydrological events.
*   **Integrated Approach:** Drainage design should be part of a holistic water management plan for irrigated areas, considering irrigation efficiency and salinity control.
*   **Losses:** Always account for all sources of water, both intentional (irrigation) and unavoidable (rainfall, seepage), in the design.
