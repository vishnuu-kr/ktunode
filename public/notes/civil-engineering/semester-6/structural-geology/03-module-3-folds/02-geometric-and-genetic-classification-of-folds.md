# Module 3: Folds - Geometric and Genetic Classification of Folds

## Introduction to Folds

Folds are wave-like undulations in rock layers that form under compressional stress. They are fundamental structures in structural geology, providing insights into the deformation history of the Earth's crust. Understanding the classification of folds is crucial for analyzing and interpreting geological structures.

---

## Learning Outcomes

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Define** key terms related to folds (e.g., limb, hinge, crest, trough, axial surface, fold axis, interlimb angle, dip isogons).
*   **Classify** folds based on their geometry (shape and orientation).
*   **Classify** folds based on their genetic origin (how they formed).
*   **Identify** different types of folds and their characteristic features.
*   **Recognize** the significance of fold classification in geological interpretation.

## Geometric Classification of Folds

Geometric classification focuses on the observable shape and orientation of folded rock layers.

### Key Terminology of Folds

*   **Limb:** The sides of a fold, connecting the hinge.
*   **Hinge:** The point or zone of maximum curvature within a fold.
*   **Crest:** The highest point of an anticline.
*   **Trough:** The lowest point of a syncline.
*   **Axial Surface (or Axial Plane):** An imaginary plane that bisects the angle between the two limbs of a fold. It passes through the hinge of the fold.
*   **Fold Axis:** The line formed by the intersection of the axial surface with a folded layer. It represents the axis of maximum curvature.
*   **Interlimb Angle:** The angle between the two limbs of a fold, measured in a cross-section perpendicular to the fold axis.
*   **Dip Isogons:** Lines drawn on a map or cross-section connecting points of equal dip on opposite limbs of a fold. The pattern of dip isogons provides information about the relative thickness of the fold limbs.

### Classification Based on Interlimb Angle

This is a common and practical way to classify folds.

*   **Gentle Folds:** Interlimb angle > 120°. Limbs are nearly horizontal or shallowly dipping.
*   **Open Folds:** 70° < Interlimb angle < 120°. Limbs have moderate dips.
*   **Tight Folds:** 30° < Interlimb angle < 70°. Limbs are steeply dipping.
*   **Very Tight/Isoclinal Folds:** Interlimb angle < 30°. Limbs are parallel or nearly parallel.

### Classification Based on Dip of Axial Surface

The orientation of the axial surface relative to the bedding planes is crucial.

*   **Symmetrical Folds:** The axial surface bisects the angle between the limbs symmetrically. The dips on either side of the axial surface are roughly equal in magnitude but opposite in direction.
*   **Asymmetrical Folds:** The axial surface does not bisect the angle between the limbs symmetrically. The dips on either side of the axial surface are unequal.
*   **Overturned Folds:** The axial surface is tilted so much that the strata on one limb dip in the same general direction as the strata on the other limb, but at different angles. Both limbs dip in the same direction.
*   **Recumbent Folds:** The axial surface is horizontal or nearly horizontal. The limbs are essentially horizontal.

### Classification Based on Shape of Fold Profile (using Dip Isogons)

This classification, developed by C.E. Tilche, uses the pattern of dip isogons to define fold classes.

*   **Class 1 Folds (Concentric Folds):**
    *   **Key Characteristic:** The thickness of the folded layer remains constant in the direction perpendicular to the bedding (i.e., the layer is parallel to itself).
    *   **Dip Isogons:** Converge towards the hinge region on the inner side and diverge on the outer side.
    *   **Subclasses:**
        *   **Class 1A:** Symmetrical convergence/divergence of isogons.
        *   **Class 1B:** Asymmetrical convergence/divergence of isogons.
        *   **Class 1C:** Highly asymmetrical convergence/divergence of isogons.
    *   **Formation:** Typically form by flexural slip, where layers slide past each other.

*   **Class 2 Folds (Similar Folds):**
    *   **Key Characteristic:** The thickness of the folded layer decreases towards the hinge region. Limbs are parallel or sub-parallel.
    *   **Dip Isogons:** Converge towards the hinge region on both sides.
    *   **Formation:** Typically form by passive flow or shear, where the rock material rearranges itself, thinning at the hinges. Common in ductile shear zones or areas with intense flattening.

*   **Class 3 Folds (Cylindrical/Neutral Folds):**
    *   **Key Characteristic:** The thickness of the folded layer remains constant in all directions perpendicular to the fold axis.
    *   **Dip Isogons:** Parallel to each other.
    *   **Formation:** Can form by various mechanisms, including flexural flow. Less common than Class 1 or Class 2.

**Important Note on Dip Isogons:**

*   **Convergent Isogons:** Indicate thickening of the inner arc and thinning of the outer arc of the fold.
*   **Divergent Isogons:** Indicate thinning of the inner arc and thickening of the outer arc of the fold.
*   **Parallel Isogons:** Indicate uniform thickness.

## Genetic Classification of Folds

Genetic classification attempts to explain how folds are formed, often based on the deformation mechanisms involved.

### 1. Flexural Folds

*   **Mechanism:** Formed by bending of rock layers, with differential movement along bedding planes (flexural slip) or within layers (flexural flow).
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Flexural Slip:** Layers maintain their thickness, but slip occurs between them. This leads to concentric folds (Class 1).
    *   **Flexural Flow:** Occurs in more ductile materials, where layers deform internally by flow, leading to thinning at the hinges and thickening on the limbs (can produce Class 2 or 3 folds).
*   **Stress Regime:** Typically associated with compressional stress where layers have different mechanical properties.

### 2. Passive Folds (or Shear Folds)

*   **Mechanism:** Formed by the rotation of initially planar layers within a deforming mass of rock, where the deformation itself is the dominant factor, not the bending of individual layers. The layers are passively carried along by the bulk deformation.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Often exhibit thinning at hinges and thickening on limbs.
    *   Frequently display similar fold geometry (Class 2).
    *   Can occur in homogeneous or layered rocks undergoing shear or flattening.
*   **Stress Regime:** Associated with shear deformation or significant bulk shortening/flattening.

### 3. Transform Folds

*   **Mechanism:** Formed by differential movement along planes of weakness (like faults or joints) that are oblique to the bedding.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Often exhibit sharp, angular hinges.
    *   The folding is controlled by the pre-existing structural fabric.
*   **Stress Regime:** Can occur in brittle or semi-brittle regimes where discrete slip surfaces develop.

### 4. Kink Folds

*   **Mechanism:** Formed by the abrupt bending of a rock layer over a relatively narrow zone (the kink band), with limbs remaining flat. This is a form of brittle or semi-brittle deformation.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Sharp, angular hinges.
    *   Limbs are typically parallel or sub-parallel.
    *   Can occur singly or in conjugate sets.
*   **Stress Regime:** Associated with compressional stress acting on layered rocks with distinct mechanical properties, often in brittle conditions.

## Types of Folds Based on Orientation and Style

Beyond geometric and genetic classifications, folds are also described by their orientation and overall style.

### 1. Anticlines and Synclines

*   **Anticline:** A fold with convex upward curvature where the oldest rocks are in the core. The limbs dip away from the crest.
*   **Syncline:** A fold with concave upward curvature where the youngest rocks are in the core. The limbs dip towards the trough.

**Important:** The terms anticline and syncline refer to the *upward* or *downward* bending of the layers, respectively. They do not inherently imply the age of the rocks in the core.

### 2. Monoclines

*   **Definition:** A fold that consists of a single limb, where layers dip in one direction over a limited area, changing their dip abruptly before returning to their original orientation.
*   **Appearance:** Looks like a step in the rock layers.
*   **Formation:** Often related to underlying faulting, where movement on the fault causes a localized flexure in the overlying strata.

### 3. Chevron Folds

*   **Definition:** A type of kink fold characterized by sharp, angular hinges and flat limbs, resembling the points of a chevron.
*   **Appearance:** V-shaped or zig-zag patterns.
*   **Formation:** Typically form in strongly layered rocks under compression, often with brittle deformation mechanisms.

### 4. Fan Folds

*   **Definition:** Folds where the limbs dip towards the axial surface at increasing angles as they approach the core.
*   **Appearance:** The fold appears to open outwards from the hinge.
*   **Formation:** Can form in various settings, including compressional zones and extensional fault blocks.

### 5. Dome and Basin Structures

*   **Dome:** A roughly circular or elliptical upfold where the strata dip away from a central point in all directions. The oldest rocks are in the center.
*   **Basin:** A roughly circular or elliptical downfold where the strata dip towards a central point in all directions. The youngest rocks are in the center.
*   **Formation:** Often associated with broad regional uplift or subsidence, or the intersection of anticlines and synclines.

---

## Significance of Fold Classification in Geology

*   **Deformation History:** Understanding fold types helps unravel the sequence and style of deformation that a rock package has undergone.
*   **Stress Analysis:** The geometry and type of folds can indicate the direction and type of stress that caused them.
*   **Resource Exploration:** Folds are important structural traps for hydrocarbons and other mineral deposits. Identifying specific fold types can aid in exploration efforts.
*   **Geological Mapping:** Accurate classification and description of folds are essential for creating meaningful geological maps.
*   **Tectonic Interpretation:** Fold patterns reveal information about large-scale tectonic processes, such as mountain building and plate interactions.

## Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
*   **Limb:** The sides of a fold, connecting the hinge.
*   **Hinge:** The point or zone of maximum curvature within a fold.
*   **Axial Surface:** An imaginary plane that bisects the angle between the two limbs of a fold and passes through the hinge.
*   **Interlimb Angle:** The angle between the two limbs of a fold, measured in a cross-section perpendicular to the fold axis.

**Answer 2:**
    a) Gentle fold (Interlimb angle is roughly 180° - (10° + 20°) = 150°, which is > 120°).
    b) Tight fold (Interlimb angle is roughly 180° - (60° + 70°) = 50°, which is between 30° and 70°).
    c) Overturned fold (The limbs dip in the same general direction). If we assume one limb dips at 10° and the other at 170° (meaning it's dipping back past vertical), the interlimb angle between their positive dips would be large, but their orientation defines it as overturned. More precisely, if both dip towards the same side, it's overturned. If the 170° is measured relative to the horizontal, it means the limb is dipping 10° past vertical in the opposite direction to the 10° limb.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Key Characteristic:** The thickness of the folded layer remains constant in the direction perpendicular to the bedding (i.e., the layer is parallel to itself).
*   **Typical Mechanism:** Flexural slip, where layers slide past each other along bedding planes.

**Answer 4:** Class 2 fold (similar fold).

**Answer 5:**
*   **Anticline:** The oldest rocks are in the core.
*   **Syncline:** The youngest rocks are in the core.

**Answer 6:** d) Recumbent fold (The axial surface is nearly horizontal, leading to nearly horizontal limbs).

**Answer 7:**
*   **Flexural Slip:** Occurs when layers maintain their thickness, but differential movement (slip) happens *between* the layers along bedding planes. This is characteristic of concentric folds (Class 1).
*   **Flexural Flow:** Occurs when layers deform internally by flow, leading to thinning at the hinges and thickening on the limbs. This can produce similar folds (Class 2) or other classes depending on the flow.

**Answer 8:** This likely describes a **chevron fold** (a type of kink fold). It suggests deformation under compressional stress, likely in a brittle or semi-brittle regime, where the layered rocks bent sharply at discrete zones.

## Important Points to Remember

*   Folds are fundamental to understanding Earth's deformation.
*   Geometric classification describes the shape and orientation of folds.
*   Genetic classification explains the mechanisms of fold formation.
*   The interlimb angle is a key parameter in geometric classification.
*   Dip isogons are crucial for classifying folds based on layer thickness changes.
*   Anticlines are upfolds (oldest in core), and synclines are downfolds (youngest in core).
*   Recumbent folds have horizontal axial surfaces.
*   Kink folds have sharp, angular hinges.
*   Fold classification is essential for interpreting tectonic history and for resource exploration.
