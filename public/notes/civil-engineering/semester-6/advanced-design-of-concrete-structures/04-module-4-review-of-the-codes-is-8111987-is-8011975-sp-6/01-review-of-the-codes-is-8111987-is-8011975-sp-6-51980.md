## Topic: Review of IS 811:1987, IS 801:1975, and SP 6-5:1980

This module focuses on understanding and applying the provisions of key Indian Standards relevant to the advanced design of concrete structures, with a particular emphasis on the specified codes: IS 811:1987, IS 801:1975, and SP 6-5:1980.

### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

This section will delve into the fundamental concepts and definitions relevant to each code.

#### 2.1. IS 811:1987 - Code of Practice for Use of Aluminium in Construction

*   **Scope:** This code provides guidance on the design, fabrication, and erection of structures utilizing aluminum and its alloys for building and construction purposes. While primarily focused on aluminum, its principles are relevant to understanding material-specific design considerations in advanced structural engineering.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Aluminum Alloys:** Different grades of aluminum alloys have varying mechanical properties (strength, ductility, corrosion resistance). The code specifies permissible stresses based on these properties.
    *   **Design Stresses:** Permissible stresses for tension, compression, bending, and shear are defined, considering factors like safety margins and material properties.
    *   **Connections:** Detailed guidelines for various types of aluminum connections (riveted, bolted, welded) are provided, emphasizing load transfer and joint integrity.
    *   **Corrosion Protection:** Aluminum's susceptibility to certain types of corrosion is addressed, with recommendations for surface treatments and protective coatings.
    *   **Fire Resistance:** Design considerations for fire performance of aluminum structures are outlined.
*   **Definitions:**
    *   **Permissible Stress:** The maximum stress that a material is allowed to withstand under service loads, determined by dividing the ultimate strength by a factor of safety.
    *   **Wrought Alloys:** Aluminum alloys formed by mechanical processes like rolling, extrusion, or forging.
    *   **Cast Alloys:** Aluminum alloys formed by pouring molten metal into molds.

#### 2.2. IS 801:1975 - Code of Practice for Use of Structural Steel in General Construction (Second Revision)

*   **Scope:** This is a foundational code for the design of structural steel members in general building construction. It covers the design of various steel elements subjected to different load conditions.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Limit State Design (Implicit):** While not as explicit as later codes, IS 801 incorporates principles that lead to safe and economical design by considering material strengths and load factors.
    *   **Allowable Stresses:** The code specifies allowable stresses for steel based on its yield strength and ultimate tensile strength, incorporating factors of safety.
    *   **Design of Tension Members:** Calculation of net effective area and provisions for staggered riveting.
    *   **Design of Compression Members:** Buckling considerations, effective length, and slenderness ratio for columns and struts.
    *   **Design of Beams:** Bending, shear, and deflection checks for various beam configurations. Local buckling of flanges and webs is also addressed.
    *   **Connections:** Design of bolted and riveted connections, including bearing, shear, and tensile capacity of fasteners. Welded connections are also covered.
    *   **Plate Girders:** Design principles for built-up girders.
    *   **Plastic Design (Limited):** Some provisions for plastic analysis and design of continuous beams are included.
*   **Definitions:**
    *   **Yield Strength ($f_y$):** The stress at which a material begins to deform plastically.
    *   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($f_u$):** The maximum stress a material can withstand before fracturing.
    *   **Slenderness Ratio:** The ratio of the effective length of a compression member to its least radius of gyration.
    *   **Effective Area:** The net cross-sectional area of a member, considering deductions for bolt holes or other perforations.
    *   **Factor of Safety:** A multiplier applied to service loads or a divisor to material strengths to ensure adequate safety margins.

#### 2.3. SP 6-5:1980 - Handbook on Concrete Mixes (Revised)

*   **Scope:** This Special Publication (SP) is a practical guide for the proportioning and design of concrete mixes to achieve specified strength and durability requirements. It is crucial for understanding the material science aspect of concrete structures.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Concrete Mix Design:** The process of selecting appropriate ingredients (cement, aggregates, water, admixtures) and their proportions to produce concrete with desired properties.
    *   **Strength Requirements:** Concrete is classified based on its characteristic compressive strength ($f_{ck}$) at 28 days.
    *   **Workability:** The ease with which fresh concrete can be mixed, placed, compacted, and finished without segregation. Measured by slump test.
    *   **Durability:** The ability of concrete to resist environmental factors and maintain its performance over its intended service life.
    *   **Aggregates:** Properties of coarse and fine aggregates (grading, shape, size, strength, deleterious materials).
    *   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):** A critical factor influencing strength and durability. Lower w/c ratio generally leads to higher strength and better durability.
    *   **Admixtures:** Materials added to concrete to modify its properties (e.g., plasticizers, accelerators, retarders, air-entraining agents).
    *   **Trial Mixes:** Experimental mixes used to determine the optimal proportions for a specific concrete mix.
*   **Definitions:**
    *   **Characteristic Strength ($f_{ck}$):** The strength of concrete below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall.
    *   **Nominal Mix:** Predetermined proportions of cement, fine aggregate, and coarse aggregate for concrete of a specified strength. (Less preferred for critical structures).
    *   **Design Mix:** Mixes designed by the engineer based on specific performance requirements, often using trial mixes.
    *   **Slump:** A measure of the workability of fresh concrete, indicating its consistency.
    *   **Workability Agents (Admixtures):** Chemicals that improve the workability of concrete.

### 3. Design Principles and Methodologies

This section will elaborate on the core design philosophies and methods employed by each code.

#### 3.1. IS 811:1987 - Aluminum Construction

*   **Design Philosophy:** Primarily based on **permissible stress design**. This means that the stresses induced by service loads should not exceed the allowable stresses for the material, which are determined by dividing the ultimate strength by a factor of safety.
*   **Methodology:**
    *   **Load Calculation:** Determining dead loads, live loads, wind loads, etc., as per relevant IS codes.
    *   **Stress Analysis:** Calculating stresses in members under service loads (tension, compression, bending, shear).
    *   **Member Selection:** Choosing appropriate aluminum sections (e.g., extrusions) based on required section properties (area, moment of inertia, section modulus).
    *   **Connection Design:** Ensuring that connections can transfer loads effectively without exceeding permissible stresses in fasteners or base materials.
    *   **Buckling Checks:** For compression members, checking for buckling stability based on effective length and cross-sectional properties.

#### 3.2. IS 801:1975 - Steel Construction

*   **Design Philosophy:** Primarily based on **allowable stress design (ASD)**. Similar to IS 811, it relies on keeping stresses within permissible limits. However, it also has elements that hint at capacity design principles for certain situations.
*   **Methodology:**
    *   **Load Combinations:** Consideration of various load combinations (dead, live, wind, seismic) with appropriate load factors.
    *   **Member Design:**
        *   **Tension Members:** Designing for net section yielding and rupture at the gross section.
        *   **Compression Members:** Designing for Euler buckling and also considering intermediate column buckling curves based on slenderness.
        *   **Beams:** Designing for bending, shear, and deflection. Lateral torsional buckling is a critical consideration for beams.
    *   **Connection Design:** Design of riveted and bolted connections is a significant part of the code, focusing on shear, bearing, and tensile capacity of fasteners. Welded connections are also covered.
    *   **Plate Girder Design:** Detailed procedures for designing built-up plate girders, including flange-to-web connections and web buckling.

#### 3.3. SP 6-5:1980 - Concrete Mix Design

*   **Design Philosophy:** Focuses on **performance-based design at the material level**. The goal is to achieve specific strength and durability characteristics through proper mix proportioning.
*   **Methodology (Iterative Process):**
    *   **Step 1: Define Requirements:**
        *   Characteristic compressive strength ($f_{ck}$).
        *   Maximum aggregate size.
        *   Required degree of workability (slump).
        *   Exposure conditions (for durability).
    *   **Step 2: Select Water-Cement Ratio:** Based on strength requirements and durability considerations (e.g., for aggressive environments).
    *   **Step 3: Determine Water Content:** Based on maximum aggregate size and required slump.
    *   **Step 4: Calculate Cement Content:** From the selected w/c ratio and determined water content.
    *   **Step 5: Determine Aggregate Proportioning:** Based on grading and maximum aggregate size, often using the concept of fineness modulus or absolute volume methods.
    *   **Step 6: Calculate Coarse and Fine Aggregate Quantities:** Based on the absolute volume of concrete and the proportions.
    *   **Step 7: Adjust for Admixtures:** If used, their dosage and impact on water demand and workability need to be considered.
    *   **Step 8: Trial Mixes:** Prepare trial mixes to verify workability, setting time, and compressive strength at 7 and 28 days.
    *   **Step 9: Adjustments:** Modify proportions based on trial mix results to achieve the target strength and workability.
    *   **Step 10: Final Mix Design:** The optimized proportions are finalized.

### 4. Critical Design Parameters and Requirements

#### 4.1. IS 811:1987 - Aluminum Construction

*   **Material Properties:**
    *   **Yield Strength ($F_{ty}$, $F_{cy}$):** For tension and compression.
    *   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($F_{tu}$):** For rupture checks.
    *   **Modulus of Elasticity (E):** For buckling calculations.
    *   **Shear Strength ($F_{sv}$):** For connection design.
*   **Geometric Properties:**
    *   **Cross-sectional Area ($A$):** For stress calculations.

    *   **Effective Length:** For compression members.

*   **Connection Parameters:**
    *   **Diameter of Rivets/Bolts:** For shear and bearing calculations.

    *   **Slenderness Ratio ($L_e/r$):**
*   **Connection Parameters:**
    *   **Shear Strength of Fasteners:**
    *   **Bearing Strength of Fasteners:**
    *   **Tensile Strength of Fasteners:**
    *   **Weld Strength:** Throat thickness, weld size.

#### 4.3. SP 6-5:1980 - Concrete Mix Design

*   **Strength:**
    *   **Characteristic Compressive Strength ($f_{ck}$):** The primary design parameter.
    *   **Target Mean Strength ($f_{cm} = f_{ck} + 1.645 \sigma$):** Used for mix design, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of strength.

    *   **Slump:** Dictated by placement method and reinforcement density.

### 5. Application Examples

This section provides simplified examples to illustrate the application of code principles.

#### 5.1. Example: Design of a Steel Tension Member (Based on IS 801:1975)

**Problem:** Design a steel tension member using a single angle section to carry an axial tensile force of $150$ kN. The steel is Fe 415, with $f_y = 415$ N/mm$^2$ and $f_u = 415$ N/mm$^2$. Assume the member is connected by $20$ mm diameter bolts.

**Solution Approach:**

1.  **Determine Required Net Area:**
    *   Allowable tensile stress in net section (from IS 801): Typically $0.6 f_y$ for the net area.
    *   Required Net Area ($A_{net}$) = Total Force / Allowable Tensile Stress
    *   $A_{net} = 150 \times 10^3 \text{ N} / (0.6 \times 415 \text{ N/mm}^2) \approx 603.6 \text{ mm}^2$.

2.  **Account for Bolt Holes:**
    *   Add allowance for bolt holes: $A_{gross} = A_{net} + \text{Area of holes}$.
    *   Area of one hole = (Diameter of bolt + 2 mm) $\times$ Thickness.
    *   Assuming 2 bolts in the connection, the net area needs to be increased to account for at least one bolt hole's area.

3.  **Select a Suitable Angle Section:**
    *   Refer to steel tables for available single angle sections (e.g., ISA 60x60x8, ISA 75x50x8, etc.).
    *   For each selected section, check:
        *   **Gross Area ($A_{gross}$):** Should be sufficient to satisfy the tension requirement after considering bolt holes.
        *   **Net Area ($A_{net}$):** The net area after deducting for bolt holes should be greater than or equal to the required net area.
        *   **Outstand Leg:** If the outstanding leg is not connected, it needs to be checked for its effective area. The effective area is usually taken as $A_{gross} \times (\text{Area}_1 + k \times \text{Area}_2) / \text{Area}_1$, where $\text{Area}_1$ is the area of the connected leg and $\text{Area}_2$ is the area of the outstanding leg, and $k$ is a factor from the code. For a single angle with one leg bolted, the net effective area should be at least $1.15$ times the required net area.

4.  **Check Connection Strength:**
    *   **Shear Strength of Bolts:** Calculate the shear capacity of the bolts based on their diameter and the allowable shear stress. Ensure the number of bolts is sufficient.
    *   **Bearing Strength of Bolts:** Calculate the bearing capacity of the angle and the steel plate where the bolts pass.

**Example Selection:** Let's assume we pick ISA $75 \times 50 \times 8$.
*   Gross Area ($A_g$) = $9.86$ cm$^2$ = $986$ mm$^2$.
*   If we assume two $20$ mm bolts are used in the connection (along the $75$ mm leg), and the holes are $22$ mm diameter.
*   Net Area ($A_n$) = $A_g - 2 \times (\text{diameter of hole}) \times \text{thickness} = 986 - 2 \times 22 \times 8 = 986 - 352 = 634$ mm$^2$.
*   Allowable tensile stress on gross section = $0.6 f_y = 0.6 \times 415 = 249$ N/mm$^2$.
*   Allowable tensile force on gross section = $A_g \times 0.6 f_y = 986 \times 249 \approx 245.5$ kN. This is sufficient.
*   Now, consider the net effective area. For a single angle with one leg connected, the effective area is reduced. Let's say the $75$ mm leg is connected. The code provides a formula for effective area based on the ratio of connected area to total area. A simplified check often involves ensuring the net area is sufficient and the outstanding leg has adequate support.
*   For the outstanding leg, the effective area could be taken as $A_{net} + k \times \text{Area of outstanding leg}$. If $k=0.6$ for a single angle with one leg connected.
    *   Area of connected leg = $75 \times 8 = 600$ mm$^2$.
    *   Area of outstanding leg = $50 \times 8 = 400$ mm$^2$.
    *   Net Area of connected leg (after hole) = $600 - 22 \times 8 = 436$ mm$^2$.
    *   Effective Area = $436 + 0.6 \times 400 = 436 + 240 = 676$ mm$^2$.
*   Allowable tensile stress on effective area is $0.6 f_u$ (as per IS 800). So, allowable force = $0.6 \times 415 \times 676 \approx 168.3$ kN.
*   This section seems adequate. A detailed check of connection bolt capacity would also be required.

**Important Note:** This is a simplified example. A full design would involve more detailed checks for net section rupture at the connection and bearing on the member.

#### 5.2. Example: Concrete Mix Design (Based on SP 6-5:1980)

**Problem:** Design a concrete mix for a column with a characteristic compressive strength ($f_{ck}$) of $35$ N/mm$^2$ at 28 days. The exposure condition is moderate. Required slump is $75-100$ mm (medium workability). The maximum aggregate size is $20$ mm. Assume the use of OPC 43 grade cement.

**Solution Approach (Illustrative Steps):**

1.  **Target Mean Strength:**
    *   For $f_{ck} = 35$ N/mm$^2$, assume a standard deviation $\sigma = 5$ N/mm$^2$ (typical for good control).
    *   Target Mean Strength ($f_{cm}$) = $35 + 1.645 \times 5 = 35 + 8.225 \approx 43.2$ N/mm$^2$.

2.  **Select Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):**
    *   For moderate exposure and $f_{ck} = 35$ N/mm$^2$, the maximum permissible w/c ratio from SP 6-5 tables is typically $0.55$. However, to achieve a target mean strength of $43.2$ N/mm$^2$, we might need a lower w/c ratio. Let's assume from experience or preliminary charts that a w/c ratio of $0.45$ is required for this strength.

3.  **Estimate Water Content:**
    *   For $20$ mm maximum aggregate size and $75-100$ mm slump, the approximate water content from SP 6-5 tables is around $190$ kg/m$^3$. (This is an illustrative value; actual tables should be consulted).

4.  **Calculate Cement Content:**
    *   Cement Content = Water Content / (w/c ratio)
    *   Cement Content = $190 \text{ kg/m}^3 / 0.45 \approx 422.2$ kg/m$^3$.
    *   Check minimum cement content for durability. For moderate exposure, it might be around $320$ kg/m$^3$. Our calculated $422.2$ kg/m$^3$ is sufficient.

5.  **Determine Aggregate Proportioning:**
    *   Assume the ratio of fine aggregate to coarse aggregate by volume is $1:2$ (typical for good grading). This proportion is often determined by trial and error or consulting tables.

6.  **Calculate Aggregate Quantities:**
    *   Total volume of concrete = $1$ m$^3$.
    *   Volume of water = Water Content / Density of water = $190 / 1000 = 0.19$ m$^3$.
    *   Volume of cement = Cement Content / Density of cement = $422.2 / 3150 = 0.134$ m$^3$. (Density of cement is approx. 3150 kg/m$^3$).
    *   Volume of aggregates = $1 - \text{Volume of water} - \text{Volume of cement} = 1 - 0.19 - 0.134 = 0.676$ m$^3$.
    *   Let $V_{fa}$ be the volume of fine aggregate and $V_{ca}$ be the volume of coarse aggregate.
    *   $V_{fa} + V_{ca} = 0.676$ m$^3$.
    *   Assume $V_{fa} / V_{ca} = 1/2$, so $V_{ca} = 2 V_{fa}$.
    *   $V_{fa} + 2 V_{fa} = 0.676 \Rightarrow 3 V_{fa} = 0.676 \Rightarrow V_{fa} = 0.225$ m$^3$.
    *   $V_{ca} = 2 \times 0.225 = 0.451$ m$^3$.

7.  **Calculate Weights of Aggregates:**
    *   Weight of Fine Aggregate = $V_{fa} \times \text{Density of FA} \times (1 - \text{Absorption})$
    *   Weight of Coarse Aggregate = $V_{ca} \times \text{Density of CA} \times (1 - \text{Absorption})$
    *   Assume S.G. of fine aggregate = $2.65$, S.G. of coarse aggregate = $2.70$. Assume absorption is negligible for simplicity or use standard values if available.
    *   Weight of Fine Aggregate $\approx 0.225 \times 2650 \approx 596$ kg.
    *   Weight of Coarse Aggregate $\approx 0.451 \times 2700 \approx 1218$ kg.

8.  **Final Mix Proportions (per m$^3$):**
    *   Cement: $422.2$ kg
    *   Water: $190$ kg
    *   Fine Aggregate: $596$ kg
    *   Coarse Aggregate: $1218$ kg
    *   w/c ratio = $190/422.2 \approx 0.45$.

**Important Note:** This is a simplified overview. Actual mix design involves more detailed consideration of aggregate properties, bulking of sand, and trial mixes for verification.

### 7. Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **IS 801:1975:**
    *   **Slenderness Ratio:** It is crucial for determining the buckling strength of compression members. A higher slenderness ratio indicates a greater susceptibility to buckling, leading to a reduction in the allowable compressive stress. IS 801 provides curves or formulas to calculate the allowable stress based on the slenderness ratio and the material's properties.
    *   **Allowable Tensile Stresses:**
        *   On the **gross section** in tension: Typically $0.6 f_y$.
        *   On the **net section** in tension: Typically $0.5 f_u$ (for connections with few fasteners) or $0.6 f_u$ (for connections with many fasteners, considering shear lag). (Note: Specific values can vary slightly based on the revision and specific clauses).

2.  **IS 811:1987:**
    *   **Permissible Stresses:** This concept implies that the stresses induced by the service loads in the aluminum member should not exceed the material's allowable stress values. These allowable stresses are derived by applying a factor of safety to the material's yield strength or ultimate strength. This ensures that the material remains elastic under service conditions and has sufficient reserve strength to prevent failure.
    *   **Aluminum Connection Considerations:**
        *   **Load Transfer:** Ensuring efficient transfer of forces between connected members.
        *   **Corrosion:** Avoiding galvanic corrosion between aluminum and dissimilar metals.
        *   **Thermal Expansion:** Accounting for the higher coefficient of thermal expansion of aluminum compared to steel.
        *   **Buckling of Connected Parts:** Ensuring that thin aluminum components in connections do not buckle.
        *   **Weldability:** If welding is used, considering the appropriate welding processes and filler materials.

3.  **SP 6-5:1980:**
    *   **Outline of Steps:**
        1.  Determine Target Mean Strength ($f_{cm} = f_{ck} + 1.645\sigma$).
        2.  Select the water-cement ratio (w/c) based on strength and durability requirements (moderate exposure and $40$ N/mm$^2$ will likely require a w/c of around $0.45$ or lower).
        3.  Estimate the water content for the given slump and aggregate size ($50-75$ mm slump with $20$ mm aggregate).
        4.  Calculate the cement content using the chosen w/c ratio and estimated water content.
        5.  Determine the proportion of fine aggregate to coarse aggregate (e.g., 1:2 by volume).
        6.  Calculate the absolute volumes of cement, water, and aggregates.
        7.  Calculate the weights of cement and aggregates per cubic meter of concrete.
        8.  (Crucially) Conduct trial mixes to verify workability and strength, and adjust proportions as needed.
    *   **Importance of w/c Ratio:** The water-cement ratio is the most critical factor influencing concrete strength and durability. A lower w/c ratio leads to higher compressive strength, reduced permeability, and improved durability. It directly affects the hydration process and the resulting pore structure of the hardened concrete.

4.  **Comparison of Design Philosophies:**
    *   **IS 801 (ASD):** Focuses on ensuring that stresses under service loads are below the *allowable stresses*. This is a **strength-based approach** but is applied to service load conditions. It relies on factors of safety applied to material strengths or stresses.
    *   **Limit State Design (Modern):** Focuses on ensuring that the structure does not reach a "limit state" (e.g., collapse, excessive deflection) under factored loads. It uses **load factors** (to increase service loads) and **material reduction factors** (to reduce material strengths), aiming for a consistent level of safety across different load combinations and material types. It considers both ultimate limit states (strength) and serviceability limit states (deflection, cracking).

5.  **Material Properties for IS 801:**
    *   **Yield Strength ($f_y$):** This is crucial for designing members to resist yielding under tensile or compressive loads, and it dictates the allowable stresses for many design checks, especially for ductile behavior.
    *   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($f_u$):** This is important for checking the failure by rupture of tension members (especially at net sections) and for determining the capacity of connections, particularly in shear and bearing failure modes. It represents the maximum stress the material can withstand before fracture.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Code Applicability:** Always ensure you are using the correct version of the code for your design. Older codes like IS 801:1975 might have been superseded by more recent codes (like IS 800:2007 for steel), but understanding them provides historical context and insight into evolving design practices.
*   **Material-Specific Design:** Each code (IS 811 for aluminum, IS 801 for steel, SP 6-5 for concrete) addresses the unique properties and behaviors of its respective material.
*   **Allowable Stress vs. Limit State:** Be aware of the fundamental difference in design philosophy between older allowable stress codes and modern limit state codes.
*   **Connections are Critical:** Codes provide detailed provisions for connections, as they are often the weakest links in a structure and require careful design for load transfer.
*   **Mix Design for Performance:** SP 6-5 emphasizes that concrete mix design is a performance-driven process aimed at achieving specific strength and durability criteria. The w/c ratio and proper aggregate grading are paramount.
*   **Buckling is a Major Concern:** For slender members in compression (steel, aluminum), buckling stability is a primary design consideration, influencing member selection and permissible stresses.
*   **Durability in Concrete:** For concrete structures, durability in relation to environmental exposure is as critical as strength and needs to be addressed from the mix design stage onwards.
