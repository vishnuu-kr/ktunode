### 1. Introduction to Water Tanks and Yield Line Analysis

*   **Purpose of Water Tanks:** Water tanks are essential structures for storing water for domestic, industrial, and agricultural purposes. Their design must ensure structural integrity, watertightness, and durability.
*   **Types of Water Tanks:**
    *   **Elevated Tanks:** Supported on columns or staging.
    *   **Ground-Level Tanks:** Built directly on the ground.
    *   **Underground Tanks:** Partially or fully buried.
    *   **Capacity:** Small domestic tanks to large municipal reservoirs.
*   **Structural Behavior of Reinforced Concrete Tanks:**
    *   Subjected to hydrostatic pressure (acting outward) and self-weight.
    *   Wall panels act as retaining walls or spanning slabs.
    *   Base slabs can be rigid, flexible, or suspended.
    *   Roofs are typically slabs or shells.
*   **What are Yield Lines?**
    *   Yield lines (or plastic hinges) are lines in a reinforced concrete slab where the concrete has reached its yield strain and the steel reinforcement has reached its yield stress.
    *   Beyond these lines, the slab deforms significantly with little increase in load-carrying capacity.
    *   They represent zones of plastic deformation and allow the slab to redistribute stresses and form a mechanism.
*   **Why Yield Line Analysis?**
    *   A highly effective method for designing reinforced concrete slabs under various loading conditions, particularly for ultimate limit state (ULS) design.
    *   It offers a more realistic assessment of the slab's ultimate load-carrying capacity compared to elastic analysis, which is often inadequate for predicting failure.
    *   It is particularly useful for slabs with complex shapes, boundary conditions, and loading patterns, which are common in water tank structures.
    *   Yield line analysis is based on plasticity theory and the concept of limit equilibrium.

### 2. Principles of Yield Line Analysis

Yield line analysis is based on two fundamental theorems of plasticity:

*   **Lower Bound Theorem (Equilibrium Theorem):** If a state of stress can be found that is in equilibrium with the applied loads and does not violate the yield criterion anywhere in the structure, then the load is a lower bound to the collapse load.
*   **Upper Bound Theorem (Mechanism Theorem):** If a mechanism of failure can be found for a structure under the applied loads, then the load associated with this mechanism is an upper bound to the collapse load.

**Yield line analysis primarily utilizes the Upper Bound Theorem.** However, the **Equilibrium Method** (which we will focus on) is a specific approach that, when applied correctly, can provide the exact collapse load. The equilibrium method is essentially a way to apply the lower bound theorem by finding an equilibrium stress distribution that is consistent with the yield moment capacity along assumed yield lines.

### 3. Yield Line Analysis by Equilibrium Method

The **Equilibrium Method** for yield line analysis involves finding a state of stress that is in equilibrium with the applied loads and satisfies the yield criterion at the yield lines. This essentially means ensuring that the internal moments generated by the stress distribution balance the external moments caused by the loads.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Yield Moment ($M_y$):** The maximum moment a unit width of the slab can resist before yielding. For a reinforced concrete slab, this is determined by the reinforcement and the concrete properties.
    *   For a singly reinforced section, $M_y = As \cdot fy \cdot (d - a/2)$, where $As$ is the area of steel per unit width, $fy$ is the yield strength of steel, $d$ is the effective depth, and $a$ is the depth of the neutral axis.
    *   For doubly reinforced sections, the calculation is more complex but follows similar principles.
*   **Moment Capacity:** The resistance moment per unit length of a yield line. This is typically represented as $M$ or $m$.
*   **Equilibrium:** The sum of forces and moments in the structure must be zero.
*   **Stress Field:** The distribution of stresses within the slab.
*   **Isostatic Lines:** Lines representing the principal stress directions. Yield lines are often assumed to coincide with the lines of principal tension.

**Steps for Yield Line Analysis using Equilibrium Method:**

1.  **Assume a Pattern of Yield Lines:** Based on the geometry of the slab and the loading, hypothesize a plausible pattern of yield lines that would form a collapse mechanism.
2.  **Determine the Moment Capacity ($M$):** Calculate the moment capacity per unit width of the slab for the assumed reinforcement. This is crucial for identifying the resistance along each yield line. Consider the reinforcement direction.
3.  **Establish Equilibrium Equations:**
    *   **Moment Equilibrium:** Apply the principle of moments about a suitable point or axis. This often involves considering the moments caused by the external loads and the internal resisting moments along the yield lines.
    *   **Force Equilibrium:** Ensure that the resultant forces are balanced.
4.  **Relate Loads to Moments:** Express the applied loads in terms of their equivalent moments acting on the slab.
5.  **Solve for the Unknown Load or Moment Capacity:** Solve the equilibrium equations to determine the ultimate load the slab can carry or the required moment capacity for a given load.

**Comparison with Virtual Work Method (Mechanism Method):**

While the problem description focuses on the equilibrium method, it's important to note its relationship and potential distinction from the virtual work method.

*   **Virtual Work Method:** Assumes a collapse mechanism and calculates the external work done by the loads and the internal work done by the yield moments. The collapse load is obtained when external work equals internal work.
*   **Equilibrium Method:** Focuses on finding a stress field in equilibrium with the loads. The yield lines are locations where the yield moment capacity is fully utilized.

In practice, for simply supported slabs, the results from both methods are often identical. However, the equilibrium method can be conceptually more challenging to apply as it requires finding a valid stress distribution. For many practical applications of yield line analysis, the virtual work method is more commonly used due to its straightforward application of mechanism formation.

Let's consider an example where the equilibrium method is particularly illuminating.

### 5. Application to Water Tank Components

Yield line analysis can be applied to various components of water tanks:

*   **Wall Panels:** Rectangular or curved wall panels subjected to hydrostatic pressure and self-weight. The pressure often varies with depth.
    *   **Example:** A rectangular wall panel of a ground-level tank, supported at the base and on vertical columns. The hydrostatic pressure increases with depth.
*   **Base Slabs:**
    *   **Rigid Base Slabs:** Can be analyzed as flat slabs with various boundary conditions (fixed, simply supported, etc.) subjected to the weight of water and soil.
    *   **Flexible Base Slabs (e.g., on piles or elastic foundations):** More complex analysis, but yield line theory can still be a useful tool for ultimate limit state design.
*   **Roofs:** Flat or domed roofs subjected to superimposed loads and sometimes hydrostatic pressure from leakage.
*   **Staging (for elevated tanks):** Columns and beams supporting the tank. While not typically a "slab" in the yield line sense, similar plasticity principles apply to beam and column design.

**Consider a rectangular wall panel of a water tank:**

*   **Dimensions:** Height $H$, width $B$.
*   **Loading:** Hydrostatic pressure $p(z) = \rho g z$, acting horizontally.
*   **Supports:** Assume simply supported at the base ($z=0$) and top ($z=H$), and fixed at vertical edges ($x=0, x=B$).
*   **Reinforcement:** Assumed to provide a uniform yield moment capacity $M$ per unit width.

**Yield Line Pattern Assumption:**
For a simply supported panel with uniform load, yield lines tend to form a grid. For pressure varying with depth, the pattern will be influenced by the pressure distribution.

**Equilibrium Method Application:**

1.  **Assume Yield Lines:** For a rectangular panel with simple supports on two sides, yield lines parallel to the supported sides are a plausible assumption. Let the yield lines be at distances $y_1$ and $y_2$ from the edges.
2.  **Moment Capacity:** $M$.
3.  **Equilibrium:**
    Consider a strip of the wall panel at depth $z$ with width $B$.
    The hydrostatic pressure at this depth is $p = \rho g z$.
    The resultant force on a unit length of this strip is $p \cdot B$.

    Let's consider the horizontal bending of the wall panel.
    Imagine the wall panel as a beam of length $B$, supported at $x=0$ and $x=B$.
    The loading is the hydrostatic pressure $p(z)$.

    The bending moment at a distance $x$ from the edge, due to the pressure $p$ on a strip of height $\Delta z$, is approximately $M(x,z) \approx p \cdot x \cdot (B-x)/2$ (for a simply supported beam).
    The yield moment capacity in this direction is $M$.
    So, $M \ge p \cdot x \cdot (B-x)/2$.

    This is elastic analysis. For yield line analysis by equilibrium, we'd assume yield lines form.
    If yield lines form parallel to the vertical edges at distances $x_1$ and $x_2$, then the moment at these lines will be $M$.

    Consider a section of the wall between two vertical yield lines.
    The load on this section needs to be balanced by the moments $M$.

**A more practical approach for water tank walls:**

Often, water tank walls are designed as:
*   **Annular Plates:** Circular walls supported at the base and top.
*   **Simply Supported or Fixed Rectangular Panels:** If the tank is rectangular.

For a rectangular wall panel of height $H$ and width $B$, simply supported at the top and bottom edges and fixed at the vertical edges.

### 6. Key Concepts and Definitions Summary

*   **Yield Line:** A line in a concrete slab where the reinforcement has reached its yield stress, and the concrete has reached its yield strain, allowing for significant plastic deformation.
*   **Yield Moment ($M$):** The ultimate bending moment capacity per unit width of the slab, determined by the reinforcement and concrete properties.
*   **Equilibrium Method:** A yield line analysis method that seeks to find an equilibrium stress distribution consistent with the applied loads and the yield criterion, with yield lines representing locations of maximum moment.
*   **Plastic Hinge:** A yield line effectively acts as a plastic hinge, allowing rotation at a constant moment capacity.
*   **Collapse Load:** The maximum load a structure can carry before undergoing a large, unrestrained deformation (failure).
*   **Ultimate Limit State (ULS):** The state of a structure where it is no longer capable of resisting the applied loads. Yield line analysis is primarily used for ULS design.
*   **Hydrostatic Pressure:** The pressure exerted by a fluid at rest, which is critical for water tank design.
*   **Moment Capacity Calculation:** Based on the depth of the neutral axis and the lever arm from the resultant compression force to the resultant tension force in the reinforcement.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Explain the fundamental principle behind the equilibrium method of yield line analysis. How does it differ conceptually from the virtual work (mechanism) method?

**Answer:**
The equilibrium method seeks to find a stress distribution that is in equilibrium with the applied loads and does not violate the yield criterion anywhere in the slab. Yield lines are identified as locations where the yield moment capacity is fully utilized. It's rooted in the lower-bound theorem of plasticity.

The virtual work (mechanism) method assumes a collapse mechanism and calculates the external work done by the loads and the internal work done by the yield moments. It's rooted in the upper-bound theorem. While both methods can yield the same collapse load, the equilibrium method focuses on stress distribution, and the virtual work method focuses on mechanism formation and energy dissipation.

**Question 2:**
A simply supported rectangular concrete slab of dimensions $4m \times 6m$ is reinforced to provide a yield moment capacity of $50 kNm/m$ in both directions. Assuming diagonal yield lines, calculate the ultimate uniformly distributed load ($w_{ult}$) the slab can carry using yield line analysis. (Assume the formula for diagonal yield lines in a rectangular slab is required or derive it if possible – note: deriving this precisely requires advanced mechanics. For practice, let's use a common simplified formula for guidance if a specific derivation isn't mandated).

**Guidance for this question (if a formula is needed for practice):**
For a simply supported rectangular slab $L \times B$ with uniform load $w$ and equal moment capacity $M$ in both directions, with diagonal yield lines, a common approximate formula for the collapse load is:
$w_{ult} = \frac{8M}{B^2} \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha^2}\right)$ where $\alpha = L/B$. *Note: This formula's derivation often involves approximations or specific assumptions. For a rigorous equilibrium derivation, more advanced techniques are needed.*

Let's use a more standard approach related to equilibrium for a square slab first, then extrapolate.

**Revised Question 2 (Focus on a simpler case first):**
A simply supported square concrete slab of dimensions $4m \times 4m$ is reinforced to provide a yield moment capacity of $40 kNm/m$ in both directions. Calculate the ultimate uniformly distributed load ($w_{ult}$) the slab can carry assuming yield lines form a cross at the center.

**Answer for Revised Question 2:**
For a simply supported square slab with yield lines forming a cross at the center, the maximum bending moment at the center is $w L^2 / 8$.
The yield moment capacity is $M$.
Therefore, $M = w_{ult} L^2 / 8$.
$w_{ult} = 8M / L^2$
Given: $M = 40 kNm/m$, $L = 4m$.
$w_{ult} = 8 \times 40 kNm/m / (4m)^2$
$w_{ult} = 320 / 16 kNm/m^2$
$w_{ult} = 20 kN/m^2$.

**Question 3:**
Consider a vertical wall panel of a water tank that is $3m$ high and $5m$ wide. It is simply supported at the top and bottom edges and fixed at the vertical edges. The water depth is $3m$, giving a maximum hydrostatic pressure of $p_{max} = 30 kN/m^2$ at the base, acting horizontally. The wall panel has a yield moment capacity of $M = 60 kNm/m$ in the horizontal direction. Using simplified yield line analysis (assume yield lines parallel to the fixed vertical edges at mid-span), estimate the collapse pressure.

**Answer for Question 3:**
The wall panel can be considered as a beam of length $B=5m$ spanning between the vertical supports. The load is the hydrostatic pressure $p(z) = \gamma_w z$. At the bottom, $p_{max} = 30 kN/m^2$.
Assume yield lines parallel to the fixed vertical edges at mid-span ($x = B/2 = 2.5m$).
Consider the strip at the maximum pressure depth.
The pressure is $p_{max}$.
The yield line is at $x = B/2$.
The moment capacity is $M$.

For a fixed-fixed beam of length $B$ with uniform load $p$, the maximum moment is $pB^2/12$.
For a simply supported beam with yield lines at mid-span, the moment at the yield line is $p \times (B/2) \times (B/2) / 2 = pB^2/8$.
The problem states yield lines parallel to fixed edges at mid-span. This implies the edge is fixed, and the yield line is at the mid-span.
The maximum moment for a simply supported beam with a yield line at mid-span is $pB^2/8$.
So, $M = p_{collapse} B^2 / 8$.
$p_{collapse} = 8M / B^2$.
Given $M = 60 kNm/m$ and $B = 5m$.
$p_{collapse} = 8 \times 60 kNm/m / (5m)^2$
$p_{collapse} = 480 / 25 kN/m^2$
$p_{collapse} = 19.2 kN/m^2$.

This is the collapse pressure for a strip at maximum depth. The actual analysis for pressure varying with depth would involve integration. This simplified approach assumes the critical condition at the maximum pressure.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Yield line analysis is an ultimate limit state design method.**
*   **The equilibrium method is based on finding a stress field in equilibrium.**
*   **Assumptions about yield line patterns are crucial and influence the results.**
*   **Moment capacity ($M$) is a key parameter calculated from reinforcement details.**
*   **Water tanks have specific loading conditions (hydrostatic pressure) that vary with depth.**
*   **The geometry of water tank components (walls, bases, roofs) dictates the likely yield line patterns.**
*   **Always check the compatibility of the assumed yield lines with the boundary conditions.**
*   **The equilibrium method can be more rigorous but often more complex to apply than the virtual work method.**
