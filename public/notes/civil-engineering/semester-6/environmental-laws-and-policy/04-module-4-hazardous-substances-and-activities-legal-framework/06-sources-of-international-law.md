# Environmental Laws and Policy: Module 4 - Hazardous Substances and Activities Legal Framework

## Topic: Sources of International Law

This module delves into the foundational elements of international law governing hazardous substances and activities. Understanding the sources of this law is crucial for comprehending how global norms and regulations are established and applied.

---

*   **LO1: Identify and explain the primary sources of international law relevant to hazardous substances and activities.**
*   **LO2: Analyze the hierarchy and interplay of different sources of international law.**
*   **LO3: Discuss the role of customary international law in regulating hazardous substances and activities.**
*   **LO4: Evaluate the significance of treaties and conventions in establishing international legal frameworks for hazardous substances and activities.**
*   **LO5: Explain the concept of general principles of law and their application in environmental law.**

### **1. Introduction: What is International Law?**

International law, also known as public international law, governs the relationships between states and other international actors (such as international organizations). It provides a framework for cooperation, dispute resolution, and the establishment of common standards on a wide range of issues, including the management of hazardous substances and activities.

---

### **2. Primary Sources of International Law (Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice - ICJ)**

Article 38(1) of the Statute of the ICJ is widely considered the authoritative statement on the sources of international law. It lists the following:

*   **(a) International conventions (treaties):**
    *   **Definition:** Formal, legally binding agreements between states (or states and international organizations) in written form and governed by international law.
    *   **Key Concepts:**
        *   **Pacta sunt servanda:** "Agreements must be kept." This fundamental principle underscores the binding nature of treaties.
        *   **Reservations:** A unilateral statement made by a state when signing, ratifying, accepting, approving, or acceding to a treaty, whereby it purports to exclude or to modify the legal effect of certain provisions of the treaty in their application to that state.
        *   **Entry into force:** The point at which a treaty becomes legally binding for the states that have consented to be bound.
    *   **Relevance to Hazardous Substances:** Many critical international agreements concerning hazardous substances are in the form of treaties.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal:** Regulates the movement of hazardous wastes across national borders.
        *   **Rotterdam Convention on the Prior Informed Consent Procedure for Certain Hazardous Chemicals and Pesticides in International Trade:** Promotes shared responsibility and cooperative efforts among parties in the international trade of certain hazardous chemicals.
        *   **Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs):** A global treaty aimed at protecting human health and the environment from POPs.
        *   **Minamata Convention on Mercury:** Addresses human activities that are a source of mercury emissions and releases.

*   **(b) International custom:**
    *   **Definition:** Evidence of a general practice accepted as law. It arises from the consistent and uniform practice of states followed by them from a sense of legal obligation (opinio juris).
    *   **Key Elements:**
        *   **State Practice:** Consistent and widespread actions or omissions by states. This can include legislation, court decisions, diplomatic statements, national policy pronouncements, and actual conduct in international affairs.
        *   **Opinio Juris (Sive Necessitatis):** A belief that the practice is required by law. States must be acting out of a sense of legal duty, not merely out of courtesy, tradition, or political expediency.
    *   **Relevance to Hazardous Substances:** Many foundational principles of environmental law, such as the prohibition of transboundary environmental harm, have emerged as customary international law.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Prohibition of Transboundary Harm:** The principle that states have a responsibility not to cause environmental damage in the territory of other states. This is enshrined in principle 21 of the Stockholm Declaration and principle 2 of the Rio Declaration.
        *   **Duty to Notify and Consult:** The customary obligation of states to notify and consult with other states when activities within their jurisdiction may cause significant transboundary environmental harm.

*   **(c) The general principles of law recognized by civilized nations:**
    *   **Definition:** Fundamental legal principles common to most national legal systems, which can be transposed to the international plane. These are often principles of legal logic or procedural fairness.
    *   **Key Concepts:**
        *   **Good Faith:** States are expected to act in good faith in their international dealings.
        *   **Res judicata:** A matter that has been finally judged by a court and may not be re-litigated.
        *   **Estoppel:** A legal principle that prevents a party from asserting something contrary to what it has previously implied by its conduct or statement.
        *   **Proportionality:** Actions should be proportionate to the objective pursued.
    *   **Relevance to Hazardous Substances:** These principles often underpin the procedural aspects of international environmental law and the interpretation of treaties. They can fill gaps where specific treaty provisions are absent.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   The principle of **"no harm"** can be seen as a general principle when applied in the context of transboundary environmental damage.
        *   The principle of **due diligence** – requiring states to take reasonable measures to prevent harm from activities within their jurisdiction.

### **3. Subsidiary Means for the Determination of Rules of International Law (Article 38(1)(d) of the ICJ Statute)**

These are not sources of law in themselves but help in identifying and interpreting the primary sources.

*   **(d) Judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations:**
    *   **Judicial Decisions:**
        *   **Definition:** Decisions of international courts and tribunals, as well as national courts dealing with international law issues. While not strictly binding precedent (stare decisis is not a doctrine in international law), they carry significant persuasive authority.
        *   **Relevance:** Help to clarify and interpret existing rules of international law and contribute to the development of customary international law.
        *   **Examples:**
            *   **Trail Smelter Arbitration (US v. Canada):** Established the principle of state responsibility for transboundary pollution.
            *   **Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project Case (Hungary v. Slovakia):** Discussed principles of treaty interpretation and state responsibility in the context of international watercourses.
    *   **Scholarly Writings (Teachings of Publicists):**
        *   **Definition:** Writings of recognized legal scholars, often found in books, articles, and commentaries.
        *   **Relevance:** Provide analysis, commentary, and criticism of international law, helping to shape understanding and identify emerging trends. They are particularly important in identifying opinio juris and state practice.
        *   **Examples:** Works by scholars like Laurence Lustig, Edith Brown Weiss, and Philippe Sands are influential in shaping environmental law discourse.

### **4. Hierarchy and Interplay of Sources**

*   **No Strict Hierarchy:** Article 38 does not establish a strict hierarchy among the primary sources (treaties, custom, general principles).
*   **Lex Specialis Derogat Legi Generali:** "Specific law repeals general law." A more specific rule (e.g., a treaty provision) generally takes precedence over a more general rule (e.g., customary international law) when they cover the same subject matter.
*   **Conflict of Norms:**
    *   **Treaties vs. Treaties:** If two treaties to which the same states are parties conflict, the earlier treaty generally prevails, unless the later treaty explicitly states otherwise or is intended to supersede the earlier one. For treaties with different parties, the one with more parties may be seen as more authoritative.
    *   **Treaties vs. Custom:** Generally, treaties are considered lex specialis. However, a treaty can also codify or modify existing customary law. Customary international law can also evolve and potentially supersede treaty provisions if it becomes sufficiently widespread and accepted as law.
    *   **Jus Cogens:** Peremptory norms of general international law from which no derogation is permitted. These are fundamental principles that bind all states, regardless of consent. Examples include the prohibition of genocide, torture, and slavery. While not explicitly listed in Article 38, they are widely recognized as a higher form of international law.
*   **Role of Subsidiary Means:** Judicial decisions and scholarly writings are crucial for interpreting and clarifying the primary sources.

### **5. Emerging Sources and Influences**

While Article 38 remains the cornerstone, other factors influence the development of international law, particularly in rapidly evolving fields like environmental law:

*   **Soft Law:**
    *   **Definition:** Non-binding instruments that contribute to the development of international law. This includes declarations, resolutions of international organizations (like UN General Assembly resolutions), action plans, and guidelines.
    *   **Relevance:** Can pave the way for future treaties and contribute to the formation of customary international law by demonstrating state practice and opinio juris.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Stockholm Declaration (1972):** A landmark declaration that articulated key principles of international environmental law.
        *   **Rio Declaration on Environment and Development (1992):** A set of principles guiding sustainable development.
        *   **UN General Assembly Resolutions:** Can reflect the consensus of the international community.
*   **Unilateral Declarations by States:** Formal statements made by states that can create legal obligations for themselves.
*   **Decisions of International Organizations:** While generally not sources of law themselves, decisions of international organizations can influence legal development and interpret existing law.

---

### **6. Importance in Hazardous Substances and Activities**

The various sources of international law are essential for:

*   **Establishing Global Standards:** Treaties and customary law set baselines for managing hazardous substances and preventing transboundary harm.
*   **Facilitating Cooperation:** International agreements foster collaboration among states on issues like chemical safety, waste management, and pollution control.
*   **Holding States Accountable:** Principles of state responsibility, derived from custom and treaties, enable states to be held accountable for environmental damage.
*   **Providing Legal Certainty:** Clear legal frameworks reduce uncertainty and promote predictable behavior in international environmental governance.

### **Practice Questions/Exercises:**

1.  **Identify and briefly explain the three primary sources of international law listed in Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute.**
    *   **Answer:** The primary sources are:
        *   **International Conventions (Treaties):** Formal, written agreements between states.
        *   **International Custom:** State practice accepted as law (requiring state practice and opinio juris).
        *   **General Principles of Law:** Principles recognized in national legal systems and applicable internationally.

2.  **What are the two essential components required for the formation of customary international law? Provide an example of a principle that has become customary international law in environmental law.**
    *   **Answer:** The two components are **state practice** (consistent and widespread actions) and **opinio juris** (a belief that the practice is legally obligatory). An example is the **prohibition of transboundary environmental harm**.

3.  **Explain the role of judicial decisions and scholarly writings in international law, referencing Article 38(1)(d).**
    *   **Answer:** Judicial decisions and scholarly writings are **subsidiary means** for determining rules of international law. They help in clarifying, interpreting, and evidencing existing rules and contribute to the development of law, although they are not primary sources themselves.

4.  **Discuss the significance of the Rotterdam Convention in the context of sources of international law.**
    *   **Answer:** The Rotterdam Convention is an example of a **treaty** (international convention). It demonstrates how states use treaties to create legally binding obligations to regulate the international trade of certain hazardous chemicals through the Prior Informed Consent (PIC) procedure, thereby contributing to global environmental protection.

5.  **What is "soft law" and how does it relate to the formation of international law, particularly in environmental matters?**
    *   **Answer:** Soft law refers to non-binding instruments like declarations and resolutions. While not legally binding, they can influence the development of hard law (treaties) and contribute to the formation of customary international law by reflecting state practice and opinio juris, acting as precursors to formal legal commitments.

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Article 38 of the ICJ Statute is the cornerstone for identifying sources of international law.**
*   **Treaties are formal, legally binding agreements and are crucial for regulating hazardous substances.**
*   **Customary international law arises from consistent state practice coupled with a belief in its legal necessity (opinio juris).**
*   **General principles of law bridge gaps and provide underlying legal logic.**
*   **Judicial decisions and scholarly writings are subsidiary means that help interpret and evidence the law.**
*   **There is no rigid hierarchy, but specific rules generally prevail over general ones (lex specialis).**
*   **Jus Cogens norms are peremptory and cannot be derogated from.**
*   **Soft law plays an increasingly important role in shaping international environmental governance.**
