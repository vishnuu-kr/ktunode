# APPLIED EARTH SYSTEMS: Module 2: Weathering

## Topic: Weathering - Relevance, Influence of and on Earth Systems, Types, and Controlling Factors

---

### Learning Outcomes:

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Explain the relevance of weathering to Earth systems.**
*   **Describe how weathering influences and is influenced by other Earth systems (atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, lithosphere).**
*   **Identify and explain the main types of weathering (physical, chemical, biological).**
*   **Analyze the key factors that control the rate and type of weathering.**

---

## 1. Relevance of Weathering to Earth Systems

Weathering is the **breakdown and alteration of rocks and minerals at or near the Earth's surface** through direct contact with the atmosphere, hydrosphere, and biosphere. It's a fundamental process that shapes the Earth's surface and has far-reaching implications for various Earth systems.

### Key Concepts:

*   **Geomorphology:** Weathering is a primary driver of landform creation and modification.
*   **Soil Formation:** Weathering is the initial stage in the development of soil, providing the raw materials for plant growth and supporting terrestrial ecosystems.
*   **Resource Management:** Weathering influences the availability of mineral resources and can impact the stability of engineered structures (e.g., dams, roads).
*   **Climate Feedback Loops:** Weathering can influence atmospheric composition, which in turn affects climate.

### Examples:

*   **Grand Canyon:** The immense scale of the Grand Canyon is a testament to the power of weathering (primarily erosion facilitated by weathering) over millions of years.
*   **Bauxite Deposits:** Many economically important mineral deposits, like bauxite (used to produce aluminum), are formed through intense chemical weathering of parent rocks.
*   **Landslides and Rockfalls:** Unweathered or poorly weathered rock formations are more susceptible to mass wasting events.

## 2. Influence of and on Earth Systems

Weathering is not an isolated process; it's intricately linked to and interacts with the other major Earth systems.

### 2.1. Influence of Weathering ON Other Earth Systems:

*   **Lithosphere:**
    *   **Surface Topography:** Weathering breaks down rocks, leading to the formation of valleys, canyons, plains, and hills.
    *   **Regolith Formation:** Weathering creates a layer of loose, fragmented rock and mineral particles called regolith, which is the precursor to soil.
    *   **Mineral Transformation:** Chemical weathering alters the composition of minerals, creating new minerals like clays.
*   **Hydrosphere:**
    *   **Water Chemistry:** Dissolved ions released by weathering affect the salinity and chemical composition of rivers, lakes, and oceans.
    *   **Sediment Transport:** Weathered material (sediments) is transported by water, contributing to river deltas, beaches, and ocean floor deposits.
    *   **Groundwater:** Weathering creates pores and fractures in rocks, increasing permeability and influencing groundwater flow and storage.
*   **Atmosphere:**
    *   **Carbon Cycle:** Chemical weathering, particularly silicate weathering, plays a crucial role in regulating atmospheric CO2 levels over geological timescales. The process consumes CO2 from the atmosphere.
    *   **Dust and Aerosols:** Fine weathered particles can become airborne, influencing atmospheric optics and acting as cloud condensation nuclei.
*   **Biosphere:**
    *   **Nutrient Availability:** Weathering releases essential nutrients (e.g., potassium, phosphorus) from rocks, making them available for plants.
    *   **Habitat Creation:** Weathered surfaces and the resulting soils provide habitats for diverse terrestrial organisms.
    *   **Plant Growth:** Plants can influence weathering through root growth (physical) and the release of organic acids (chemical).

### 2.2. Influence of Other Earth Systems ON Weathering:

*   **Atmosphere:**
    *   **Temperature:** Affects the rate of chemical reactions and the effectiveness of physical weathering processes like freeze-thaw.
    *   **Precipitation:** Provides the water necessary for chemical weathering and frost wedging.
    *   **Atmospheric Gases:** Presence of CO2 (forming carbonic acid), O2 (oxidation), and SO2 (acid rain) drives chemical weathering.
*   **Hydrosphere:**
    *   **Water as a Solvent:** Water is the primary medium for chemical reactions and the transport of dissolved ions.
    *   **Flow and Abrasion:** Flowing water can physically break down rocks through abrasion and hydraulic action.
    *   **Ice:** Freeze-thaw cycles (frost wedging) are significant physical weathering processes driven by the presence of water and freezing temperatures.
*   **Biosphere:**
    *   **Biological Activity:** Roots penetrate rocks, lichen produce acids, and microorganisms can catalyze or inhibit weathering reactions.
    *   **Organic Matter:** The decomposition of organic matter produces acids that enhance chemical weathering.
*   **Lithosphere:**
    *   **Rock Type and Mineralogy:** The inherent resistance of different rocks and minerals to weathering dictates the rate and type of breakdown. For example, quartz is more resistant than feldspar.
    *   **Structure and Texture:** Jointing, faulting, and grain size within rocks influence their susceptibility to weathering.

## 3. Types of Weathering

Weathering can be broadly categorized into three main types:

### 3.1. Physical Weathering (Mechanical Weathering)

The breakdown of rocks into smaller pieces **without changing their chemical composition**.

#### Key Concepts:

*   **Fragmentation:** Increasing the surface area of a rock.
*   **Mechanical Forces:** Driven by physical processes.

#### Types and Controlling Factors:

*   **Frost Wedging (or Freeze-Thaw Weathering):**
    *   **Process:** Water seeps into cracks in rocks, freezes and expands (by ~9%), exerting pressure. Repeated freezing and thawing widens and deepens the cracks, eventually breaking the rock.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of water, temperature fluctuations around freezing point, presence of pre-existing cracks.
    *   **Examples:** Common in mountainous regions with diurnal freeze-thaw cycles; "talus slopes" or "scree slopes" are formed by accumulation of frost-wedged debris.

*   **Thermal Expansion and Contraction (Exfoliation/Pressure Release):**
    *   **Process:** Rocks expand when heated and contract when cooled. This repeated cycle causes stress within the rock, leading to the outer layers flaking off. Pressure release occurs when overlying rock is removed by erosion, allowing the underlying rock to expand and fracture (often forming onion-like layers).
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Significant diurnal or seasonal temperature variations, removal of overlying rock.
    *   **Examples:** Onion-like peeling of granite in deserts (exfoliation domes), Yosemite National Park's El Capitan.

*   **Abrasion:**
    *   **Process:** Rocks are worn down by friction and impact with other rocks, sand, or sediment carried by wind, water, or ice.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of transporting agents (wind, water, glaciers), density of abrasive particles.
    *   **Examples:** Smooth, polished surfaces on river stones; sandblasted rocks in deserts; glacial striations on bedrock.

*   **Wetting and Drying:**
    *   **Process:** The repeated swelling and shrinking of clay minerals upon wetting and drying can break down rocks.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of clay minerals, cyclical wetting and drying.
    *   **Examples:** Breakdown of shale and mudstone.

### 3.2. Chemical Weathering

The **breakdown of rocks through chemical reactions**, altering their mineral composition.

#### Key Concepts:

*   **Dissolution:** The process where minerals dissolve in water.
*   **Oxidation:** Reaction with oxygen, often changing the color of minerals.
*   **Hydrolysis:** Reaction with water, breaking down mineral structures.
*   **Carbonation:** Reaction with carbonic acid (formed from CO2 and water).

#### Types and Controlling Factors:

*   **Dissolution:**
    *   **Process:** Soluble minerals like halite (rock salt) and calcite (in limestone) dissolve directly in water, especially slightly acidic water.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Solubility of the mineral, presence of water, acidity of water.
    *   **Examples:** Formation of sinkholes and caves in limestone landscapes (karst topography).

*   **Oxidation:**
    *   **Process:** Minerals containing iron react with oxygen, forming iron oxides and hydroxides. This often results in a reddish-brown or yellowish-brown color.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of iron-bearing minerals, presence of oxygen and water.
    *   **Examples:** Rusting of iron-rich rocks and minerals (e.g., mafic minerals like olivine and pyroxene), giving a reddish hue to weathered surfaces.

*   **Hydrolysis:**
    *   **Process:** Water reacts with minerals, breaking down their chemical bonds and forming new compounds, often clays. This is particularly effective on silicate minerals.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of water, mineral composition (especially silicates).
    *   **Examples:** Weathering of feldspar to clay minerals like kaolinite. This is a major process in the formation of soils.

*   **Carbonation:**
    *   **Process:** Carbon dioxide from the atmosphere dissolves in rainwater to form weak carbonic acid ($H_2CO_3$). This acid reacts with carbonate rocks like limestone (calcite) to form soluble calcium bicarbonate, which is then dissolved and carried away.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Amount of atmospheric CO2, amount of rainfall, presence of carbonate rocks.
    *   **Examples:** Formation of caves and distinctive landforms in limestone regions. This is a significant process in the long-term regulation of atmospheric CO2.

### 3.3. Biological Weathering

The **breakdown of rocks and minerals by living organisms**.

#### Key Concepts:

*   **Physical Action:** Mechanical disruption by organisms.
*   **Chemical Action:** Release of acids or chelating agents by organisms.

#### Types and Controlling Factors:

*   **Root Wedging:**
    *   **Process:** Plant roots grow into cracks and joints in rocks. As the roots grow, they exert pressure, widening the cracks (similar to frost wedging).
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of plants, availability of cracks.
    *   **Examples:** Tree roots breaking apart pavement or rocks.

*   **Acid Secretion:**
    *   **Process:** Organisms like lichens, mosses, and some bacteria produce organic acids or chelating agents that can dissolve minerals directly from the rock surface.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of specific organisms, rock mineralogy.
    *   **Examples:** Lichens growing on rocks can initiate chemical weathering, breaking down the rock substrate for their own nutrient needs.

*   **Burrowing Animals:**
    *   **Process:** Animals that burrow into soil and rock can expose fresh surfaces to weathering processes.
    *   **Controlling Factors:** Presence of burrowing animals.
    *   **Examples:** Rabbits or prairie dogs excavating burrows can destabilize soil and expose underlying rock.

## 4. Controlling Factors of Weathering

The rate and type of weathering are influenced by a complex interplay of factors:

### 4.1. Climate

*   **Temperature:**
    *   **Warm, Wet Climates:** Accelerate chemical weathering (e.g., tropical rainforests). Higher temperatures increase reaction rates.
    *   **Cold Climates:** Promote physical weathering (e.g., frost wedging).
    *   **Arid Climates:** Can have significant thermal expansion/contraction and abrasion, with limited chemical weathering due to lack of moisture.
*   **Precipitation:**
    *   **Amount:** More precipitation generally leads to more chemical weathering and facilitates physical weathering processes.
    *   **Acidity:** Acid rain (due to atmospheric pollutants like SO2 and NOx) significantly enhances chemical weathering.

### 4.2. Rock Type and Mineralogy

*   **Resistance:** Rocks composed of more resistant minerals (e.g., quartz) weather more slowly than those composed of less resistant minerals (e.g., olivine, pyroxene).
*   **Chemical Bonds:** Minerals with weaker chemical bonds weather faster.
*   **Solubility:** Minerals that are more soluble in water will weather through dissolution more readily.
*   **Examples:** Granites weather more slowly than basalts; limestone weathers readily via carbonation.

### 4.3. Surface Area and Rock Structure

*   **Surface Area to Volume Ratio:** The more fractured or porous a rock is, the greater its surface area exposed to weathering agents, leading to faster breakdown.
*   **Joints and Fractures:** Pre-existing cracks provide pathways for water and ice, accelerating physical weathering and increasing the surface area for chemical attack.
*   **Grain Size and Shape:** Fine-grained rocks might weather differently than coarse-grained rocks.

### 4.4. Time

*   Weathering is a **slow, continuous process**. Over longer periods, even resistant rocks will be significantly weathered. The amount of weathering is directly related to the duration of exposure.

### 4.5. Biological Activity

*   As discussed in biological weathering, the presence of plants, microorganisms, and animals can significantly influence weathering rates.

---

## Important Points to Remember:

*   Weathering is the **first step in the rock cycle**, breaking down rocks into sediments.
*   It is essential for **soil formation**, supporting terrestrial life.
*   Weathering plays a critical role in **regulating atmospheric CO2** through silicate weathering.
*   Physical weathering **increases the surface area** available for chemical weathering.
*   Chemical weathering **changes the composition of rocks**.
*   **Warm, wet climates** are generally conducive to faster weathering rates, especially chemical weathering.
*   The **resistance of the parent rock** is a primary factor in how quickly it weathers.

## Answers to Practice Questions:

**Multiple Choice:**

1.  **b) Oxidation** (Oxidation is a chemical weathering process.)
2.  **b) Dissolution by carbonic acid** (Carbonic acid dissolves calcite in limestone.)
3.  **c) Tropical, wet** (Warm temperatures and abundant moisture accelerate chemical reactions.)

**Short Answer:**

4.  **Influence on the hydrosphere:** Weathering releases dissolved ions into rivers, lakes, and oceans, affecting their chemical composition and salinity. Weathered material (sediments) is transported by water, shaping coastlines and ocean floor deposits. Weathering also creates pores and fractures in rocks, which influences groundwater flow and storage.
5.  **Frost wedging:** Water seeps into cracks in rocks. When the temperature drops below freezing, the water freezes and expands by approximately 9%. This expansion exerts pressure on the rock. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing cause the crack to widen and deepen, eventually breaking the rock apart. Conditions required include the presence of water and temperature fluctuations around the freezing point.
6.  **Influence of biosphere on lithosphere:** The biosphere influences the lithosphere through biological weathering. Plant roots can physically break apart rocks by growing into cracks (root wedging). Organisms like lichens and bacteria produce acids that chemically break down rock surfaces. Burrowing animals can also expose rock to weathering.
7.  **Quartz vs. Feldspar resistance:** Quartz (SiO2) has a strong, stable crystal structure with strong silicon-oxygen bonds. Feldspar, while also a silicate, has a more complex structure containing elements like aluminum and alkali metals (e.g., potassium, sodium) that are more susceptible to attack by water and acids during hydrolysis and carbonation.

**Application/Analysis:**

8.  **Dominant weathering:** In this scenario, **frost wedging** would be expected to be the most dominant weathering process due to the temperature fluctuations around 0°C and the presence of rainfall.
    **Observed features:** You would likely observe **angular rock fragments** at the base of cliffs or rock slopes, forming **talus slopes** or **scree slopes**. Cracks and fissures in the rocks would be enlarged, and the rocks might show signs of breaking apart along bedding planes or joints.
9.  **Granite in different environments:**
    *   **Hot, humid tropical rainforest:** The dominant weathering would be **chemical weathering**, specifically **hydrolysis** and **carbonation**. The warm temperatures and abundant moisture accelerate these reactions. You would expect the granite to show signs of alteration, such as **feldspar minerals being converted into clay minerals**, leading to a softer, crumbly surface. The rock might appear discolored due to oxidation of iron-bearing minerals.
    *   **Cold, dry desert:** The dominant weathering processes would be **physical weathering**, primarily **thermal expansion and contraction** (due to large diurnal temperature swings) and **abrasion** by windblown sand. Chemical weathering would be significantly slower due to the lack of moisture, though any moisture present could be slightly acidic, causing minor chemical attack. You would expect the granite to be **more intact**, possibly showing **exfoliation (onion-like peeling)** on exposed surfaces due to thermal stress, and potentially smoother surfaces from sand abrasion. The overall rate of weathering would be much slower than in the humid tropics.
