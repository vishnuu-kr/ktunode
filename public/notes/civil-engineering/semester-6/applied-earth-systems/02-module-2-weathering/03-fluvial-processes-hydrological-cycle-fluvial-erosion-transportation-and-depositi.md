# Applied Earth Systems: Module 2 - Weathering

## Topic: Fluvial Processes

### Introduction to Fluvial Processes

Fluvial processes refer to the actions of rivers and streams on the Earth's surface. These processes are fundamental to shaping landscapes through erosion, transportation, and deposition of sediment. Understanding fluvial processes is crucial for fields like hydrology, geomorphology, and environmental management.

---

### Learning Outcome 1: Understand the hydrological cycle and its role in fluvial processes.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Hydrological Cycle (Water Cycle):** The continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. It is the driving force behind fluvial processes.
    *   **Evaporation:** The process by which water changes from a liquid to a gas or vapor. Primarily from oceans, lakes, and rivers.
    *   **Transpiration:** The process by which moisture is carried through plants from roots to small pores on the underside of leaves, where it changes to vapor and is released to the atmosphere.
    *   **Evapotranspiration:** The combined process of evaporation and transpiration.
    *   **Condensation:** The process by which water vapor in the air is changed into liquid water, forming clouds.
    *   **Precipitation:** Water released from clouds in the form of rain, freezing rain, sleet, snow, or hail. This is the direct source of water for rivers.
    *   **Infiltration:** The process by which water on the ground surface enters the soil.
    *   **Percolation:** The downward movement of water through soil and rock.
    *   **Surface Runoff:** Water that flows over the land surface when the ground is saturated or when precipitation rates exceed infiltration rates. This is the water that feeds rivers.
    *   **Groundwater Flow:** Water that moves through the subsurface, eventually feeding rivers and oceans.

*   **Role of the Hydrological Cycle in Fluvial Processes:**
    *   **Water Source:** Precipitation is the primary source of water for rivers. The amount and intensity of precipitation directly influence river discharge and erosive power.
    *   **Driving Force:** The gravitational pull on water, which drives flow from higher elevations to lower elevations, is a direct consequence of the hydrological cycle.
    *   **Sediment Transport Medium:** Water acts as the medium for eroding, transporting, and depositing sediments.
    *   **Weathering Enhancement:** The presence of water facilitates chemical weathering processes that break down rocks, providing material for fluvial erosion.

**Examples:**

*   A heavy rainfall event increases surface runoff, leading to higher river discharge and greater erosive potential in a stream.
*   Snowmelt in mountainous regions is a significant contributor to river flow during spring and summer, driving fluvial processes in downstream areas.
*   Evaporation from a large lake reduces the water available for downstream river flow.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The hydrological cycle is a closed system, meaning water is constantly recycled.
*   Fluvial processes are directly powered by the gravitational pull on water that has been lifted into the atmosphere and returned to the Earth's surface via precipitation.
*   Variations in precipitation patterns (amount, intensity, seasonality) have a profound impact on fluvial geomorphology.

### Learning Outcome 2: Explain fluvial erosion.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Fluvial Erosion:** The process by which rivers remove rock and soil from their channels and surrounding land. This is a key process in landscape evolution.
*   **Competence:** The maximum size of sediment particle a river can transport. It is controlled by the velocity of the water.
*   **Capacity:** The total amount of sediment a river can transport. It is controlled by both velocity and discharge.
*   **Factors Affecting Fluvial Erosion:**
    *   **Velocity:** Higher velocity means more kinetic energy, leading to increased erosive power. Velocity is influenced by gradient, discharge, and channel shape.
    *   **Discharge:** The volume of water flowing in a river per unit time (e.g., cubic meters per second). Higher discharge generally means more erosive power and greater capacity to transport sediment.
    *   **Gradient (Slope):** Steeper gradients result in higher water velocities and thus greater erosive power.
    *   **Channel Shape and Roughness:** Narrow, deep channels are more efficient and have higher velocities. Roughness (from rocks, vegetation) slows down water and reduces erosion.
    *   **Load:** The amount and type of sediment being carried by the river. A higher sediment load can increase erosive power by acting as an abrasive.
    *   **Rock Type and Structure:** The resistance of the bedrock and underlying material to erosion plays a significant role. Softer rocks erode more easily.

*   **Types of Fluvial Erosion:**
    *   **Corrasion (Abrasion):** The grinding and scraping of rock fragments (carried by the river) against the channel bed and walls. This is the most significant form of river erosion.
        *   *Example:* Boulders and pebbles tumbling along the riverbed act like sandpaper, wearing down the channel.
    *   **Hydraulic Action:** The erosive force of water itself. Fast-moving water can pry loose particles from the channel bed and banks, especially in cracks and joints. Air can be compressed into these cracks, increasing the pressure and widening them.
        *   *Example:* The force of turbulent water can dislodge loose sediment from river banks.
    *   **Attrition:** The process by which sediment particles carried by a river collide with each other, becoming smaller, more rounded, and smoother.
        *   *Example:* Boulders and cobbles carried in a river are reduced in size and become more rounded as they are constantly ground against each other.
    *   **Solution (Corrosion):** The dissolution of soluble rocks (like limestone) by the slightly acidic water in the river.
        *   *Example:* Carbonic acid in rainwater can dissolve calcium carbonate in limestone, leading to the formation of caves and karst features.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Erosion by a river is a combination of the force of the water and the abrasive action of the sediment it carries.
*   Competence and capacity are critical concepts for understanding a river's ability to move sediment.
*   The gradient and discharge of a river are primary determinants of its erosive power.

### Learning Outcome 3: Describe fluvial transportation and deposition.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Fluvial Transportation:** The movement of eroded material (sediment) by a river.
*   **Fluvial Deposition:** The settling and accumulation of sediment that has been transported by a river. This occurs when the river loses energy.

*   **Modes of Sediment Transportation (The River Load):**
    *   **Solution:** Dissolved material that is carried invisibly within the water.
        *   *Example:* Dissolved salts like sodium chloride and calcium bicarbonate are transported in solution.
    *   **Suspension:** Fine sediment particles (silt and clay) that are held up within the water column by turbulence.
        *   *Example:* Muddy water is an example of sediment in suspension.
    *   **Saltation:** Larger particles (sand and gravel) that are bounced along the riverbed. They are lifted by the current, travel a short distance, and then fall back to the bed, dislodging other particles.
        *   *Example:* Small pebbles skipping along the bottom of a stream.
    *   **Traction:** The rolling or dragging of the largest and heaviest sediment particles (boulders, cobbles) along the riverbed.
        *   *Example:* Large rocks being pushed and rolled downstream by a powerful current.

*   **Factors Affecting Transportation:**
    *   **Velocity:** Higher velocity allows for the transport of larger and heavier particles and greater quantities of sediment.
    *   **Discharge:** Higher discharge means more water to carry sediment and greater turbulence.
    *   **Sediment Characteristics:** Size, shape, and density of sediment particles influence how they are transported.

*   **Factors Affecting Deposition:**
    *   **Decreased Velocity:** When a river's velocity decreases, its energy drops, and it can no longer carry all of its sediment load. The heaviest particles are deposited first.
    *   **Decreased Discharge:** A reduction in the volume of water flowing can also lead to deposition.
    *   **Channel Widening:** As a river widens, its velocity often decreases due to increased friction with the banks, leading to deposition.
    *   **Obstructions:** Man-made structures (dams, bridges) or natural obstacles (e.g., fallen trees) can slow down the water and cause deposition.
    *   **Confluence:** Where two rivers meet, the combined flow might have a different velocity or sediment load, leading to deposition.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Rivers transport sediment in four main ways: solution, suspension, saltation, and traction.
*   The mode of transport depends on the size of the sediment and the river's velocity.
*   Deposition occurs when a river loses energy, typically due to a decrease in velocity or discharge.

### Learning Outcome 4: Identify and describe common fluvial landforms.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

Fluvial landforms are features created by the processes of erosion, transportation, and deposition by rivers.

**1. Landforms of Erosion:**

*   **V-shaped Valleys:**
    *   **Description:** Steep-sided valleys with a profile resembling the letter 'V'. Formed by a combination of vertical erosion (downcutting) by the river and mass wasting (slope retreat) on the valley sides.
    *   **Example:** The Grand Canyon, Yosemite Valley.
    *   **Formation:** Occurs in the upper course of a river where the gradient is steep and the river has high erosive power.
*   **Gorges/Canyons:**
    *   **Description:** Very deep, narrow V-shaped valleys with near-vertical sides. Often cut into resistant rock.
    *   **Example:** The Gorges du Verdon (France), the Copper Canyon (Mexico).
    *   **Formation:** Result of intense vertical erosion by rivers in areas with resistant rock and significant uplift.
*   **Waterfalls:**
    *   **Description:** A sudden drop in the river channel where the water plunges over a steep drop or cliff.
    *   **Example:** Niagara Falls, Victoria Falls.
    *   **Formation:** Typically form where a river flows over a band of harder rock overlying softer rock. The softer rock is eroded more quickly by hydraulic action and abrasion, creating a step. The overhang of harder rock eventually collapses, causing the waterfall to retreat upstream.
*   **Potholes:**
    *   **Description:** Cylindrical or bowl-shaped depressions in the riverbed, formed by the swirling action of water and the abrasive action of pebbles.
    *   **Example:** Found in many fast-flowing streams with rocky beds.
    *   **Formation:** When water swirls around a depression in the bedrock, it creates a vortex that can entrain small pebbles. These pebbles then grind against the bedrock, widening and deepening the depression.
*   **River Terraces:**
    *   **Description:** Flat, step-like surfaces left behind as a river cuts down into its valley floor. They represent former floodplains.
    *   **Example:** Many major river valleys exhibit terraces on either side.
    *   **Formation:** Can be formed by:
        *   **Entrenchment:** A river with a high sediment load might deposit a wide floodplain. If the river's base level falls (e.g., due to tectonic uplift or sea-level fall), the river will incise into its floodplain, leaving the old floodplain surface as a terrace.
        *   **Change in Discharge/Sediment Load:** Variations in flow or sediment can cause a river to widen its channel, deposit sediment, and then later downcut, leaving terraces.

**2. Landforms of Transportation and Deposition:**

*   **Meanders:**
    *   **Description:** Sinuous, winding curves in a river channel.
    *   **Example:** The River Thames in England, the Mississippi River.
    *   **Formation:**
        *   **Formation of initial bend:** Often starts with an irregularity in the riverbed.
        *   **Erosion on the outer bank (cut bank):** Higher velocity on the outer bend causes erosion (hydraulic action, abrasion).
        *   **Deposition on the inner bank (point bar):** Lower velocity on the inner bend causes deposition of sediment.
        *   **Migration:** Over time, the outer bank erodes and the inner bank deposits, causing the meander to migrate laterally across the floodplain.
*   **Oxbow Lakes:**
    *   **Description:** Crescent-shaped lakes formed when a meander loop is cut off from the main river channel.
    *   **Example:** Found in floodplains of meandering rivers worldwide.
    *   **Formation:** As meanders migrate and become more pronounced, the neck of the meander narrows. During a flood, the river may cut across the neck, creating a shorter, straighter channel. Sediment deposition then seals off the old meander loop, forming an oxbow lake.
*   **Floodplains:**
    *   **Description:** Flat, low-lying areas adjacent to a river that are subject to flooding.
    *   **Example:** The Nile Delta, the Amazon Basin.
    *   **Formation:** Formed by the lateral erosion and deposition of meanders. During floods, the river overflows its banks, depositing fine sediment (alluvium) across the floodplain.
*   **Levees:**
    *   **Description:** Natural raised banks along the sides of a river channel.
    *   **Example:** Found along major rivers like the Mississippi.
    *   **Formation:** Built up by deposition of sediment. During floods, when the river overtops its banks, the velocity of the water decreases rapidly, causing coarser sediment to be deposited immediately adjacent to the channel, building up the levee.
*   **Deltas:**
    *   **Description:** Depositional landforms formed at the mouth of a river where it enters a body of standing water (ocean, lake, sea).
    *   **Example:** The Nile Delta, the Ganges Delta.
    *   **Formation:** As the river enters the larger body of water, its velocity drops drastically, causing it to deposit its sediment load. These deposits build up over time, creating a triangular or fan-shaped landform.
*   **Alluvial Fans:**
    *   **Description:** Fan-shaped deposits of sediment formed where a fast-flowing stream emerges from a narrow valley onto a broader, flatter plain.
    *   **Example:** Found at the foot of mountain ranges, such as in arid and semi-arid regions.
    *   **Formation:** Similar to deltas, but formed on land. The stream's velocity decreases rapidly as it leaves the confined valley, leading to deposition. The sediment is spread out in a fan shape.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Fluvial landforms are a direct result of the interplay between erosion and deposition.
*   The location within the river system (upper, middle, lower course) influences the dominant processes and resulting landforms.
*   Understanding the processes of meander formation and migration is key to understanding floodplains and oxbow lakes.

### Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:**
Which of the following is NOT a primary process in the hydrological cycle that directly contributes water to rivers?
a) Evaporation
b) Precipitation
c) Transpiration
d) Condensation

**Question 2:**
Describe the process of hydraulic action and provide an example of where it might be observed in a river channel.

**Question 3:**
Explain the difference between a river's competence and its capacity. What factors influence each?

**Question 4:**
How does a point bar form within a meandering river?

**Question 5:**
If a river is flowing over a band of hard rock followed by a band of softer rock, what type of erosional landform is likely to develop? Briefly explain its formation.

**Question 6:**
List the four main ways that sediment is transported by a river and give an example of the type of sediment typically carried in each mode.

**Question 7:**
What is an oxbow lake, and how is it formed from a meandering river?

---

### Answers to Practice Questions:

**Answer 1:**
a) Evaporation - Evaporation returns water to the atmosphere, not directly to rivers as a source of flow. Precipitation is the direct source.

**Answer 2:**
Hydraulic action is the erosive force of water itself. It occurs when fast-moving water, often in turbulent flow, exerts pressure on the riverbed and banks. This pressure can dislodge particles from the channel, especially where there are cracks or joints in the rock. An example is the force of a strong current eroding the bank of a river, causing it to slump or collapse.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Competence** refers to the *maximum size* of sediment particle a river can transport. It is primarily controlled by the river's **velocity**. A faster river has higher competence and can move larger, heavier particles.
*   **Capacity** refers to the *total amount* of sediment a river can transport. It is controlled by both the river's **velocity** and its **discharge**. A river with higher discharge and velocity generally has a greater capacity to carry sediment.

**Answer 4:**
A point bar forms on the inner bank of a meander. On the inner bank, the river's velocity is slower due to less friction. This reduced velocity causes the river to lose energy, and it deposits the sediment (like sand and gravel) that it was carrying. This deposition builds up a gently sloping accumulation of sediment, known as a point bar.

**Answer 5:**
A **waterfall** is likely to develop. It forms because the river erodes the softer rock more quickly than the harder rock. This differential erosion creates a step or cliff. The force of the water and abrasion of sediment carried by the water then erodes the base of the waterfall, causing the overhanging harder rock to eventually collapse, making the waterfall retreat upstream over time.

**Answer 6:**
The four main modes of sediment transport are:
1.  **Solution:** Dissolved minerals (e.g., salts, calcium bicarbonate).
2.  **Suspension:** Fine particles like silt and clay (e.g., muddy water).
3.  **Saltation:** Medium-sized particles like sand and small gravel that bounce along the bed (e.g., skipping pebbles).
4.  **Traction:** Large, heavy particles like boulders and cobbles that are rolled or dragged along the bed (e.g., large rocks being pushed by a strong current).

**Answer 7:**
An oxbow lake is a crescent-shaped body of water that is formed when a meander loop of a river is cut off from the main channel. This typically happens when the neck of a meander becomes progressively narrower due to erosion on the outer banks and deposition on the inner banks. During a flood event, the river may break through the narrow neck, creating a shorter, straighter course. Sediment is then deposited at the entrances to the abandoned meander loop, effectively sealing it off and forming an oxbow lake.
