# Transportation Engineering Lab: Module 2 - Specific Gravity and Water Absorption Test

## Topic: Shape Test - Angularity Number

### 1. Introduction to Aggregate Shape and its Importance

Aggregates, the granular materials used in concrete and asphalt mixtures, are characterized by various physical properties that significantly influence the performance of the resulting pavement. Among these properties, **shape** plays a crucial role.

*   **Definition:** Aggregate shape refers to the general form and surface texture of the individual particles. It is influenced by the source of the aggregate (e.g., crushed rock, gravel, sand) and the crushing process.
*   **Importance in Pavement Engineering:**
    *   **Workability:** Well-shaped, rounded aggregates generally lead to more workable concrete mixtures, requiring less water and effort for placement and compaction.
    *   **Strength:** Angular and flaky/elongated aggregates can create voids and interlock better, potentially leading to stronger mixtures, but can also negatively impact workability.
    *   **Durability:** The shape can influence the resistance of the aggregate to weathering and abrasion.
    *   **Stability:** Angularity contributes to better mechanical interlocking, enhancing the stability and load-carrying capacity of asphalt mixtures.
    *   **Void Content:** The shape and texture of aggregates affect the amount of paste or binder needed to fill the voids between them. More angular aggregates tend to create larger voids.

### 2. Types of Aggregate Shapes

Aggregates can be broadly classified into the following shapes:

*   **Rounded:** Typically found in riverbeds or glacial deposits, these particles have smooth, rounded edges and surfaces due to natural erosion.
    *   *Example:* Beach sand, rounded gravel.
*   **Cubical/Blocky:** Particles that are approximately equal in all dimensions, with roughly cubical shapes.
    *   *Example:* Well-crushed coarse aggregates with minimal flaky or elongated particles.
*   **Angular:** Particles with sharp edges and corners, usually produced by crushing rock.
    *   *Example:* Crushed stone.
*   **Flaky:** Particles that are significantly thinner in one dimension compared to the other two.
    *   *Example:* Mica schist fragments.
*   **Elongated:** Particles that are significantly longer in one dimension compared to the other two.
    *   *Example:* Certain types of crushed granite.
*   **Flaky and Elongated:** Particles exhibiting both flaky and elongated characteristics.

### 3. The Angularity Number Test

The Angularity Number test is a laboratory procedure used to quantify the degree of angularity of coarse aggregates. It measures the void content in a compacted sample of aggregate, which is directly related to its shape.

*   **Purpose:** To assess the relative angularity of coarse aggregate samples. A lower angularity number indicates more rounded or cubical aggregates, while a higher number signifies more angular or irregular shapes.
*   **Principle:** The test relies on the fact that angular aggregates, due to their rough surfaces and sharp edges, pack less efficiently than rounded or cubical aggregates, resulting in a higher void content when compacted in a standard manner. The void content is determined by filling these voids with a standard packing material (e.g., fine aggregate).

#### 3.1. Apparatus Required

*   **Cylindrical container:** A standard size steel cylinder with specific dimensions (as per IS 2386 Part I). Common sizes are 150 mm diameter and 170 mm height or 100 mm diameter and 110 mm height.
*   **Tamper:** A steel tamper with a specific diameter and weight, used for compacting the aggregate.
*   **Calibrated scoop:** For measuring the fine aggregate.
*   **Balance:** To weigh the aggregate and fine aggregate.
*   **Oven:** For drying the aggregate.
*   **Sieve:** For grading the aggregate sample.

#### 3.2. Materials Required

*   **Coarse Aggregate Sample:** The aggregate to be tested, typically passing through a 10 mm sieve and retained on a 4.75 mm sieve (size range as per IS code). It should be clean, dry, and free from dust.
*   **Fine Aggregate (Standard Sand):** A clean, dry, and well-graded standard sand (typically passing 2.36 mm sieve and retained on 1.18 mm sieve) to fill the voids in the coarse aggregate.

#### 3.3. Test Procedure

1.  **Preparation of Coarse Aggregate:**
    *   Obtain a representative sample of coarse aggregate.
    *   Ensure the aggregate is clean and dry. If not, dry it in an oven at 105-110°C until a constant weight is achieved.
    *   Sieve the aggregate to obtain the desired size fraction (e.g., passing 10 mm sieve and retained on 4.75 mm sieve).

2.  **Filling the Container with Coarse Aggregate:**
    *   Fill the cylindrical container in three layers, each approximately one-third of the container's volume.
    *   Compact each layer by 35 strokes of the tamper. The tamper should be dropped from a height of approximately 15 mm above the surface of the aggregate layer. Distribute the strokes uniformly over the surface.
    *   After compacting the third layer, strike off the excess aggregate from the top of the container using a straight edge.

3.  **Determining the Volume of Voids:**
    *   Fill the voids in the compacted coarse aggregate with the standard fine aggregate.
    *   Use a calibrated scoop to add the fine aggregate, ensuring it fills all the voids without excessive disturbance to the coarse aggregate.
    *   Add the fine aggregate until the surface of the fine aggregate is flush with the top of the coarse aggregate in the cylinder.
    *   Gently tap the side of the cylinder to help the fine aggregate settle.
    *   Strike off any excess fine aggregate with a straight edge.

4.  **Weighing the Fine Aggregate:**
    *   Carefully remove the compacted coarse aggregate from the cylinder.
    *   Transfer the fine aggregate that filled the voids into a container.
    *   Weigh the fine aggregate to the nearest gram. Let this weight be $W_f$.

5.  **Determining the Specific Gravity of Fine Aggregate:**
    *   Determine the specific gravity of the standard fine aggregate (usually it's a known value, e.g., 2.65 or 2.70). Let this be $G_f$.

6.  **Calculating the Volume of Fine Aggregate:**
    *   The volume of the fine aggregate that filled the voids in the coarse aggregate can be calculated using its weight and specific gravity:
        $$V_f = \frac{W_f}{G_f \times \rho_w}$$
        Where:
        *   $V_f$ is the volume of fine aggregate in the voids.
        *   $W_f$ is the weight of fine aggregate in the voids.
        *   $G_f$ is the specific gravity of the fine aggregate.
        *   $\rho_w$ is the density of water (1000 kg/m³ or 1 g/cm³).

7.  **Calculating the Volume of Coarse Aggregate:**
    *   Determine the bulk volume of the cylinder. Let this be $V_c$.
    *   The volume occupied by the coarse aggregate particles themselves (solid volume) can be calculated by subtracting the volume of voids ($V_f$) from the bulk volume of the cylinder.
    *   However, the standard procedure is to calculate the volume occupied by the coarse aggregate *including* the voids it creates:
        $$V_{bulk\_agg} = V_c$$
    *   The volume of solid coarse aggregate can be found by first determining the weight of coarse aggregate used ($W_{agg}$) and its bulk specific gravity ($G_{bulk}$).
        $$V_{solid\_agg} = \frac{W_{agg}}{G_{bulk} \times \rho_w}$$
        But for the angularity number test, we directly use the volume of voids relative to the volume of the cylinder.

8.  **Calculating the Percentage of Voids in the Compacted Aggregate:**
    *   The percentage of voids in the compacted coarse aggregate ($V\%$) is calculated as:
        $$V\% = \frac{V_f}{V_c} \times 100$$

9.  **Calculating the Angularity Number:**
    *   The Angularity Number ($A$) is defined as the percentage of voids in the compacted aggregate minus a value representing the void content of perfectly rounded aggregate under the same conditions. For well-compacted, perfectly rounded aggregates, the void content is typically around 33%.
    *   Therefore, the Angularity Number is calculated as:
        $$A = V\% - 33$$

#### 3.4. Example Calculation

**Given:**

*   Volume of cylindrical container ($V_c$) = 5 liters = 0.005 m³
*   Weight of fine aggregate used to fill voids ($W_f$) = 3.5 kg
*   Specific Gravity of fine aggregate ($G_f$) = 2.70
*   Density of water ($\rho_w$) = 1000 kg/m³

**Steps:**

1.  **Calculate the volume of fine aggregate ($V_f$):**
    $$V_f = \frac{W_f}{G_f \times \rho_w} = \frac{3.5 \text{ kg}}{2.70 \times 1000 \text{ kg/m³}} = 0.001296 \text{ m³}$$

2.  **Calculate the percentage of voids ($V\%$):**
    $$V\% = \frac{V_f}{V_c} \times 100 = \frac{0.001296 \text{ m³}}{0.005 \text{ m³}} \times 100 = 25.92\%$$

3.  **Calculate the Angularity Number ($A$):**
    $$A = V\% - 33 = 25.92 - 33 = -7.08$$

**Interpretation:** Since the angularity number cannot be negative, the formula is often expressed in a way that acknowledges a baseline void content. A more practical way to think about it is the *excess* void content.

**Revised Angularity Number Calculation (common practice):**

The percentage of voids in the *total volume* of the sample is calculated.
$$V\% = \frac{\text{Volume of Voids}}{\text{Volume of Cylinder}} \times 100$$

The Angularity Number is the *difference* between the percentage of voids in the sample and the assumed percentage of voids in perfectly rounded aggregate (typically 33%).

If $V\%$ is *less* than 33%, it implies the aggregate is more rounded or cubical than the reference. The angularity number is typically considered to be 0 in such cases or sometimes reported as negative. However, for practical purposes, the intent is to quantify *angularity*, so we focus on the excess void content.

Let's re-examine the calculation:
If $V\% = 25.92\%$, this means the voids are *less* than 33%. This indicates a relatively rounded or cubical aggregate. The Angularity Number would be considered close to 0 or potentially negative.

**Let's consider a case with more angular aggregate:**
Suppose $W_f = 5.0$ kg.
$V_f = \frac{5.0 \text{ kg}}{2.70 \times 1000 \text{ kg/m³}} = 0.001852 \text{ m³}$
$V\% = \frac{0.001852 \text{ m³}}{0.005 \text{ m³}} \times 100 = 37.04\%$

$A = V\% - 33 = 37.04 - 33 = 4.04$

**Interpretation for $A = 4.04$:** This positive value indicates that the aggregate is more angular than the reference material, and the degree of angularity is represented by approximately 4.04.

#### 3.5. Typical Values of Angularity Number

*   **Rounded Aggregates:** Angularity number is generally **0 to 4**.
*   **Cubical Aggregates:** Angularity number is generally **4 to 8**.
*   **Angular Aggregates:** Angularity number is generally **8 to 12**.
*   **Highly Angular/Irregular Aggregates:** Angularity number can be **greater than 12**.

**IMPORTANT:** The specific values and their interpretation depend on the exact test procedure and the reference standard (e.g., IS code, BS code, ASTM). The 33% void content is an empirical value.

**Question 5 (Calculation):** A coarse aggregate sample (passing 10 mm, retained on 4.75 mm) is tested for angularity. A 5-liter cylindrical container is used. After compaction, 3.2 kg of standard sand (specific gravity = 2.7) is used to fill the voids. Calculate the percentage of voids and the angularity number of the aggregate.

---

**Answers to Practice Questions:**

**Answer 1:** c) To quantify the degree of angularity of coarse aggregates.

**Answer 2:** d) Rounded. Rounded aggregates pack more efficiently, leading to fewer voids compared to angular or flaky/elongated shapes.

**Answer 3:** c) -5. Angularity Number = Percentage of Voids - 33% = 28% - 33% = -5. (Note: In practice, if the number is negative, it often implies the aggregate is more rounded than the reference, and the angularity number might be reported as 0 or simply interpreted as having low angularity).

**Answer 4:** b) To fill the voids within the compacted coarse aggregate.

**Answer 5 (Calculation):**
*   Volume of cylindrical container ($V_c$) = 5 liters = 0.005 m³
*   Weight of fine aggregate ($W_f$) = 3.2 kg
*   Specific Gravity of fine aggregate ($G_f$) = 2.7
*   Density of water ($\rho_w$) = 1000 kg/m³

1.  **Calculate the volume of fine aggregate ($V_f$):**
    $$V_f = \frac{W_f}{G_f \times \rho_w} = \frac{3.2 \text{ kg}}{2.7 \times 1000 \text{ kg/m³}} = 0.001185 \text{ m³}$$

2.  **Calculate the percentage of voids ($V\%$):**
    $$V\% = \frac{V_f}{V_c} \times 100 = \frac{0.001185 \text{ m³}}{0.005 \text{ m³}} \times 100 = 23.7\%$$

3.  **Calculate the Angularity Number ($A$):**
    $$A = V\% - 33 = 23.7 - 33 = -9.3$$

**Result:** The percentage of voids is 23.7%, and the angularity number is -9.3. This indicates a highly rounded or cubical aggregate.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Aggregate Shape Matters:** The shape of aggregate particles significantly impacts the performance of asphalt and concrete mixtures.
*   **Angularity Number Quantifies Angularity:** It's a measure of how sharp and irregular the aggregate particles are.
*   **Void Content is Key:** The test indirectly measures void content, which is directly linked to particle shape and texture.
*   **Standardization is Crucial:** The test requires specific equipment dimensions, compaction effort, and a standard fine aggregate for reproducible results.
*   **Interpretation:** Lower angularity numbers suggest rounded or cubical aggregates, while higher numbers indicate more angular particles.
*   **Negative Angularity Numbers:** Indicate that the aggregate has fewer voids than the reference material, meaning it's more rounded than the assumed "perfectly rounded" aggregate.
*   **Application:** Used for selecting aggregates for specific pavement applications (e.g., dense-graded mixes often prefer cubical/angular aggregates for interlocking and stability, while certain concrete mixes may benefit from rounded aggregates for workability).
