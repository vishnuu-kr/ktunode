# Transportation Engineering Lab: Module 2 - Specific Gravity and Water Absorption Test

## Topic: Los Angeles Abrasion Test

This module delves into essential tests for characterizing aggregate properties, crucial for designing durable and stable pavement structures. The Los Angeles Abrasion Test specifically evaluates the resistance of aggregates to crushing, degradation, and disintegration under impact and abrasive forces.

### **2. Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Aggregate:** Granular material, such as gravel, crushed stone, or sand, used in asphaltic concrete, hydraulic cement concrete, and road construction.
*   **Abrasion:** The process of wearing away or rubbing away by mechanical action.
*   **Degradation:** The breakdown of aggregate particles into smaller pieces due to mechanical forces.
*   **Disintegration:** The process of breaking down into constituent parts or fragments.
*   **Los Angeles Abrasion Value (LAV):** A numerical value representing the percentage of aggregate mass lost due to abrasion and impact in the Los Angeles Abrasion machine. A *lower* LAV indicates *higher* resistance to degradation.
*   **Impact:** A forceful collision or shock.
*   **Abrasive Action:** The grinding or scraping effect caused by the aggregate particles rubbing against each other and the steel cylinder.

---

### **3. Purpose and Significance of the Los Angeles Abrasion Test**

The Los Angeles Abrasion Test is a critical quality control test for aggregates used in road construction. Its primary purposes are:

*   **Assessing Durability:** It simulates the abrasive and impact forces that aggregates experience during handling, placement, compaction, and under traffic loads on a road surface.
*   **Evaluating Resistance to Degradation:** It measures how well the aggregate can withstand crushing and breakdown into smaller particles, which can negatively impact pavement performance.
*   **Predicting Service Life:** Aggregates with a lower LAV are generally more durable and contribute to longer-lasting pavements, reducing maintenance costs.
*   **Ensuring Pavement Stability:** Excessive degradation of aggregates can lead to a loss of inter-particle friction and interlocking, compromising the structural integrity and stability of the pavement.
*   **Meeting Specification Requirements:** Road construction projects have specific LAD requirements for different pavement layers (e.g., base course, wearing course) to ensure the pavement performs as intended.

**Example:**
Consider two types of crushed stone for a road's wearing course. Stone A has an LAV of 25%, while Stone B has an LAV of 45%. Stone A is expected to perform better under traffic, showing less wear and tear, and thus contributing to a more durable road surface compared to Stone B.

### **4. Equipment and Apparatus Required**

The following equipment is essential for conducting the Los Angeles Abrasion Test:

*   **Los Angeles Abrasion Machine:** A rotating steel cylinder (drum) with a shelf.
    *   **Dimensions of the drum:** Typically 700 mm (27.5 inches) in diameter and 500 mm (20 inches) in length.
    *   **Shelf:** A mild steel shelf, 89 mm (3.5 inches) wide and extending across the internal length of the cylinder, is bolted to the inside. It is positioned to project outward and downward from the top toward the bottom of the cylinder.
*   **Abrasive Charge:** Standardized hardened steel spheres.
    *   **Size and Weight:** The number and weight of the steel spheres depend on the aggregate size fraction being tested (as specified in standards like ASTM D4124 or IS 2386 Part IV). For example, for aggregate passing 19.0 mm (3/4 inch) sieve and retained on 9.5 mm (3/8 inch) sieve, a standard charge is typically 11 spheres weighing 5 kg (11 lb).
*   **Weighing Balance:** Accurate to at least 0.1% of the mass of the sample.
*   **Sieves:** A set of standard sieves to classify the aggregate sample into specific size fractions (e.g., 19.0 mm, 12.5 mm, 9.5 mm).
*   **Oven:** For drying the aggregate sample to a constant weight.
*   **Sample Container:** For holding and transferring the aggregate.
*   **Brush/Broom:** For cleaning the drum and sample.

---

### **5. Step-by-Step Procedure for the Los Angeles Abrasion Test**

The procedure generally follows standard specifications (e.g., ASTM D4124 or IS 2386 Part IV). Here's a generalized procedure:

1.  **Sample Preparation:**
    *   Obtain a representative sample of the aggregate to be tested.
    *   Sieve the aggregate into the specified size fraction(s) as per the relevant standard. The most common fractions are:
        *   **Grading A:** Passing 19.0 mm (3/4 inch) sieve and retained on 9.5 mm (3/8 inch) sieve.
        *   **Grading B:** Passing 12.5 mm (1/2 inch) sieve and retained on 9.5 mm (3/8 inch) sieve.
        *   **Grading C:** Passing 12.5 mm (1/2 inch) sieve and retained on 6.3 mm (1/4 inch) sieve.
        *   **Grading D:** Passing 9.5 mm (3/8 inch) sieve and retained on 4.75 mm (No. 4) sieve.
    *   *The mass of the sample for each grading is also specified by the standard.* For example, for Grading A, the sample mass is typically 5000 ± 10 grams.

2.  **Oven Drying:**
    *   Dry the prepared aggregate sample in an oven at a temperature of 100°C to 110°C (212°F to 230°F) until it reaches a constant weight.
    *   Allow the sample to cool to room temperature.

3.  **Initial Weighing:**
    *   Accurately weigh the dried aggregate sample. Let this be $W_1$.

4.  **Loading the Abrasion Machine:**
    *   Place the weighed aggregate sample into the Los Angeles Abrasion machine drum.
    *   Add the specified number and weight of steel spheres (abrasive charge) to the drum. *The number and weight of spheres depend on the aggregate grading.*

5.  **Running the Test:**
    *   Securely close the drum of the Los Angeles Abrasion machine.
    *   Rotate the drum at a speed of 30 to 33 revolutions per minute (rpm).
    *   The number of revolutions is also specified by the standard and depends on the aggregate grading. For example, for Gradings A, B, C, and D, the test is typically run for 500 revolutions.

6.  **Unloading and Sieving:**
    *   After the specified number of revolutions, stop the machine.
    *   Remove the aggregate sample and the steel spheres from the drum.
    *   Separate the aggregate from the steel spheres.
    *   Sieve the aggregate sample using a sieve with the same aperture size as the lower limit of the initial grading (e.g., 1.70 mm (No. 10) sieve for Gradings A, B, C, and D).

7.  **Final Weighing:**
    *   Dry the aggregate that passes the specified sieve in an oven at 100°C to 110°C until a constant weight is achieved.
    *   Allow the sample to cool to room temperature.
    *   Accurately weigh the dried aggregate that passed the sieve. Let this be $W_2$.

### **6. Calculation of the Los Angeles Abrasion Value (LAV)**

The Los Angeles Abrasion Value (LAV) is calculated as the percentage of the original aggregate mass that passes the specified sieve after the test.

**Formula:**

$LAV = \frac{W_2}{W_1} \times 100$

Where:
*   $W_1$ = Initial weight of the oven-dried sample (grams).
*   $W_2$ = Final weight of the oven-dried sample that passed the specified sieve (grams).

**Example Calculation:**
Suppose an initial aggregate sample (Grading A, 19.0 mm to 9.5 mm) weighs $W_1 = 5000$ grams after drying. After the test and sieving over the 1.70 mm sieve, the material passing the sieve weighs $W_2 = 850$ grams after drying.

$LAV = \frac{850 \text{ g}}{5000 \text{ g}} \times 100 = 17.0\%$

---

### **7. Interpretation of the Los Angeles Abrasion Value (LAV)**

The LAV provides a measure of the aggregate's resistance to degradation. Lower values indicate better quality aggregates. Typical specifications for different pavement layers are as follows (these values can vary based on local standards and project requirements):

| Pavement Layer               | Typical Maximum LAV (%) |
| :--------------------------- | :---------------------- |
| Wearing Course (Surface)     | 25 - 35                 |
| Binder Course / Base Course  | 30 - 45                 |
| Sub-base Course              | 40 - 55                 |

**Important Notes on Interpretation:**

*   **Lower LAV is better:** A lower LAV means the aggregate is less likely to break down under traffic and weathering.
*   **Application Specific:** The acceptable LAV varies depending on the intended use of the aggregate in the pavement structure. Wearing courses experience the most direct traffic impact and abrasion, hence requiring more resistant aggregates.
*   **Source Variability:** Different aggregate sources will yield different LAV values, even for the same type of rock.
*   **Combined Grading:** For a specific pavement mix, the overall grading of the aggregate used is also critical, not just individual size fractions.

**Example Interpretation:**
If a wearing course requires a maximum LAV of 30%, an aggregate with an LAV of 25% would be considered suitable, while an aggregate with an LAV of 35% would likely be rejected for this application.

### **8. Limitations and Potential Sources of Error**

While a valuable test, the Los Angeles Abrasion Test has limitations and potential sources of error that need to be considered:

**Limitations:**

*   **Simulated Conditions:** The test simulates wear, but it doesn't perfectly replicate all the complex conditions experienced by aggregates in a real pavement (e.g., freeze-thaw cycles, chemical attack, long-term weathering).
*   **Grading Influence:** The results can be influenced by the grading of the aggregate sample.
*   **Type of Degradation:** It primarily measures wear due to abrasion and impact, not necessarily other forms of degradation like crushing strength or soundness.

**Potential Sources of Error:**

*   **Inaccurate Sample Preparation:** Incorrect sieving or non-representative sampling can lead to misleading results.
*   **Incomplete Drying:** Insufficient drying of the sample before initial or final weighing will result in a lower, incorrect LAV.
*   **Loss of Fines During Sieving:** Careless sieving can lead to a loss of fine material that should have passed the sieve, artificially lowering the $W_2$ and thus the LAV.
*   **Incorrect Number/Weight of Steel Spheres:** Using the wrong number or weight of spheres as per the standard will alter the test conditions.
*   **Machine Speed/Revolutions:** Inaccurate calibration of the machine's speed or revolution counter can affect the outcome.
*   **Improper Cleaning:** Residual fine material from previous tests in the drum can contaminate the sample.
*   **Contamination with Steel Spheres:** If fine material adheres to the steel spheres and is not properly removed, it can lead to an underestimation of the loss.

### **10. Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**
**(c)** To assess the resistance of aggregates to crushing, degradation, and disintegration.

**Answer 2:**
$LAV = \frac{W_2}{W_1} \times 100 = \frac{520 \text{ g}}{4000 \text{ g}} \times 100 = 13.0\%$
The correct answer is **(b)**.

**Answer 3:**
**(d)** 25%
A lower LAV indicates greater resistance to degradation, making it more suitable for a wearing course.

**Answer 4:**
**(b)** The steel shelf and the abrasive charge. The steel shelf causes impact, and the abrasive charge (steel spheres) along with the aggregate causes abrasion and impact.

**Answer 5:**
Two potential sources of error that could lead to a lower-than-actual LAV result include:
1.  **Improper cleaning of the drum:** Residual fines from previous tests could be included in the current sample, increasing $W_2$ and decreasing the calculated LAV.
2.  **Loss of fines during sieving (beyond what should pass):** Careless sieving might cause more material to pass the specified sieve than intended, artificially reducing $W_2$ and thus the LAV.
3.  **Incomplete drying of the sample:** If $W_1$ or $W_2$ is weighed before the sample is completely dry, the recorded weight will be higher than the actual dry weight, leading to a lower LAV calculation.

### **11. Important Points to Remember**

*   **LAV is inversely proportional to quality:** Lower LAV = better quality aggregate for wear resistance.
*   **Always follow the specific standard:** Refer to the relevant standard (ASTM, IS, etc.) for precise sample sizes, number of revolutions, and abrasive charge details.
*   **Accurate weighing is crucial:** Use a calibrated balance for all weighings.
*   **Proper drying is essential:** Ensure aggregates are oven-dried to a constant weight before initial and final weighings.
*   **Aggregate grading matters:** The test is performed on specific size fractions, and the LAV interpretation depends on the intended use of that fraction.
*   **The test simulates mechanical wear:** It's a key indicator of durability under traffic and handling.
*   **Report results clearly:** Always report the LAV along with the aggregate source and intended application.
