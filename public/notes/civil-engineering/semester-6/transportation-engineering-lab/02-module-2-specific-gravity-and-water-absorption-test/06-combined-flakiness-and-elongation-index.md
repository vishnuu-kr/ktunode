# Transportation Engineering Lab: Module 2 - Aggregates & Bitumen Tests

## Topic: Combined Flakiness and Elongation Index

---

### 1. Introduction to Aggregate Shape Characteristics

Aggregates, being the backbone of most road construction materials (like asphalt concrete and cement concrete), play a crucial role in the overall performance of the pavement. The shape of aggregate particles significantly influences the properties of the mix, including workability, density, strength, and durability. Two important shape characteristics are **flakiness** and **elongation**.

*   **Flakiness:** Refers to the tendency of aggregate particles to be flat or thin in relation to their width.
*   **Elongation:** Refers to the tendency of aggregate particles to be elongated or long in relation to their thickness.

While often tested and reported individually, understanding their combined effect is also important for a holistic assessment of aggregate quality. This topic focuses on the **combined flakiness and elongation index** test.

### 3. Key Concepts and Definitions

#### 3.1. Flakiness

*   **Definition:** An aggregate particle is considered flaky if its least dimension (thickness) is less than 0.6 times its average dimension (length and width).
*   **Significance:** Flaky particles have a large surface area relative to their volume. They can lead to:
    *   Poor workability in mixes.
    *   Increased voids, potentially leading to weaker mixes.
    *   Increased susceptibility to crushing under traffic loads.
    *   Difficulty in compaction.

#### 3.2. Elongation

*   **Definition:** An aggregate particle is considered elongated if its largest dimension (length) is greater than 1.8 times its average dimension (width and thickness).
*   **Significance:** Elongated particles can also lead to:
    *   Poor interlocking and stability in the mix.
    *   Difficulty in compaction.
    *   Potential for breakage under traffic loads due to stress concentrations.

#### 3.3. Combined Flakiness and Elongation Index

*   **Definition:** The combined flakiness and elongation index is a measure that quantifies the proportion of aggregate particles that are either flaky or elongated, or both, in a given sample. It is typically expressed as a percentage by weight of the total sample.

*   **Importance:** While individual indices highlight specific shape issues, the combined index provides a more comprehensive assessment of the overall suitability of an aggregate for use in pavement construction. It accounts for particles that might be problematic due to flatness, lengthiness, or a combination of both.

### 4. Test Procedure (Based on IS 2386 Part 1 / BS 812 Part 105.1)

The combined flakiness and elongation index test is typically performed on aggregate samples that have been sieved into specific size fractions.

**Objective:** To determine the percentage by weight of flaky and/or elongated particles in a sample of aggregate retained on a 6.3 mm sieve.

**Equipment:**

1.  **Weighing balance:** With sufficient capacity and accuracy.
2.  **Set of sieves:** Primarily 6.3 mm, and other sieves as per the specification (e.g., 9.5 mm, 12.5 mm, 19 mm, 25 mm).
3.  **Thickness gauge (for flakiness):** A metal gauge with slots of specific widths (e.g., 1.5 mm, 3.2 mm, 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm, 14 mm, 19 mm, 25 mm, 32 mm, 38 mm, 45 mm, 54 mm).
4.  **Length gauge (for elongation):** A metal gauge with slots of specific lengths (e.g., 2 x average dimension).
5.  **Spatula or scoop:** For handling aggregate.
6.  **Sample splitter (optional):** For obtaining representative sub-samples.
7.  **Drying oven:** To dry the aggregate sample to a constant weight.

**Procedure:**

1.  **Sample Preparation:**
    *   Obtain a representative sample of the aggregate.
    *   If the sample contains material passing the 6.3 mm sieve, remove it by sieving and discard. The test is performed on the material retained on the 6.3 mm sieve.
    *   Divide the aggregate sample into fractions retained between consecutive sieves, as specified (e.g., 6.3 mm to 9.5 mm, 9.5 mm to 12.5 mm, 12.5 mm to 19 mm, etc.).
    *   For each size fraction, take a representative sample by quartering or using a sample splitter, such that the sample weight is approximately 500 particles. If a precise number of particles cannot be obtained easily, aim for a weight that is a significant portion of the fraction.

2.  **Testing for Flakiness:**
    *   For each size fraction, carefully examine each particle individually.
    *   Using the appropriate thickness gauge (which has slots of varying widths), determine if a particle is flaky.
    *   A particle is considered flaky if it passes through a slot in the thickness gauge which is wider than 1.8 times its thickness, and its thickness is less than 0.6 times its average width and length.
    *   Alternatively, and more practically in the lab, you check if the particle passes through the slot corresponding to 0.6 times its average dimension. If it passes, and its thickness is less than this value, it's flaky.
    *   **Simpler Method:** For each particle, measure its thickness (smallest dimension). Compare this thickness to the *average width and length* of that particle. If the thickness is less than 0.6 times the average width and length, it is flaky.

3.  **Testing for Elongation:**
    *   For each size fraction, examine each particle individually.
    *   Using the appropriate length gauge (which has slots of varying lengths), determine if a particle is elongated.
    *   A particle is considered elongated if it passes through a slot in the length gauge which is longer than 1.8 times its average width and thickness, and its length is greater than 1.8 times its average width and thickness.
    *   **Simpler Method:** For each particle, measure its length (largest dimension). Compare this length to the *average width and thickness* of that particle. If the length is greater than 1.8 times the average width and thickness, it is elongated.

4.  **Combined Flakiness and Elongation Assessment:**
    *   The standard procedure for **combined flakiness and elongation index** (as per IS 2386 Part 1) often refers to a more direct measurement using a standard set of slotted gauges.
    *   **Gauge Dimensions:** The test uses a set of slotted gauges. For a particle to be considered flaky *or* elongated, it must fit through a specific slot *or* not fit through a specific slot based on its dimensions.
    *   **The Combined Test (Simplified Explanation):**
        *   For each size fraction, take a sample of 500 particles.
        *   **Flaky Particles:** A particle is flaky if its thickness is less than 0.6 times its average dimension (width and length). The test gauge has slots where the width of the slot corresponds to 1.8 times the thickness. If the particle passes through a slot that is 1.8 times its thickness, it's not flaky. The inverse is that if it *doesn't* pass through a slot that is 0.6 times its average dimension, it is considered flaky.
        *   **Elongated Particles:** A particle is elongated if its length is greater than 1.8 times its average dimension (width and thickness). The test gauge has slots where the width of the slot corresponds to 1.8 times its average dimension (width and thickness). If the particle *doesn't* pass through a slot that is 1.8 times its average dimension, it is considered elongated.
        *   **Combined Approach:** The test often involves using a set of gauges designed to identify particles that are *either* flaky *or* elongated. For a given size range, a specific gauge is used. A particle is considered *neither* flaky *nor* elongated if it can be passed through the gauge, and its dimensions are within acceptable limits. If it fails to pass through, it's deemed problematic (either flaky or elongated).
        *   **IS 2386 Method:** The standard specifies using a set of gauges. For a particle to be *neither* flaky *nor* elongated, it must pass through the gauge if its thickness is not less than 0.6 times its average dimension AND its length is not greater than 1.8 times its average dimension.
        *   The test typically involves passing particles through slots.
            *   **To identify flaky particles:** A particle is flaky if its thickness is less than 0.6 times its average dimension. The gauge is designed such that if a particle passes through a slot whose width is 1.8 times its thickness, it is not considered flaky. The *test* actually checks if it *fails to pass* through a slot that is 0.6 times its average dimension.
            *   **To identify elongated particles:** A particle is elongated if its length is greater than 1.8 times its average dimension. The gauge is designed such that if a particle passes through a slot whose length is 1.8 times its average dimension, it is not considered elongated. The *test* actually checks if it *fails to pass* through a slot that is 1.8 times its average dimension.
        *   **Combined Measurement:** The common procedure for the combined index involves using a single gauge. For a specific size fraction, a gauge is used. A particle is considered *neither* flaky *nor* elongated if it passes through the gauge and its dimensions meet the criteria. If it fails to pass, it is counted as a combined flaky and/or elongated particle. The exact dimensions of these gauges are critical and are specified in the relevant standard.

5.  **Counting and Weighing:**
    *   For each size fraction, count the total number of particles ($N$).
    *   Count the number of flaky particles ($F$) and the number of elongated particles ($E$). In the combined test, you count the number of particles that fail to pass the designated gauge ($P_{fail}$).
    *   Weigh the total sample for that fraction ($W_{total}$).
    *   Weigh the flaky particles ($W_F$) and the elongated particles ($W_E$). In the combined test, weigh the particles that failed to pass the gauge ($W_{fail}$).

6.  **Calculation:**
    *   The combined flakiness and elongation index is calculated as the percentage by weight of particles that are either flaky or elongated (or both).

    $$ \text{Combined Flakiness and Elongation Index} (\%) = \frac{\text{Weight of flaky and/or elongated particles}}{\text{Total weight of sample}} \times 100 $$

    *   Using the combined gauge method (counting particles that fail to pass):
        $$ \text{Combined Flakiness and Elongation Index} (\%) = \frac{W_{fail}}{W_{total}} \times 100 $$

    *   If testing individually and summing (less common for "combined index" in a single report, but conceptually relevant):
        $$ \text{Flakiness Index} (\%) = \frac{W_F}{W_{total}} \times 100 $$
        $$ \text{Elongation Index} (\%) = \frac{W_E}{W_{total}} \times 100 $$
        The combined index is usually reported as a single value derived from the direct measurement.

---

### 5. Interpretation of Results

*   **Limiting Values:** Standard specifications (like IRC or MoRTH specifications) provide limiting values for the combined flakiness and elongation index, depending on the type of pavement layer and traffic conditions.
    *   For example, a typical limit for base and sub-base courses might be around **15-25%**.
    *   For surface courses, the limit might be stricter, perhaps **10-15%**.
*   **Significance of High Index:** A high combined flakiness and elongation index indicates that the aggregate has a poor shape, which can negatively impact the performance of the pavement.
*   **Suitability:** Aggregates with high flakiness and elongation indices are generally considered unsuitable for use in critical pavement layers, especially wearing courses, where good interlocking and stability are paramount. They might be acceptable for lower layers or if the percentage is within specified limits.

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Sample Size:** Using a sufficient number of particles (around 500 per size fraction) is crucial for statistically reliable results.
*   **Representative Sample:** Ensure the sample tested accurately represents the bulk material.
*   **Accurate Measurement:** Precise measurement of particle dimensions and careful use of the gauges are essential.
*   **Size Fractions:** The test is performed on specific size fractions, and the results are usually reported for the aggregate retained on the 6.3 mm sieve.
*   **Standard Specifications:** Always refer to the relevant Indian Standards (IS 2386 Part 1) or other applicable codes (like BS 812) for exact gauge dimensions and procedures.
*   **Workability vs. Shape:** While shape affects workability, it's not the only factor. Other factors like grading and particle texture also play a role.
*   **Cost vs. Quality:** Aggregates with better shape characteristics often come at a higher cost due to processing. A balance between cost and performance is usually sought.

### 7. Practice Questions & Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define flakiness index and elongation index. Why is the shape of aggregates important in road construction?

**Answer:**
*   **Flakiness Index:** The percentage by weight of flaky particles in a sample of aggregate. A particle is flaky if its least dimension (thickness) is less than 0.6 times its average dimension (length and width).
*   **Elongation Index:** The percentage by weight of elongated particles in a sample of aggregate. A particle is elongated if its largest dimension (length) is greater than 1.8 times its average dimension (width and thickness).
*   **Importance:** The shape of aggregates significantly impacts the workability, density, strength, durability, and stability of pavement mixes. Flaky and elongated particles can lead to poor interlocking, increased voids, reduced strength, and difficulty in compaction, ultimately affecting pavement performance.

---

**Question 2:**
A sample of aggregate retained on a 9.5 mm sieve and passing a 12.5 mm sieve has a total weight of 500 grams. When tested using the combined flakiness and elongation gauge, 45 grams of particles failed to pass through the gauge. Calculate the combined flakiness and elongation index for this fraction.

**Answer:**
*   Total weight of the sample ($W_{total}$) = 500 g
*   Weight of particles failing to pass the gauge ($W_{fail}$) = 45 g

$$ \text{Combined Flakiness and Elongation Index} (\%) = \frac{W_{fail}}{W_{total}} \times 100 $$
$$ \text{Combined Flakiness and Elongation Index} (\%) = \frac{45 \text{ g}}{500 \text{ g}} \times 100 $$
$$ \text{Combined Flakiness and Elongation Index} (\%) = 0.09 \times 100 = 9\% $$

The combined flakiness and elongation index for this fraction is 9%.

**Question 3:**
What are the potential negative consequences of using aggregates with a high combined flakiness and elongation index in the wearing course of a flexible pavement?

**Answer:**
Aggregates with a high combined flakiness and elongation index can lead to:
*   **Poor interlocking:** Particles cannot pack efficiently, reducing the stability and shear strength of the mix.
*   **Increased voids:** The irregular shapes create more void space, potentially leading to a weaker mix and increased susceptibility to water ingress.
*   **Reduced workability:** The mix may be harder to compact uniformly.
*   **Brittleness and breakage:** Elongated and flaky particles are more prone to breaking under repeated traffic loads, leading to premature pavement distress.
*   **Lower durability:** The overall strength and lifespan of the pavement layer can be compromised.

**Question 4:**
Describe the role of the thickness gauge and length gauge in assessing aggregate shape.

**Answer:**
*   **Thickness Gauge:** Used to determine if an aggregate particle is flaky. A particle is considered flaky if its thickness is less than 0.6 times its average dimension. The gauge has slots of varying widths, and a particle is checked against these slots. If a particle passes through a slot that is wider than 1.8 times its thickness, it's not considered flaky by definition. However, the test procedure typically involves identifying particles whose thickness is less than the critical ratio (0.6 times average dimension).
*   **Length Gauge:** Used to determine if an aggregate particle is elongated. A particle is considered elongated if its length is greater than 1.8 times its average dimension. The gauge has slots of varying lengths. If a particle fails to pass through a slot that is 1.8 times its average dimension, it is considered elongated.

The combined test often uses a single gauge set designed to identify particles that violate either the flakiness or elongation criteria (or both).

### 8. Summary and Conclusion

The combined flakiness and elongation index test is a vital quality control test for aggregates used in transportation engineering. It assesses the shape characteristics of aggregate particles, which have a direct impact on the performance and durability of pavement structures. By identifying and quantifying flaky and/or elongated particles, engineers can ensure that only suitable aggregates are used in critical pavement layers, leading to safer and longer-lasting roads. Understanding the test procedure, the equipment used, and the interpretation of results is crucial for any student or professional in transportation engineering.
