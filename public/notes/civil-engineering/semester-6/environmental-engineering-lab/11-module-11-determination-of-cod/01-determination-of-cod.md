# Environmental Engineering Lab: Module 11 - Determination of Chemical Oxygen Demand (C.O.D.)

## 1. Introduction to C.O.D.

The Chemical Oxygen Demand (C.O.D.) is a crucial parameter in wastewater analysis, representing the amount of oxygen required by microorganisms to decompose organic matter in a water sample under specific laboratory conditions. It's a measure of the **oxidizable organic pollution** present in water.

### 1.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Oxidation:** A chemical reaction involving the loss of electrons or an increase in oxidation state. In the context of C.O.D., it refers to the breakdown of organic compounds by an oxidizing agent.
*   **Organic Matter:** Compounds containing carbon, typically derived from living organisms. In wastewater, this includes domestic sewage, industrial effluents, and agricultural runoff.
*   **Oxidizing Agent:** A substance that causes oxidation in another substance. In C.O.D. determination, the primary oxidizing agent is potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇).
*   **Biochemical Oxygen Demand (B.O.D.):** Another measure of organic pollution, B.O.D. measures the oxygen consumed by microorganisms during the biological decomposition of organic matter over a specific period (typically 5 days at 20°C). C.O.D. is generally higher than B.O.D. because it oxidizes both biologically degradable and non-biodegradable organic matter.

### 1.2. Importance of C.O.D. Determination

*   **Wastewater Treatment Efficiency:** C.O.D. is used to monitor the effectiveness of wastewater treatment processes. A reduction in C.O.D. indicates successful removal of organic pollutants.
*   **Water Quality Assessment:** It helps in assessing the level of organic pollution in natural water bodies, which can impact aquatic life and water usability.
*   **Regulatory Compliance:** Many environmental regulations set limits on the C.O.D. levels in treated wastewater discharges.
*   **Industrial Process Monitoring:** Industries can use C.O.D. to monitor the organic load of their wastewater streams before discharge.

## 2. Principle of C.O.D. Determination

The C.O.D. test involves digesting a water sample with a strong oxidizing agent, typically **potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇)**, in a highly acidic medium (sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄) and in the presence of a catalyst (silver sulfate, Ag₂SO₄). Heat is applied to ensure complete oxidation of the organic matter.

**The overall reaction can be generalized as:**

Organic Matter + K₂Cr₂O₇ + H₂SO₄ (excess) + Heat → CO₂ + H₂O + Cr³⁺ + K₂SO₄ + H₂O

The amount of potassium dichromate consumed during the oxidation of organic matter is then determined by **titration** with a standardized solution of **ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS)**.

### 2.1. Key Reagents and Their Roles

*   **Potassium Dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇):** The primary oxidizing agent. It is reduced from Cr⁶⁺ (orange) to Cr³⁺ (green) during the reaction.
*   **Sulfuric Acid (H₂SO₄):** Provides the acidic medium necessary for the reaction and acts as a dehydrating agent.
*   **Silver Sulfate (Ag₂SO₄):** Acts as a catalyst to promote the complete oxidation of organic matter, particularly compounds with straight-chain aliphatic structures. It helps to overcome the inertness of some organic molecules.
*   **Mercuric Sulfate (HgSO₄) (Optional but common):** Added to complex with and eliminate interference from chloride ions (Cl⁻). Chlorides can also be oxidized by dichromate, consuming the titrant and leading to falsely high C.O.D. values.
*   **Ferrous Ammonium Sulfate (FAS - (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O):** The titrant used to quantify the unreacted potassium dichromate. It is oxidized to ferric ions (Fe³⁺).
*   **Ferroin Indicator:** Used to detect the endpoint of the titration. It changes color from red to blue-green when all the FAS has reacted with the unreacted dichromate.

### 2.2. The Redox Reaction During Titration

The titration involves a redox reaction between the unreacted dichromate and FAS:

**Unreacted K₂Cr₂O₇ + FAS → Oxidized FAS + Reduced Cr³⁺**

The key is that FAS reacts with the *remaining* dichromate after the organic matter has been oxidized.

## 3. Procedure for C.O.D. Determination

The standard method for C.O.D. determination involves the following steps:

### 3.1. Sample Preparation

1.  **Neutralization (if necessary):** If the sample is highly acidic or alkaline, it may need to be neutralized to a pH of around 7. However, for C.O.D., samples are typically digested in a strongly acidic medium, so pre-neutralization is usually not required.
2.  **Filtration (Optional):** For samples containing significant amounts of suspended solids, filtration through a glass fiber filter might be considered. However, this can remove some organic matter, so it's often omitted unless specifically required.
3.  **Digestion Setup:** Measure a specific volume of the water sample (e.g., 20 mL or 50 mL) into a clean C.O.D. digestion flask (e.g., 300 mL volumetric flask or Erlenmeyer flask with a ground-glass joint and condenser).

### 3.2. Digestion Process

1.  **Add Reagents:** Carefully add the following reagents to the digestion flask containing the sample:
    *   **Concentrated Sulfuric Acid (H₂SO₄):** Add slowly and swirl to mix. This will cause a significant temperature rise.
    *   **Potassium Dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) Solution:** Add a known volume of standardized K₂Cr₂O₇ solution. The amount added depends on the expected C.O.D. of the sample.
    *   **Mercuric Sulfate (HgSO₄) (if used):** Add a small amount to complex with chlorides.
    *   **Catalyst (Ag₂SO₄):** Add a small amount.
2.  **Connect Condenser:** Attach a reflux condenser to the digestion flask to prevent loss of volatile organic compounds and reagents during heating.
3.  **Heating:** Place the flask on a C.O.D. heating block or mantlet and heat at 105°C (or as per standard method) for **2 hours**. This duration ensures complete oxidation of most organic compounds.

### 3.3. Titration

1.  **Cooling:** After 2 hours of heating, allow the flask to cool to room temperature.
2.  **Dilution:** Carefully add distilled water to the flask to dilute the contents and bring the volume to a suitable level for titration (e.g., 140 mL).
3.  **Acidification:** Add more concentrated sulfuric acid to the flask. This represses the oxidation of Cr³⁺ back to Cr⁶⁺ and maintains the acidic environment.
4.  **Titration with FAS:**
    *   Add 2-3 drops of Ferroin indicator. The solution will turn a characteristic blue-green color.
    *   Titrate with standardized ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS) solution until the endpoint is reached. The endpoint is indicated by a **sharp color change from blue-green to red**.
    *   Record the volume of FAS used.

### 3.4. Blank Determination

A **reagent blank** must be run concurrently with the sample. The blank is prepared and treated exactly like the sample, but using distilled water instead of the water sample. This is crucial to account for the oxidizing power of the reagents themselves and any impurities.

*   **Blank Reagents:** Distilled water, K₂Cr₂O₇ solution, H₂SO₄, HgSO₄ (if used), Ag₂SO₄.
*   **Blank Titration:** Titrate the blank with FAS using Ferroin indicator, just like the sample. Record the volume of FAS used for the blank.

## 4. Calculations

The C.O.D. is calculated based on the difference in the volume of FAS used for the sample and the blank.

### 4.1. Formulas

**1. Calculate the C.O.D. of the sample in mg/L:**

$$ \text{C.O.D. (mg/L)} = \frac{(V_s - V_b) \times M_{FAS} \times 8 \times 1000}{V_{sample}} $$

Where:

*   $V_s$ = Volume of FAS used for the sample titration (mL)
*   $V_b$ = Volume of FAS used for the blank titration (mL)
*   $M_{FAS}$ = Molarity of the FAS solution (mol/L)
*   $8$ = Equivalent weight of oxygen (mg/meq)
*   $1000$ = Conversion factor from L to mL for sample volume
*   $V_{sample}$ = Volume of water sample taken for digestion (mL)

**Explanation of the '8' factor:**

The reaction involves the reduction of dichromate (Cr⁶⁺ to Cr³⁺, a change of 3 electrons) and the oxidation of Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ (a change of 1 electron). The C.O.D. is expressed in terms of oxygen's oxidizing capacity.

The molarity of FAS is often given as a factor related to mg O₂ per mL of FAS. For example, if a 0.025 M FAS solution is used and it's standardized against a known weight of potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP), the factor will be specific. A common way to express this is that 1 mL of 0.025 N FAS is equivalent to 2 mg of O₂. If your FAS is 0.025 M (and thus 0.025 N for this reaction), then the factor 8 is implicitly incorporated in how the FAS is standardized or its concentration is expressed in terms of oxygen equivalent.

**A more direct derivation for the factor '8' (if M_FAS is in mol/L):**

*   From the reaction, the moles of Fe²⁺ reacted are equal to the moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ reacted.
*   The moles of O₂ equivalent to the moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ are 3 times the moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ (since Cr⁶⁺ -> Cr³⁺ is a 3-electron change, and O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ -> 2H₂O means O₂ is a 4-electron acceptor).

Let's re-examine the titration and the standard definition:

The titration is typically set up such that the molarity of FAS corresponds to a known amount of oxygen. For instance, if you standardize your FAS against KHP, you know how many mg of oxygen it can oxidize.

**A commonly used simplified formula assumes FAS is standardized to a specific normality for oxygen:**

If FAS is standardized such that **1 mL of FAS = X mg O₂**:

$$ \text{C.O.D. (mg/L)} = \frac{(V_s - V_b) \times X \times 1000}{V_{sample}} $$

Where:
*   $X$ is the "oxygen equivalent" of the FAS solution in mg O₂/mL.

**Let's stick to the more general form using molarity:**

The half-reaction for FAS is:
Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻

The half-reaction for dichromate is:
Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O

Since the titration is of unreacted dichromate with FAS, the number of moles of FAS reacted equals 6 times the number of moles of dichromate reacted.

The number of moles of dichromate consumed by organic matter is equivalent to the number of moles of oxygen that could oxidize the organic matter. This is where the oxygen equivalent comes in.

**Consider the oxidation of organic matter by dichromate:**
Let's assume a simplified organic matter like glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O (Oxidation by oxygen)
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 2Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 16H⁺ → 12CO₂ + 11H₂O + 4Cr³⁺ (Oxidation by dichromate)

Here, 1 mole of glucose requires 6 moles of O₂ or 2 moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻.
This means that the moles of O₂ equivalent to moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ is:
Moles of O₂ = Moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ * (6 moles O₂ / 2 moles Cr₂O₇²⁻) = Moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ * 3

Now, relating to FAS:
Moles of FAS reacted = 6 * Moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻
So, Moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ = Moles of FAS reacted / 6

Therefore, Moles of O₂ = (Moles of FAS reacted / 6) * 3 = Moles of FAS reacted / 2

**If $M_{FAS}$ is the molarity of FAS (mol/L):**

*   Moles of FAS reacted = $V_s \times M_{FAS}$ (in Liters) = $\frac{V_s \times M_{FAS}}{1000}$ (in moles)
*   Moles of O₂ = $\frac{V_s \times M_{FAS}}{1000 \times 2}$
*   Mass of O₂ (mg) = Moles of O₂ * Molecular Weight of O₂ (32 g/mol) * 1000 (mg/g)
*   Mass of O₂ (mg) = $\frac{V_s \times M_{FAS}}{1000 \times 2} \times 32 \times 1000 = V_s \times M_{FAS} \times 16$

This is for the sample. For the blank, it's $V_b \times M_{FAS} \times 16$.
The difference in oxygen consumed by the sample is:
$(V_s \times M_{FAS} \times 16) - (V_b \times M_{FAS} \times 16) = (V_s - V_b) \times M_{FAS} \times 16$

This gives the mass of oxygen in mg.
To get C.O.D. in mg/L, divide by the volume of the sample in Liters:

$$ \text{C.O.D. (mg/L)} = \frac{(V_s - V_b) \times M_{FAS} \times 16 \times 1000}{V_{sample} (\text{in mL})} $$

The factor '8' in the initially presented formula likely comes from a specific standardization or a slightly different interpretation of equivalents. **It's crucial to use the factor derived from the actual standardization of your FAS or the accepted standard method's convention for your lab.**

**Let's re-verify with a common standardization:**
If FAS is prepared as 0.025 N and standardized, it reacts with dichromate. The normality relates to the number of equivalents. For dichromate, the equivalent weight is MW/6. For FAS, it's MW/1.
When determining C.O.D., the FAS is used to titrate unreacted dichromate. The molar ratio of FAS to Cr₂O₇²⁻ is 6:1.
The oxygen equivalent to Cr₂O₇²⁻ is 3:1 (as shown above).
So, moles of O₂ = moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ * 3
moles of FAS = moles of Cr₂O₇²⁻ * 6
Therefore, moles of O₂ = moles of FAS / 2.

This confirms the factor of 16 (32/2) when using molarity of FAS.

**Common Factor '8' origin:**
If FAS is standardized such that 1 mL of FAS = 2 mg O₂, then the normality is often expressed as 0.025 N.
0.025 N FAS means 0.025 equivalents of reactive species per liter.
Since oxygen is a 4-electron acceptor, 1 equivalent of oxygen is 32/4 = 8 grams.
So, 0.025 N FAS is equivalent to 0.025 * 8 = 0.2 grams of oxygen per liter, or 200 mg O₂/L.
Thus, 1 mL of 0.025 N FAS = 0.2 mg O₂.  This doesn't match 2 mg O₂.

Let's use the "2 mg O₂ per mL of 0.025 N FAS" convention, which is common in many labs.
If 1 mL FAS = 2 mg O₂, then the factor is directly the "mg O₂ per mL FAS".
$$ \text{C.O.D. (mg/L)} = \frac{(V_s - V_b) \times (\text{mg O}_2/\text{mL FAS}) \times 1000}{V_{sample} (\text{in mL})} $$

If the FAS is 0.025 M, then the molarity is 0.025 mol/L. As derived, this leads to 16.

**Let's consider the definition of Normality for FAS:**
FAS (FeSO₄(NH₄)₂SO₄·6H₂O) has a molar mass of 392.13 g/mol.
The reaction is Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. So, the equivalent weight is the molar mass.
Normality (N) = Molarity (M) * n-factor. Here n-factor = 1 for Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺.
So, N = M for FAS.

If you have 0.025 M FAS, you have 0.025 N FAS.
The oxygen equivalent of 0.025 N is 0.025 * 8 = 0.2 mg/mL O₂. This is still not matching.

**Key Takeaway for Calculation:** The most reliable way is to use the factor derived from your lab's specific FAS standardization against a known primary standard like KHP or sodium oxalate. If the FAS is standardized to be equivalent to 2 mg O₂/mL, use that directly.

Let's assume for the purpose of this note that the FAS solution is standardized such that **1 mL of FAS = 2 mg O₂**.

$$ \text{C.O.D. (mg/L)} = \frac{(V_s - V_b) \times 2 \times 1000}{V_{sample}} $$

**2. Calculate C.O.D. for diluted samples:**

If the sample was diluted before digestion, the calculation needs to account for the dilution factor.
Let $V_{sample}$ be the volume of the original sample and $V_{dil}$ be the final volume of the diluted sample used for digestion.
Dilution Factor (DF) = $V_{dil} / V_{sample}$

$$ \text{C.O.D. (mg/L)} = \frac{(V_s - V_b) \times (\text{mg O}_2/\text{mL FAS}) \times 1000}{V_{sample}} \times \text{DF} $$

Or, if $V_{dig}$ is the volume of the diluted sample taken for digestion:

$$ \text{C.O.D. (mg/L)} = \frac{(V_s - V_b) \times (\text{mg O}_2/\text{mL FAS}) \times 1000}{V_{dig}} \times \frac{V_{total\_diluted}}{V_{original\_sample}} $$

**3. Calculate C.O.D. for high concentration samples (using lower FAS normality or sample volume):**

If the FAS volume used is too high (e.g., > 40 mL), it indicates the sample has a very high C.O.D. In such cases, you might need to use a lower normality FAS solution or a smaller sample volume for digestion.

**Example:**
If you used 50 mL of FAS, and your FAS is 0.025 N (or equivalent to 2 mg O₂/mL), then the C.O.D. contributed by that volume alone (if it were pure) would be 50 mL * 2 mg/mL = 100 mg O₂. If your sample volume was only 20 mL, this would translate to a very high C.O.D.

## 5. Sources of Error and Interferences

*   **Chlorides:** As mentioned, chlorides can consume dichromate, leading to falsely high C.O.D. Mercuric sulfate is added to mitigate this.
*   **Volatile Organics:** Highly volatile organic compounds can be lost during heating if the reflux condenser is not efficient, leading to lower C.O.D. values.
*   **Inorganic Reducing Substances:** Sulfides, ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), and nitrites (NO₂⁻) can be oxidized by dichromate, contributing to the C.O.D. value.
    *   **Sulfide (S²⁻):** Oxidized to sulfate.
    *   **Ferrous Iron (Fe²⁺):** Oxidized to ferric iron (Fe³⁺).
    *   **Nitrite (NO₂⁻):** Oxidized to nitrate (NO₃⁻).
*   **Non-biodegradable Organic Matter:** Some organic compounds, like aromatic compounds or complex polymers, are resistant to oxidation by dichromate under these conditions, leading to a lower C.O.D. compared to complete theoretical oxidation.
*   **Temperature and Time of Digestion:** Insufficient heating time or temperature can lead to incomplete oxidation. Excessive time or temperature might cause decomposition of the oxidizing agent itself.
*   **Catalyst Effectiveness:** The amount and activity of the silver sulfate catalyst are crucial for complete oxidation.
*   **Titration Endpoint Detection:** Subjectivity in identifying the exact endpoint can lead to variations in results.
*   **Reagent Purity and Standardization:** Inaccurate standardization of FAS or impure reagents will directly impact the C.O.D. calculation.

C.O.D. (mg/L) = 30 mg O₂ / (25 mL / 1000 mL/L)
C.O.D. (mg/L) = 30 mg / 0.025 L
**C.O.D. (mg/L) = 1200 mg/L**

## 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **C.O.D. is always higher than or equal to B.O.D.** because it oxidizes both biodegradable and non-biodegradable organic matter.
*   **The C.O.D. test is faster than B.O.D.** (2 hours vs. 5 days), making it useful for rapid assessment of wastewater strength and treatment efficiency.
*   **Use appropriate glassware:** C.O.D. digestion flasks with reflux condensers are essential to prevent reagent and volatile sample loss.
*   **Safety Precautions:** Concentrated sulfuric acid is highly corrosive. Potassium dichromate is a strong oxidizing agent and a suspected carcinogen. Handle all chemicals with care and wear appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) such as gloves, lab coats, and eye protection.
*   **Reagent Blank is Crucial:** Always run a reagent blank to account for the oxidizing capacity of the reagents themselves.
*   **High C.O.D. Samples:** For samples with high C.O.D. (> ~200 mg/L), dilution is necessary to ensure that not all the dichromate is consumed, allowing for a reliable titration.
*   **Interferences:** Be aware of potential interferences (chlorides, nitrites, ferrous iron, sulfides) and take appropriate measures (e.g., using HgSO₄) if necessary.
*   **Standardization of FAS:** The accuracy of C.O.D. determination relies heavily on the accurate standardization of the FAS solution.
