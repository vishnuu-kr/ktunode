# Environmental Engineering Lab: Module 6 - Determination of Iron

## Introduction

Iron is a ubiquitous element found in natural waters, wastewater, and industrial effluents. Its presence can impact water quality, aesthetic properties (taste and color), and can contribute to the growth of iron bacteria, leading to pipe fouling and reduced flow. Therefore, accurately determining the concentration of iron in water samples is crucial for various environmental engineering applications, including water treatment design, process control, and regulatory compliance.

This module focuses on the laboratory determination of iron in water samples, primarily using the **spectrophotometric method**.

## Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this module, you will be able to:

*   Understand the significance of iron determination in environmental engineering.
*   Identify the different forms of iron present in water.
*   Explain the principle behind the spectrophotometric determination of iron using the 1,10-phenanthroline method.
*   Prepare standard iron solutions and calibrate the spectrophotometer.
*   Determine the concentration of iron in unknown water samples using the calibrated spectrophotometer.
*   Perform quality control measures to ensure accurate results.
*   Interpret and report the results of iron determination.

## 1. Significance of Iron Determination in Environmental Engineering

### Why is Iron Important?

*   **Aesthetic Concerns:**
    *   **Color:** Even at low concentrations (0.1-0.3 mg/L), iron can impart a reddish-brown or yellow color to water.
    *   **Taste:** Iron can contribute to an unpleasant metallic taste.
    *   **Staining:** Iron can stain laundry, fixtures, and porcelain.
*   **Operational Problems:**
    *   **Iron Bacteria Growth:** Certain bacteria (e.g., *Gallionella*, *Leptothrix*) metabolize dissolved iron, forming slimy, reddish-brown deposits (sclerotes) that can clog pipes, filter beds, and valves, reducing flow rates and increasing pumping costs.
    *   **Turbidity:** Precipitation of iron compounds can increase the turbidity of water.
*   **Health Concerns (Indirect):** While iron is an essential nutrient, excessive levels are not directly toxic. However, high iron concentrations can interfere with disinfection processes and indicate the presence of other contaminants.
*   **Industrial Processes:** Iron content in process water can affect product quality, especially in industries like food and beverage, textiles, and paper manufacturing.
*   **Wastewater Treatment:** Monitoring iron in wastewater is important for understanding the effectiveness of treatment processes and preventing operational issues in receiving waters.

## 2. Forms of Iron in Water

Iron exists in water in various forms, which is important to consider for sample preservation and analytical methods.

*   **Ferrous Iron (Fe²⁺):**
    *   **Characteristics:** Dissolved, typically colorless and odorless.
    *   **Occurrence:** Dominant form in oxygen-deficient environments (e.g., groundwater, anaerobic sediments).
    *   **Oxidation:** Readily oxidizes to ferric iron (Fe³⁺) in the presence of oxygen, especially at higher pH.
*   **Ferric Iron (Fe³⁺):**
    *   **Characteristics:** Can be dissolved (as hydrated ions or complexes) or in particulate form (colloidal or suspended precipitates, often as hydroxides or oxides).
    *   **Occurrence:** Dominant form in oxygenated waters. Ferric ions are less soluble than ferrous ions and tend to precipitate as ferric hydroxide (Fe(OH)₃) at neutral to alkaline pH.
*   **Organic Iron Complexes:** Iron can be chelated or complexed with organic matter, influencing its solubility and reactivity.
*   **Particulate Iron:** Iron can be present as suspended solids or in granular form.

**Important Note:** The method described in this module (1,10-phenanthroline) determines **total iron**, which includes both ferrous and ferric iron after they have been converted to the ferrous state and complexed with the indicator. If only dissolved or specific forms of iron are required, sample pretreatment (e.g., filtration) might be necessary.

## 3. Spectrophotometric Determination of Iron: The 1,10-Phenanthroline Method

### 3.1 Principle of the Method

The spectrophotometric determination of iron is based on the reaction between ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) and the organic indicator **1,10-phenanthroline**.

1.  **Reduction of Ferric Iron:** Any ferric iron (Fe³⁺) present in the sample is reduced to ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) using a reducing agent, commonly **hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH₂OH·HCl)**. This step ensures that both original Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ are quantified as Fe²⁺.
2.  **Complex Formation:** The ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) then reacts with 1,10-phenanthroline in a buffered solution to form a stable, intensely **orange-red colored complex**. The reaction occurs under slightly acidic to neutral conditions (pH 3.5-7.0).
    $$ \text{Fe}^{2+} + 3 \times \text{C}_{12}\text{H}_8\text{N}_2 \rightarrow [\text{Fe(C}_{12}\text{H}_8\text{N}_2)_3]^{2+} $$
    (Ferrous Iron + 1,10-Phenanthroline $\rightarrow$ Tris(1,10-phenanthroline)iron(II) complex)
3.  **Color Development:** The intensity of the orange-red color is directly proportional to the concentration of ferrous iron in the solution.
4.  **Spectrophotometry:** The absorbance of this colored complex is measured using a **spectrophotometer** at a specific wavelength, typically **510 nm**, where the complex exhibits maximum absorbance.
5.  **Quantification:** By comparing the absorbance of the unknown sample to a calibration curve prepared from standard iron solutions of known concentrations, the iron concentration in the unknown sample can be determined.

### 3.2 Reagents and Apparatus

*   **Reagents:**
    *   **Standard Iron Solution (Stock Solution):** Prepared from a high-purity iron compound (e.g., ferric ammonium sulfate, FeNH₄(SO₄)₂·12H₂O or pure iron wire).
    *   **Dilute Iron Standard Solutions:** Prepared by serial dilution of the stock solution.
    *   **Reducing Agent:** Hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution (e.g., 10% or 20% NH₂OH·HCl).
    *   **Buffer Solution:** Acetate buffer (e.g., sodium acetate and acetic acid) to maintain the optimal pH.
    *   **Color Reagent:** 1,10-Phenanthroline solution (e.g., 0.1% or 0.25% C₁₂H₈N₂·H₂O).
    *   **Acids/Bases:** For pH adjustment if necessary (e.g., HCl, NaOH).
    *   **Distilled or Deionized Water:** For reagent preparation and sample dilution.

*   **Apparatus:**
    *   **Spectrophotometer:** Visible range spectrophotometer with appropriate cuvettes (e.g., quartz or glass).
    *   **Volumetric Flasks:** Various sizes (10 mL, 25 mL, 50 mL, 100 mL, 250 mL, 500 mL, 1000 mL) for preparing standards and dilutions.
    *   **Pipettes:** Volumetric and graduated pipettes for accurate volume transfer.
    *   **Burettes:** For titrations or accurate dispensing of reagents.
    *   **Beakers and Erlenmeyer Flasks:** For mixing and reactions.
    *   **pH Meter or pH Paper:** For pH measurement and adjustment.
    *   **Wash Bottles:** For distilled/deionized water.
    *   **Analytical Balance:** For weighing reagents.
    *   **Stirring Rods/Magnetic Stirrer:** For proper mixing.

### 3.3 Procedure for Iron Determination

**Phase 1: Preparation of Standard Solutions**

1.  **Prepare Stock Iron Solution:**
    *   Accurately weigh a known amount of high-purity iron salt (e.g., 0.7022 g of FeNH₄(SO₄)₂·12H₂O for a 100 mg/L stock).
    *   Dissolve the salt in a minimal amount of dilute acid (e.g., 1 mL of concentrated HCl) to prevent hydrolysis.
    *   Transfer the solution quantitatively to a 1000 mL volumetric flask.
    *   Dilute to the mark with distilled/deionized water. Mix thoroughly.
    *   **Calculate the exact concentration of the stock solution** based on the weight of the iron salt and its purity.

    *Example Calculation:*
    If you weigh 0.7022 g of FeNH₄(SO₄)₂·12H₂O (Molecular Weight = 482.17 g/mol) and it has 99% purity, the mass of pure Fe is:
    0.7022 g $\times$ 0.99 = 0.695178 g Fe
    Volume of stock solution = 1000 mL = 1 L
    Concentration of stock solution = 0.695178 g / 1 L = 695.178 mg/L = **695.18 mg/L Fe**

2.  **Prepare Working Standard Solutions:**
    *   Use the stock solution to prepare a series of working standards with concentrations in the expected range of the unknown samples. For example, prepare standards of 0.5 mg/L, 1.0 mg/L, 2.0 mg/L, 5.0 mg/L, and 10.0 mg/L.
    *   Use the formula: $C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$
        *   $C_1$ = Concentration of stock solution
        *   $V_1$ = Volume of stock solution needed
        *   $C_2$ = Desired concentration of working standard
        *   $V_2$ = Final volume of working standard

    *Example:* To prepare 100 mL of a 2.0 mg/L standard from a 695.18 mg/L stock:
    $V_1 = (C_2 \times V_2) / C_1 = (2.0 \text{ mg/L} \times 100 \text{ mL}) / 695.18 \text{ mg/L} = 0.2877 \text{ mL}$
    Pipette 0.2877 mL of the stock solution into a 100 mL volumetric flask and dilute to the mark with distilled/deionized water.

**Phase 2: Color Development and Spectrophotometric Measurement**

1.  **Sample Preparation:**
    *   If the sample is turbid, it may need to be filtered through a fine filter paper or centrifuged to remove suspended solids before analysis, especially if dissolved iron is to be determined. For total iron, analyze the unfiltered sample.
    *   If the iron concentration is expected to be high, dilute the sample with distilled/deionized water to bring it within the calibration range.

2.  **Color Development Procedure (for each standard and unknown sample):**
    *   Take a known volume of the standard or sample (e.g., 25 mL) in a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask or volumetric flask.
    *   Add 1 mL of **hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution** (reducing agent). Mix well.
    *   Add 5 mL of **acetate buffer solution**. Mix well. (Check and adjust pH to 3.5-7.0 if necessary).
    *   Add 2-3 mL of **1,10-phenanthroline solution** (color reagent). Mix well.
    *   Allow the color to develop for at least **15-30 minutes** at room temperature.
    *   If the final volume is not 50 mL, quantitatively transfer the colored solution to a 50 mL volumetric flask and dilute to the mark with distilled/deionized water. Mix thoroughly.

3.  **Spectrophotometric Measurement:**
    *   Set the spectrophotometer to the wavelength of maximum absorbance (λmax) for the iron-phenanthroline complex, which is **510 nm**.
    *   **Zero the spectrophotometer** using a "blank" solution. The blank should contain all reagents (hydroxylamine, buffer, phenanthroline) but no iron, diluted to the same final volume as the standards and samples.
    *   Measure the absorbance of each **standard solution** at 510 nm.
    *   Measure the absorbance of the **unknown sample(s)** at 510 nm.

**Phase 3: Data Analysis and Reporting**

1.  **Prepare Calibration Curve:**
    *   Plot the absorbance values (y-axis) against the corresponding iron concentrations (x-axis) of the standard solutions.
    *   Draw the best-fit straight line through the data points (a linear regression analysis can be used). This is the calibration curve.

2.  **Determine Unknown Sample Concentration:**
    *   Locate the absorbance value of the unknown sample on the y-axis of the calibration curve.
    *   Draw a horizontal line across to intersect the calibration line.
    *   From the intersection point, draw a vertical line down to the x-axis to read the corresponding iron concentration.
    *   Alternatively, use the equation of the line ($y = mx + c$) obtained from linear regression, where $y$ is absorbance and $x$ is concentration, to calculate the concentration.

3.  **Account for Dilutions:**
    *   If the original sample was diluted before color development, multiply the determined concentration by the dilution factor to obtain the iron concentration in the original sample.
    *   Dilution Factor = (Final Volume of diluted sample) / (Initial Volume of sample taken)

4.  **Report Results:**
    *   Report the iron concentration in mg/L (or ppm).
    *   Include units.
    *   Mention the method used (1,10-phenanthroline spectrophotometric method).
    *   Record any relevant observations or quality control results.

### 3.4 Quality Control (QC)

*   **Blank Analysis:** Essential for zeroing the spectrophotometer and checking for reagent contamination.
*   **Replicate Analysis:** Analyze samples in duplicate or triplicate to assess the precision of the measurements.
*   **Spiked Sample Analysis:** Add a known amount of iron standard to a sample and re-analyze to check for matrix interference and recovery.
*   **Check Standard:** Analyze a standard solution of known concentration alongside the unknowns to verify the calibration curve's accuracy.

## 4. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
You prepare a stock iron solution by dissolving 0.7022 g of FeNH₄(SO₄)₂·12H₂O (99% purity) in 1 L of distilled water, acidified with 1 mL of concentrated HCl. Calculate the concentration of this stock solution in mg/L of Fe.

**Answer 1:**
Mass of pure Fe = 0.7022 g $\times$ 0.99 = 0.695178 g
Volume = 1 L
Concentration = 0.695178 g / 1 L = 695.178 mg/L = **695.18 mg/L Fe**

**Question 2:**
Using the stock solution from Question 1, you need to prepare 50 mL of a working standard solution with a concentration of 3.0 mg/L Fe. What volume of the stock solution is required?

**Answer 2:**
Using $C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$:
$V_1 = (C_2 \times V_2) / C_1 = (3.0 \text{ mg/L} \times 50 \text{ mL}) / 695.18 \text{ mg/L}$
$V_1 = 150 / 695.18 \approx **0.216 \text{ mL}$**

**Question 3:**
You analyze a series of iron standards and obtain the following data:

| Iron Concentration (mg/L) | Absorbance (at 510 nm) |
| :------------------------ | :--------------------- |
| 0.5                       | 0.120                  |
| 1.0                       | 0.245                  |
| 2.0                       | 0.490                  |
| 5.0                       | 1.225                  |

You then analyze an unknown water sample and obtain an absorbance of 0.735. Assuming the calibration curve is linear and passes through the origin, calculate the concentration of iron in the unknown sample.

**Answer 3:**
From the data, we can see a linear relationship. Let's determine the slope (m) of the calibration curve:
m = Absorbance / Concentration
For the 2.0 mg/L standard: m = 0.490 / 2.0 = 0.245
For the 5.0 mg/L standard: m = 1.225 / 5.0 = 0.245
The slope is consistent.

Now, to find the concentration of the unknown sample:
Concentration = Absorbance / m
Concentration = 0.735 / 0.245 = **3.0 mg/L Fe**

**Question 4:**
An unknown water sample was diluted 1:10 (1 mL sample diluted to 10 mL with distilled water) before analysis. The diluted sample produced an absorbance corresponding to 1.5 mg/L Fe on the calibration curve. What is the concentration of iron in the original sample?

**Answer 4:**
Dilution Factor = 10 mL / 1 mL = 10
Concentration in original sample = Concentration in diluted sample $\times$ Dilution Factor
Concentration = 1.5 mg/L $\times$ 10 = **15.0 mg/L Fe**

## 5. Important Points to Remember

*   **Sample Preservation:** Iron can oxidize or precipitate. Samples for iron analysis should ideally be analyzed promptly. If storage is necessary, acidify the sample with a preservative (e.g., concentrated HCl to pH < 2) and store at 4°C.
*   **Total Iron vs. Dissolved Iron:** The 1,10-phenanthroline method as described determines total iron after reduction. For dissolved iron, the sample must be filtered through a 0.45 µm filter *before* acidification and analysis.
*   **Interferences:** Certain substances can interfere with the color development or absorbance.
    *   **Turbidity and Color:** If the sample is inherently colored or turbid, these may interfere. Color correction can be done by measuring the sample's absorbance before adding reagents, or by preparing a "blank" for each sample if the turbidity is due to non-iron precipitates.
    *   **Strong Oxidizing/Reducing Agents:** Can affect the reduction step.
    *   **High Concentrations of Other Metals:** Metals like copper, nickel, and cobalt can form colored complexes with phenanthroline, potentially interfering. However, the recommended pH and reagent concentrations minimize most common interferences.
*   **pH Control:** Maintaining the correct pH range (3.5-7.0) during color development is crucial for accurate results.
*   **Color Development Time:** Ensure sufficient time (15-30 minutes) for the color to fully develop.
*   **Wavelength Selection:** Using the correct wavelength (510 nm) maximizes sensitivity and accuracy.
*   **Calibration Curve Quality:** A good calibration curve is essential. Ensure linearity and proper plotting of standards.
