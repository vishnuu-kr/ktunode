## Module 3: Under reamed piles

### Topic: Cantilever Sheet Pile Walls with Cohesive Backfill - Depth of Embedment

This module focuses on the design of cantilever sheet pile walls, specifically when subjected to a cohesive backfill. Understanding the depth of embedment is crucial for ensuring the stability and safety of these retaining structures.

---

### 1. Introduction to Cantilever Sheet Pile Walls

Cantilever sheet pile walls are a type of retaining wall constructed by driving interlocking sheets of steel into the ground. They function as a rigid, monolithic structure that resists the lateral earth pressure without the need for external bracing.

*   **Principle of Operation:** The wall acts as a cantilever, meaning it bends under the applied lateral earth pressure and is anchored at the base by the passive resistance of the soil.
*   **Applications:**
    *   Temporary excavations
    *   Permanent retaining walls in waterfront structures
    *   Cofferdams
    *   Dams and barrages
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Relatively simple to construct.
    *   Can be used in a wide range of soil conditions.
    *   Interlocking nature provides a relatively watertight barrier.
    *   Can be easily extracted if used temporarily.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Limited to moderate excavation depths due to bending stresses.
    *   Requires careful alignment during driving.
    *   Susceptible to corrosion.

### 2. Lateral Earth Pressure in Cohesive Backfill

When dealing with cohesive soils, the lateral earth pressure distribution is more complex than in granular soils.

*   **Active Earth Pressure Coefficient ($K_a$):** For cohesive soils, the active earth pressure is not solely dependent on the friction angle ($\phi$). It also involves the cohesion ($c$) of the soil.
    *   **Rankine's Theory for Cohesive Soils:**
        *   Lateral earth pressure ($p_a$) at a depth $z$: $p_a = K_a \gamma z - 2c\sqrt{K_a}$
        *   Where:
            *   $K_a = \tan^2(45^\circ - \phi/2)$
            *   $\gamma$ = unit weight of the soil
            *   $c$ = cohesion of the soil
    *   **Important Observation:** The term $-2c\sqrt{K_a}$ indicates a tensile component at shallow depths. This means that the soil near the surface can develop a "tensile crack" where there is no lateral pressure from the soil itself. The pressure then starts from zero at a certain depth and increases linearly.

*   **Pressure Distribution Diagram (Cohesive Backfill):**
    *   At the surface ($z=0$), $p_a = -2c\sqrt{K_a}$. This negative pressure indicates tension.
    *   At a depth $z_0 = \frac{2c\sqrt{K_a}}{\gamma}$, the lateral pressure is zero. This is the depth of the tensile crack.
    *   Below $z_0$, the pressure increases linearly with depth: $p_a = (\gamma z - 2c\sqrt{K_a})$.
    *   At a depth $z$, the lateral pressure is the sum of the overburden pressure and the cohesive term, with the cohesive term reducing the effective pressure.

### 3. Principles of Cantilever Sheet Pile Wall Design

The design of a cantilever sheet pile wall involves determining the required depth of embedment to resist the lateral earth pressures and moments generated by the retained soil.

*   **Forces Acting on the Wall:**
    *   **Active Earth Pressure:** Acting on the back of the wall.
    *   **Passive Earth Pressure:** Acting on the front of the wall, providing the anchoring force.
    *   **Water Pressure:** If groundwater is present.
    *   **Surcharge Load:** Any load applied on the surface of the backfill.

*   **Failure Mechanism:** A cantilever sheet pile wall fails by rotation or excessive bending. The depth of embedment is determined by ensuring that the wall does not rotate excessively and that the bending moments are within the allowable limits.

*   **Methods of Design:**
    *   **Free Earth Support Method:** Assumes that the toe of the sheet pile is free to rotate and that the passive pressure is developed only by the soil below the excavation level.
    *   **Fixed Earth Support Method:** Assumes that the toe of the sheet pile is fixed or built into a rigid stratum, providing a point of contraflexure (zero bending moment) within the embedded portion.

### 4. Free Earth Support Method for Cohesive Backfill

This is the most common method for determining the depth of embedment for cantilever sheet pile walls. It involves analyzing the forces and moments acting on the wall.

*   **Assumptions:**
    *   The wall rotates about a pivot point at the excavation level.
    *   The backfill is a cohesive soil.
    *   The passive resistance is developed by the soil below the excavation level.

*   **Steps in Design:**

    1.  **Determine the Lateral Earth Pressure Distribution:**
        *   Calculate the active earth pressure ($p_a$) for the cohesive backfill.
        *   Identify the depth of the tensile crack ($z_0$).
        *   The pressure at the excavation level ($H + D$), where $H$ is the exposed height and $D$ is the depth of embedment.

    2.  **Determine the Passive Earth Pressure Distribution:**
        *   Assume a passive earth pressure coefficient ($K_p$). For cohesive soils, the passive pressure is often calculated using a simplified approach or Coulomb's theory.
        *   A common assumption for the passive pressure distribution below the excavation level is that it acts as a wedge providing resistance.
        *   A simplified approach often considers a triangular distribution of passive pressure starting from the excavation level.
        *   The passive pressure ($p_p$) at a depth $D$ below the excavation level is often approximated as: $p_p = K_{pp} \gamma D - 2c\sqrt{K_{pp}}$ (where $K_{pp}$ is the passive earth pressure coefficient). However, a more simplified approach for the total passive force is often used in the free earth method.

    3.  **Equilibrium Conditions:**
        *   **Translational Equilibrium:** The sum of all horizontal forces must be zero.
            *   Sum of active forces = Sum of passive forces
        *   **Rotational Equilibrium:** The sum of all moments about any point must be zero. Typically, moments are taken about the point of rotation (top of the wall, or the point where the active and passive forces intersect).

    4.  **Determining the Depth of Embedment ($D$):**

        *   **Force Equilibrium:** The total active force ($F_a$) must be balanced by the total passive force ($F_p$).
            *   $F_a$ is the area under the active pressure diagram from the ground surface to the excavation level, considering the tensile crack.
            *   $F_p$ is the area under the passive pressure diagram from the excavation level downwards.

        *   **Moment Equilibrium:**
            *   Take moments about the point of rotation (e.g., the toe of the wall at the excavation level).
            *   Moments due to active pressure = Moments due to passive pressure.

    5.  **Trial-and-Error Approach (Commonly Used):**
        *   Assume a value for the depth of embedment ($D$).
        *   Calculate the active forces and their moments about the assumed point of rotation.
        *   Calculate the passive forces and their moments about the assumed point of rotation.
        *   Check if translational and rotational equilibrium are satisfied.
        *   Adjust $D$ and repeat until equilibrium is achieved.

    6.  **Simplified Analytical Approach (for Triangular Pressure Distribution):**

        *   **Active Pressure at Excavation Level ($p_{ae}$):** $p_{ae} = \gamma(H + D) - 2c\sqrt{K_a}$ (ensure $p_{ae} \ge 0$).
        *   **Active Force ($F_a$):** The force is distributed as a trapezoid (or a triangle and a rectangle, considering the tensile crack).
            *   The net active force typically acts at a certain resultant point.
        *   **Passive Pressure:** The passive pressure is assumed to start at the excavation level and increase linearly with depth. A common simplified approach for the free earth support method is to consider the maximum passive pressure occurring at the bottom of the embedment.
        *   A common approach is to assume that the net active force is balanced by the passive resistance acting over the embedded depth.

### 5. Fixed Earth Support Method

In this method, the wall is assumed to be rigidly fixed at the excavation level. This introduces a point of contraflexure (zero bending moment) within the embedded portion of the wall.

*   **Assumptions:**
    *   The wall is built into a rigid stratum or is rigidly fixed at the excavation level.
    *   This fixing reduces the bending moment in the upper portion of the wall.
    *   The depth of embedment is generally less than in the free earth support method.

*   **Design Approach:**
    *   Involves analyzing the bending moments, shear forces, and deflections.
    *   The point of contraflexure is located at a certain depth below the excavation level.
    *   The depth of embedment is determined by satisfying equilibrium and moment conditions, considering the fixing at the excavation level.

*   **Advantages:**
    *   Reduces the maximum bending moment in the wall.
    *   Allows for shallower embedment depths compared to the free earth support method.

*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   The assumption of perfect fixing at the excavation level is often difficult to achieve in practice.

**Question 2:** Explain the difference between the Free Earth Support Method and the Fixed Earth Support Method for cantilever sheet pile walls.

**Answer 2:**

*   **Free Earth Support Method:**
    *   **Assumption:** The toe of the sheet pile wall is free to rotate at the excavation level.
    *   **Mechanism:** The wall acts as a simple cantilever, with the passive resistance of the soil below the excavation level providing the necessary support.
    *   **Result:** This method generally requires a greater depth of embedment and results in larger bending moments in the wall compared to the fixed earth support method. The wall is assumed to pivot about the toe.

*   **Fixed Earth Support Method:**
    *   **Assumption:** The toe of the sheet pile wall is rigidly fixed at the excavation level, preventing rotation. This means there is a point of contraflexure (zero bending moment) within the embedded portion of the wall.
    *   **Mechanism:** The fixing at the excavation level significantly reduces the bending moments in the upper portion of the wall.
    *   **Result:** This method typically requires a shallower depth of embedment and leads to smaller maximum bending moments. However, the assumption of perfect fixing can be difficult to achieve in practice.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Cohesive Backfill Pressure:** The presence of cohesion ($c$) reduces the lateral earth pressure at shallow depths due to a tensile zone (tensile crack). The active earth pressure is $p_a = \gamma z - 2c\sqrt{K_a}$.
*   **Depth of Tensile Crack ($z_0$):** $z_0 = \frac{2c\sqrt{K_a}}{\gamma}$. The soil above $z_0$ does not contribute to the lateral pressure.
*   **Free Earth Support Method:** The most common method, assumes rotation at the toe. Requires balancing forces and moments about the toe. Leads to greater embedment depths and bending moments.
*   **Fixed Earth Support Method:** Assumes fixing at the excavation level, reducing bending moments and embedment depth. This assumption is often idealized.
*   **Design Process:** Typically involves iterative calculations or the use of established formulas derived from equilibrium principles.
*   **Factor of Safety:** Always apply a factor of safety to the calculated depth of embedment.
*   **Groundwater:** If groundwater is present, hydrostatic pressures must be included in the analysis, which can significantly increase the design loads.
*   **Cohesive Soils ($\phi=0$):** The calculation of depth of embedment for purely cohesive soils ($\phi=0$) is critical and involves careful consideration of the pressure distribution due to cohesion. The depth of embedment is generally larger than for granular soils for the same retained height due to the nature of the stress distribution.
