## Module 4: Behavior of Vertical Piles Under Lateral Loading

### Topic: Charts for Estimating Lateral Resistance and Deflection (Broms)

This module delves into the behavior of vertical piles subjected to lateral loads. We will focus on understanding the failure mechanisms of short piles in cohesive and granular soils, as well as utilizing Broms' charts for estimating ultimate lateral resistance in sandy soils and lateral deflection in clayey soils.

---

### 1. Introduction to Lateral Loading on Piles

*   **Lateral loads** on piles can arise from various sources:
    *   Wind loads on superstructures.
    *   Earthquake forces.
    *   Wave forces on offshore structures.
    *   Impact forces.
    *   Surcharge loads on the soil adjacent to the pile.
*   **Importance of analyzing lateral behavior:** Excessive lateral deflection can lead to serviceability issues in the superstructure, while exceeding the ultimate lateral resistance can result in catastrophic failure.

---

### 2. Failure Mechanisms of Short Piles Under Lateral Loading (Broms)

Broms' work provides a simplified approach to understanding the failure mechanisms of piles under lateral loads. The key distinction is between **short** and **long** piles, which is determined by the pile's slenderness ratio.

*   **Short Piles:** Piles that fail in **flexure** or **soil failure** before buckling. The pile's stiffness is generally lower than the soil's resistance.
*   **Long Piles:** Piles that fail due to **buckling** before reaching their ultimate soil resistance. The pile's stiffness is generally higher than the soil's resistance.

Broms categorized failure mechanisms for short piles based on the soil type and the pile's fixity at the head.

#### 2.1. Failure Mechanisms in Cohesive Soils (Clays)

In cohesive soils, the lateral resistance is primarily governed by the undrained shear strength ($s_u$). Broms' analysis considers two main failure modes for short piles:

*   **Soil Failure:**
    *   **Failure by yielding of the soil:** The soil adjacent to the pile yields, leading to a progressive failure. This typically occurs when the pile is relatively short and stiff.
    *   **Failure by flow around the pile:** For very short and stiff piles, the soil can flow around the pile, leading to failure.

*   **Flexural Failure of the Pile:**
    *   When the lateral load is significant, the pile can experience bending, leading to yielding of the pile material itself. This is more common in longer piles but can occur in short piles if the load is extreme.

#### 2.2. Failure Mechanisms in Granular Soils (Sands)

In granular soils, the lateral resistance is primarily governed by the angle of internal friction ($\phi$) and the soil density. Broms' analysis for short piles in granular soils also considers:

*   **Soil Failure:**
    *   **Passive pressure failure:** The soil on the loaded side of the pile is pushed against the pile, creating passive earth pressure. Failure occurs when this passive pressure reaches a limit.
    *   **Active pressure failure:** On the leeward side of the pile, the soil can move away, creating active earth pressure.

*   **Flexural Failure of the Pile:**
    *   Similar to cohesive soils, significant bending in the pile can lead to flexural failure.

### 3. Charts for Estimating Ultimate Lateral Resistance of Short and Long Piles in Sandy Soils (Broms)

Broms developed charts to estimate the ultimate lateral resistance of piles in sandy soils. These charts are based on the concept of **ultimate bearing capacity of the soil against the pile**.

#### 3.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Ultimate Lateral Resistance ($P_u$):** The maximum lateral load a pile can withstand before significant failure occurs.
*   **Group Factor:** When piles are installed in a group, their individual lateral resistances are reduced due to interaction effects. This is often accounted for by a group factor.
*   **Effective Unit Weight of Soil ($\gamma'$):** The unit weight of the soil below the groundwater table.
*   **Angle of Internal Friction ($\phi$):** A critical parameter for sandy soils, representing the soil's shear strength.
*   **Pile Diameter ($D$):** The lateral resistance is proportional to the diameter of the pile.
*   **Pile Length ($L$):** The length of the pile significantly influences its resistance.
*   **Slenderness Ratio ($L/D$):** Crucial for distinguishing between short and long piles.

#### 3.2. Broms' Approach for Sandy Soils

Broms simplified the problem by considering two primary failure mechanisms in sandy soils:

1.  **Short Piles in Sands:**
    *   **Failure Mode:** Typically characterized by **soil failure** in passive pressure. The pile is considered to fail when the passive soil resistance along its length is mobilized.
    *   **Chart Usage:** The charts for short piles relate the ultimate lateral resistance per unit length of the pile to the soil properties and the embedment depth.

2.  **Long Piles in Sands:**
    *   **Failure Mode:** Typically characterized by **pile buckling** due to lateral soil restraint. The soil provides a resisting moment along the length of the pile.
    *   **Chart Usage:** The charts for long piles relate the ultimate lateral resistance to the pile stiffness ($EI$), soil properties, and pile length.

#### 3.3. Understanding the Charts (General Principles)

While specific charts require access to the original Broms' publications or compiled data, here's how to generally interpret them:

*   **Axes:** Typically, one axis will represent a soil parameter (e.g., $\phi$, $\gamma'$), and the other will represent the ultimate lateral resistance per unit length (for short piles) or the total ultimate lateral resistance (for long piles).
*   **Curves:** Different curves on the chart will represent variations in pile diameter, pile length, or degree of fixity at the pile head.
*   **Working Loads vs. Ultimate Loads:** These charts are for estimating *ultimate* lateral resistance. For design under working loads, a significant factor of safety is applied.

#### 3.4. Example of Chart Interpretation (Conceptual)

**Scenario:** Estimate the ultimate lateral resistance of a short pile in dense sand.

*   **Chart:** You would use the chart specifically for "Short Piles in Sandy Soils."
*   **Inputs:** You would need the angle of internal friction ($\phi$) of the sand, the effective unit weight ($\gamma'$), and the pile diameter ($D$).
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Locate the relevant $\phi$ value on one axis.
    2.  Follow the corresponding curve for your pile diameter and soil conditions.
    3.  Read the ultimate lateral resistance per unit length from the other axis.
    4.  Multiply by the pile length ($L$) to get the total ultimate lateral resistance.

### 4. Chart for Estimating Lateral Deflection at Ground Level for Piles in Clayey Soils Under Working Loads (Broms)

Broms also provided charts to estimate the lateral deflection of piles in clayey soils under working loads. This is crucial for serviceability checks.

#### 4.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Lateral Deflection at Ground Level ($\delta$):** The horizontal displacement of the pile head at the ground surface.
*   **Working Load ($P$):** The lateral load applied to the pile under service conditions, which is significantly lower than the ultimate load.
*   **Undrained Shear Strength ($s_u$):** The shear strength of the clay under undrained conditions.
*   **Pile Stiffness ($EI$):** The flexural rigidity of the pile, calculated as the product of the Young's modulus of the pile material ($E$) and the moment of inertia of the pile cross-section ($I$).
*   **Coeffcient of Subgrade Reaction ($k$):** A parameter that relates the soil pressure to the soil deformation. Broms' charts often implicitly use or are derived from methods involving subgrade reactions.
*   **Characteristic Length ($\lambda$):** A parameter that accounts for the soil-pile interaction, often defined as $(\frac{EI}{k L_c})^{1/4}$, where $L_c$ is a characteristic length of the soil or pile. For clayey soils, the effective stiffness of the soil plays a role.

#### 4.2. Broms' Approach for Clayey Soils

Broms' method for clayey soils focuses on the **flexural behavior of the pile** and the **resistance offered by the surrounding soil**, typically modeled as a series of springs (Winkler foundation model).

*   **Failure Mode:** Under working loads, the primary concern is excessive deflection, not ultimate soil failure. The pile bends, and the soil provides a resisting moment and shear force.
*   **Chart Usage:** The charts relate the lateral deflection at ground level to the applied lateral load, pile properties ($EI$), and soil properties ($s_u$).

#### 4.3. Understanding the Charts for Clayey Soils

*   **Axes:** Typically, one axis will be the applied lateral load ($P$) or a dimensionless load parameter. The other axis will be the lateral deflection at ground level ($\delta$) or a dimensionless deflection parameter.
*   **Curves:** Different curves represent variations in pile stiffness ($EI$), undrained shear strength ($s_u$), pile diameter ($D$), and pile length ($L$). The degree of fixity at the pile head is also a critical factor.
*   **Working Load Context:** These charts are specifically designed for estimating deflections under *working loads*.

#### 4.4. Example of Chart Interpretation (Conceptual)

**Scenario:** Estimate the lateral deflection at ground level for a pile in soft clay under a working load.

*   **Chart:** You would use the chart specifically for "Lateral Deflection in Clayey Soils."
*   **Inputs:** You would need the applied working lateral load ($P$), the undrained shear strength ($s_u$) of the clay, the pile's flexural rigidity ($EI$), and the pile length ($L$). You would also need to know the fixity condition at the pile head (e.g., free head, fixed head).
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Locate the applied working load ($P$) or its dimensionless equivalent on one axis.
    2.  Consider the curve corresponding to your pile's $EI$ and the soil's $s_u$.
    3.  Follow the curve to intersect with the relevant pile length and fixity condition.
    4.  Read the lateral deflection at ground level ($\delta$) from the other axis.

### 5. Important Points to Remember

*   **Distinction between Short and Long Piles:** This is fundamental to applying the correct chart and understanding the failure mechanism. The slenderness ratio ($L/D$) and the soil-pile stiffness interaction determine this.
*   **Soil Type is Crucial:** Failure mechanisms and resistance characteristics differ significantly between cohesive (clays) and granular (sands) soils.
*   **Pile Head Fixity:** Whether the pile head is unrestrained (free) or restrained (fixed) significantly impacts its lateral behavior and resistance.
*   **Ultimate vs. Working Loads:** Broms' charts are used for different purposes: ultimate resistance charts for ultimate load capacity and deflection charts for serviceability under working loads.
*   **Factors of Safety:** Always apply appropriate factors of safety when using ultimate resistance charts for design.
*   **Group Effects:** For piles in a group, consider reduction factors or specific group design charts, as individual pile capacities can be reduced.
*   **Limitations:** Broms' methods are simplified models. For complex soil conditions or loading scenarios, more advanced numerical analyses (e.g., finite element methods) may be required.
*   **Groundwater Table:** The location of the groundwater table affects the effective unit weight of sandy soils, which influences lateral resistance.

**Question 1:**
A single pile in a sandy soil has a diameter of 0.5 m and is embedded to a depth of 10 m. The sand has an angle of internal friction $\phi = 35^\circ$ and an effective unit weight $\gamma' = 18 \text{ kN/m}^3$. If the pile is considered "short," how would you estimate its ultimate lateral resistance using Broms' charts? What are the key inputs required?

**Answer 1:**
To estimate the ultimate lateral resistance of a short pile in sandy soil using Broms' charts, you would:
1.  Consult the chart for "Short Piles in Sandy Soils."
2.  Locate the value of $\phi = 35^\circ$ and $\gamma' = 18 \text{ kN/m}^3$.
3.  Find the curve corresponding to the pile diameter (0.5 m).
4.  Read the ultimate lateral resistance per unit length from the chart.
5.  Multiply this value by the pile length (10 m) to get the total ultimate lateral resistance.

The key inputs required are: pile diameter, pile length, angle of internal friction of the sand, and effective unit weight of the sand.

**Question 2:**
A pile in soft clay has an undrained shear strength $s_u = 25 \text{ kPa}$. The pile has a flexural rigidity $EI = 5000 \text{ kNm}^2$ and a length $L = 15 \text{ m}$. The pile head is free. If a working lateral load of $P = 50 \text{ kN}$ is applied, describe how you would use Broms' charts to estimate the lateral deflection at ground level. What factors would influence the deflection value obtained from the chart?

**Answer 2:**
To estimate the lateral deflection at ground level for a pile in soft clay under working loads using Broms' charts, you would:
1.  Consult the chart for "Lateral Deflection in Clayey Soils."
2.  Locate the applied working load $P = 50 \text{ kN}$ or its dimensionless equivalent.
3.  Identify the curve corresponding to the pile's $EI = 5000 \text{ kNm}^2$ and the soil's $s_u = 25 \text{ kPa}$.
4.  Consider the specific curve or section of the chart that represents a "free head" condition.
5.  Read the estimated lateral deflection at ground level ($\delta$) from the chart.

The factors influencing the deflection value obtained from the chart are:
*   Applied working lateral load ($P$).
*   Undrained shear strength of the clay ($s_u$).
*   Pile's flexural rigidity ($EI$).
*   Pile length ($L$).
*   Fixity condition at the pile head (free head in this case).
*   Possibly the pile diameter, which might be implicitly included in the charts or affect the characteristic length.

**Question 3:**
Differentiate between the failure mechanisms of short piles in cohesive soils and sandy soils as described by Broms.

**Answer 3:**
The primary difference in failure mechanisms for short piles under lateral loading between cohesive and sandy soils, according to Broms, lies in the governing soil properties and the stress states involved:

*   **Cohesive Soils (Clays):** Failure is primarily governed by the **undrained shear strength ($s_u$)**. The mechanism often involves:
    *   **Soil Failure:** Yielding of the soil mass around the pile due to exceeding its shear strength. This can manifest as a spreading of the soil or flow.
    *   **Flexural Failure:** While less common for truly "short" piles, significant bending can cause the pile material itself to yield.

*   **Sandy Soils (Granular):** Failure is primarily governed by the **angle of internal friction ($\phi$) and density**. The mechanism often involves:
    *   **Soil Failure:** Mobilization of **passive earth pressure** on the loaded side of the pile. Failure occurs when this passive resistance, amplified by the soil's friction angle, is overcome.
    *   **Flexural Failure:** Similar to cohesive soils, the pile can also fail due to excessive bending.

In essence, clays fail due to their inherent cohesive strength, while sands fail due to the interlocking and friction between particles, which generates significant passive resistance.
