# Ground Improvement Techniques: Module 2 - Compaction: Field Procedure, Surface Compaction

## Introduction to Compaction

Compaction is a process of densifying soil by expelling air from the voids between soil particles. This results in an increase in the soil's dry unit weight, strength, and stiffness, while reducing its permeability and compressibility.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Dry Unit Weight ($\gamma_d$):** The weight of solids per unit volume of soil. Compaction aims to maximize $\gamma_d$.
*   **Optimum Moisture Content (OMC):** The moisture content at which a soil can be compacted to its maximum dry unit weight for a given compactive effort.
*   **Maximum Dry Unit Weight ($\gamma_{d,max}$):** The highest dry unit weight achievable for a soil at a specific compactive effort.
*   **Moisture Content (w):** The ratio of the weight of water to the weight of solids in a soil.

**Importance of Compaction in the Field:**

*   **Foundation Support:** Improved strength and stiffness reduce settlement of structures.
*   **Roads and Embankments:** Enhanced load-bearing capacity and stability, reduced deformation.
*   **Earth Dams:** Reduced permeability, preventing seepage and improving stability.
*   **Backfills:** Preventing future settlement and collapse.

## Learning Outcome 1: Describe the standard field procedure for compaction.

The standard field procedure for compaction involves a systematic approach to achieve uniform and effective densification of soil.

### Steps in the Standard Field Procedure:

1.  **Site Preparation:**
    *   **Clearing and Grubbing:** Removal of vegetation, roots, and debris from the site.
    *   **Stripping Topsoil:** Removal of organic-rich topsoil, which has poor load-bearing capacity.
    *   **Proof Rolling:** Rolling the subgrade with a heavy roller to identify soft spots or areas of poor compaction. Soft areas are excavated and replaced with suitable fill.

2.  **Fill Placement:**
    *   **Layering:** Soil is placed in layers of controlled thickness. The thickness of each layer is determined by the type of compaction equipment used. Typical layer thicknesses range from 150 mm to 300 mm (loose lift).
    *   **Spreading:** The fill material is spread evenly across the layer.
    *   **Moisture Conditioning:** The fill material is brought to a moisture content close to the OMC determined in the laboratory. This is crucial for achieving effective compaction. Water is added if the soil is too dry, and air-drying or mixing is employed if it's too wet.

3.  **Compaction Operation:**
    *   **Equipment Selection:** Choosing the appropriate type of roller based on soil type, layer thickness, and required compactive effort.
    *   **Rolling Pattern:** A systematic overlapping pattern is used to ensure uniform coverage of the entire layer. Passes are made in a consistent direction.
    *   **Number of Passes:** The number of passes is predetermined based on the equipment's capacity and the desired density. It's adjusted based on field density tests.

4.  **Field Density Testing:**
    *   **Purpose:** To verify that the desired degree of compaction has been achieved.
    *   **Methods:**
        *   **Nuclear Gauge (Troxler Gauge):** Measures both dry density and moisture content directly and quickly. This is the most common method in modern construction.
        *   **Sand Cone Method:** Involves excavating a small hole, measuring the volume of excavated soil, and then determining its dry density in the lab.
        *   **Rubber Balloon Method:** Similar to the sand cone method but uses a balloon filled with water to measure the volume of the excavated hole.
    *   **Frequency:** Tests are conducted at regular intervals across the compacted layer (e.g., every 500-1000 m² or as per project specifications).

5.  **Layer Acceptance:**
    *   A layer is considered accepted for compaction when the field density tests meet the specified criteria (e.g., 95% of $\gamma_{d,max}$).
    *   If a layer fails to meet the specifications, it may require additional passes, moisture adjustment, or even removal and recompaction.

## Learning Outcome 2: Discuss the different types of equipment used for surface compaction and their applications.

Surface compaction is achieved using various types of rollers, each suited for different soil types and applications.

### Types of Compaction Equipment:

1.  **Smooth Wheel Rollers (Static Smooth Wheel Rollers):**
    *   **Description:** Heavy steel drums with smooth surfaces. Available in static and vibratory versions.
    *   **Principle of Operation:** Apply pressure through contact area. Vibration significantly increases compactive effort.
    *   **Applications:**
        *   **Fine-grained soils (clays, silts):** Effective on cohesive soils when moisture content is near OMC.
        *   **Base and sub-base courses:** Compaction of granular materials, especially with vibration.
        *   **Finishing:** Provides a smooth surface finish to asphalt pavements.
    *   **Limitations:** Can "plow" or "shave" granular materials if not properly operated or if the soil is too dry. Less effective on very wet cohesive soils.

2.  **Pneumatic Tired Rollers (Rubber Tired Rollers):**
    *   **Description:** Multiple rubber tires that oscillate independently, providing a kneading action. Tire pressure and number of tires can vary.
    *   **Principle of Operation:** Compaction through a combination of pressure, manipulation (kneading), and vibration (inherent due to tire deflection). The weight is distributed over a larger contact area.
    *   **Applications:**
        *   **Cohesive soils (clays, silts):** Excellent for creating a good bond between layers and preventing slippage.
        *   **Granular soils:** Effective for compacting base and sub-base materials.
        *   **Asphalt compaction:** Used in early to intermediate stages of asphalt paving to achieve initial densification and "kneading."
    *   **Advantages:** Good surface coverage and can compact in a wider range of moisture conditions compared to smooth wheel rollers. The kneading action helps to close surface cracks.

3.  **Sheepsfoot Rollers (Tamping Rollers):**
    *   **Description:** Steel drums with projecting "feet" or lugs.
    *   **Principle of Operation:** Compaction through high contact pressure concentrated on the feet. The feet penetrate the soil, compacting it from the bottom up and compacting the core of the lift.
    *   **Applications:**
        *   **Cohesive soils (clays, silts):** Highly effective for compacting clayey soils, especially at lower to moderate moisture contents.
        *   **Shallow lifts:** Work best on thinner layers.
    *   **Advantages:** Effective for compacting cohesive soils by achieving deep penetration and densifying the soil from the bottom upwards. The feet help break down clods.
    *   **Limitations:** Less effective on granular soils and can leave the surface in a rough state. Requires a finishing pass with a smooth drum roller.

4.  **Vibratory Rollers:**
    *   **Description:** Smooth wheel or sheepsfoot rollers equipped with eccentric weights or vibrators that generate vibrations.
    *   **Principle of Operation:** Vibration reduces the internal friction of soil particles, allowing them to rearrange into a denser configuration more easily. This significantly increases compactive effort.
    *   **Applications:**
        *   **Granular soils (sands, gravels):** Highly effective for achieving maximum density in granular materials.
        *   **Cohesive soils (with care):** Can be used on cohesive soils, but the frequency and amplitude need to be carefully selected to avoid "liquefaction" or over-compaction.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides the highest compactive effort for a given roller weight. Very efficient for granular fills.
    *   **Types of Vibratory Rollers:**
        *   **Vibratory Smooth Wheel Rollers:** For granular and some cohesive soils.
        *   **Vibratory Sheepsfoot Rollers:** For cohesive soils, providing both tamping and vibration.

5.  **Rammers (Tampers):**
    *   **Description:** Mechanical devices that deliver a sharp blow to the soil surface. Can be hand-operated (jumping jacks) or machine-mounted.
    *   **Principle of Operation:** Impact compaction.
    *   **Applications:**
        *   **Trench backfills:** Ideal for compacting soil in confined spaces and trenches where larger rollers cannot access.
        *   **Around foundations and structures:** Similar to trench backfills.
        *   **Small areas:** For patching or localized compaction.
    *   **Advantages:** Excellent for confined spaces.
    *   **Limitations:** Low production rate for large areas.

## Learning Outcome 3: Explain the importance of moisture content in field compaction.

Moisture content plays a critical role in the effectiveness of compaction. It influences the soil's plasticity and the efficiency of air expulsion.

### Role of Moisture Content:

*   **Lubrication:** Water acts as a lubricant between soil particles, allowing them to slide past each other and rearrange into a denser configuration.
*   **Surface Tension:** At very low moisture contents, surface tension forces between water and soil particles can create a "suction" or "crust" that resists compaction.
*   **Near Saturation:** As moisture content increases towards OMC, lubrication improves, leading to higher dry densities.
*   **Above OMC:** If the moisture content exceeds OMC, the excess water fills the pore spaces. As the soil is compacted, this water is trapped and cannot escape easily. The trapped water exerts hydrostatic pressure, resisting further densification and preventing air from being expelled effectively. This leads to a decrease in dry unit weight.
*   **Hydrostatic Pressure:** At high moisture contents, the trapped water creates pore water pressure, which reduces the effective stress between soil particles, thus reducing the soil's strength and stiffness.

**Visualizing the Impact (Moisture-Density Curve):**

The relationship between moisture content and dry unit weight for a given compactive effort is typically represented by a bell-shaped curve (moisture-density curve).

*   **Dry Side:** As moisture increases from very low values, dry density increases.
*   **Optimum Moisture Content (OMC):** The peak of the curve, where $\gamma_{d,max}$ is achieved.
*   **Wet Side:** As moisture content increases beyond OMC, dry density decreases.

**Importance in Field Procedure:**

*   **Achieving Target Density:** Compacting at or near OMC is essential to achieve the maximum achievable dry density for the given compactive effort.
*   **Efficiency of Compaction:** Soil that is too dry will require more passes and effort. Soil that is too wet will be difficult to compact effectively and may lead to poor performance.
*   **Bonding Between Layers:** For cohesive soils, proper moisture content is crucial for achieving a good bond between successive compacted layers, preventing slippage.

**Field Adjustments:**

*   **If Too Dry:** Water is added and the material is mixed thoroughly to ensure uniform distribution of moisture.
*   **If Too Wet:** The soil may be air-dried by spreading it out in thin layers and allowing it to dry through evaporation, or mixed with drier material if available.

## Learning Outcome 4: Explain the criteria for evaluating the degree of compaction in the field.

The degree of compaction refers to how well the soil has been densified in the field relative to the maximum density achievable in the laboratory.

### Criteria for Evaluating Degree of Compaction:

1.  **Dry Unit Weight ($\gamma_d$):**
    *   **Field Target:** A specific minimum dry unit weight is usually specified for the compacted fill.
    *   **Calculation:** $\gamma_{d,field} = \frac{W_{solids,field}}{V_{total,field}}$

2.  **Relative Compaction ($RC$):** This is the most common method. It compares the field dry unit weight to the maximum dry unit weight determined in the laboratory under a specified compactive effort.

    *   **Formula:**
        $RC = \frac{\gamma_{d,field}}{\gamma_{d,max,lab}} \times 100\%$

    *   **Laboratory Compaction Tests:**
        *   **Standard Proctor Test:** Uses a compactive effort of 56.5 kJ/m³ (based on a 2.5 kg hammer falling from 305 mm).
        *   **Modified Proctor Test:** Uses a higher compactive effort of 2573 kJ/m³ (based on a 4.9 kg hammer falling from 457 mm). The Modified Proctor test generally yields higher $\gamma_{d,max}$ and lower OMC values.

    *   **Project Specifications:** Project specifications will typically require a minimum relative compaction (e.g., 95% of Standard Proctor or 90% of Modified Proctor). The choice of Proctor test depends on the expected loading conditions. Higher loads generally require compaction to Modified Proctor standards.

3.  **Moisture Content:**
    *   **Field Moisture Content ($w_{field}$):** The moisture content of the soil in the field at the time of testing.
    *   **Optimum Moisture Content ($OMC$):** The moisture content at which $\gamma_{d,max}$ was achieved in the laboratory.
    *   **Moisture Content Tolerance:** Project specifications often define an acceptable range of moisture content relative to OMC for field compaction (e.g., OMC ± 2% or OMC ± 4%). This is because compacting too far from OMC can lead to poor results.

**Summary of Evaluation:**

The primary evaluation criterion is usually the **relative compaction**. However, it's also crucial to ensure that the **field moisture content** is within the acceptable range specified for the project to ensure effective compaction. If the field dry unit weight meets the relative compaction requirement but the moisture content is too far from OMC, the compaction might still be deemed unsatisfactory.

## Learning Outcome 5: Discuss the significance of compaction in relation to soil properties.

Compaction fundamentally alters the engineering properties of a soil, making it more suitable for various construction applications.

### Significance of Compaction on Soil Properties:

1.  **Increased Dry Unit Weight ($\gamma_d$):**
    *   **Significance:** Denser soil has more particles packed into a given volume, leading to better mechanical interlock and support.

2.  **Increased Shear Strength:**
    *   **Significance:** Denser soils have higher internal friction angles and cohesion (especially clays), leading to improved load-bearing capacity and resistance to shear failure. This is critical for the stability of embankments, foundations, and roads.
    *   **Example:** A compacted clay layer under a road will resist the deformation caused by traffic loads much better than an uncompacted clay layer.

3.  **Increased Stiffness (Modulus of Elasticity):**
    *   **Significance:** Compacted soils deform less under load, leading to reduced settlements and improved performance of pavements and structures.
    *   **Example:** Foundations built on highly compacted soil will experience less differential settlement, which can cause structural damage.

4.  **Decreased Compressibility and Settlement:**
    *   **Significance:** By pre-densifying the soil, future settlement under load is reduced. This is crucial for structures sensitive to settlement.
    *   **Example:** Compacting the subgrade of an airport runway prevents excessive settlement under the weight of aircraft.

5.  **Decreased Permeability:**
    *   **Significance:** As soil particles are brought closer together, the void spaces become smaller and more tortuous, reducing the rate at which water can flow through the soil. This is important for controlling seepage in earth dams and preventing frost heave in roadways.
    *   **Example:** The core of an earth dam is often made of clay compacted to very low permeability to prevent water from seeping through.

6.  **Reduced Plasticity and Swelling (for Clays):**
    *   **Significance:** Compacting clayey soils to a dry side of optimum can reduce their tendency to swell when they absorb moisture, which can cause significant damage to overlying structures.

**Overall Impact:**

Compaction is a fundamental process in geotechnical engineering that transforms weak, compressible soils into strong, stable materials capable of supporting substantial loads and resisting environmental forces.

## Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
C. To increase the dry unit weight, shear strength, and stiffness, while decreasing permeability and compressibility.

**Answer 2:**
C. Vibratory Smooth Wheel Roller. The vibration helps to efficiently rearrange granular particles into a denser state.

**Answer 3:**
B. Spread the soil in thin layers and allow it to air-dry. This removes excess moisture, bringing the soil closer to its optimum moisture content.

**Answer 4:**
B. The ratio of field dry unit weight to the maximum dry unit weight achieved in the laboratory.

**Answer 5:**
A sheepsfoot roller is effective for cohesive soils because its projecting feet penetrate the soil mass. This compacts the soil from the bottom up, densifying the core of the lift. The kneading action also helps to break down clods and improve the bonding between layers. The high contact pressure on the feet is efficient for consolidating fine-grained materials.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Moisture Content is Key:** Compacting soil at its Optimum Moisture Content (OMC) is critical for achieving maximum dry unit weight.
*   **Equipment Selection Matters:** Choose the right roller for the soil type and application (e.g., vibratory for granular, sheepsfoot for clay).
*   **Layer Thickness is Crucial:** The thickness of each lifted layer must be compatible with the compactor's capabilities.
*   **Uniformity is Essential:** Ensure consistent coverage and avoid "skipping" areas during rolling.
*   **Field Testing is Mandatory:** Regularly check field densities (using nuclear gauges, sand cones, etc.) to verify that specifications are being met.
*   **Relative Compaction:** This is the primary metric for evaluating compaction quality in the field.
*   **Compaction Enhances Properties:** It significantly improves strength, stiffness, and stability while reducing settlement and permeability, making soils suitable for construction.
