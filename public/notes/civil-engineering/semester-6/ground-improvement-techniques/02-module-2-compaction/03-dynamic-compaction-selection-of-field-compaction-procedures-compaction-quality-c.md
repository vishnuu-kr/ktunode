# Ground Improvement Techniques: Module 2 - Compaction

## Topic: Dynamic Compaction, Selection of Field Compaction Procedures, Compaction Quality Control

---

### Learning Outcomes:

*   **Understand the principles and applications of dynamic compaction.**
*   **Be able to select appropriate field compaction procedures based on site conditions and project requirements.**
*   **Understand the importance and methods of compaction quality control.**

---

### 1. Dynamic Compaction (DC)

#### 1.1. Introduction and Principles

*   **Definition:** Dynamic Compaction is a ground improvement technique that uses the energy of repeated high-impact drops of a heavy tamper to densify loose, granular soils or to reduce the void ratio of cohesive soils.
*   **Mechanism:** The high-energy impact creates significant seismic waves and pore water pressure increases within the soil. This causes a rapid rearrangement of soil particles, leading to densification and an increase in shear strength and stiffness.
*   **Key Principle:** Energy transfer through impact. The kinetic energy of the falling tamper is transferred to the soil, causing it to deform and compact.

#### 1.2. Equipment and Methodology

*   **Tamper:** A heavy, typically square or circular, steel weight (e.g., 10-50 tons or more).
*   **Crane/Dropping Device:** A heavy-duty crane or specialized equipment capable of lifting the tamper to significant heights.
*   **Drop Height:** Typically ranges from 10 to 30 meters (30 to 100 feet).
*   **Grid Pattern:** The tamper is dropped in a systematic grid pattern across the site to ensure uniform densification.
*   **Number of Passes:** Multiple passes (typically 3-5) are made over the site. The number of passes and drop locations are adjusted based on the achieved densification.
*   **Layer Thickness:** Effective for densifying soil layers up to 6-10 meters (20-30 feet) thick, depending on the soil type and tamper weight.
*   **Watering:** Often, the site is lightly watered between passes to help reduce friction and improve energy transfer.

#### 1.3. Applications

*   **Densification of Loose Granular Soils:**
    *   Sand and gravel fills
    *   Over-consolidated clays
    *   Organic soils with low moisture content
*   **Reduction of Liquefaction Potential:** Significantly increases the relative density of granular soils, making them less susceptible to liquefaction during seismic events.
*   **Improvement of Bearing Capacity:** Increases the shear strength and stiffness of the soil, leading to higher bearing capacity.
*   **Settlement Control:** Reduces post-construction settlements by pre-compacting the soil.
*   **Backfill Behind Structures:** Can be used to compact large fills behind retaining walls or bridge abutments.

#### 1.4. Advantages

*   **Cost-Effective:** Can be more economical than other deep ground improvement techniques for certain applications.
*   **Rapid:** Relatively fast installation compared to some other methods.
*   **Deep Penetration:** Can densify significant depths of soil.
*   **Effective for Large Areas:** Well-suited for improving large sites.
*   **Reduces Liquefaction Risk:** Highly effective in mitigating liquefaction potential in sandy soils.

#### 1.5. Disadvantages and Limitations

*   **Noise and Vibration:** Generates significant noise and ground vibrations, which can be a concern for nearby structures or sensitive environments.
*   **Limited Effectiveness in Saturated Clays:** Less effective in highly saturated, soft cohesive soils where pore water pressure dissipation is slow, leading to limited volumetric strain.
*   **Surface Layer Improvement:** The very top few feet may not be effectively compacted, requiring a top layer of conventional compaction.
*   **Requires Careful Monitoring:** Effective implementation requires rigorous quality control and monitoring to ensure desired results.
*   **Environmental Concerns:** Noise pollution can be a significant issue.

#### 1.6. Factors Affecting DC Performance

*   **Tamper Weight and Drop Height:** Higher weight and drop height result in greater energy input.
*   **Soil Type and Properties:** Granular soils with low fines content are most responsive.
*   **Initial Void Ratio:** Denser initial conditions will result in less improvement.
*   **Water Content:** Optimal moisture content is important for effective energy transfer.
*   **Layer Thickness:** The depth of improvement is influenced by soil properties and energy applied.
*   **Number of Passes and Grid Pattern:** Crucial for achieving uniform densification.

### 2. Selection of Field Compaction Procedures

#### 2.1. Factors Influencing Selection

The choice of field compaction equipment and procedures depends on several critical factors:

*   **Soil Type:**
    *   **Cohesionless Soils (Sands, Gravels):** Vibratory rollers (smooth drum, sheepsfoot with vibration), plate compactors, tamping foot rollers. Dynamic compaction is also very effective.
    *   **Cohesive Soils (Clays, Silts):** Sheepsfoot rollers, tamping foot rollers, padfoot rollers. Vibratory rollers with sheepsfoot-like feet can also be used. Static rollers (smooth wheel) are less effective for cohesive soils.
*   **Moisture Content:**
    *   **Cohesionless Soils:** Water content plays a role in friction. Optimum moisture content for the soil is beneficial for compaction.
    *   **Cohesive Soils:** Crucial. Compaction is most effective when the soil is at or slightly above its optimum moisture content (Proctor optimum). Compacting drier than optimum can lead to shrinkage and cracking; wetter than optimum leads to a softer, less dense layer.
*   **Layer Thickness:**
    *   **Thin Layers (e.g., up to 0.5m):** Plate compactors, walk-behind vibratory rollers.
    *   **Medium Layers (e.g., 0.5m - 1m):** Ride-on vibratory rollers (smooth drum or sheepsfoot), tamping foot rollers.
    *   **Thick Layers (e.g., >1m):** Dynamic Compaction, heavy vibratory rollers, tamping foot rollers with multiple passes.
*   **Project Requirements:**
    *   **Desired Degree of Compaction:** Standard Proctor, Modified Proctor, or specific in-situ density targets.
    *   **Load Requirements:** For heavier loads, higher degrees of compaction are needed.
    *   **Settlement Criteria:** Stringent settlement requirements necessitate thorough compaction.
*   **Site Constraints:**
    *   **Access and Maneuverability:** Small or confined sites may require smaller equipment (e.g., plate compactors, walk-behind rollers).
    *   **Proximity to Structures:** Noise and vibration from heavy equipment (especially dynamic compaction and large vibratory rollers) can be a concern.
    *   **Topography:** Slopes may require specialized equipment or techniques.
*   **Equipment Availability and Cost:** The economic feasibility of using specific equipment.

#### 2.2. Common Compaction Equipment

*   **Smooth Wheel Rollers (Static):**
    *   **Description:** Heavy drums that apply static pressure.
    *   **Application:** Primarily for finishing layers of granular soils, breaking down clods, and compacting cohesive soils (less effective).
*   **Vibratory Rollers (Smooth Drum):**
    *   **Description:** Combines static weight with vibration. Vibration reduces internal friction, allowing particles to rearrange more easily.
    *   **Application:** Highly effective for granular soils (sands, gravels). Can be used on cohesive soils if vibration is set appropriately.
*   **Sheepsfoot Rollers:**
    *   **Description:** Drums with protruding, roughly rectangular feet (lugs).
    *   **Application:** Excellent for compacting cohesive soils (clays, silts). The feet penetrate the soil, knead it, and compact it from the bottom up. As compaction progresses, the feet are covered by the compacted soil, and finer-grained rollers are used.
*   **Tamping Foot / Padfoot Rollers:**
    *   **Description:** Similar to sheepsfoot but with shorter, broader, and often more numerous feet.
    *   **Application:** Effective for both cohesive and some granular soils. Provide good "walkout" (ability to compact without getting stuck).
*   **Pneumatic-Tired Rollers:**
    *   **Description:** Multiple rubber tires that provide a kneading action.
    *   **Application:** Good for a wide range of soils, especially when a smooth surface is required. Effective in compacting asphalt.
*   **Plate Compactors (Vibratory):**
    *   **Description:** Smaller, portable units that vibrate.
    *   **Application:** For shallow lifts and confined areas (trenches, around pipes).
*   **Rammers / Jumping Jacks:**
    *   **Description:** Impact compactors that deliver a sharp blow from a tamping foot.
    *   **Application:** For very cohesive soils or in trenches where rollers cannot access.

#### 2.3. Typical Compaction Procedures

1.  **Soil Preparation:**
    *   **Moisture Conditioning:** Adjusting the moisture content of the soil to near optimum by adding water or allowing it to dry.
    *   **Breakdown of Clods:** Ripping or crushing large soil clumps.
2.  **Spreading and Lifts:**
    *   Soil is spread in uniform, thin layers (lifts) of specified thickness (e.g., 150mm - 300mm loose for cohesive soils, potentially thicker for granular).
3.  **Compaction Passes:**
    *   The selected equipment makes multiple passes over the lift. The number of passes is determined by the equipment's capability and the required density.
    *   **Overlapping Passes:** Passes are typically overlapped to ensure uniform compaction.
4.  **Density Testing:**
    *   After each lift is compacted, density tests are performed to verify that the required degree of compaction has been achieved.
5.  **Layering:**
    *   Once a lift meets compaction requirements, the next lift is spread and compacted.

### 3. Compaction Quality Control (QC)

#### 3.1. Importance of QC

*   **Ensuring Design Performance:** Proper compaction is critical for achieving the intended strength, stiffness, and bearing capacity of the soil.
*   **Preventing Future Problems:** Inadequate compaction can lead to excessive settlements, instability, erosion, and structural damage.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** Identifying and correcting deficiencies during construction is far cheaper than rectifying problems after completion.
*   **Compliance with Specifications:** Ensures the project meets contractual and engineering requirements.

#### 3.2. Key Parameters to Control

*   **Lift Thickness:** The loose and compacted thickness of each layer.
*   **Moisture Content:** Maintaining moisture content close to optimum for the soil.
*   **Equipment Type and Operations:** Ensuring the correct equipment is used and operated correctly (e.g., correct number of passes, vibration settings).
*   **Degree of Compaction:** Achieving the specified density and/or shear strength.

#### 3.3. Field Testing Methods

**a) Density and Moisture Content Tests:**

*   **Nuclear Density Gauge (Troxler Gauge):**
    *   **Principle:** Emits gamma rays and neutrons into the soil. The number of backscattered gamma rays indicates the dry density, and the number of slowed-down neutrons indicates the moisture content.
    *   **Advantages:** Rapid, non-destructive (mostly), can be used on a wide range of soils.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires calibration, potential radiation safety concerns, accuracy can be affected by soil type and presence of large particles.
*   **Sand Cone Method ( a.k.a. Core Method ):**
    *   **Principle:** A known volume of dry, standardized sand is poured into a prepared hole in the compacted soil. The weight of the sand filling the hole, along with its known density, determines the in-situ volume. Soil from the hole is weighed, dried in an oven, and weighed again to determine the in-situ moisture content and total density. Dry density is then calculated.
    *   **Advantages:** Considered highly accurate, less dependent on soil type than nuclear gauges.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Time-consuming, labor-intensive, requires digging a hole.
*   **Water Balloon Method:**
    *   **Principle:** Similar to the sand cone method, but uses a balloon filled with water to measure the volume of the hole.
    *   **Advantages:** Can be used in softer soils where sand cones might be problematic.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be difficult to use in very coarse soils, potential for balloon breakage.
*   **Drive Cylinder Method:**
    *   **Principle:** A known volume cylinder is driven into the compacted soil. The soil inside is removed and weighed.
    *   **Advantages:** Simple, direct measurement of volume.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Only suitable for soils that can be easily penetrated by the cylinder, can disturb the soil near the edges of the hole.

**b) Other Tests:**

*   **Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP):**
    *   **Principle:** Measures the penetration resistance of the soil to a series of hammer blows.
    *   **Application:** Primarily used to assess the relative density of granular soils and the stiffness of cohesive soils. Can be correlated to CBR (California Bearing Ratio).
*   **Shear Strength Tests (e.g., Vane Shear Test):**
    *   **Principle:** Measures the shear strength of cohesive soils.
    *   **Application:** Used to confirm that the compacted cohesive soil has achieved the required shear strength.

#### 3.4. Compaction Specifications

*   **Relative Compaction (RC):**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of the in-situ dry density to the maximum dry density determined from a laboratory compaction test (e.g., Standard Proctor or Modified Proctor).
    *   **Formula:** $RC = (\rho_d - \text{in-situ}) / (\rho_d - \text{max lab}) \times 100\%$
    *   **Typical Specification:** e.g., 95% or 98% of Modified Proctor maximum dry density.
*   **In-situ Dry Density:** Sometimes, a minimum in-situ dry density is specified directly.
*   **Moisture Content:** Specifications often include a range for moisture content relative to the optimum moisture content (e.g., optimum +/- 2%).
*   **Bearing Capacity or Shear Strength:** For critical applications, minimum bearing capacity or shear strength values might be specified.

#### 3.5. Role of the Geotechnical Engineer

*   **Supervision:** Overseeing the compaction process.
*   **Testing:** Directing and interpreting field tests.
*   **Adjustments:** Recommending adjustments to procedures if tests indicate insufficient compaction (e.g., additional passes, moisture adjustments).
*   **Sign-off:** Approving compacted lifts before the next layer is placed.

### Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1: Dynamic Compaction**
*   **Principle:** Dynamic Compaction (DC) uses the energy of repeated high-impact drops of a heavy tamper (10-50 tons+) from significant heights (10-30m) to densify loose granular soils or reduce the void ratio of cohesive soils. The high-energy impact generates seismic waves and pore water pressure, causing a rapid rearrangement and densification of soil particles.
*   **Applications:**
    1.  **Densification of loose sand and gravel fills** for improved bearing capacity and settlement control.
    2.  **Reduction of liquefaction potential** in sandy soils, especially in seismic zones.
    3.  **Compacting thick layers of fill material** behind retaining walls or bridge abutments where conventional equipment may have limited access or effectiveness.
    4.  **Ground improvement for large industrial facilities** or landfills.

**Answer 2: Selection of Field Compaction Procedures**
*   **Appropriate Equipment:** For a silty clay soil (cohesive), a **Sheepsfoot Roller** or a **Tamping Foot Roller** would be most appropriate for compacting the lower layers of the embankment.
    *   **Reasoning:** These rollers have feet that penetrate the cohesive soil, kneading it and compacting it from the bottom up. This is essential for achieving good density in cohesive materials. As the soil becomes denser, the feet can "walk out" of the surface, allowing for continued effective compaction.
*   **Adjusting Moisture Content (Too Wet):** If the fill material is too wet of optimum, it needs to be dried. This can be achieved by:
    1.  **Aeration/Drying:** Spreading the soil thinly and allowing it to air dry, possibly with the aid of scarifying or tilling to increase surface area and promote evaporation.
    2.  **Mixing with Dry Material:** If available, mixing the wet soil with a drier granular material can help absorb excess moisture.
    3.  **Reducing Lift Thickness:** Compacting thinner lifts can sometimes help when the material is too wet, as it reduces the load and allows for better soil rearrangement.

**Answer 3: Compaction Quality Control**
a) Two common field tests for in-situ dry density and moisture content are:
    1.  **Nuclear Density Gauge (Troxler Gauge)**
    2.  **Sand Cone Method (Core Method)**

b) **Principle of the Sand Cone Method:**
    1.  A small hole is excavated in the compacted soil layer.
    2.  The excavated soil is weighed, then dried in an oven to determine its moisture content and calculate the in-situ wet density and dry density.
    3.  A conical mould filled with a standardized, dry, fine sand of known density is placed over the hole. The sand is allowed to flow, filling the hole and the cone.
    4.  The volume of the hole is calculated from the weight of the sand used to fill it and the known density of the sand.

c) **Relative Compaction (RC):**
    *   Relative Compaction is the ratio of the in-situ dry density achieved in the field to the maximum dry density determined from a laboratory compaction test (like the Modified Proctor test), expressed as a percentage.
    *   **Formula:**
        $RC = \left( \frac{\text{In-situ Dry Density}}{\text{Maximum Dry Density (Lab)}} \right) \times 100\%$
    *   **Example Specification:** A common specification is 95% RC, meaning the field dry density must be at least 95% of the lab-determined maximum dry density.

**Answer 4: Scenario Analysis**
a) **Most Effective Ground Improvement Technique:**
    *   **Dynamic Compaction (DC)** is highly suitable for densifying loose sandy fill and reducing liquefaction potential. Its high-energy impact effectively rearranges sand particles, increasing density and shear strength.

b) **Primary Concerns/Drawbacks of DC near Sensitive Structures:**
    1.  **Ground Vibrations:** The high-energy impacts generate significant ground vibrations. If these vibrations are too strong, they can cause damage to nearby existing structures (cracking of walls, settlement of foundations).
    2.  **Noise Pollution:** DC is an extremely noisy operation, which can be a significant issue if there are occupied buildings or residential areas nearby.
    3.  **Pore Water Pressure Build-up:** While DC is effective in sands, in marginal conditions or with a higher fines content, the rapid pore water pressure generation could temporarily reduce soil strength if not managed properly.
    4.  **Settlement of Adjacent Structures:** The vibrations can also induce temporary or even permanent settlements in nearby structures due to soil densification or displacement.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Dynamic Compaction:** Best for loose sands/gravels, deep layers, liquefaction mitigation. Be mindful of noise/vibration.
*   **Field Compaction Selection:** Always consider soil type, moisture content, layer thickness, and project requirements.
*   **Cohesionless Soils:** Vibratory rollers are highly effective.
*   **Cohesive Soils:** Sheepsfoot/Tamping foot rollers are crucial; moisture control is paramount.
*   **Quality Control is Non-Negotiable:** It's the backbone of successful compaction.
*   **Field Testing:** Nuclear gauges and sand cones are standard for density/moisture.
*   **Relative Compaction:** The most common specification for density.
*   **Geotechnical Engineer's Role:** Essential for oversight, testing, and ensuring compliance.
*   **Moisture is Key:** For cohesive soils, compaction is most effective at or slightly above optimum moisture content.
