# DESIGN OF STEEL STRUCTURES - MODULE 1: INTRODUCTION TO STEEL AND STEEL STRUCTURES

## TOPIC: TYPE OF FASTENERS - BOLTS AND WELDS

---

### LEARNING OUTCOMES:

Upon successful completion of this topic, you will be able to:

*   Identify and differentiate between various types of bolts used in steel structures.
*   Understand the functions and types of welds used for connecting steel members.
*   Recognize the advantages and disadvantages of using bolts and welds as fasteners.
*   Explain the basic principles of load transfer through bolted and welded connections.

### 1. INTRODUCTION TO FASTENERS IN STEEL STRUCTURES

*   **Definition:** Fasteners are devices used to join two or more structural members together. In steel structures, they are crucial for creating monolithic and load-bearing connections.
*   **Purpose:**
    *   To transfer forces (axial, shear, bending moment) between connected members.
    *   To maintain the geometry and stability of the structure.
    *   To allow for ease of fabrication, erection, and dismantling (especially with bolted connections).
*   **Key Considerations for Selection:**
    *   Type and magnitude of loads.
    *   Material properties of connected members.
    *   Joint configuration and accessibility.
    *   Cost and speed of construction.
    *   Environmental conditions.
    *   Maintenance requirements.

### 2. BOLTS AS FASTENERS

Bolts are removable threaded fasteners that create a clamping force to hold members together. They are widely used for their ease of installation and ability to be disassembled.

#### 2.1. Types of Structural Bolts:

##### 2.1.1. Black Bolts (Ordinary or Unfinished Bolts):

*   **Description:** These are the simplest type of bolts, manufactured with a nominal thread fit. They are typically used in less critical connections where precise alignment and high strength are not paramount.
*   **Threads:** Coarse or fine threads.
*   **Material:** Generally lower strength steel.
*   **Usage:** In connections where shear forces are moderate and bearing stresses are controlled. Often used for non-load-bearing connections or temporary bracing.
*   **Limitations:** Prone to loosening under vibration, lower shear and bearing capacity compared to high-strength bolts.

##### 2.1.2. High-Strength Bolts (HS Bolts):

*   **Description:** These bolts are made from higher strength steel and are designed to be tightened to a specific pre-tension. This pre-tension is critical for creating a rigid and strong connection that can resist both shear and tensile forces effectively.
*   **Material:** Higher tensile strength steel grades (e.g., Grade 4.6, 4.8, 8.8, 10.9 as per IS 1367, or ASTM A325, A490).
*   **Components:** Bolt, nut, and washers.
*   **Tightening:** Achieved using calibrated torque wrenches, tension control bolts (TC bolts), or direct tension indicators (DTIs).
*   **Function:**
    *   **Friction Grip Connections:** The high pre-tension creates significant clamping force between the connected members. Friction between the faying surfaces resists shear. This is the preferred method for high-strength bolts.
    *   **Bearing Type Connections:** The bolt shank resists shear by bearing against the edges of the holes in the connected members. This is less efficient than friction grip but is still a valid mode of resistance.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Higher strength and stiffness.
    *   Reduced slip under service loads (in friction grip).
    *   Can be used in thinner plates due to less reliance on bearing.
    *   Faster erection time compared to field welding.
    *   Allow for pre-tensioning, which is crucial for fatigue resistance.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Require specialized installation tools and techniques.
    *   Higher initial cost per bolt.
    *   Washing the faying surfaces to achieve the required coefficient of friction is necessary for friction grip.

#### 2.2. Bolt Grades and Standards (Commonly referred to):

*   **IS Standards (India):**
    *   **Grade 4.6:** Low tensile strength, used for black bolts.
    *   **Grade 4.8:** Higher tensile strength than 4.6.
    *   **Grade 8.8:** High tensile strength, commonly used for high-strength bolts in pre-tensioned or bearing applications.
    *   **Grade 10.9:** Very high tensile strength, used for demanding applications.
*   **ASTM Standards (USA):**
    *   **A325:** High-strength bolts (equivalent to approx. Grade 8.8).
    *   **A490:** Very high-strength bolts (equivalent to approx. Grade 10.9).

*   **Important Point:** The two-digit number in the grade (e.g., 4.6) indicates:
    *   The first digit (4) is approximately 1/100th of the ultimate tensile strength in MPa (e.g., 400 MPa).
    *   The second digit (6) is 10 times the ratio of yield strength to ultimate tensile strength (e.g., yield strength is 0.6 times ultimate tensile strength).

#### 2.3. Holes for Bolts:

*   **Clearance Holes:** Standard diameter, providing a small gap between the bolt shank and the edge of the hole. This is typical for black bolts and some HS bolt applications.
*   **Close Tolerance Holes:** Precisely manufactured to match the bolt diameter, used for high-strength bolts in friction grip connections to minimize slip.
*   **Oversized Holes:** Larger than standard clearance holes, used to allow for adjustments during erection, especially in complex geometries or when dealing with slight inaccuracies. Can reduce the effective bearing area and shear strength.
*   **Slotted Holes:** Elongated holes, used to accommodate significant movement or thermal expansion.

#### 2.4. Load Transfer Mechanism (Bolted Connections):

*   **Shear Resistance:**
    *   **Shear Yielding:** The bolt material yields in shear.
    *   **Shear Fracture:** The bolt material fractures due to excessive shear stress.
    *   **Bearing:** The bolt bears against the edge of the hole, causing deformation or failure of the connected plate or bolt.
*   **Tensile Resistance:**
    *   **Tension Yielding:** The bolt material yields in tension.
    *   **Tension Fracture:** The bolt material fractures in tension, usually at the threads.
    *   **Shear Lag:** Stress concentration at the ends of the connected members can lead to premature failure.
*   **Friction Grip:** In HS bolts, the clamping force creates a frictional resistance between the faying surfaces, preventing slip. This friction is the primary mode of shear resistance in friction grip connections.

#### 2.5. Common Bolted Joints:

*   **Butt Joints:** Members are joined end-to-end.
*   **Lap Joints:** Members overlap.
*   **T-Joints:** One member is perpendicular to another.
*   **Corner Joints:** Members meet at corners.

### 3. WELDS AS FASTENERS

Welding is a process that joins materials by melting and fusing them together, often with the addition of a filler material. It creates a permanent, monolithic connection.

#### 3.1. Types of Welds:

##### 3.1.1. Fillet Welds:

*   **Description:** The most common type of weld, forming a triangular cross-section. They are typically used to join two members at an angle to each other (e.g., T-joints, lap joints).
*   **Cross-Section:** A right-angled triangle.
*   **Size:** Designated by the "throat thickness" (the shortest distance from the root to the face of the weld).
*   **Usage:** Connecting flanges to webs, connecting angles to plates, lap joints.
*   **Types of Fillet Welds:**
    *   **Single Fillet Weld:** Applied to one side of the joint.
    *   **Double Fillet Weld:** Applied to both sides of the joint for increased strength.
    *   **Reverse-Intermittent Fillet Weld:** Welds applied intermittently on opposite sides of the joint.
    *   **Chain-Intermittent Fillet Weld:** Welds applied intermittently on the same side of the joint.

##### 3.1.2. Butt Welds (or Groove Welds):

*   **Description:** Used to join two members end-to-end, with the weld metal filling the gap between them. The edges of the members are prepared (e.g., beveled) to allow for full penetration.
*   **Types of Butt Welds based on Edge Preparation:**
    *   **Square Groove:** For thin plates, no beveling.
    *   **Single V-Groove:** One member is beveled.
    *   **Double V-Groove:** Both members are beveled on opposite sides.
    *   **Single U-Groove:** A U-shaped groove on one member.
    *   **Double U-Groove:** A U-shaped groove on both members.
    *   **Single Bevel-Groove:** Similar to V-groove but only one side is prepared.
    *   **Double Bevel-Groove:** Similar to V-groove but both sides are prepared.
*   **Usage:** Joining structural plates, beams, columns where direct end-to-end connection is required and high strength is needed.
*   **Key Concept:** **Full Penetration Weld** is essential for butt welds to achieve the full strength of the base metal.

##### 3.1.3. Plug Welds and Slot Welds:

*   **Description:** Used to join overlapping members where fillet or butt welds are not practical. Holes are made in the outer member, and welds are made within these holes (plug welds) or slots (slot welds) to join the two members.
*   **Usage:** Reinforcing lap joints, preventing buckling of the inner member in lap joints.

##### 3.1.4. Arc Spot Welds (or Projection Welds):

*   **Description:** Similar to plug welds but formed without a complete hole, creating a "spot" of weld.
*   **Usage:** Primarily for connecting thin sheets or in situations where aesthetic appearance is important. Less common in heavy structural steel.

#### 3.2. Welding Processes (Commonly Used):

*   **Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) / Manual Metal Arc Welding (MMAW):** Uses a coated electrode that melts and provides filler metal and shielding gas. Versatile and widely used.
*   **Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW) / Metal Inert Gas (MIG) Welding:** Uses a continuous wire electrode and a shielding gas. Faster than SMAW.
*   **Flux-Cored Arc Welding (FCAW):** Similar to GMAW but uses a tubular wire electrode containing flux. Can be used for thicker materials and in windy conditions.
*   **Submerged Arc Welding (SAW):** Uses a continuous wire electrode and a granular flux that covers the arc. High deposition rates, used for heavy sections and shop fabrication.

#### 3.3. Load Transfer Mechanism (Welded Connections):

*   **Fillet Welds:** Primarily resist shear by the strength of the weld throat. They also resist tension and bending moment through the combined strength of the weld and the base metal.
*   **Butt Welds:** Designed for full penetration, they transfer loads directly through the weld metal, behaving similarly to the base metal. Resistance is primarily through tensile, compressive, and shear stresses in the weld.

#### 3.4. Advantages and Disadvantages of Welding:

*   **Advantages:**
    *   **Monolithic Connection:** Creates a continuous and rigid joint.
    *   **Aesthetically Pleasing:** Can result in smoother connections compared to bolted joints.
    *   **Higher Efficiency:** Can achieve full strength of the connected members if designed and executed properly (especially butt welds).
    *   **No Slip:** Welded connections are generally rigid and do not exhibit slip under service loads.
    *   **Less Material:** Often requires fewer fastening elements compared to bolted connections.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Permanent Connection:** Difficult and costly to dismantle or modify.
    *   **Residual Stresses and Distortion:** The heating and cooling process can introduce residual stresses and distortions in the connected members, requiring careful control.
    *   **Quality Control:** Requires skilled welders and stringent inspection (visual, NDT) to ensure weld integrity.
    *   **Field Welding Challenges:** Can be affected by weather conditions (wind, rain), accessibility, and requires portable equipment.
    *   **Not Suitable for Certain Environments:** May require special welding procedures in extremely cold or humid conditions.

### 4. COMPARISON BETWEEN BOLTS AND WELDS

| Feature               | Bolts                                      | Welds                                         |
| :-------------------- | :----------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- |
| **Connection Type**   | Semi-rigid, detachable                     | Rigid, permanent                              |
| **Installation**      | Easier, faster erection, less skilled labor | Requires skilled labor, more complex process  |
| **Dismantling**       | Easy                                       | Difficult, costly                             |
| **Residual Stress**   | Lower (due to clamping)                    | Higher (due to heating/cooling)               |
| **Distortion**        | Minimal                                    | Can be significant                            |
| **Noise**             | Moderate (during tightening)               | Low                                           |
| **Appearance**        | Visible bolt heads and nuts                | Can be aesthetically cleaner                  |
| **Quality Control**   | Easier (visual inspection, torque check)   | Requires skilled inspection (visual, NDT)     |
| **Cost**              | Initial cost per fastener higher, overall can be competitive | Lower material cost per connection, labor intensive |
| **Load Transfer**     | Shear, tension, bearing, friction          | Shear, tension, bending moment                |
| **Fatigue Resistance**| Generally good (especially HS bolts)       | Can be susceptible to fatigue at weld defects |

### 5. PRACTICE QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS

**Question 1:** Differentiate between black bolts and high-strength bolts used in steel structures.

**Answer:**
*   **Black Bolts (Ordinary/Unfinished):** Lower strength steel, used for less critical connections, no pre-tensioning required, susceptible to loosening under vibration.
*   **High-Strength Bolts (HS Bolts):** Higher strength steel, tightened to a specific pre-tension, create clamping force for friction grip or resist shear by bearing, provide more rigid and stronger connections, reduce slip.

**Question 2:** What is the primary mode of shear resistance in a friction grip connection using high-strength bolts?

**Answer:** The primary mode of shear resistance in a friction grip connection is **friction** between the faying surfaces, created by the high pre-tension in the bolts.

**Question 3:** List three common types of welds used in structural steel connections and briefly describe their application.

**Answer:**
1.  **Fillet Welds:** Used to join members at an angle (T-joints, lap joints). Applied to the sides of the joint.
2.  **Butt Welds (Groove Welds):** Used to join members end-to-end. Requires edge preparation for full penetration and transfers loads directly.
3.  **Plug Welds/Slot Welds:** Used to join overlapping members by welding through holes or slots in the outer member.

**Question 4:** What are the main advantages of using welded connections over bolted connections?

**Answer:** Welded connections offer a monolithic and rigid joint, are often aesthetically cleaner, have no slip under service loads, and can achieve full strength if properly executed. They are also generally more efficient in terms of material usage for the connection itself.

**Question 5:** A lap joint is to be made using fillet welds. If the required throat thickness is 6 mm and the effective length of the weld is 100 mm on each side of the overlap, calculate the total effective throat area of the fillet welds.

**Answer:**
*   Throat thickness = 6 mm
*   Effective length of weld per side = 100 mm
*   Number of sides with weld = 2 (assuming a double fillet weld is used for sufficient strength)
*   Total effective throat area = 2 * (throat thickness * effective length)
*   Total effective throat area = 2 * (6 mm * 100 mm) = 1200 mm²

### 6. IMPORTANT POINTS TO REMEMBER

*   **Bolt Grade:** The grade of the bolt (e.g., 4.6, 8.8) dictates its tensile strength and is crucial for connection design.
*   **Pre-tension:** High-strength bolts rely on significant pre-tension for effective performance, especially in friction grip connections.
*   **Friction Grip vs. Bearing Type:** Understand the difference in how these connections transfer shear loads and the implications for design.
*   **Weld Type:** The type of weld (fillet, butt) and its size (throat thickness) determine the connection's strength.
*   **Full Penetration:** Essential for butt welds to ensure they can carry loads as effectively as the base metal.
*   **Quality Control:** Both bolted and welded connections require diligent quality control during fabrication and erection to ensure safety and performance.
*   **Residual Stresses:** Be aware that welding introduces residual stresses, which can affect the behavior of the structure, particularly under fatigue or brittle fracture conditions.
*   **Code Provisions:** Always refer to relevant building codes and standards (e.g., IS codes in India, AISC in the US) for specific design requirements, material properties, and workmanship standards.
