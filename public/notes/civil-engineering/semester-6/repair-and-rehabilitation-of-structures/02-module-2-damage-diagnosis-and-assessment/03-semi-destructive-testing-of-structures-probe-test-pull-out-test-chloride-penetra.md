# REPAIR AND REHABILITATION OF STRUCTURES

## Module 2: Damage Diagnosis and Assessment

### Topic: Semi-Destructive Testing of Structures

This module focuses on understanding the various methods used to diagnose and assess the condition of existing structures, particularly employing semi-destructive testing techniques. These methods provide valuable information about the material properties and the extent of deterioration without causing significant damage to the structure.

---

### **Learning Outcomes:**

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Understand the principles and applications of various semi-destructive testing methods.
*   Explain the procedures for performing probe tests, pull-out tests, chloride penetration tests, and carbonation depth testing.
*   Describe methods for measuring corrosion activity and performing core tests.
*   Interpret the results obtained from these tests for damage assessment.
*   Identify the advantages and limitations of each semi-destructive testing method.
*   Select appropriate semi-destructive testing methods based on the type of damage and structural element.

### **1. Probe Test**

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Probe Test:** A simple, visual, and qualitative method to assess the integrity of concrete. It involves inserting a pointed tool (probe) into the concrete surface to detect soft, deteriorated, or delaminated areas.
*   **Purpose:** To identify localized areas of weak or deteriorated concrete that may not be apparent from visual inspection alone.
*   **Principle:** Relies on the difference in resistance encountered when probing different zones of concrete. Sound concrete offers high resistance, while deteriorated concrete offers low resistance.

**Procedure:**

1.  **Visual Inspection:** Start with a thorough visual inspection of the concrete surface for obvious signs of cracking, spalling, or efflorescence.
2.  **Probing:** A sharp, hardened steel probe (e.g., a masonry chisel or a specialized probe tool) is used.
3.  **Application of Force:** The probe is driven into the concrete with moderate force, typically with a hammer.
4.  **Observation:** The depth of penetration and the resistance felt are observed.
    *   **Sound Concrete:** Little to no penetration, high resistance.
    *   **Deteriorated Concrete:** Significant penetration, low resistance.
    *   **Cracked/Delaminated Areas:** The probe may easily enter cracks or voids.

**Examples:**

*   Checking the surface of a concrete beam to identify spalled areas caused by reinforcement corrosion.
*   Assessing the integrity of concrete in a bridge deck where de-icing salts have been applied.

**Advantages:**

*   Simple, quick, and inexpensive.
*   Requires minimal specialized equipment.
*   Provides an immediate indication of surface deterioration.

**Limitations:**

*   Highly subjective and qualitative.
*   Limited to surface and near-surface assessment.
*   Not suitable for quantifying the extent of deterioration.
*   Can be influenced by the operator's experience.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Use a consistent force when probing.
*   Multiple probes should be made in a grid pattern to cover the area of interest.
*   Combine with visual inspection for a more reliable assessment.

### **2. Pull-Out Test**

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Pull-Out Test:** A semi-destructive test used to determine the in-situ compressive strength of concrete. It measures the force required to pull out a specially designed embedded element (pull-out disc or bolt) from the concrete.
*   **Purpose:** To estimate the compressive strength of hardened concrete in existing structures.
*   **Principle:** The force required to pull out an embedded element is related to the bond strength between the concrete and the element, which in turn is correlated with the compressive strength of the concrete.

**Types of Pull-Out Tests:**

*   **Cylindrical Inserts (e.g., Densitometer):** A cylindrical insert with a flanged base is cast into the concrete or embedded during construction. The test involves pulling the insert using a hydraulic jack.
*   **Surface-Mounted Devices (e.g., Pull-Off Test - though technically a different category, the principle is similar in concept for surface strength):** While pull-off tests are typically for coatings, internal pull-out tests are for concrete strength. However, some variations involve internal anchors.
*   **Embedded Steel Bars/Bolts:** A standard steel bolt or bar with a specific head or anchor is embedded.

**Procedure (General for embedded inserts):**

1.  **Embedment:** A pull-out test element (e.g., a steel disc with a threaded rod) is embedded in the concrete at the time of casting, or a pre-drilled hole is used for post-construction testing. The embedment depth is critical.
2.  **Curing:** The concrete is allowed to cure.
3.  **Loading:** A calibrated hydraulic jack is attached to the exposed end of the pull-out element.
4.  **Pulling Force:** The jack applies a gradually increasing tensile force to the element.
5.  **Measurement:** The force at which the element pulls out or failure occurs is recorded.
6.  **Correlation:** The measured pull-out force is converted to an estimated compressive strength using empirical calibration curves provided by the manufacturer or established standards.

**Examples:**

*   Estimating the strength of concrete in a newly constructed foundation where cylinder breaks are not feasible or representative.
*   Assessing the strength of concrete in an existing bridge pier to determine if it meets design requirements.

**Advantages:**

*   Provides a direct measure of in-situ concrete strength.
*   Can be performed on hardened concrete.
*   Relatively simple to perform.

**Limitations:**

*   Requires embedment during construction or pre-drilled holes.
*   Results are empirical and depend on calibration.
*   The failure mode can be complex (bond failure, concrete cone failure).
*   Can cause localized damage to the concrete surface.
*   Affected by the depth of embedment and the size of the insert.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Accurate calibration of the loading device is crucial.
*   The embedment depth and distance from edges/cracks significantly affect results.
*   Refer to manufacturer's data or relevant standards for conversion of pull-out force to compressive strength.

### **3. Chloride Penetration Test**

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Chloride Penetration Test:** A method to determine the depth to which chloride ions have penetrated into concrete. High concentrations of chlorides can cause corrosion of reinforcing steel.
*   **Purpose:** To assess the risk of reinforcement corrosion, particularly in structures exposed to marine environments or de-icing salts.
*   **Principle:** Concrete samples are taken, and the chloride content at various depths is analyzed using chemical titration or instrumental methods.

**Procedure:**

1.  **Sample Collection:**
    *   **Drilling Dust:** Small amounts of concrete dust are collected at different depths using a small drill bit. This is the most common method.
    *   **Core Samples:** Larger concrete cores are extracted and then ground to obtain dust at specific depths.
2.  **Chloride Extraction:** The collected dust is mixed with a solvent (e.g., distilled water, nitric acid) to extract the soluble chlorides.
3.  **Chloride Analysis:** The concentration of chloride ions in the extracted solution is measured using:
    *   **Titration:** Chemical titration with silver nitrate is a common method.
    *   **Colorimetric Methods:** Using indicators that change color in the presence of chloride ions.
    *   **Ion-Selective Electrodes (ISE):** Provides a more direct and accurate measurement.
    *   **X-ray Fluorescence (XRF):** Can also be used for elemental analysis, including chloride.
4.  **Plotting and Interpretation:** The chloride concentration is plotted against the depth of penetration. The depth at which the chloride concentration exceeds a critical threshold (typically around 0.4% by weight of cement for reinforced concrete) is determined.

**Examples:**

*   Assessing the condition of concrete in a coastal bridge pier where seawater splash is a concern.
*   Evaluating the potential for rebar corrosion in a concrete structure exposed to de-icing salts on roads.

**Advantages:**

*   Quantifies the extent of chloride ingress.
*   Directly related to the risk of reinforcement corrosion.
*   Can be performed on existing structures.

**Limitations:**

*   Requires drilling or core extraction, causing localized damage.
*   Chloride distribution can be uneven.
*   Laboratory analysis is required, which can be time-consuming and costly.
*   The accuracy depends on the sampling depth interval and extraction efficiency.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The critical chloride threshold varies based on standards and the presence of corrosion inhibitors.
*   A systematic sampling strategy is essential for reliable results.
*   Consider the depth of carbonation when interpreting chloride penetration results, as it affects the pH and can influence corrosion initiation.

### **4. Carbonation**

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Carbonation:** A chemical process where atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) reacts with calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) in hydrated cement paste to form calcium carbonate (CaCO3).
*   **Purpose of Testing:** To determine the depth of carbonation in concrete. Carbonation reduces the alkalinity of the concrete, which protects the reinforcing steel from corrosion. When the carbonation front reaches the rebar, the passive layer breaks down, and corrosion can initiate if moisture and oxygen are present.
*   **Principle:** Carbonated concrete has a lower pH (around 8-9) compared to uncarbonated concrete (pH 12.5-13.5). This pH difference is detected using an indicator.

**5. Carbonation Depth Testing**

**Procedure:**

1.  **Surface Preparation:** Clean the concrete surface to remove any loose material or contaminants.
2.  **Exposure of Fresh Concrete:**
    *   **For Existing Structures:** A fresh concrete surface is exposed by chipping or grinding a small area.
    *   **For Freshly Cast Concrete:** If assessing recent work, a freshly broken edge or a core sample can be used.
3.  **Application of Phenolphthalein Indicator:** A solution of phenolphthalein indicator (typically 1% in alcohol) is sprayed or brushed onto the freshly exposed concrete surface.
4.  **Observation and Measurement:**
    *   **Uncarbonated Concrete:** The phenolphthalein will turn pink or purple, indicating a high pH.
    *   **Carbonated Concrete:** The phenolphthalein will remain colorless, indicating a reduced pH.
5.  **Measurement of Carbonation Depth:** The depth of the colorless zone (carbonated layer) is measured from the concrete surface using a ruler or caliper. This is typically done at multiple locations to obtain an average.

**Examples:**

*   Assessing the risk of corrosion in a concrete facade of an older building exposed to urban air.
*   Checking the effectiveness of a protective coating by testing for carbonation depth in underlying concrete.

**Advantages:**

*   Simple, inexpensive, and quick to perform.
*   Provides a clear visual indication of carbonation.
*   Requires minimal equipment.

**Limitations:**

*   Requires exposure of fresh concrete.
*   The indicator can be affected by other substances in the concrete.
*   Only indicates the extent of carbonation, not the underlying causes of deterioration.
*   Accuracy can be affected by surface irregularities or moisture content.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Ensure the indicator solution is fresh and at the correct concentration.
*   Perform measurements on a freshly broken surface for best results.
*   The rate of carbonation is influenced by concrete quality, environmental conditions (CO2 concentration, humidity), and cover depth.

### **6. Corrosion Activity Measurement**

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Corrosion Activity Measurement:** Techniques used to assess the likelihood or rate of active corrosion occurring in reinforcing steel embedded within concrete.
*   **Purpose:** To identify whether corrosion is active and to estimate its rate, which is crucial for prioritizing repair strategies.
*   **Principle:** Corrosion is an electrochemical process. These tests measure electrical potentials or currents related to the electrochemical activity of the steel.

**Common Methods:**

*   **Half-Cell Potential Measurement (Corrosion Potential):**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the likelihood of active corrosion.
    *   **Principle:** Measures the potential difference between the reinforcing steel (cathode) and a reference electrode (e.g., copper/copper sulfate electrode - CSE) placed on the concrete surface.
    *   **Procedure:** A saturated CSE is placed on the moist concrete surface, and the potential difference is measured with a high-impedance voltmeter.
    *   **Interpretation:** Higher negative potentials (e.g., less than -350 mV vs CSE) indicate a higher probability of active corrosion.
    *   **Advantages:** Non-destructive, provides a broad assessment of corrosion risk over a large area.
    *   **Limitations:** Qualitative/semi-quantitative, affected by moisture content, concrete resistivity, and presence of other conductive materials.

*   **Linear Polarization Resistance (LPR) Measurement:**
    *   **Purpose:** To estimate the rate of corrosion (corrosion current density).
    *   **Principle:** Applies a small potential perturbation to the reinforcing steel and measures the resulting current response. The resistance to this polarization is inversely proportional to the corrosion rate.
    *   **Procedure:** Requires a more sophisticated setup with a reference electrode, counter electrode, and working electrode (reinforcing steel).
    *   **Interpretation:** Lower polarization resistance values indicate higher corrosion rates.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides a quantitative measure of corrosion rate.
    *   **Limitations:** More complex and time-consuming, requires direct electrical connection to the rebar, localized measurements.

*   **Galvanostatic Pulse (GSP) / Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS):**
    *   **Purpose:** Similar to LPR, to quantify corrosion rates.
    *   **Principle:** Uses different electrochemical techniques to measure the impedance of the steel-concrete interface, which is related to corrosion activity.
    *   **Advantages:** Can provide more detailed information about the corrosion process.
    *   **Limitations:** Requires specialized equipment and expertise.

**Examples:**

*   Assessing a bridge deck for active corrosion to guide decisions on patching or overlay.
*   Monitoring the effectiveness of corrosion inhibitors or cathodic protection systems.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Moisture is essential for electrochemical corrosion. Ensure the concrete surface is adequately moist for accurate potential measurements.
*   A grid pattern of measurements is recommended for comprehensive assessment.
*   Combine potential measurements with resistivity tests for a more reliable diagnosis.

### **7. Core Test**

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Core Test:** A destructive testing method that involves extracting cylindrical samples (cores) from hardened concrete for laboratory analysis.
*   **Purpose:** To obtain representative samples of concrete for various laboratory tests to determine its properties and assess deterioration.
*   **Principle:** Physically removing a section of the structure to examine its internal condition and perform standardized tests.

**Common Laboratory Tests Performed on Cores:**

*   **Compressive Strength Test:** The most common test, where cores are capped and tested in a compression testing machine to determine their in-situ compressive strength. This is a benchmark for quality control and strength assessment.
*   **Tensile Strength Tests (e.g., Split Tensile Strength, Flexural Strength):** To determine the concrete's resistance to tensile stresses.
*   **Modulus of Elasticity:** To assess the stiffness of the concrete.
*   **Density/Unit Weight:** To determine the mass per unit volume, indicating compaction and potential for voids.
*   **Chloride Content Analysis:** As described previously, for detailed chloride profiling.
*   **Sulfate Content Analysis:** To detect the presence of sulfates that can cause expansive reactions and deterioration.
*   **Petrographic Analysis:** Microscopic examination of the concrete constituents (cement, aggregates, admixtures) and microstructure to identify causes of deterioration (e.g., alkali-aggregate reaction, freeze-thaw damage, fire damage).
*   **Carbonation Depth Measurement:** Performed on the freshly exposed core surface.
*   **Air Void Analysis:** To assess the presence and characteristics of entrained air, important for freeze-thaw resistance.

**Procedure:**

1.  **Core Drilling:** A diamond-tipped core drill is used to extract cylindrical samples from the structure. The diameter and length of the core are important parameters.
2.  **Sample Protection:** The extracted cores are carefully handled and transported to the laboratory.
3.  **Laboratory Testing:** The cores are prepared (e.g., capping for strength tests) and subjected to various standardized laboratory tests.

**Examples:**

*   Determining the compressive strength of concrete in an existing bridge pier to assess its load-carrying capacity.
*   Performing petrographic analysis on a core from a spalled concrete pavement to identify the cause of failure (e.g., freeze-thaw attack).

**Advantages:**

*   Provides direct and accurate determination of concrete properties.
*   Allows for detailed laboratory analysis of material constituents and microstructure.
*   Crucial for forensic investigations and failure analysis.

**Limitations:**

*   Destructive, causing permanent damage to the structure.
*   Requires specialized drilling equipment and laboratory facilities.
*   Can be expensive and time-consuming.
*   The representativeness of the core depends on proper sampling location and technique.
*   Core strength can be lower than cylinder strength due to damage during drilling and confinement effects.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The location and orientation of core extraction should be carefully selected to be representative of the element being assessed.
*   Cores for strength testing should be drilled perpendicular to the surface where possible.
*   Proper capping of cores is essential for accurate compressive strength testing.
*   Consider the conversion factors between core strength and cylinder strength, as cores may exhibit slightly lower strength due to the drilling process.

### **Practice Questions & Exercises:**

1.  **Question:** You are assessing a concrete bridge deck exposed to de-icing salts. Which semi-destructive tests would be most appropriate for determining the risk of reinforcement corrosion? Explain your reasoning.
    *   **Answer:** Chloride penetration test (to quantify chloride ingress) and corrosion potential (half-cell potential) measurement (to assess if corrosion is active). Carbonation depth testing might also be relevant as carbonation reduces alkalinity, but chloride ingress is the primary driver of corrosion in this scenario.

2.  **Question:** Describe the principle behind the pull-out test and explain why it is considered semi-destructive.
    *   **Answer:** The pull-out test measures the force required to extract an embedded element, which is correlated to the concrete's compressive strength. It is semi-destructive because it causes localized damage to the concrete surface where the element is pulled out, but it doesn't compromise the overall structural integrity of the element being tested, unlike a fully destructive test like taking a large chunk of the structure.

3.  **Question:** What is the primary purpose of using phenolphthalein indicator in carbonation depth testing, and what color change indicates a carbonated zone?
    *   **Answer:** Phenolphthalein is an acid-base indicator. Its primary purpose is to differentiate between carbonated concrete (which has a lower pH due to reaction with CO2) and uncarbonated concrete (which has a high, alkaline pH due to calcium hydroxide). A colorless result indicates a carbonated zone, while a pink or purple color indicates uncarbonated concrete.

4.  **Question:** List three advantages and three limitations of the core test.
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Advantages:** Provides direct and accurate property determination, allows detailed laboratory analysis, crucial for forensic investigations.
        *   **Limitations:** Destructive, requires specialized equipment and labs, can be expensive and time-consuming, representativeness depends on sampling.

5.  **Question:** Explain the relationship between carbonation and reinforcement corrosion.
    *   **Answer:** Uncarbonated concrete has a high pH (around 12.5-13.5) due to the presence of calcium hydroxide. This high alkalinity forms a passive protective film on the reinforcing steel, preventing corrosion. Carbonation reduces the pH of the concrete. When the carbonation front reaches the reinforcing steel, the passive film breaks down, and if moisture and oxygen are present, reinforcement corrosion can initiate.

### **Key Takeaways:**

*   Semi-destructive testing methods offer a balance between obtaining valuable information about concrete condition and minimizing damage to the structure.
*   Each test has specific applications, procedures, advantages, and limitations.
*   Understanding the underlying principles of each test is crucial for accurate interpretation of results.
*   The choice of testing method depends on the type of damage suspected, the structural element, and the required level of detail.
*   Always refer to relevant standards and manufacturer guidelines for specific test procedures and interpretation criteria.
*   Combining multiple testing methods often provides a more comprehensive and reliable assessment of the structure's condition.
