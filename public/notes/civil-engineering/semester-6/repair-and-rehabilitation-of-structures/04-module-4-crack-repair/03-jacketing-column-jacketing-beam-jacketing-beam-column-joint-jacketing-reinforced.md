## 1. Introduction to Jacketing

Jacketing is a widely used technique for the **repair and rehabilitation of structural members** that have been damaged or require strengthening. It involves encasing the existing member with a new layer of material to enhance its load-carrying capacity, stiffness, or durability. This new layer acts in conjunction with the original member to resist applied loads.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Purpose:** To increase the strength, stiffness, ductility, or durability of existing structural elements.
*   **Mechanism:** The jacketing material forms a composite section with the original member, sharing the load.
*   **Common Applications:** Strengthening of columns, beams, and beam-column joints.
*   **Advantages:** Can significantly improve performance, relatively straightforward to implement, and can restore lost capacity.
*   **Disadvantages:** Can increase the size of the member, potentially affecting aesthetics and clearances; may require careful detailing for load transfer; can be labor-intensive.

## 2. Types of Jacketing

Jacketing can be broadly categorized based on the **type of material used** and the **structural element being repaired**.

### 2.1. Column Jacketing

Columns are critical load-bearing elements, and their deterioration can lead to structural instability. Column jacketing aims to restore or enhance their axial load capacity, shear capacity, and/or flexural capacity.

**Types of Column Jacketing:**

*   **Reinforced Concrete Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** The existing column is reinforced with additional reinforcing bars (longitudinal and transverse), and then encased in a new layer of concrete.
    *   **Mechanism:** The new concrete and reinforcing steel bond with the existing column to form a stronger composite section.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Surface preparation: Cleaning, removing loose concrete, and exposing original reinforcement.
        2.  Installing new reinforcement: Lapping existing bars or providing starter bars, placing new longitudinal and transverse bars (stirrups or spirals).
        3.  Formwork: Erecting formwork around the existing column.
        4.  Concrete casting: Pouring and vibrating new concrete.
        5.  Curing: Ensuring proper curing of the new concrete.
    *   **Enhancements:** Can significantly increase axial strength, bending capacity, and shear resistance. It also improves confinement, leading to better ductility.
    *   **Example:** In a bridge pier that has spalled concrete and corroded rebar due to environmental exposure, reinforced concrete jacketing can restore its load-carrying capacity and protect the internal structure.

*   **Steel Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** The existing column is reinforced using steel plates, angles, or hollow sections, typically attached by bolting or welding.
    *   **Mechanism:** The steel jacket provides significant confinement to the concrete core, increasing its compressive strength and ductility. It also carries a portion of the applied load.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Surface preparation: Cleaning the existing column.
        2.  Fabrication of steel members: Cutting and shaping steel plates or sections.
        3.  Installation of steel members: Placing steel plates around the column and securing them with bolts or welding. Often, a gap is left between the steel and concrete and filled with grout or epoxy for better load transfer and confinement.
        4.  Diaphragm formation: Steel plates or angles are often used to create diaphragms at the top and bottom of the jacket to ensure composite action.
    *   **Enhancements:** Highly effective for enhancing axial load capacity and ductility (especially seismic performance). It can also provide shear resistance.
    *   **Example:** Strengthening existing school buildings for seismic retrofitting often involves steel jacketing of columns to improve their resistance to earthquake forces.

*   **FRP Jacketing (Fiber Reinforced Polymer):**
    *   **Description:** The existing column is wrapped with unidirectional or bidirectional FRP fabrics (e.g., carbon fiber, glass fiber, aramid fiber) saturated with epoxy resin.
    *   **Mechanism:** The FRP provides external confinement to the concrete core, significantly increasing its compressive strength and ductility. The FRP layers carry tensile stresses.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Surface preparation: Cleaning, smoothing, and repairing the existing column surface.
        2.  Priming: Applying a primer to the concrete surface.
        3.  Wrapping: Applying FRP fabric layers in specific orientations (e.g., circumferential for confinement, axial for flexural support) using a wet layup or pre-preg technique.
        4.  Curing: Allowing the epoxy resin to cure.
    *   **Enhancements:** Excellent for enhancing compressive strength and ductility. It is lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and can be applied in situ with minimal disruption.
    *   **Example:** Strengthening columns in historic buildings where the original structural integrity is compromised and minimal alteration to the existing appearance is desired. FRP jacketing is also used in aggressive environments where corrosion is a concern.

### 2.2. Beam Jacketing

Beams are primarily subjected to bending and shear. Beam jacketing aims to increase their flexural capacity, shear capacity, or both.

*   **Reinforced Concrete Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** Similar to column jacketing, additional reinforcing bars are placed and the beam is encased in new concrete. Additional stirrups are often added to enhance shear capacity.
    *   **Mechanism:** The new concrete and steel form a composite section with the original beam, increasing its bending moment and shear resistance.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Support: Temporarily supporting the beam to relieve load.
        2.  Surface preparation: Similar to column jacketing.
        3.  Reinforcement: Installing new longitudinal bars (often lapped with existing bars or using starter bars) and new shear reinforcement (stirrups).
        4.  Formwork: Erecting formwork around the beam.
        5.  Casting: Pouring and vibrating new concrete.
        6.  Curing.
    *   **Enhancements:** Increases flexural capacity, shear capacity, and stiffness. Can also help repair spalling and corrosion.
    *   **Example:** A concrete beam in a building showing signs of sagging and cracking due to overloads can be strengthened by reinforced concrete jacketing.

*   **Steel Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** Steel plates or angles are attached to the flanges and web of the existing concrete beam to augment its flexural and shear capacity.
    *   **Mechanism:** Steel plates on the tension face increase flexural capacity, while steel angles or plates on the web enhance shear resistance.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Support: Temporarily supporting the beam.
        2.  Surface preparation: Cleaning the beam surface.
        3.  Attachment: Steel plates (often bolted or bonded with epoxy) are attached to the tension face (bottom for a simply supported beam), and steel angles are attached to the web for shear reinforcement.
        4.  Grouting/Bonding: Ensuring good contact and load transfer between steel and concrete.
    *   **Enhancements:** Effective for increasing flexural strength, especially in situations where increasing the beam depth is not feasible. Can also improve shear capacity.
    *   **Example:** Strengthening a railway bridge girder to accommodate heavier train loads, where adding depth to the girder might be impractical.

*   **FRP Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** FRP strips or fabrics are bonded to the soffit (tension face) of the beam to increase its flexural capacity, and to the sides for shear reinforcement.
    *   **Mechanism:** The FRP layers carry tensile stresses in flexure and shear stresses in the web, acting as external reinforcement.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Surface preparation: Cleaning and ensuring a smooth surface.
        2.  Bonding: Applying a suitable adhesive and bonding FRP strips or fabrics to the beam's soffit and sides.
        3.  Curing: Allowing the adhesive to cure.
    *   **Enhancements:** Significant increase in flexural capacity. Lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and can be applied in situ with minimal disruption.
    *   **Example:** Strengthening concrete beams in corrosive environments, such as chemical plants or marine structures, where traditional steel reinforcement is susceptible to corrosion.

### 2.3. Beam-Column Joint Jacketing

Beam-column joints are critical nodes in a structure, especially in seismic zones. They transfer forces between beams and columns and are prone to damage due to stress concentrations. Joint jacketing aims to enhance their shear capacity and confinement.

*   **Reinforced Concrete Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** The joint area is widened and strengthened with additional reinforcing bars and new concrete.
    *   **Mechanism:** The increased cross-section and additional reinforcement, particularly confinement reinforcement, improve the shear strength and ductility of the joint.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Formwork: Erecting formwork around the joint region.
        2.  Surface preparation: Cleaning and preparing the joint surfaces.
        3.  Reinforcement: Installing additional stirrups, ties, or hoops within the joint area, often with proper anchorage to beams and columns.
        4.  Casting: Pouring and vibrating new concrete.
        5.  Curing.
    *   **Enhancements:** Increases shear capacity, improves confinement, and enhances the overall ductility and load transfer capability of the joint.
    *   **Example:** In older reinforced concrete buildings designed before modern seismic codes, beam-column joints often lack sufficient shear reinforcement and confinement. Jacketing these joints is crucial for seismic retrofitting.

*   **Steel Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** Steel plates are used to encase the joint area, providing confinement and shear resistance.
    *   **Mechanism:** The steel jacket resists shear forces and provides confinement to the concrete within the joint, improving its capacity and ductility.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Surface preparation: Cleaning the joint area.
        2.  Fabrication and installation: Fabricating and fitting steel plates around the joint.
        3.  Connection: Steel plates are typically bolted or welded to form a closed box section.
        4.  Grouting: Filling any gaps with grout to ensure effective load transfer and confinement.
    *   **Enhancements:** Effective in increasing shear strength and providing confinement to the joint, thereby enhancing its seismic performance.
    *   **Example:** Retrofitting beam-column joints in multi-story buildings to meet current seismic standards, especially when minimal disruption is desired.

*   **FRP Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** FRP fabrics are wrapped around the beam-column joint to provide confinement and shear reinforcement.
    *   **Mechanism:** The FRP wrapping provides external confinement, increasing the compressive strength and shear capacity of the joint concrete.
    *   **Procedure:**
        1.  Surface preparation: Cleaning and preparing the joint surface.
        2.  Wrapping: Applying multiple layers of FRP fabric in specific orientations (e.g., hoop wraps around the column extending into the joint, and wraps along the beam-column interfaces) with appropriate resin.
        3.  Curing: Allowing the resin to cure.
    *   **Enhancements:** Enhances shear capacity and confinement of the joint, improving its seismic performance. It is lightweight and corrosion-resistant.
    *   **Example:** Strengthening beam-column joints in bridges or buildings exposed to corrosive environments.

## 3. Considerations for Jacketing

Successful jacketing requires careful planning and execution. Several factors need to be considered:

*   **Load Transfer:** Effective load transfer between the existing member and the jacketing material is crucial. This is achieved through:
    *   **Mechanical Anchorage:** Starter bars or dowels embedded in the existing concrete and extending into the new jacketing material.
    *   **Bonding:** Achieved through roughened surfaces, grout, or epoxy adhesives.
    *   **Confinement:** Steel or FRP jackets provide confinement, which inherently enhances the composite action.
*   **Material Compatibility:** The properties of the jacketing material should be compatible with the existing material (e.g., similar thermal expansion coefficients to minimize stress due to temperature changes).
*   **Surface Preparation:** A clean, sound, and properly prepared surface is essential for good bonding and composite action.
*   **Durability:** The jacketing material should be durable and resistant to the environmental conditions the structure is exposed to.
*   **Connection Details:** Proper detailing of lap splices, anchorage, and connections between different jacketing components is critical.
*   **Increase in Member Size and Weight:** Jacketing increases the dimensions and self-weight of the structural member, which must be accounted for in the structural analysis.
*   **Aesthetics:** The visual impact of the jacketing should be considered, especially in visible elements.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** The chosen jacketing method should be cost-effective for the desired outcome.

## 4. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
What is the primary advantage of using steel jacketing for columns compared to reinforced concrete jacketing in terms of seismic performance?

**Answer:** Steel jacketing provides excellent confinement to the concrete core, significantly increasing its ductility and energy dissipation capacity, which are crucial for seismic resistance. While RC jacketing also enhances ductility, steel's inherent high strength and ability to form a continuous confining jacket are often more pronounced.

**Question 2:**
When jacketing a beam for flexural strengthening, where is the FRP typically applied?

**Answer:** FRP is typically applied to the soffit (tension face) of the beam to carry the tensile stresses induced by bending.

**Question 3:**
List three key considerations for achieving effective load transfer between an existing concrete column and its steel jacket.

**Answer:**
1.  **Grouting/Epoxy Bonding:** Filling the gap between the steel and concrete with grout or epoxy to create a strong bond and uniform load distribution.
2.  **Mechanical Anchorage:** Using bolts or other mechanical fasteners to create a direct connection and prevent slip.
3.  **Confinement:** The inherent confinement provided by the steel jacket itself helps in transferring loads and resisting deformations.

**Question 4:**
Explain the purpose of adding starter bars when performing reinforced concrete jacketing on a column.

**Answer:** Starter bars are essential to create a mechanical connection and ensure continuity of reinforcement between the existing column and the new concrete jacket. They are typically anchored into the existing column and extend into the new jacket, allowing for proper load transfer in both axial and flexural directions.

**Question 5:**
Which type of jacketing is generally preferred for structures in aggressive corrosive environments and why?

**Answer:** FRP jacketing is generally preferred for structures in aggressive corrosive environments because FRP materials are inherently resistant to corrosion, unlike steel reinforcement or concrete which can degrade over time.

## 5. Important Points to Remember

*   **Jacketing is a strengthening and repair technique that involves encasing an existing structural member with a new layer of material.**
*   **The primary goal is to improve load-carrying capacity, stiffness, ductility, or durability.**
*   **Common jacketing materials include reinforced concrete, steel, and FRP.**
*   **Column jacketing focuses on axial, flexural, and shear capacity enhancement and confinement.**
*   **Beam jacketing primarily addresses flexural and shear capacity.**
*   **Beam-column joint jacketing is critical for enhancing shear capacity and confinement in these critical connection zones.**
*   **Effective load transfer, surface preparation, and material compatibility are crucial for successful jacketing.**
*   **Each jacketing type has specific advantages and applications depending on the required performance and environmental conditions.**
