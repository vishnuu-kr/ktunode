# REPAIR AND REHABILITATION OF STRUCTURES

## Module 4: Crack Repair

### Topic: Strengthening of Structural Elements due to Fire, Leakage, Earthquake

---

This module focuses on the critical aspect of strengthening structural elements that have been compromised by various detrimental factors like fire, leakage, and seismic events. Understanding the specific damage mechanisms and implementing appropriate strengthening techniques is crucial for ensuring the safety and longevity of existing structures.

---

### Learning Outcomes:

By the end of this topic, you will be able to:

*   **LO1: Identify and explain the typical damage patterns in structural elements caused by fire.**
*   **LO2: Explain the common causes and consequences of structural distress due to leakage.**
*   **LO3: Understand the behavior of structures under seismic loads and identify damage.**
*   **LO4: Discuss the principles and methods for strengthening concrete elements damaged by fire.**
*   **LO5: Discuss the principles and methods for strengthening concrete elements damaged by leakage.**
*   **LO6: Discuss the principles and methods for strengthening concrete elements damaged by seismic loads.**
*   **LO7: Compare and contrast different strengthening techniques for each damage scenario.**
*   **LO8: Evaluate the suitability of various strengthening techniques based on the extent of damage and functional requirements.**

## 1. Damage Assessment and Causes

### 1.1 Damage due to Fire

**Key Concepts:**

*   **High Temperatures:** Exposure to extreme heat causes significant degradation of concrete and steel.
*   **Spalling:** Rapid heating of concrete can lead to the explosive removal of surface layers, exposing reinforcing steel. This is due to the trapped moisture within the concrete turning into steam and expanding.
*   **Loss of Strength:** Elevated temperatures reduce the compressive strength of concrete and the yield strength of steel.
*   **Thermal Expansion/Contraction:** Differential expansion and contraction between concrete and steel can induce internal stresses and cracking.
*   **Chemical Changes:** High temperatures can cause dehydration and carbonation of concrete, altering its chemical composition and properties.

**Typical Damage Patterns:**

*   **Surface Discoloration:** Concrete may turn pink, red, or white depending on the temperature reached.
*   **Cracking:** Cracks appear on the surface, often in a polygonal pattern due to thermal stresses.
*   **Spalling:** Chunks of concrete breaking away from the surface.
*   **Exposed Reinforcement:** Spalling can expose the steel rebar, leading to corrosion if not addressed.
*   **Deformation:** Structural members like beams and columns can undergo significant deformation or buckling.
*   **Loss of Bond:** The bond between concrete and steel can be compromised.

**Causes:**

*   Building fires in residential, commercial, or industrial buildings.
*   Industrial accidents involving flammable materials.
*   Intentional arson.

### 1.2 Damage due to Leakage

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Infiltration of Water:** Water entering the concrete structure.
*   **Reinforcement Corrosion:** The primary consequence of water ingress is the corrosion of embedded steel reinforcement.
*   **Alkali-Silica Reaction (ASR):** In some cases, the presence of water can facilitate ASR, a deleterious reaction between alkali in cement paste and certain types of silica in aggregates.
*   **Freeze-Thaw Cycles:** In colder climates, water within the pores of concrete can freeze and expand, causing internal stresses and cracking.
*   **Efflorescence:** Salt deposits on the surface of concrete, indicating water movement.
*   **Deterioration of Protective Layers:** Leakage can damage coatings, sealants, and finishes.

**Typical Damage Patterns:**

*   **Staining and Discoloration:** Water stains on surfaces, often with salt deposits (efflorescence).
*   **Cracking:** Cracks can appear due to the expansion of rust products or freeze-thaw damage.
*   **Spalling (Rust Spalling):** Concrete cover breaks away from the reinforcing bars due to the expansion of rust.
*   **Deterioration of Bond:** The bond between concrete and steel is weakened.
*   **Reinforcement Corrosion:** Visible rust on exposed rebar.
*   **Increased Permeability:** Damaged concrete becomes more permeable, allowing further ingress of water and aggressive agents.

**Causes:**

*   Poor waterproofing or lack of it.
*   Cracks in the structure (due to design, construction, or external loads).
*   Inadequate joint sealing.
*   Defective drainage systems.
*   High hydrostatic pressure.
*   Aggressive environments (e.g., saline environments, freeze-thaw cycles).

### 1.3 Damage due to Earthquake (Seismic Loads)

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Inertial Forces:** Earthquakes induce dynamic inertial forces on structures due to their mass and acceleration.
*   **Lateral Loads:** The primary forces experienced during an earthquake are horizontal (lateral) forces.
*   **Shear Forces and Bending Moments:** These lateral loads cause significant shear forces and bending moments in structural members.
*   **Ductility:** The ability of a structure or material to deform significantly without fracturing. This is a crucial property for seismic resistance.
*   **Energy Dissipation:** Effective seismic design aims to dissipate seismic energy through ductile behavior.
*   **Progressive Collapse:** Failure of one structural element can lead to the collapse of adjacent elements.

**Typical Damage Patterns:**

*   **Cracking:**
    *   **Flexural Cracks:** Typically oriented perpendicular to the bending axis in beams and columns.
    *   **Shear Cracks:** Diagonal cracks in beams, columns, and walls, often forming an "X" pattern in shear walls.
    *   **Diagonal Cracks in Columns:** Indicative of shear failure.
    *   **Buckling of Reinforcement:** Compressive reinforcement can buckle, especially under severe seismic loading.
    *   **Crushing of Concrete:** Concrete in compression zones can crush.
*   **Beam-Column Joint Failure:** Failure at the connections between beams and columns, a critical point for seismic performance.
*   **Punching Shear Failure:** In slabs, especially around columns.
*   **Wall Instability:** Out-of-plane buckling of shear walls.
*   **Soft Story/Weak Story:** A storey that is significantly less stiff or strong than the storeys above or below it, making it vulnerable to collapse.
*   **Pounding:** Adjacent buildings colliding during an earthquake due to differential motion.

**Causes:**

*   Ground shaking during an earthquake.
*   Inadequate structural design for seismic loads.
*   Poor construction quality and detailing.
*   Insufficient reinforcement or inadequate confinement of concrete.
*   Building configuration issues (e.g., soft/weak stories, irregularities).

## 2. Strengthening Principles and Techniques

### 2.1 Strengthening due to Fire Damage

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Assessment of Remaining Capacity:** Evaluate the extent of damage and the remaining load-carrying capacity of the affected elements.
*   **Removal of Damaged Material:** Spalled or weakened concrete must be removed.
*   **Surface Preparation:** Cleaning and preparing the concrete surface for repair and strengthening.
*   **Restoration of Integrity:** Re-establishing the structural continuity and load transfer mechanisms.

**Strengthening Principles:**

*   **Restore Sectional Properties:** Repair the loss of concrete cover and section size.
*   **Enhance Strength and Stiffness:** Increase the load-bearing capacity and reduce deflections.
*   **Protect Against Future Fire Exposure:** Apply fire-resistant coatings or materials if the risk is ongoing.

**Strengthening Techniques:**

1.  **Concrete Patching/Repair:**
    *   **Description:** Removal of damaged concrete and replacement with a suitable repair mortar or concrete.
    *   **Process:** Cleaning the damaged area, preparing the substrate (e.g., roughening, applying bonding agent), applying the repair material, and curing.
    *   **Materials:** Polymer-modified cementitious mortars, epoxy-based repair materials.
    *   **When to Use:** Minor to moderate spalling and cracking.

2.  **Concrete Jacketing/Encasing:**
    *   **Description:** Adding a new layer of concrete (often with improved properties) around the existing damaged member. This is particularly effective for columns and beams.
    *   **Process:** Removing loose concrete, cleaning the surface, fixing new reinforcement (often tied to existing rebar), erecting formwork, and casting new concrete.
    *   **Materials:** High-strength concrete, often with steel fibers.
    *   **When to Use:** Significant loss of concrete section, buckling of rebar, or when increased load capacity is required. This significantly enhances stiffness and strength.

3.  **Fiber Reinforced Polymer (FRP) Wrapping:**
    *   **Description:** Applying FRP fabrics (e.g., carbon FRP, glass FRP) saturated with epoxy resin to the surface of the damaged element.
    *   **Process:** Surface preparation, application of primer, impregnation of FRP fabric with resin, wrapping the member, and curing.
    *   **Benefits:** High strength-to-weight ratio, corrosion resistance, relatively quick installation.
    *   **When to Use:** Restoring lost strength, increasing confinement, preventing further spalling, particularly effective for columns and beams. Can also provide some fire protection if combined with intumescent coatings.

4.  **Steel Jacketing/Encasing:**
    *   **Description:** Encasing the damaged element with steel plates or channels, often filled with concrete or grout.
    *   **Process:** Cleaning and preparing the surface, fitting steel plates/channels and bolting/welding them in place, and then filling the void with grout or concrete.
    *   **When to Use:** Significant damage, need for substantial increase in strength and stiffness, especially for columns prone to buckling. Provides excellent confinement.

5.  **Post-Tensioning:**
    *   **Description:** Introducing compressive forces into the structural member using high-strength steel tendons to counteract tensile stresses caused by loads or damage.
    *   **When to Use:** To restore load-carrying capacity and reduce crack widths, especially in beams and slabs that have experienced significant bending due to fire.

**Important Points to Remember for Fire Damage:**

*   **Thorough inspection is crucial** to determine the extent of heat penetration and material degradation.
*   **Steel reinforcement's yield strength can be significantly reduced** even at temperatures below 400°C.
*   **Fire-resistant coatings** can be applied to new or repaired members for future protection.

### 2.2 Strengthening due to Leakage Damage

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Stopping Water Ingress:** The primary goal is to prevent further water penetration.
*   **Addressing Reinforcement Corrosion:** Removing rust and protecting the steel.
*   **Restoring Concrete Integrity:** Repairing cracks and damaged concrete.

**Strengthening Principles:**

*   **Protecting Reinforcement:** Prevent further corrosion by sealing the concrete or protecting the steel.
*   **Restoring Cover:** Replace lost concrete cover to provide adequate protection to rebar.
*   **Increasing Durability:** Enhance the resistance of the concrete to future water ingress and aggressive agents.

**Strengthening Techniques:**

1.  **Concrete Surface Treatment and Sealing:**
    *   **Description:** Applying water-repellent coatings, sealants, or membranes to the concrete surface to prevent water penetration.
    *   **Materials:** Silanes, siloxanes, acrylic coatings, epoxy coatings, polyurethane membranes, bitumen-based coatings.
    *   **When to Use:** For minor leakage and to prevent future ingress. Essential as a first step or in conjunction with other repairs.

2.  **Crack Injection/Sealing:**
    *   **Description:** Injecting epoxy or polyurethane resins into cracks to seal them and prevent water flow.
    *   **Process:** Cleaning cracks, drilling injection ports, injecting the resin under pressure.
    *   **When to Use:** To seal cracks, restore continuity, and prevent water ingress. Epoxy is generally stronger and more rigid, while polyurethane is more flexible and can accommodate movement.

3.  **Removal of Corroded Reinforcement and Repair:**
    *   **Description:** Removing the concrete cover, cleaning or replacing corroded steel, applying corrosion inhibitors, and then repairing the concrete.
    *   **Process:** Carefully excavating to expose the rebar, removing rust (wire brushing, sandblasting), applying anti-corrosion coatings to the steel, and then patching or jacketing the repaired area.
    *   **When to Use:** When significant reinforcement corrosion and rust spalling have occurred.

4.  **Concrete Repair Mortars:**
    *   **Description:** Using specialized cementitious or polymer-modified mortars designed for repair of damaged concrete. These often have reduced permeability.
    *   **When to Use:** To replace lost concrete cover after rust removal.

5.  **FRP Wrapping:**
    *   **Description:** Similar to fire damage, FRP wrapping can be used to increase the strength and stiffness of elements damaged by corrosion and to provide a barrier against further environmental attack if properly sealed.
    *   **When to Use:** To restore lost section, enhance load capacity, and as a protective layer, especially when combined with sealing.

6.  **Cathodic Protection:**
    *   **Description:** An electrochemical technique to prevent or arrest the corrosion of reinforcing steel.
    *   **Types:** Sacrificial anode systems, impressed current systems.
    *   **When to Use:** In severe corrosive environments where simpler methods are insufficient, particularly for large structures.

**Important Points to Remember for Leakage Damage:**

*   **Identify the source of leakage** is paramount before commencing repairs.
*   **Corrosion products occupy a larger volume** than the original steel, leading to cracking and spalling.
*   **Low permeability repair materials** are essential to prevent future ingress.

### 2.3 Strengthening due to Earthquake Damage

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Ductile Detailing:** Ensuring adequate confinement of concrete in critical regions (e.g., beam ends, column ends) to prevent brittle failure.
*   **Increased Shear Strength:** Enhancing the shear capacity of beams, columns, and walls.
*   **Increased Flexural Strength:** Restoring or increasing the bending capacity of members.
*   **Improved Confinement:** Preventing buckling of longitudinal reinforcement and crushing of concrete.
*   **Torsion Resistance:** Strengthening members subjected to torsional forces.
*   **Diaphragm Action:** Ensuring the integrity of horizontal diaphragms (slabs) to transfer lateral loads.

**Strengthening Principles:**

*   **Enhance Inherent Ductility:** Improve the ability of members and connections to deform without sudden failure.
*   **Increase Strength and Stiffness:** Resist higher seismic forces and reduce displacements.
*   **Prevent Brittle Failure Modes:** Ensure ductile failure mechanisms govern over brittle ones.
*   **Provide Redundancy:** Create alternative load paths in case of localized failure.

**Strengthening Techniques:**

1.  **Concrete Jacketing/Encasing:**
    *   **Description:** Adding a new layer of reinforced concrete around the existing member. This is a very effective method for enhancing both strength and ductility.
    *   **Process:** Chipping away damaged concrete, cleaning, adding new rebar (often with confinement detailing like closely spaced stirrups), forming, and casting new concrete.
    *   **Benefits:** Significantly increases shear and flexural strength, improves confinement, and adds stiffness.
    *   **When to Use:** Columns, beams, and walls needing substantial strengthening and improved seismic performance.

2.  **Steel Jacketing/Encasing:**
    *   **Description:** Encasing the element with steel plates or sections.
    *   **Process:** Similar to concrete jacketing but with steel. Steel plates are often welded or bolted to the existing member and then grouted or filled with concrete.
    *   **Benefits:** Very effective for increasing shear and axial strength, and especially for improving confinement of columns, preventing buckling of longitudinal bars.
    *   **When to Use:** Columns requiring significant enhancement in strength and ductility, particularly in seismic zones. Can also be used for beams.

3.  **Fiber Reinforced Polymer (FRP) Wrapping:**
    *   **Description:** Wrapping members with FRP fabrics and epoxy resin.
    *   **Benefits:**
        *   **Confinement:** Hoop wrapping of columns significantly increases their ductility and prevents buckling of longitudinal bars and crushing of concrete.
        *   **Shear Strengthening:** Wrapping in a shear-critical pattern (e.g., diagonal wraps) can enhance shear capacity.
        *   **Flexural Strengthening:** Applying FRP strips or sheets to the tension and compression zones of beams can increase flexural capacity.
    *   **When to Use:** Widely used for column confinement, beam shear strengthening, and flexural strengthening. Relatively lightweight and fast to install.

4.  **Adding Shear Walls or Bracing:**
    *   **Description:** Introducing new stiff elements into the structure to resist lateral loads.
    *   **Shear Walls:** Stiff vertical elements (often reinforced concrete) that resist lateral forces through shear and bending.
    *   **Bracing:** Diagonal members (steel or concrete) that form triangulated systems to resist lateral loads.
    *   **When to Use:** When the existing lateral load resisting system is inadequate. This is a global strengthening strategy.

5.  **Base Isolation and Dampers:**
    *   **Description:**
        *   **Base Isolation:** Placing flexible bearings (e.g., elastomeric bearings, sliding bearings) between the foundation and the superstructure to decouple the building from ground motion.
        *   **Dampers:** Devices (e.g., viscous dampers, hysteretic dampers) installed within the structure to dissipate seismic energy.
    *   **When to Use:** For high-value or critical facilities, or when significant retrofitting of the superstructure is not feasible. These are complex and expensive solutions but highly effective.

6.  **Strengthening of Beam-Column Joints:**
    *   **Description:** Joints are critical. Strengthening can involve adding steel plates, FRP wraps, or increasing reinforcement (if feasible through concrete removal and addition).
    *   **When to Use:** When joint shear capacity or confinement is deficient.

7.  **Reinforcement Strengthening:**
    *   **Description:** In some cases, existing rebar can be supplemented by adding new rebar, especially in areas where confinement is insufficient.
    *   **When to Use:** To increase flexural capacity or provide additional shear reinforcement.

**Important Points to Remember for Earthquake Damage:**

*   **Seismic retrofitting aims to provide ductile behavior**, not necessarily to make the structure rigid.
*   **Confinement reinforcement is crucial for columns** to prevent shear failure and buckling.
*   **Beam-column joints are critical and often require special attention.**
*   **The entire structural system's response** must be considered, not just individual elements.

## 4. Evaluation of Suitability

The choice of strengthening technique depends on several factors:

*   **Extent and Nature of Damage:** Minor cracking vs. significant section loss or structural instability.
*   **Type of Structural Element:** Column, beam, slab, wall, joint.
*   **Cause of Damage:** Fire, leakage, or seismic loads.
*   **Required Performance Improvement:** Increase in strength, stiffness, ductility, or durability.
*   **Functional Requirements:** Architectural constraints, serviceability limits.
*   **Economic Considerations:** Cost of materials, labor, and overall project budget.
*   **Time Constraints:** Speed of construction.
*   **Environmental Conditions:** Exposure to aggressive agents.

**General Considerations for Each Damage Scenario:**

*   **Fire Damage:** Focus on restoring concrete section, preventing further degradation, and potentially enhancing fire resistance. Concrete jacketing and steel jacketing are very effective. FRP needs careful consideration for fire.
*   **Leakage Damage:** Prioritize stopping water ingress and protecting steel from corrosion. Surface treatments, crack injection, and repair mortars are key. FRP can provide a durable barrier.
*   **Seismic Damage:** Focus on enhancing ductility, shear strength, and preventing brittle failure. FRP confinement for columns, concrete/steel jacketing for overall strengthening, and adding new lateral load resisting elements are common.

## 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

A concrete column in a multi-storey building has suffered significant spalling of concrete cover and visible rust on the reinforcement due to a past fire incident. The column needs to be strengthened to restore its load-carrying capacity. Briefly describe two suitable strengthening techniques for this scenario and explain their advantages.

**Answer:**

Two suitable techniques are:

1.  **Concrete Jacketing:** This involves removing the damaged concrete, cleaning the exposed steel, adding new reinforcing bars (stirrups and longitudinal bars), erecting formwork around the column, and casting a new layer of high-strength concrete.
    *   **Advantages:** Significantly increases the column's axial and shear strength, improves confinement of the core concrete, and restores the protective cover. It's a robust and durable solution.

2.  **Steel Jacketing:** This involves encasing the column with steel plates or channels, which are then bolted or welded and grouted or filled with concrete.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides excellent confinement to the concrete, prevents buckling of the existing reinforcement, and offers a rapid installation method. It significantly increases the column's strength and ductility.

**Question 2:**

Identify the primary cause of structural distress in concrete elements subjected to prolonged leakage and describe one method to address this issue.

**Answer:**

The primary cause of structural distress due to prolonged leakage is the **corrosion of embedded steel reinforcement**. Water, often carrying dissolved salts or other aggressive agents, penetrates the concrete, reaches the steel, and initiates a chemical reaction that forms rust. Rust occupies a larger volume than the original steel, causing internal pressure that leads to cracking and spalling of the concrete cover.

One method to address this is **crack injection using epoxy resin**. This involves cleaning the cracks, drilling injection ports, and injecting a low-viscosity epoxy resin under pressure. The epoxy fills the crack, seals it against further water ingress, and restores some structural continuity.

**Question 3:**

During a seismic event, a reinforced concrete beam showed diagonal cracking in the web, indicating a potential shear failure. What strengthening technique would be most effective for this specific damage? Explain why.

**Answer:**

The most effective strengthening technique for a beam experiencing shear distress and diagonal cracking is **Fiber Reinforced Polymer (FRP) wrapping in a shear-critical pattern**.

**Explanation:**

*   FRP fabrics (like carbon or glass fiber) can be applied in a continuous hoop or U-shaped pattern around the beam, oriented at an angle (often 45 degrees) to the potential shear cracks.
*   When the FRP is saturated with epoxy resin and cures, it provides a strong hoop confinement that resists the tensile stresses that develop due to shear.
*   This significantly increases the shear capacity of the beam and helps prevent further shear-induced cracking and brittle failure. It is also relatively lightweight and can be applied externally without significantly increasing the member's size or weight.

**Question 4:**

Compare the suitability of FRP wrapping and concrete jacketing for strengthening a column that has suffered moderate fire damage, focusing on their effectiveness and limitations.

**Answer:**

*   **FRP Wrapping:**
    *   **Effectiveness:** Can restore some lost strength and stiffness and provide confinement. The lightweight nature is an advantage.
    *   **Limitations:** **Crucially, FRP has poor fire resistance.** If the column is expected to be re-exposed to fire, FRP alone is insufficient and needs to be protected with fire-rated coatings (e.g., intumescent paints). It's primarily for structural restoration but not for ongoing fire protection.

*   **Concrete Jacketing:**
    *   **Effectiveness:** Very effective. It restores the lost concrete section, adds significant strength and stiffness, and provides excellent confinement to the existing column, significantly enhancing its load-carrying capacity and ductility. The new concrete layer itself has inherent fire resistance.
    *   **Limitations:** Adds significant weight and bulk to the column, requires formwork, and is more labor-intensive and time-consuming than FRP wrapping.

**Conclusion:** For moderate fire damage where structural restoration is the primary goal and future fire exposure is a concern, concrete jacketing is generally a more suitable and robust solution due to its inherent fire resistance and greater capacity for strengthening. If fire protection is not a primary concern for the repaired element, FRP might be considered for its lightweight and quick installation, but with the critical caveat of requiring additional fire protection if needed.

## Key Points to Remember:

*   **Damage Assessment is Paramount:** Always begin with a thorough inspection and diagnosis of the cause and extent of damage.
*   **Understand the Mechanism:** Different damage types (fire, leakage, earthquake) have distinct mechanisms and require tailored strengthening approaches.
*   **Ductility vs. Strength:** For seismic strengthening, prioritizing ductility is often as important as increasing strength.
*   **Confining Reinforcement:** Essential for columns in seismic zones to prevent buckling and crushing.
*   **FRP Limitations:** Be mindful of FRP's limited fire resistance.
*   **Combined Techniques:** Often, a combination of techniques may be necessary for optimal results.
*   **Surface Preparation:** Proper surface preparation is critical for the success of most repair and strengthening methods.
*   **Cost-Benefit Analysis:** Evaluate the effectiveness of a technique against its cost and practicality.
*   **Professional Expertise:** Strengthening of structures is a specialized field requiring experienced engineers and technicians.
