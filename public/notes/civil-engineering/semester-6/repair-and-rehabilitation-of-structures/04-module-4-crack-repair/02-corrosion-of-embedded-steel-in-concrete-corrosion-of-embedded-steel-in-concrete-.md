## Topic: Corrosion of Embedded Steel in Concrete

### 1. Corrosion of Embedded Steel in Concrete

**1.1 Introduction**

*   **Definition:** Corrosion of embedded steel in concrete refers to the electrochemical process where reinforcing steel bars (rebar) within a concrete structure degrade due to chemical reactions, primarily oxidation.
*   **Significance:** This is a major cause of deterioration in reinforced concrete structures, leading to cracking, spalling (chipping or flaking of concrete), loss of structural integrity, and reduced service life.
*   **Why does it happen?** Concrete normally provides a protective alkaline environment for steel. However, this protection can be compromised by external factors.

**1.2 Factors Influencing Corrosion**

*   **Presence of Oxygen:** Essential for the electrochemical reaction.
*   **Presence of Moisture:** Acts as an electrolyte, facilitating ion movement.
*   **Presence of Corrosive Agents:**
    *   **Chlorides (Cl⁻):** From de-icing salts, seawater, or contaminated aggregates. Chlorides break down the passive layer on steel.
    *   **Carbonation (CO₂):** Atmospheric carbon dioxide penetrates concrete, reacting with calcium hydroxide to form calcium carbonate, reducing alkalinity. This is a significant factor in non-marine environments.
*   **Concrete Quality:**
    *   **Permeability:** High permeability allows easier ingress of moisture and aggressive ions.
    *   **Cracking:** Existing cracks provide direct pathways for aggressive agents to reach the rebar.
    *   **Cover Thickness:** Insufficient concrete cover over the rebar offers less protection.
    *   **Concrete Mix Design:** Poor quality concrete with low cement content or high water-cement ratio is more susceptible.
*   **Steel Properties:**
    *   **Type of Steel:** While most common reinforcing steels are susceptible, some specialized steels may offer better resistance.
    *   **Surface Condition:** Mill scale can sometimes act as a cathodic site, initiating corrosion.

### 2. Mechanism of Corrosion

Corrosion of embedded steel in concrete is an electrochemical process involving anodic and cathodic reactions.

**2.1 Electrochemical Principles**

*   **Anode:** Site where oxidation occurs. Metal ions are released into the electrolyte, and electrons are produced. In the case of steel, this is where the iron atoms lose electrons:
    *   **Anodic Reaction:** $Fe \rightarrow Fe^{2+} + 2e^-$ (Iron loses electrons and becomes ferrous ions)
*   **Cathode:** Site where reduction occurs. Electrons produced at the anode are consumed. In the presence of oxygen and water, the most common cathodic reaction in concrete is:
    *   **Cathodic Reaction:** $O_2 + 2H_2O + 4e^- \rightarrow 4OH^-$ (Oxygen and water react with electrons to form hydroxide ions)
*   **Electrolyte:** The moisture present in the concrete pores acts as the electrolyte, allowing the movement of ions between the anode and cathode.
*   **Electron Pathway:** The metal itself provides the pathway for electrons to travel from the anode to the cathode.

**2.2 The Role of the Passive Layer**

*   **Passive Layer Formation:** In normal concrete, the high alkalinity (pH around 12.5-13.5) due to the presence of calcium hydroxide ($Ca(OH)_2$) forms a thin, protective passive film (primarily iron oxides and hydroxides) on the surface of the steel. This film acts as a barrier against electrochemical reactions.
*   **Breakdown of Passive Layer:**
    *   **By Chlorides:** Chloride ions ($Cl^-$) penetrate the concrete and react with the passive film, breaking it down and allowing corrosion to start at localized sites.
    *   **By Carbonation:** As carbon dioxide penetrates, it reacts with calcium hydroxide, reducing the alkalinity. When the pH drops below approximately 9-10, the passive layer becomes unstable and dissolves, making the steel susceptible to corrosion.
        *   **Carbonation Reaction:** $CO_2 + Ca(OH)_2 \rightarrow CaCO_3 + H_2O$

**2.3 Formation of Rust**

*   The ferrous ions ($Fe^{2+}$) produced at the anode react further with oxygen and water to form rust, which is primarily hydrated ferric oxide ($Fe_2O_3 \cdot nH_2O$).
*   **Rust Formation:** $Fe^{2+} + 2OH^- \rightarrow Fe(OH)_2$ (Ferrous hydroxide)
    *   $4Fe(OH)_2 + O_2 + 2H_2O \rightarrow 4Fe(OH)_3$ (Ferric hydroxide)
    *   $2Fe(OH)_3 \rightarrow Fe_2O_3 \cdot nH_2O + (3-n)H_2O$ (Hydrated ferric oxide - rust)

**2.4 Expansion and Cracking**

*   **Volume Expansion:** Rust occupies a significantly larger volume (approximately 2-6 times) than the original steel.
*   **Internal Stresses:** This volume expansion creates tensile stresses within the surrounding concrete.
*   **Cracking and Spalling:** When these tensile stresses exceed the tensile strength of the concrete, cracks form, and eventually, pieces of concrete spall off from the surface, exposing the corroding rebar.

### 3. Stages of Corrosion Damage

Corrosion of embedded steel progresses through distinct stages, each with identifiable characteristics.

**3.1 Stage 1: Initiation (Passive Film Breakdown)**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   The protective passive layer on the steel surface is still intact or has just begun to break down.
    *   No visible signs of corrosion on the concrete surface.
    *   This stage can be very long, depending on the rate of ingress of aggressive agents.
*   **Key Drivers:** Ingress of chlorides or carbonation front reaching the rebar.

**3.2 Stage 2: Propagation (Active Corrosion)**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   The passive layer has been compromised, and active electrochemical corrosion is occurring.
    *   Rust is forming around the steel.
    *   Still no visible signs on the concrete surface, or very minor discolouration may appear.
    *   The rate of corrosion is often slow initially and can accelerate as conditions become more favorable.
*   **Key Drivers:** Presence of electrolyte (moisture) and oxygen, with active anodic and cathodic sites.

**3.3 Stage 3: Crack Initiation and Propagation**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Accumulation of rust products around the rebar.
    *   The expanding volume of rust creates internal tensile stresses in the concrete.
    *   **Micro-cracking:** Fine cracks begin to form around the rebar.
    *   **Staining:** Rust stains may appear on the concrete surface due to moisture movement carrying dissolved rust.
*   **Key Drivers:** Volume expansion of rust exceeding concrete tensile strength.

**3.4 Stage 4: Delamination and Spalling**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Cracks widen and deepen.
    *   **Delamination:** The rust layer separates the concrete cover from the reinforcing steel.
    *   **Spalling:** Pieces of concrete cover detach from the structure, exposing the corroding rebar.
    *   Significant loss of bond between steel and concrete.
    *   Reduced load-carrying capacity of the structural element.
*   **Key Drivers:** Progressive expansion of rust, moisture ingress into cracks, freeze-thaw cycles (which can worsen existing cracks).

**3.5 Stage 5: Loss of Structural Integrity**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Significant loss of rebar cross-sectional area due to active corrosion.
    *   Reduced bond strength between steel and concrete.
    *   Potential for buckling of reinforcing bars.
    *   The structural element may no longer be able to carry its intended loads safely.
*   **Key Drivers:** Continuous active corrosion leading to significant material loss.

### 4. Repair of Corrosion Damages by Cathodic Protection (CP)

Cathodic protection is a technique used to prevent or reduce the corrosion of metals by making the corroding metal the cathode of an electrochemical cell.

**4.1 Principle of Cathodic Protection**

*   **Making the Rebar the Cathode:** In a corroding structure, the steel rebar acts as the anode (where metal is lost) and other areas act as cathodes (where reduction occurs). CP forces the entire rebar to become a cathode.
*   **How it Works:** By supplying electrons to the rebar, CP reverses the direction of the electrochemical reaction, preventing the metal from oxidizing.

**4.2 Types of Cathodic Protection Systems for Reinforced Concrete**

There are two main types of CP systems:

**4.2.1 Galvanic Anode Cathodic Protection (GACP)**

*   **How it Works:**
    *   Uses a more "active" metal (e.g., zinc, magnesium, aluminum alloys) as a sacrificial anode.
    *   This sacrificial anode is electrically connected to the reinforcing steel.
    *   The sacrificial anode corrodes preferentially, supplying electrons to the rebar.
    *   The system requires an electrolyte (the concrete pore solution).
*   **Components:**
    *   **Sacrificial Anodes:** Bars, grids, or plates of the active metal.
    *   **Electrical Connection:** Conductive wires connecting the anode to the rebar.
    *   **Reference Electrode (Optional but recommended):** For monitoring the potential of the rebar.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Simpler to install, no external power source required.
    *   Self-regulating and self-adjusting to environmental changes.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Limited lifespan of the anode (depends on anode consumption rate).
    *   May not provide sufficient current for large or severely corroded structures.
    *   Anodes need to be replaced when depleted.
*   **Example Application:** Small bridge decks, parking garages, marine structures with moderate chloride contamination.

**4.2.2 Impressed Current Cathodic Protection (ICCP)**

*   **How it Works:**
    *   Uses an inert anode (which does not corrode itself) and an external DC power source.
    *   The power source (rectifier) forces a current from the inert anode through the electrolyte (concrete) to the reinforcing steel (cathode).
    *   The reinforcing steel becomes the cathode, and corrosion is suppressed.
*   **Components:**
    *   **Inert Anodes:** Typically made of materials like mixed metal oxides (MMO) on titanium, conductive polymers, or graphite. These are often embedded in a compatible overlay material.
    *   **DC Power Source:** A rectifier that converts AC mains power to low-voltage DC.
    *   **Reference Electrodes:** Used to monitor the potential of the rebar and control the rectifier output.
    *   **Electrical Connections:** Wiring to connect anodes, rebar, and the rectifier.
    *   **Overlay Material (Common):** Often a cementitious overlay containing the anodes to provide a conductive path and protect the anodes.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Can provide sufficient current for large and severely corroded structures.
    *   Anodes have a very long lifespan.
    *   Current output can be precisely controlled and adjusted.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   More complex to design and install.
    *   Requires an external power source and ongoing maintenance of the rectifier.
    *   Risk of overprotection or stray current corrosion if not designed and monitored correctly.
*   **Example Application:** Large infrastructure projects like bridges, tunnels, marine piers, buildings with significant corrosion issues.

**4.3 Application of CP for Repair of Corrosion Damages**

CP is typically applied after initial surface repairs (e.g., concrete removal and patching) are completed, especially in cases of spalling and cracking.

1.  **Surface Preparation:**
    *   Remove loose and spalled concrete to expose the corroding rebar.
    *   Clean the exposed rebar to remove loose rust.
    *   Patch the affected areas with suitable repair mortar.

2.  **Installation of CP System:**
    *   **GACP:** Sacrificial anodes are attached to the prepared surface or embedded in an overlay, connected electrically to the rebar.
    *   **ICCP:** Inert anodes (often in mesh or strip form, embedded in a conductive overlay) are installed over the repaired areas and connected to the rectifier. Reference electrodes are also placed.

3.  **Commissioning and Monitoring:**
    *   The system is activated, and current flow is established (for ICCP) or naturally occurs (for GACP).
    *   Regular monitoring of rebar potentials using reference electrodes is crucial to ensure the structure is adequately protected and to track the effectiveness of the system. The target is to shift the rebar potential to a protected range (e.g., below -850 mV vs. CSE for ICCP).

4.  **Ongoing Maintenance:**
    *   Regular inspections of the CP system components and performance.
    *   Replacement of sacrificial anodes when depleted (GACP).
    *   Maintenance of rectifiers and monitoring equipment (ICCP).

**4.4 When is CP Recommended?**

*   When active corrosion is confirmed (e.g., by electrochemical tests like linear polarization resistance or potential measurements).
*   When chlorides are the primary cause of corrosion and have penetrated to the rebar.
*   When the passive layer has been broken down, and concrete alkalinity is reduced.
*   As a long-term measure to prevent rebar corrosion in aggressive environments.

### 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
What are the two primary environmental factors that cause the breakdown of the passive layer on reinforcing steel in concrete?

**Answer 1:**
The two primary factors are **chlorides** (e.g., from de-icing salts or seawater) and **carbonation** (penetration of atmospheric carbon dioxide).

---

**Question 2:**
Explain the electrochemical process of steel corrosion in concrete. Identify the anodic and cathodic reactions.

**Answer 2:**
Corrosion is an electrochemical process involving:
*   **Anode:** Site of oxidation where iron loses electrons: $Fe \rightarrow Fe^{2+} + 2e^-$
*   **Cathode:** Site of reduction where electrons are consumed, typically by oxygen and water: $O_2 + 2H_2O + 4e^- \rightarrow 4OH^-$
*   **Electrolyte:** Moisture in the concrete pores facilitates ion movement.
*   **Electron Pathway:** The steel itself conducts electrons from anode to cathode.

---

**Question 3:**
Describe the main difference between Galvanic Anode Cathodic Protection (GACP) and Impressed Current Cathodic Protection (ICCP).

**Answer 3:**
The main difference lies in the **power source and anode material**:
*   **GACP:** Uses **sacrificial (active) anodes** that corrode preferentially and **no external power source** is required.
*   **ICCP:** Uses **inert anodes** and an **external DC power source** (rectifier) to drive the protective current.

---

**Question 4:**
List the stages of corrosion damage in reinforced concrete, starting from the initial breakdown of protection.

**Answer 4:**
1.  **Initiation** (Passive film breakdown)
2.  **Propagation** (Active corrosion)
3.  **Crack Initiation and Propagation**
4.  **Delamination and Spalling**
5.  **Loss of Structural Integrity**

---

**Question 5:**
Why is cathodic protection an effective method for repairing corrosion damage?

**Answer 5:**
Cathodic protection is effective because it converts the corroding steel (anode) into a cathode by supplying it with electrons. This electrochemical process **suppresses the oxidation of iron**, thereby preventing further loss of steel material and halting the deterioration caused by rust expansion.

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Concrete's Protective Role:** Concrete's alkalinity is crucial for forming and maintaining the passive layer on steel.
*   **Aggressive Agents:** Chlorides and carbonation are the primary culprits that break down this protective layer.
*   **Rust Expansion:** The significant volume expansion of rust is the direct cause of cracking and spalling in concrete.
*   **Electrochemical Process:** Corrosion is an electrochemical reaction, not just a simple chemical one.
*   **Cathodic Protection Goal:** To make the rebar the cathode of an electrochemical cell.
*   **GACP vs. ICCP:** Understand the fundamental differences in their power sources and anode types, and their respective applications and limitations.
*   **Monitoring is Key:** Effective CP systems require proper design, installation, and continuous monitoring to ensure adequate protection.
