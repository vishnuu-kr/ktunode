# Quantity Surveying and Valuation: Module 4 - Valuation (Part 2: Income, Outgoings & Depreciation)

This module delves into the core concepts of property valuation, focusing on income-generating properties and the factors that influence their value. We will explore various financial terms and mechanisms used in valuation, as well as the impact of depreciation and obsolescence.

---

## 1. Income in Property Valuation

The primary method for valuing income-producing properties is the **Income Approach**. This approach assumes that the value of a property is directly related to the income it generates.

### 1.1. Gross Income

**Definition:** Gross income is the total potential rental income a property could generate if it were fully occupied and all rent was collected, without any deductions for expenses or vacancies.

**Key Components:**

*   **Gross Contractual Rent:** The total rent due from all tenants as per their lease agreements.
*   **Other Income:** This can include income from ancillary services like parking fees, laundry facilities, signage, vending machines, etc.

**Calculation Example:**

A commercial building has 10 units, each with a potential rent of $1,000 per month.
*   Gross Contractual Rent = 10 units * $1,000/unit/month * 12 months/year = $120,000 per year.
*   If the building also generates $5,000 per year from parking fees, then:
*   **Gross Income = $120,000 + $5,000 = $125,000**

**Important Point:** Gross income is a theoretical maximum and rarely achieved in practice.

### 1.2. Net Income

**Definition:** Net income is the income remaining after deducting all operating expenses and accounting for vacancies and collection losses from the gross income. It represents the actual income available to the owner after the property has been managed and operated.

**Calculation:**

**Net Income = Gross Income - Operating Expenses - Vacancy & Collection Losses**

**Key Components:**

*   **Operating Expenses:** These are the recurring costs associated with operating and maintaining the property. They do not include mortgage payments, depreciation, or capital expenditures (improvements). Examples include:
    *   Property Taxes
    *   Insurance Premiums
    *   Property Management Fees
    *   Repairs and Maintenance (routine)
    *   Utilities (if paid by owner)
    *   Janitorial Services
    *   Landscaping
    *   Advertising and Leasing Costs
*   **Vacancy and Collection Losses:** An allowance for periods when units are vacant and for potential rent defaults. This is typically expressed as a percentage of gross income.

**Calculation Example (Continuing from above):**

Gross Income = $125,000

Operating Expenses:
*   Property Taxes: $10,000
*   Insurance: $3,000
*   Property Management: $6,000 (5% of Gross Contractual Rent)
*   Repairs & Maintenance: $5,000
*   Utilities: $4,000
*   **Total Operating Expenses = $28,000**

Vacancy & Collection Losses: Assume 5% of Gross Contractual Rent ($120,000)
*   **Vacancy & Collection Losses = 0.05 * $120,000 = $6,000**

*   **Net Income = $125,000 (Gross Income) - $28,000 (Operating Expenses) - $6,000 (Vacancy) = $91,000**

**Important Point:** Net income is the basis for most income capitalization methods used in valuation.

## 2. Outgoings

**Definition:** Outgoings are all the expenses incurred in the ownership, operation, and maintenance of a property. This is essentially the same as "Operating Expenses" as discussed under Net Income.

**Categories of Outgoings:**

*   **Fixed Outgoings:** Expenses that remain relatively constant regardless of the occupancy level, such as property taxes and insurance premiums.
*   **Variable Outgoings:** Expenses that fluctuate depending on the usage and occupancy of the property, such as repairs and maintenance, utilities, and cleaning costs.

**Key Considerations for Valuers:**

*   **Reasonableness:** Outgoings must be reasonable and necessary for the operation of the property.
*   **Owner's Responsibility:** Only expenses that are typically borne by the owner should be included.
*   **Capital Expenditures vs. Revenue Expenditures:** Capital expenditures (for improvements) are not outgoings; they are dealt with separately through depreciation or a sinking fund.

## 3. Annuity and Sinking Fund

These concepts are crucial for understanding how capital is recouped and how to account for the eventual replacement of building components.

### 3.1. Annuity

**Definition:** In financial mathematics and valuation, an annuity is a series of equal payments made at regular intervals. In property valuation, it's often used to represent the periodic return on an investment.

**Types relevant to Valuation:**

*   **Annuity Due:** Payments made at the beginning of each period.
*   **Ordinary Annuity:** Payments made at the end of each period.

**Application in Valuation:**

When we talk about a property "returning" an income annually, we are implicitly treating that income stream as an annuity. The capital value is the present value of this future annuity.

### 3.2. Sinking Fund

**Definition:** A sinking fund is a fund set up by accumulating periodic contributions (like an annuity) over a period of time to meet a future debt or capital expenditure. In valuation, it's used to replace assets or parts of a building that have a limited lifespan.

**Purpose in Valuation:**

To provide for the eventual replacement of short-lived items within a building (e.g., roofing, boilers, lifts, carpets) or the entire building at the end of its economic life. The sinking fund allows the owner to recover the cost of these items without depleting the net income.

**Calculation:**

The sinking fund factor calculates the periodic amount needed to accumulate a specific sum at a future date, considering interest.

**Formula for Sinking Fund Factor (SF):**

$SF = \frac{i}{(1+i)^n - 1}$

Where:
*   `i` = Interest rate per annum (often the yield rate or a conservative rate)
*   `n` = Number of years for accumulation

**Example:**

A boiler costs $10,000 and needs to be replaced in 10 years. The sinking fund can earn 5% per annum.

*   SF = $\frac{0.05}{(1+0.05)^{10} - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.05}{(1.05)^{10} - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.05}{1.62889 - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.05}{0.62889}$
*   SF ≈ 0.07951

This means an annual contribution of approximately $0.07951 for every $1 of the boiler's cost is needed.
*   Annual Sinking Fund Contribution = $10,000 * 0.07951 = $795.10

**Important Point:** The sinking fund contribution is treated as an outgoing and deducted from the net income before capitalization.

## 4. Year's Purchase (YP)

**Definition:** Year's Purchase (YP) is a factor that represents the capital sum required to be invested at a given rate of interest to produce a return of one unit of currency per annum, in perpetuity or for a specific term, taking into account the return of the principal.

**Purpose:** YP is used to convert a perpetual or terminable income stream into a capital sum (value).

**Types of YP:**

### 4.1. YP in Perpetuity

**Definition:** The capital sum required to produce a return of one unit of currency perpetually.

**Calculation:**

**YP in Perpetuity = 1 / Rate of Interest (Yield)**

**Example:**

If the required rate of return (yield) is 10% per annum (0.10):
*   YP in Perpetuity = 1 / 0.10 = 10

This means that to receive $1 per year forever, you need to invest $10 at a 10% annual interest rate.

### 4.2. YP in Perpetuity, Accruing Interest (with Return of Principal)

This is used when the capital is expected to be returned at the end of a specific period.

**Calculation:**

**YP in Perpetuity, Accruing Interest = YP in Perpetuity + Sinking Fund Factor**

**YP = 1/i + SF**

Where:
*   `i` = Interest rate (yield)
*   `SF` = Sinking Fund Factor for the term of the investment

**Example:**

To receive $1 per year for 20 years, with the principal being returned at the end of 20 years, at a yield of 10% per annum.

First, calculate the Sinking Fund Factor (SF) for 20 years at 10%:
*   SF = $\frac{0.10}{(1+0.10)^{20} - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.10}{(1.10)^{20} - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.10}{6.7275 - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.10}{5.7275}$
*   SF ≈ 0.01746

Now, calculate YP:
*   YP = (1 / 0.10) + 0.01746
*   YP = 10 + 0.01746
*   YP = 10.01746

This means $10.01746 invested now at 10% will yield $1 per year for 20 years and at the end of the 20th year, the original capital will be recovered.

### 4.3. YP for a Term Certain (Term Certain)

**Definition:** The capital sum required to produce a return of one unit of currency per annum for a specific number of years, with the principal being returned at the end of that term.

**Calculation:**

**YP for a Term Certain = (1 - Present Value of Reversion) / Interest Rate**

**YP = $\frac{1 - (1+i)^{-n}}{i}$**

Where:
*   `i` = Interest rate (yield)
*   `n` = Number of years in the term

**Example:**

To receive $1 per year for 20 years, with the principal being returned at the end of 20 years, at a yield of 10% per annum.

*   YP = $\frac{1 - (1+0.10)^{-20}}{0.10}$
*   YP = $\frac{1 - (1.10)^{-20}}{0.10}$
*   YP = $\frac{1 - 0.14864}{0.10}$
*   YP = $\frac{0.85136}{0.10}$
*   YP = 8.5136

This is the factor to multiply the annual income by to find the present value of that income stream for 20 years.

**Relationship between YP for a Term Certain and YP in Perpetuity Accruing Interest:**

**YP in Perpetuity, Accruing Interest = YP for a Term Certain + Present Value of Reversion**

Where:
*   Present Value of Reversion = $(1+i)^{-n}$

Using the previous example:
*   YP in Perpetuity, Accruing Interest = 8.5136 + 0.14864 = 10.01746 (Matches our previous calculation)

**Important Point:** The choice of YP depends on the expected life of the investment and how the capital is to be recovered.

## 5. Depreciation and Obsolescence

These factors reduce the value of a property over time, independent of the income it generates.

### 5.1. Depreciation

**Definition:** Depreciation refers to the loss of value of a physical asset due to wear and tear, usage, and the passage of time. In property valuation, it primarily relates to the physical deterioration of the building structure and its components.

**Types of Depreciation:**

*   **Physical Deterioration:** Wear and tear from use, age, and the elements (e.g., leaky roofs, worn-out plumbing).
*   **Functional Obsolescence:** Loss of value due to poor design, outdated features, or inefficiency that makes the property less desirable or functional compared to modern alternatives (e.g., low ceiling heights, inefficient layouts, lack of modern amenities).
*   **Economic/External Obsolescence:** Loss of value due to factors outside the property itself, such as a decline in the desirability of the neighborhood, changes in zoning laws, or increased competition from newer properties.

**Methods of Accounting for Depreciation in Valuation:**

*   **Straight-Line Depreciation:** Spreads the loss of value evenly over the economic life of the asset.
    *   Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Salvage Value) / Economic Life
*   **Sinking Fund Method:** Accounts for depreciation by setting aside a periodic amount that, with accumulated interest, will replace the asset at the end of its life. This is essentially what the sinking fund factor does.
*   **Reducing Balance Method:** Charges a higher depreciation amount in the earlier years of an asset's life and a lower amount in later years.

**In Income Capitalization:**

Depreciation is often accounted for implicitly when using the sinking fund method within the YP calculation for a term certain. For properties valued in perpetuity, the concept of "renewal" through ongoing maintenance and eventual replacement is key.

### 5.2. Obsolescence

**Definition:** Obsolescence is the loss of value due to causes other than physical deterioration. It's about a property becoming outdated or less desirable in relation to current standards, technology, or market preferences.

**Distinction from Depreciation:**

While depreciation focuses on physical wear and tear, obsolescence relates more to functional and economic shortcomings.

**Examples:**

*   **Functional Obsolescence:** A building with a layout that is no longer practical for modern office tenants, or a residential property lacking ensuite bathrooms in bedrooms.
*   **Economic Obsolescence:** A factory located in an area that has transitioned to residential use, making its continued operation difficult due to zoning or community objections. A retail property in a declining shopping district.

**Impact on Valuation:**

Obsolescence can significantly reduce the potential income a property can generate or increase the cost of renovations needed to make it competitive, thereby lowering its market value. Valuers must identify and quantify these losses.

## 6. Freehold and Leasehold Properties

The nature of property ownership significantly impacts valuation methods.

### 6.1. Freehold Property

**Definition:** Freehold ownership means owning the property and the land it stands on outright, in perpetuity, with no time limit. The owner has full rights of possession, use, and disposal.

**Valuation Considerations:**

*   **Income Approach:** Can be applied if the property is income-producing. The value is typically the capitalized value of the net income.
*   **Direct Comparison Approach:** Comparing with similar freehold properties that have recently sold.
*   **Cost Approach:** Estimating the cost to replace the property, less depreciation, plus the land value.

### 6.2. Leasehold Property

**Definition:** Leasehold ownership means owning the right to occupy and use a property for a specified period under a lease agreement. The ownership of the land itself remains with the "freeholder" or "landlord."

**Valuation Considerations:**

Leasehold properties require more complex valuation because the value is for the unexpired term of the lease, not in perpetuity.

*   **Valuation of the Leasehold Interest:**
    *   **Income Approach for Leaseholder:** If the leaseholder sub-lets the property, their income is the difference between the rent received from sub-tenants and the rent paid to the landlord. The value of the leasehold interest is the capitalized value of this surplus income.
    *   **Capitalized Value of Ground Rent:** The value of the landlord's interest is the capitalized value of the ground rent (the rent paid by the leaseholder to the landlord), often considered as a perpetuity.
    *   **YP for a Term Certain:** The YP used to capitalize the income from a leasehold property is always a "YP for a Term Certain" corresponding to the unexpired term of the lease.

**Example:**

A leaseholder pays a ground rent of $500 per year. They sub-let the property for $15,000 per year, with 10 years remaining on the original lease. The required yield is 12%.

**Valuation of Leaseholder's Interest:**

*   Net annual income received by leaseholder = $15,000 (Sub-rent) - $500 (Ground Rent) = $14,500
*   This income is for the unexpired term of the lease (10 years).
*   We need to capitalize this income using a YP for a Term Certain of 10 years at 12%.

Calculate YP for a Term Certain (10 years, 12%):
*   YP = $\frac{1 - (1+0.12)^{-10}}{0.12}$
*   YP = $\frac{1 - (1.12)^{-10}}{0.12}$
*   YP = $\frac{1 - 0.32197}{0.12}$
*   YP = $\frac{0.67803}{0.12}$
*   YP = 5.65025

*   **Value of Leasehold Interest = $14,500 * 5.65025 = $81,928.63**

**Valuation of Landlord's Interest:**

*   The landlord receives a perpetual ground rent of $500.
*   YP in Perpetuity = 1 / 0.12 = 8.3333

*   **Value of Landlord's Interest = $500 * 8.3333 = $4,166.65**

**Total Value (for illustrative purposes) = Leaseholder's Interest + Landlord's Interest**

**Important Point:** When valuing leasehold properties, it's essential to understand the terms of the lease, including the unexpired term, ground rent, and any other covenants.

## Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A commercial property has a gross potential rental income of $200,000 per annum. The estimated vacancy and collection loss is 5% of gross potential income. Operating expenses are estimated at $50,000 per annum. Calculate the Net Income.

**Question 2:**
An investor wants to buy a property that generates a net income of $75,000 per year in perpetuity. If the investor's required rate of return (yield) is 9%, calculate the Year's Purchase in perpetuity.

**Question 3:**
A building component costs $25,000 and needs replacement in 15 years. If the sinking fund can earn 6% per annum, calculate the annual sinking fund contribution required.

**Question 4:**
Calculate the Year's Purchase for a term of 25 years at a yield of 8% per annum.

**Question 5:**
Explain the difference between depreciation and obsolescence with an example of each.

**Answer 1:**
*   Gross Potential Income = $200,000
*   Vacancy & Collection Loss = 5% of $200,000 = $10,000
*   Effective Gross Income = $200,000 - $10,000 = $190,000
*   Operating Expenses = $50,000
*   **Net Income = $190,000 - $50,000 = $140,000**

**Answer 2:**
*   YP in Perpetuity = 1 / Yield Rate
*   YP in Perpetuity = 1 / 0.09 = **11.11** (approximately)
*   Value of Property = Net Income * YP in Perpetuity
*   Value = $75,000 * 11.11 = $833,250 (approximately)

**Answer 3:**
*   SF = $\frac{i}{(1+i)^n - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.06}{(1+0.06)^{15} - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.06}{(1.06)^{15} - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.06}{2.39656 - 1}$
*   SF = $\frac{0.06}{1.39656}$
*   SF ≈ 0.04296

*   Annual Sinking Fund Contribution = Cost * SF
*   Annual Contribution = $25,000 * 0.04296 = **$1,074** (approximately)

**Answer 4:**
*   YP for a Term Certain = $\frac{1 - (1+i)^{-n}}{i}$
*   YP = $\frac{1 - (1+0.08)^{-25}}{0.08}$
*   YP = $\frac{1 - (1.08)^{-25}}{0.08}$
*   YP = $\frac{1 - 0.13629}{0.08}$
*   YP = $\frac{0.86371}{0.08}$
*   YP = **10.796** (approximately)

**Answer 5:**
*   **Depreciation:** Loss of value due to physical wear and tear.
    *   *Example:* A concrete foundation showing signs of cracking due to age and exposure to the elements.
*   **Obsolescence:** Loss of value due to being outdated, inefficient, or undesirable compared to modern alternatives or market preferences.
    *   *Example:* A residential property with a single bathroom and no ensuite facilities, making it less attractive to modern buyers compared to properties with multiple bathrooms.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Net Income is Key:** Most income capitalization methods are based on Net Income, not Gross Income.
*   **Operating Expenses are Crucial:** Accurately estimating all reasonable operating expenses is vital for determining Net Income.
*   **YP Choice Matters:** The correct Year's Purchase (perpetuity or term certain) must be used based on the investment's expected life and capital recovery method.
*   **Sinking Fund for Replacement:** The sinking fund accounts for the eventual replacement of short-lived assets.
*   **Leasehold Complexity:** Valuation of leasehold properties involves considering the unexpired term and the interests of both the leaseholder and the landlord.
*   **Obsolescence Reduces Value:** Functional and economic obsolescence can significantly impact a property's market value, often requiring adjustments beyond physical depreciation.
*   **Yield Reflects Risk:** The yield (interest rate) used in YP calculations reflects the risk associated with the investment. Higher risk generally means a higher yield and a lower YP.
