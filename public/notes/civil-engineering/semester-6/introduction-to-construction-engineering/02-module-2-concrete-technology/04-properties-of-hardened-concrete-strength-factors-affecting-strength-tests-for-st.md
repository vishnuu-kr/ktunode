# Module 2: Concrete Technology - Properties of Hardened Concrete

## Topic: Strength, Factors Affecting Strength, Tests for Strength of Concrete in Compression, Tension, and Flexure

---

## 1. Properties of Hardened Concrete: Strength

Hardened concrete possesses a range of properties that dictate its performance in structural applications. Among these, **strength** is arguably the most critical. Strength refers to the ability of hardened concrete to resist applied loads without failure.

### Key Concepts & Definitions:

*   **Compressive Strength ($f'_c$):** The maximum compressive stress that concrete can withstand before crushing. This is the most common and important measure of concrete strength.
*   **Tensile Strength:** The ability of concrete to resist tensile stresses. Concrete is inherently weak in tension due to the presence of micro-cracks.
*   **Flexural Strength (Modulus of Rupture):** The tensile stress in the outermost fibers of a concrete beam or slab at the point of failure in a bending test. It is a measure of the concrete's resistance to cracking under bending.
*   **Bond Strength:** The ability of concrete to adhere to reinforcement (steel bars) and transfer stresses between them.

## 2. Factors Affecting Strength of Concrete

The strength of hardened concrete is not an inherent property of its constituents but rather a result of the complex chemical and physical interactions that occur during hydration and hardening. Several factors influence this outcome:

### 2.1. Water-Cement Ratio ($w/c$):

*   **Key Concept:** The ratio of the weight of water to the weight of cement in the mix.
*   **Impact:** This is the **most significant factor** influencing concrete strength.
    *   **Lower $w/c$ ratio:** Leads to higher strength. More cementitious material is available for hydration, resulting in a denser, less porous hardened cement paste.
    *   **Higher $w/c$ ratio:** Leads to lower strength. Excess water creates larger pores and voids within the cement paste, weakening the structure.
*   **Example:** A mix with a $w/c$ of 0.4 will generally be stronger than a mix with a $w/c$ of 0.6, assuming all other factors are equal.

### 2.2. Cement Content and Type:

*   **Cement Content:** Higher cement content (while maintaining a low $w/c$) generally leads to higher strength.
*   **Type of Cement:** Different types of Portland cement have varying chemical compositions and fineness, affecting their rate of hydration and ultimate strength development.
    *   **Type I (Ordinary Portland Cement):** Standard cement for general use.
    *   **Type III (High Early Strength Portland Cement):** Sets and gains strength faster, suitable for applications requiring rapid construction.
    *   **Type II (Moderate Sulfate Resistance):** Gains strength more slowly.
*   **Fineness of Cement:** Finer cement particles hydrate more rapidly and contribute to earlier strength gain, but can also lead to increased heat of hydration.

### 2.3. Aggregate Properties:

*   **Aggregate-Cement Ratio:** The proportion of aggregates to cementitious material.
*   **Strength of Aggregates:** Stronger aggregates can withstand higher stresses, preventing aggregate fracture from being the failure mechanism.
*   **Texture and Shape of Aggregates:** Rough-textured, angular aggregates provide better mechanical interlock and bond with the cement paste than smooth, rounded aggregates, leading to higher strength.
*   **Gradation of Aggregates:** Well-graded aggregates (a good mix of different sizes) result in a denser concrete with fewer voids, improving strength. Poorly graded aggregates can lead to a weaker mix.
*   **Cleanliness of Aggregates:** Aggregates should be free from dirt, clay, organic matter, and other impurities that can interfere with the bond between the aggregate and cement paste.

### 2.4. Curing Conditions:

*   **Key Concept:** Curing is the process of maintaining adequate moisture content and temperature in concrete for a specified period after placement to allow hydration to continue.
*   **Impact:** Proper curing is crucial for achieving the designed strength.
    *   **Moisture:** Essential for hydration. Insufficient moisture stops or slows down the chemical reaction, resulting in weaker concrete.
    *   **Temperature:** Affects the rate of hydration. Higher temperatures generally accelerate strength gain, but excessively high temperatures can lead to a weaker, more porous paste. Low temperatures can significantly slow down or even halt hydration.
*   **Methods:** Water curing (ponding, spraying), membrane curing (plastic sheeting, curing compounds).

### 2.5. Admixtures:

*   **Key Concept:** Materials added to concrete at the time of mixing to modify its properties.
*   **Impact on Strength:**
    *   **Water-Reducing Admixtures (Plasticizers/Superplasticizers):** Allow for a lower $w/c$ ratio for a given workability, thus increasing strength.
    *   **Air-Entraining Admixtures:** Introduce microscopic air bubbles, improving freeze-thaw resistance but can slightly reduce strength.
    *   **Pozzolanic Admixtures (Fly Ash, Silica Fume):** React with calcium hydroxide (a byproduct of cement hydration) to form additional cementitious compounds, improving long-term strength and durability.

### 2.6. Age of Concrete:

*   **Impact:** Concrete strength generally increases with age as hydration continues.
*   **Rate of Gain:** The rate of strength gain is highest in the early stages and slows down over time. Standard design strengths are typically specified at 28 days.

### 2.7. Compaction:

*   **Key Concept:** The process of removing entrapped air from fresh concrete.
*   **Impact:** Good compaction (e.g., through vibration) reduces voids and air pockets, leading to a denser and stronger concrete. Poorly compacted concrete will have lower strength.

## 3. Tests for Strength of Concrete

To ensure concrete meets design specifications, various tests are performed on hardened concrete. These tests are typically carried out on specimens made from the same batch of concrete used in the construction.

### 3.1. Compression Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the compressive strength of concrete. This is the most common and important test.
*   **Specimen:** Typically a cylindrical or cubical specimen (e.g., 150mm diameter x 300mm height cylinder, or 150mm cube).
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Cylindrical or cubical specimens are cast and cured under standard laboratory conditions for a specified period (e.g., 7, 14, or 28 days).
    2.  The specimens are placed in a compression testing machine.
    3.  A gradually increasing load is applied axially to the specimen until it fails (crushes).
    4.  The maximum load at failure is recorded.
*   **Calculation:**
    *   Compressive Strength = Maximum Load / Cross-sectional Area of Specimen
*   **Results:** The strength is usually expressed in MPa (Megapascals) or psi (pounds per square inch). The design compressive strength ($f'_c$) is the characteristic strength (a specified lower limit, usually the 28-day strength) adjusted for factors like specimen shape and size.

### 3.2. Tension Test (Splitting Tensile Strength Test):

*   **Purpose:** To estimate the tensile strength of concrete. Direct tensile tests are difficult to perform accurately due to difficulties in gripping the specimen and ensuring uniform stress distribution. The splitting tensile strength test is an indirect method.
*   **Specimen:** Cylindrical specimens are commonly used.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Cylindrical specimens are cured as for compression tests.
    2.  The specimen is placed horizontally in a compression testing machine.
    3.  A line load is applied along the vertical diameter of the cylinder.
    4.  The tensile stresses develop horizontally across the vertical diameter, causing the specimen to split into two halves.
    5.  The maximum load at splitting is recorded.
*   **Calculation:**
    *   Splitting Tensile Strength = (2 * Maximum Load) / (π * Diameter * Length)
*   **Relation to Compressive Strength:** Splitting tensile strength is typically about 10-15% of the compressive strength.

### 3.3. Flexure Test (Modulus of Rupture Test):

*   **Purpose:** To determine the flexural strength (modulus of rupture) of concrete, which is important for pavements and slabs.
*   **Specimen:** Typically a rectangular prism or beam specimen.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Beam specimens are cast and cured.
    2.  The specimen is placed on two supports.
    3.  A load is applied at the mid-span or at two points along the beam.
    4.  The load is increased until the beam fails due to bending.
    5.  The maximum load at failure is recorded.
*   **Calculation (for a simply supported beam with load at mid-span):**
    *   Modulus of Rupture = (3 * Load * Length) / (2 * Width * Depth²)
*   **Relation to Compressive Strength:** Flexural strength is generally higher than splitting tensile strength but still significantly lower than compressive strength.

## 5. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:** The water-cement ratio ($w/c$) is the most critical factor affecting the strength of concrete.

**Answer 2:** A high water-cement ratio leads to a weaker concrete because it creates more pores and voids within the cement paste, reducing its density and internal bonding.

**Answer 3:** Two types of admixtures that can improve concrete strength are:
    *   Water-reducing admixtures (plasticizers/superplasticizers)
    *   Pozzolanic admixtures (e.g., fly ash, silica fume)

**Answer 4:**
    1.  Concrete cylinder specimens are cast and cured for a specified period (e.g., 28 days).
    2.  The cured specimen is placed in a compression testing machine.
    3.  A gradually increasing load is applied axially to the specimen.
    4.  The maximum load at which the specimen fails (crushes) is recorded.
    5.  Compressive strength is calculated by dividing the maximum load by the cross-sectional area of the cylinder.

**Answer 5:** The splitting tensile strength test is used to estimate the tensile strength of concrete because direct tensile tests are difficult to perform accurately. The results of this test are typically about 10-15% of the concrete's compressive strength.

## Important Points to Remember:

*   **Water-Cement Ratio is King:** Always remember the inverse relationship between $w/c$ and strength.
*   **Curing is Crucial:** Proper curing is as important as the mix design for achieving desired strength.
*   **Compression is Key:** Compressive strength is the primary measure for concrete in most structural applications.
*   **Aggregates Matter:** The quality, shape, and grading of aggregates significantly influence concrete strength.
*   **Standardization:** Tests are performed on standardized specimens under controlled conditions to ensure comparability of results.
