### 1. Introduction to Financial Statements

Financial statements are formal records of the financial activities and position of a business, person, or other entity. They provide a snapshot of the entity's financial health and performance over a specific period. For engineers, understanding these statements is crucial for making informed decisions related to project costs, resource allocation, profitability, and the financial viability of engineering solutions.

**Purpose of Financial Statements:**

*   **Information for Decision Making:** They help stakeholders (investors, creditors, management, government agencies) make informed economic decisions.
*   **Performance Evaluation:** They show how well a company has performed in terms of profitability and efficiency.
*   **Financial Position Assessment:** They reveal the company's assets, liabilities, and equity, indicating its financial strength.
*   **Accountability:** They demonstrate how management has utilized the company's resources.

**Key Financial Statements:**

The primary financial statements are:

*   **Profit & Loss Account (P&L Account) / Income Statement:** Shows the profitability of an entity over a period.
*   **Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position:** Shows the financial position of an entity at a specific point in time.
*   **Cash Flow Statement:** (Not covered in this introductory topic but important to note) Shows the movement of cash into and out of the business.
*   **Statement of Changes in Equity:** (Not covered in this introductory topic) Shows changes in the owners' equity over a period.

### 2. The Profit & Loss Account (Income Statement)

The Profit & Loss Account (P&L Account), also known as the Income Statement, summarizes a company's revenues, expenses, gains, and losses over a specific accounting period (e.g., a month, quarter, or year). Its primary goal is to determine the **net profit** or **net loss** for that period.

**Basic Structure:**

The P&L Account follows a simple structure:

**Revenue**
\- **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**
= **Gross Profit**
\- **Operating Expenses**
= **Operating Profit (or EBIT - Earnings Before Interest and Taxes)**
\+ **Other Income**
\- **Interest Expense**
= **Profit Before Tax (PBT)**
\- **Income Tax Expense**
= **Net Profit (or Net Income)**

**Key Components:**

*   **Revenue/Sales:** The income generated from the primary business activities (e.g., selling engineering services, products).
*   **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS):** The direct costs attributable to the production or purchase of the goods or services sold by a company. For engineering firms, this might include direct labor costs of engineers working on projects and direct material costs.
*   **Gross Profit:** Revenue minus COGS. It represents the profit a company makes after deducting the costs associated with making and selling its products or services.
*   **Operating Expenses (or Selling, General & Administrative Expenses - SG&A):** Expenses incurred in the normal course of business, not directly related to the production of goods or services. Examples include:
    *   Salaries (of administrative staff, sales force)
    *   Rent for office space
    *   Utilities
    *   Marketing and Advertising
    *   Depreciation (on office equipment, buildings)
    *   Professional Fees (legal, accounting)
*   **Operating Profit (EBIT):** Gross Profit minus Operating Expenses. It shows the profit from the company's core operations before considering financing and tax costs.
*   **Other Income:** Income generated from non-primary business activities (e.g., interest earned on investments, gains from selling assets).
*   **Interest Expense:** Cost incurred for borrowing money.
*   **Profit Before Tax (PBT):** Operating Profit plus Other Income minus Interest Expense.
*   **Income Tax Expense:** The amount of tax payable on the company's profits.
*   **Net Profit (Net Income):** The final profit remaining after all expenses, including taxes, have been deducted from total revenue. This is the "bottom line."

**Important Note for Engineers:** For engineering projects, you might see costs related to project execution directly. For example, direct labor of engineers on a project, materials used in a prototype, and project-specific equipment rental would likely fall under COGS or direct project costs. Overhead costs (like office rent, administrative salaries) would be operating expenses.

### 3. Preparation of a Simple Profit & Loss Account

To prepare a P&L Account, you need a list of revenues and expenses. These are typically derived from a trial balance or a list of accounts.

**Process:**

1.  **Identify Revenue and Expense Accounts:** Categorize all accounts into revenues and expenses.
2.  **Calculate Gross Profit:** Subtract COGS from Sales Revenue.
3.  **Calculate Operating Profit:** Subtract all operating expenses from Gross Profit.
4.  **Calculate Net Profit:** Adjust operating profit for other income, interest expense, and income tax.

### 4. The Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position)

The Balance Sheet, also known as the Statement of Financial Position, presents a company's financial **snapshot** at a **specific point in time** (e.g., as of December 31st). It outlines what the company owns (assets), what it owes to others (liabilities), and the owners' stake in the company (equity).

**Fundamental Accounting Equation:**

The Balance Sheet is based on the fundamental accounting equation:

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

This equation must always balance.

**Structure:**

The Balance Sheet is typically presented in a classified format, grouping similar items together.

**Assets:** Resources controlled by the entity from which future economic benefits are expected to flow.
*   **Current Assets:** Assets expected to be converted to cash or used up within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer.
    *   Cash and Cash Equivalents
    *   Accounts Receivable (money owed by customers)
    *   Inventory (for manufacturing or trading firms)
    *   Prepaid Expenses (e.g., prepaid rent, insurance)
*   **Non-Current Assets (Fixed Assets/Long-Term Assets):** Assets held for long-term use, not expected to be sold within one year.
    *   Property, Plant, and Equipment (PPE) - e.g., buildings, machinery, vehicles, office equipment.
    *   Intangible Assets (e.g., patents, copyrights, goodwill)
    *   Long-Term Investments

**Liabilities:** Obligations of the entity arising from past transactions, the settlement of which is expected to result in an outflow of resources.
*   **Current Liabilities:** Obligations due within one year or the operating cycle.
    *   Accounts Payable (money owed to suppliers)
    *   Salaries Payable
    *   Utilities Payable
    *   Short-Term Loans
    *   Accrued Expenses
*   **Non-Current Liabilities (Long-Term Liabilities):** Obligations due after one year.
    *   Long-Term Loans Payable
    *   Bonds Payable
    *   Deferred Tax Liabilities

**Equity:** The residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting all its liabilities. It represents the owners' claims on the business.
*   **Share Capital/Owner's Capital:** The amount invested by the owners.
*   **Retained Earnings:** Accumulated profits that have not been distributed to owners as dividends.

**Important Note for Engineers:** Assets can include specialized engineering equipment, software licenses, and even intellectual property developed through R&D. Liabilities might include loans for purchasing equipment or funding large projects. Equity represents the investment by owners or shareholders.

### 5. Preparation of a Simple Balance Sheet

To prepare a Balance Sheet, you need a list of asset, liability, and equity accounts, along with their balances. These are typically derived from the trial balance.

**Process:**

1.  **Identify Asset, Liability, and Equity Accounts:** Categorize all accounts.
2.  **Classify Assets and Liabilities:** Separate them into current and non-current categories.
3.  **Calculate Total Assets:** Sum up all current and non-current assets.
4.  **Calculate Total Liabilities:** Sum up all current and non-current liabilities.
5.  **Calculate Total Equity:** Sum up share capital and retained earnings.
6.  **Verify the Accounting Equation:** Ensure Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Total Equity.

### **Practice Problem 2: Balance Sheet Preparation**

**Data:**
From the records of "BuildRight Engineering" as of December 31, 2023:
*   Cash: $45,000

**Solution:**

The Net Profit of $62,000 increases the equity, specifically the Retained Earnings.

*   New Retained Earnings = $25,000 + $62,000 = $87,000

**Error Check:** Total Assets ($165,000) != Total Liabilities and Equity ($217,000). This means the initial data provided for the Balance Sheet preparation needs to be consistent.

Let's adjust the **Owner's Capital** and **Opening Retained Earnings** to make the Balance Sheet balance, assuming the Net Profit of $62,000 is correct.

If Total Assets = $165,000, then Total Liabilities + Total Equity must equal $165,000.
Total Liabilities = $55,000.
Therefore, Total Equity must be $165,000 - $55,000 = $110,000.
If Owner's Capital is $75,000, then Retained Earnings must be $110,000 - $75,000 = $35,000.

This means the opening retained earnings, before considering the current year's profit, should have been $35,000 - $62,000 = -$27,000 (a deficit). This highlights how crucial accurate starting data is.

*   Long-Term Loan Payable: $30,000
*   Owner's Capital: $75,000
*   **Retained Earnings (Opening Balance): $25,000** (This is the figure we need to verify is consistent or adjust to make the balance sheet balance)

Total Assets = $45,000 + $30,000 + $90,000 = $165,000

Total Liabilities = $18,000 + $7,000 + $30,000 = $55,000

For the Balance Sheet to balance:
Total Equity = Total Assets - Total Liabilities
Total Equity = $165,000 - $55,000 = $110,000

Equity = Owner's Capital + Retained Earnings
$110,000 = $75,000 + Retained Earnings
Retained Earnings = $110,000 - $75,000 = $35,000

So, the opening Retained Earnings should have been $35,000 for the balance sheet to balance *before* adding the current year's profit.

**If we *must* use the provided opening Retained Earnings of $25,000:**
Then, we have a problem with the provided data.
Let's assume the **Owner's Capital** is what needs to be adjusted to balance.
If Opening Retained Earnings = $25,000, and Net Profit = $62,000, then Current Retained Earnings = $25,000 + $62,000 = $87,000.
Total Equity needed = $110,000.
Owner's Capital needed = $110,000 - $87,000 = $23,000.

### 6. Relationship Between P&L Account and Balance Sheet

The Profit & Loss Account and the Balance Sheet are interconnected:

*   **Net Profit/Loss is the Link:** The Net Profit (or Net Loss) calculated in the P&L Account for a period is transferred to the **Retained Earnings** account on the Balance Sheet.
*   **Balance Sheet Reflects P&L Impact:** The P&L shows the financial performance over a period, and the resulting profit or loss impacts the company's overall financial position as reported on the Balance Sheet. A profitable period increases equity, making the company financially stronger.

---

### **Key Points to Remember:**

*   **P&L:** Measures **performance** (profitability) over a **period of time**.
*   **Balance Sheet:** Shows **financial position** at a **specific point in time**.
*   **Assets = Liabilities + Equity** is the fundamental equation for the Balance Sheet.
*   **Net Profit** from the P&L is added to (or Net Loss deducted from) **Retained Earnings** on the Balance Sheet.
*   **Current** assets/liabilities are expected to be converted to cash or settled within one year.
*   **Non-current** assets are long-term assets; **non-current** liabilities are long-term obligations.
*   For engineers, understanding these statements helps in evaluating project feasibility, budgeting, and financial decision-making for engineering projects.
