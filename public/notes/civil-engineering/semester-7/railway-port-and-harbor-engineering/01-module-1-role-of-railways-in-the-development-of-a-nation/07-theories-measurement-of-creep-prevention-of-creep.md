# RAILWAY, PORT AND HARBOR ENGINEERING

## Module 1: Role of Railways in the Development of a Nation

### Topic: Creep in Railway Tracks - Theories, Measurement, and Prevention

---

### **1. Introduction to Creep**

*   **Definition:** Creep is the **longitudinal movement of rails** in the direction of traffic, usually in the direction of the dominant traffic flow. It's a phenomenon that occurs on railway tracks, particularly on the running rails.
*   **Significance:**
    *   Can lead to **buckling of rails** if not controlled, which is a major safety hazard.
    *   Can cause **distortion of the track geometry**, affecting ride quality and increasing maintenance needs.
    *   Can lead to **failures of rail fastenings** and damage to other track components.
*   **Causes (Overview):** Creep is a complex phenomenon resulting from a combination of forces acting on the rails. These forces are primarily transmitted from the wheels of moving trains.

---

### **2. Theories Explaining Creep**

Several theories attempt to explain the causes of creep. While no single theory is universally accepted as explaining all aspects of creep, they collectively provide a good understanding of the forces involved.

#### **2.1. Wheel Slip Theory (Adhesion Theory)**

*   **Core Idea:** This theory attributes creep to the **slipping action of driving wheels** on the rail surface, particularly during acceleration and braking.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   When a locomotive accelerates, the driving wheels try to rotate but are met with resistance from the train's inertia.
    *   If the tractive effort exceeds the adhesion between the wheel and the rail, the wheel will slip forward, trying to "pull" the rail along with it.
    *   Conversely, during braking, the wheels decelerate, and if the braking force is significant, they might slip backward, attempting to "push" the rail backward.
*   **Dominant Direction:** The direction of creep is usually dictated by the direction of the **dominant traffic flow**. In most railway systems, the majority of traffic moves in one direction, leading to a net forward creep.
*   **Factors Influencing:**
    *   **Tractive Effort/Braking Force:** Higher forces lead to more slip.
    *   **Adhesion:** Poor adhesion (e.g., due to wet or oily rails) increases the likelihood of slip.
    *   **Wheel Profile:** The conicity of the wheel plays a role in how it interacts with the rail.
*   **Example:** Imagine pushing a heavy box on the floor. If you push too hard and the floor is slippery, your feet might slide forward, effectively moving the floor backward relative to you. In railways, the wheels are the "feet," and the rails are the "floor."

#### **2.2. Impact or Hammer Blow Theory**

*   **Core Idea:** This theory suggests that creep is caused by the **vertical impact of rolling stock wheels** on the rail.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   When wheels roll over rail joints, imperfections in the rail surface, or other track irregularities, they experience vertical impacts.
    *   These impacts transmit forces that are not perfectly vertical and can have a slight horizontal component.
    *   Repeated impacts, particularly from unbalanced rotating parts in locomotives (like connecting rods), can cause a "hammering" effect that pushes the rail forward.
*   **Factors Influencing:**
    *   **Track Irregularities:** Worn rails, poor joints, and uneven ballast increase impact.
    *   **Unbalanced Rotating Parts:** Especially prominent in older steam locomotives.
    *   **Speed:** Higher speeds generally mean more frequent impacts.
*   **Example:** Imagine hitting a small nail with a hammer. Each strike imparts a force. If you hit it slightly at an angle, it might slightly shift its position. On a larger scale, wheel impacts can contribute to rail movement.

#### **2.3. Wave Propagation Theory**

*   **Core Idea:** This theory posits that creep is due to the **propagation of vertical waves** generated by the wheels moving along the rail.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   As a wheel rolls, it deforms the rail and ballast, creating a stress wave that travels along the rail.
    *   The interaction of this wave with the rail head and the supporting ballast can result in a net forward movement of the rail.
    *   The conicity of the wheel and the elasticity of the track structure are crucial in this process.
*   **Factors Influencing:**
    *   **Wheel Conicity:** The taper of the wheel tread influences how it steers along the rail.
    *   **Rail Elasticity:** The ability of the rail to deform and recover.
    *   **Ballast Support:** The stiffness and uniformity of the ballast bed.
*   **Example:** Think of pushing a flexible spring along a surface. As you push, the spring deforms and then springs back, and this continuous deformation and recovery can lead to a slow movement of the spring itself.

#### **2.4. Thermal Expansion and Contraction**

*   **Core Idea:** While not the primary cause of creep in the context of traffic-induced movement, thermal expansion and contraction of rails contribute to stresses that can be released in a creep-like manner, especially during rail relay or when expansion gaps are not properly managed.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   Rails expand when heated and contract when cooled.
    *   In older track designs with expansion joints, this expansion caused rails to push against each other or against buffer stops.
    *   In continuously welded rails (CWR), the rail is stressed in tension when hot and in compression when cold. If these stresses are released, the rail can move.
*   **Factors Influencing:**
    *   **Ambient Temperature:** The range of temperature fluctuations.
    *   **Rail Length:** Longer rails experience greater expansion.
    *   **Track Restraint:** The ability of the fastenings and ballast to hold the rail in place.
*   **Note:** This is more about stress build-up and release rather than the continuous longitudinal movement observed with traffic. However, it can exacerbate creep or cause sudden movements if not managed.

### **3. Measurement of Creep**

Accurate measurement of creep is essential for monitoring track condition and implementing timely preventive measures.

#### **3.1. Methods of Measurement**

*   **1. Direct Measurement using Reference Pillars:**
    *   **Description:** This is the most common and accurate method. Permanent reference pillars are installed in the ground (ideally anchored to bedrock or a very stable foundation) adjacent to the track.
    *   **Process:**
        *   A reference mark is made on the rail.
        *   The distance between the reference pillar and the rail mark is measured.
        *   This measurement is taken periodically (e.g., weekly or monthly).
        *   A change in this distance indicates creep.
    *   **Advantages:** High accuracy, provides a fixed reference point.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires installation of permanent infrastructure, labor-intensive.
    *   **Example:** Installing concrete pillars about 2 meters away from the track centerline, with a precise metal stud on top. A measuring tape or a laser device is used to measure the distance from the stud to a marked point on the rail web.

*   **2. Measurement at Rail Joints (Less Accurate for Net Creep):**
    *   **Description:** Observing the gap at rail joints.
    *   **Process:** Measuring the opening or closing of gaps at welded joints or expansion gaps.
    *   **Limitations:** Primarily indicates movement at specific locations, not the net creep over the entire rail length. Can be affected by other factors like rail buckling or vertical displacement.

*   **3. Optical/Laser Measurement Systems:**
    *   **Description:** Advanced systems using lasers and digital cameras to track rail movement relative to fixed points.
    *   **Process:** High-precision lasers measure the position of the rail over time.
    *   **Advantages:** Can be automated, potentially faster.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires sophisticated equipment and calibration.

*   **4. Using Existing Track Features:**
    *   **Description:** In the absence of dedicated pillars, sometimes features like bridge abutments or large concrete structures can be used as fixed reference points.
    *   **Process:** Similar to using reference pillars but with less precision and reliance on the stability of the chosen feature.

#### **3.2. Creep Measurement Frequency and Thresholds**

*   **Frequency:** Creep is typically measured at regular intervals, often weekly or monthly, depending on the criticality of the section and the observed creep rates.
*   **Thresholds:** Railway authorities set maximum permissible creep limits. When creep exceeds these limits, corrective action is required. These limits vary depending on the track type, speed, and railway standards, but a common trigger for action might be around **10-15 mm per month** or a cumulative creep of **30-50 mm**. Exceeding these can indicate an increased risk of buckling.

### **4. Prevention of Creep**

Preventing creep is crucial for maintaining track safety and integrity. Strategies involve increasing the resistance of the rails to longitudinal movement.

#### **4.1. Methods of Creep Prevention**

*   **1. Rail Anchors (Creep Restrainers):**
    *   **Description:** These are devices fitted to the rail web and bear against the sleeper or ballast, providing resistance to longitudinal movement.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Spring Steel Anchors (e.g., Cleveland, Nord, Hunt-Spanner):** These are typically spring steel clips that grip the rail head and press against the side of the sleeper. They rely on the spring action to maintain a tight fit.
        *   **Rigid Anchors:** These are generally heavier and more rigid devices that are clamped to the rail and then secured to the sleeper or ballast.
    *   **Mechanism:** They create a frictional or positive mechanical restraint that opposes the forces causing creep.
    *   **Installation:** Usually fitted in pairs, on opposing rails, and at regular intervals along the track (e.g., every 1 in 3 or 1 in 4 sleepers). The direction of fitting is critical – they should be installed to resist the dominant direction of creep.
    *   **Example:** A spring steel clip fitted around the rail web and clipped onto the side of a concrete sleeper.

*   **2. Elastic Rail Fastenings (ERFs):**
    *   **Description:** Modern fastening systems (e.g., Pandrol, Deenbandhu) that use resilient clips and shoulders to hold the rail to the baseplate.
    *   **Mechanism:** These systems provide a degree of elastic resistance to longitudinal movement due to the spring action of the clips and the interface friction. While not their primary function, they contribute to creep resistance.
    *   **Example:** A Pandrol clip that clips into a cast iron shoulder, holding the rail down and preventing it from lifting. The clip also provides some resistance to lateral and longitudinal movement.

*   **3. Increased Fastening Force/Stiffness:**
    *   **Description:** Using stronger or more numerous fastenings, or ensuring all fastenings are correctly tensioned.
    *   **Mechanism:** A tighter grip of the rail to the sleeper increases the friction and mechanical resistance to creep.
    *   **Example:** Ensuring all clips are properly seated and tensioned during maintenance. Using additional clips on critical sections.

*   **4. Ballast Resistance:**
    *   **Description:** The resistance provided by the ballast bed to the movement of the sleepers and rails.
    *   **Mechanism:** Well-compacted, clean ballast offers greater resistance to sleeper movement, which in turn resists rail creep. Fouled or poorly drained ballast reduces this resistance.
    *   **Maintenance:** Regular tamping and dressing of ballast are crucial.

*   **5. Insulated Joints and Expansion Joints (Older Systems):**
    *   **Description:** While expansion joints can be a source of creep initiation in older systems, they were designed to manage thermal expansion.
    *   **Mechanism:** They allow controlled movement, but the mechanisms at the joints themselves can be points where creep is initiated due to impact and play.
    *   **Note:** Modern track design predominantly uses continuously welded rail (CWR) without expansion joints, minimizing this specific issue but requiring robust creep prevention measures.

*   **6. Rail Grinding and Profiling:**
    *   **Description:** Maintaining smooth rail profiles and removing any burrs or irregularities.
    *   **Mechanism:** Reduces the likelihood of wheels snagging or causing impacts that can initiate creep.

#### **4.2. Factors Influencing Creep and Prevention Effectiveness**

*   **Traffic Density and Type:** High-density traffic, especially with frequent acceleration/braking, leads to more creep. Heavy freight trains can cause significant forces.
*   **Track Geometry:** Gradients, curves (especially tight curves), and turnouts can influence creep.
*   **Rail Temperature:** High temperatures can increase the forces required to overcome ballast resistance, potentially leading to creep during periods of rapid cooling.
*   **Ballast Condition:** The quality, drainage, and compaction of the ballast are critical.
*   **Fastening Condition:** Worn or loose fastenings significantly reduce creep resistance.
*   **Rail Material and Profile:** The condition and profile of the rail head can influence wheel-rail interaction.

---

### **5. Key Points to Remember**

*   **Creep is longitudinal rail movement driven by traffic.**
*   **Wheel slip during acceleration/braking is a primary cause.**
*   **Impacts and wave propagation also contribute.**
*   **The direction of creep is usually the dominant traffic direction.**
*   **High creep rates pose a significant buckling risk.**
*   **Reference pillars are the most reliable method for creep measurement.**
*   **Rail anchors are the primary method of creep prevention.**
*   **Maintaining ballast and fastenings is crucial.**
*   **Continuous monitoring and timely intervention are essential.**

### **6. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:** Define creep in the context of railway engineering.
**Answer:** Creep is the longitudinal movement of rails in the direction of traffic, typically caused by the forces exerted by moving wheels.

**Question 2:** Briefly explain the Wheel Slip Theory of creep.
**Answer:** This theory states that creep occurs due to the slipping action of driving wheels on the rail surface during acceleration or braking. If the tractive or braking effort exceeds the adhesion, the wheel slips, causing the rail to move.

**Question 3:** What is the most accurate method for measuring creep?
**Answer:** Direct measurement using permanent reference pillars installed in the ground adjacent to the track.

**Question 4:** Name two common methods used for preventing creep.
**Answer:** Rail anchors (e.g., spring steel clips) and elastic rail fastenings.

**Question 5:** What are the potential hazards associated with uncontrolled creep?
**Answer:** The primary hazard is **rail buckling**, which can lead to derailment. Other hazards include distortion of track geometry, failure of rail fastenings, and damage to track components.

**Question 6 (Scenario-based):** A track section experiences heavy freight traffic in one direction. Measurements show that the rails are consistently moving forward at an average rate of 12 mm per month. Is this rate a cause for concern, and what action might be required?
**Answer:** A creep rate of 12 mm per month is likely a cause for concern, as it may exceed typical permissible thresholds. Action required would typically involve inspecting the effectiveness of existing rail anchors, ensuring fastenings are tight, and potentially installing additional rail anchors or enhancing ballast support to resist the movement.
