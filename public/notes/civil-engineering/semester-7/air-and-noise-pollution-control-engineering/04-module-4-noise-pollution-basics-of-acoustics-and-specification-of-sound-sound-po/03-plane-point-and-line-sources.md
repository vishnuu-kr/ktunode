# Module 4: Noise Pollution - Basics of Acoustics and Sound Power

## Topic: Plane, Point, and Line Sources of Sound

This module delves into the fundamental principles of acoustics and how sound power is characterized. Understanding different types of sound sources is crucial for analyzing and controlling noise pollution effectively.

---

### 1. Introduction to Sound Sources

Sound is generated by vibrating objects. The way these vibrations propagate through a medium (like air) determines the characteristics of the sound field. Different source geometries lead to different sound propagation patterns, which we categorize as point, line, or plane sources.

---

### 2. Sound Power (W)

*   **Definition:** Sound power is the rate at which sound energy is radiated by a source. It is the total acoustic energy emitted per unit time.
*   **Units:** Watts (W)
*   **Key Concept:** Sound power is an **intrinsic property of the source** itself, independent of the environment or the listener's position. It's like the wattage of a light bulb – it doesn't change whether you're standing next to it or across the room.
*   **Measurement:** Typically measured in an anechoic (echo-free) environment or calculated using sound pressure measurements at various distances.

---

### 3. Sound Pressure Level (SPL)

*   **Definition:** Sound pressure is the local deviation from atmospheric pressure caused by a sound wave. Sound Pressure Level (SPL) is the logarithmic measure of the RMS sound pressure compared to a reference pressure.
*   **Units:** Decibels (dB)
*   **Key Concept:** SPL is **dependent on the distance from the source and the environment** (reflections, absorption). It's what we actually "hear" and measure at a specific location.
*   **Reference Pressure:** $20 \mu Pa$ (Pascal), the approximate threshold of human hearing.

---

### 4. Characterizing Sound Sources: Different Geometries

The way sound energy spreads out depends on the physical shape and size of the source. This is why we classify sources into point, line, and plane sources.

#### 4.1. Point Sources

*   **Definition:** A point source is an idealized sound source that radiates sound equally in all directions (isotropically). Its physical dimensions are negligible compared to the distances at which its sound is measured.
*   **Examples:**
    *   A small, isolated loudspeaker.
    *   A single-point source of combustion or explosion.
    *   A small vibrating object.
*   **Sound Propagation:** Sound energy spreads out spherically from a point source.
*   **Relationship between Sound Power and Sound Pressure:**
    *   The intensity (I) of sound from a point source decreases with the square of the distance (r) from the source.
        *   $I \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$
    *   Since Intensity is related to the square of Sound Pressure ($I \propto p^2$), Sound Pressure Level (SPL) decreases by **6 dB for every doubling of distance**.
        *   $SPL(r_2) = SPL(r_1) - 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)^2 = SPL(r_1) - 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)$
*   **Key Formula for Point Sources (Free Field):**
    *   $SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{Q}{4\pi r^2}\right) + K$
        *   Where:
            *   $SPL$ is the Sound Pressure Level in dB
            *   $PWL$ is the Sound Power Level in dB (derived from Sound Power, $PWL = 10 \log_{10}(W/W_0)$, where $W_0 = 10^{-12}$ W)
            *   $Q$ is the directivity factor (for an isotropic source, $Q=1$)
            *   $r$ is the distance from the source in meters
            *   $K$ is a correction factor for environmental conditions (e.g., absorption, reflections). In a free field with no reflections, $K \approx 0$ dB (relative to a reference pressure of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa).

#### 4.2. Line Sources

*   **Definition:** A line source is a sound source that extends along a line. Its length is significant compared to the distance from the source.
*   **Examples:**
    *   A long conveyor belt with multiple noise-generating components.
    *   A long pipe carrying fluid (e.g., a leaking pipe).
    *   A road with heavy traffic (often approximated as a line source).
    *   A train track.
*   **Sound Propagation:** Sound energy spreads out in a cylindrical pattern from a line source.
*   **Relationship between Sound Power and Sound Pressure:**
    *   The intensity (I) of sound from a line source decreases with the distance (r) from the source.
        *   $I \propto \frac{1}{r}$
    *   Sound Pressure Level (SPL) decreases by **3 dB for every doubling of distance**.
        *   $SPL(r_2) = SPL(r_1) - 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)$
*   **Key Formula for Line Sources (Free Field):**
    *   $SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{Q}{2\pi r L}\right) + K$
        *   Where:
            *   $PWL$ is the Sound Power Level per unit length (dB/m)
            *   $r$ is the distance from the line source in meters
            *   $L$ is the length of the source (often implicitly handled in PWL per unit length)
            *   $Q$ is the directivity factor (for a source emitting uniformly along a line, $Q=1$ in the horizontal plane perpendicular to the line).

#### 4.3. Plane Sources

*   **Definition:** A plane source is an idealized sound source that radiates sound uniformly over a surface. Its surface area is significant compared to the distance from the source.
*   **Examples:**
    *   A large, vibrating wall or panel.
    *   A large fan or ventilation outlet.
    *   A broad façade of a building emitting noise.
*   **Sound Propagation:** Sound energy spreads out in a fan-like manner. In the far-field, it can be approximated as cylindrical.
*   **Relationship between Sound Power and Sound Pressure:**
    *   In the ideal case, the sound intensity (I) from an infinite plane source in a free field remains **constant with distance**.
        *   $I \propto constant$
    *   Therefore, Sound Pressure Level (SPL) **does not decrease with distance** in the ideal far-field of an infinite plane source.
        *   $SPL(r_2) = SPL(r_1)$
*   **Practical Considerations:**
    *   Finite plane sources will exhibit some decrease in SPL with distance, but it will be less than that of point or line sources.
    *   The concept of "distance" is less straightforward for plane sources; often, the distance from the nearest edge or the center of the plane is considered.
*   **Key Formula for Infinite Plane Sources (Free Field, Far-Field):**
    *   $SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{Q}{A}\right) + K$
        *   Where:
            *   $PWL$ is the Sound Power Level per unit area (dB/m²)
            *   $A$ is the surface area of the source in m²
            *   $Q$ is the directivity factor (often $Q=1$ for uniform radiation)

---

### 5. Importance of Source Identification

*   **Accurate Noise Prediction:** Correctly identifying the source type (point, line, plane) is crucial for predicting how noise levels will change with distance and for designing effective noise control measures.
*   **Targeted Mitigation:** Different control strategies are effective for different source types.
    *   **Point Sources:** Enclosures, barriers, damping.
    *   **Line Sources:** Barriers placed close to the source, mufflers on individual components, or treating the entire line.
    *   **Plane Sources:** Surface treatments, vibration damping, enclosing large surfaces.
*   **Regulatory Compliance:** Noise regulations often specify limits at specific distances from defined source types.

---

### 6. Key Points to Remember

*   **Sound Power (W):** Source property, independent of distance or environment.
*   **Sound Pressure Level (SPL):** Measurement at a point, dependent on distance and environment.
*   **Point Source:** Spherical radiation, SPL drops **6 dB per doubling of distance**.
*   **Line Source:** Cylindrical radiation, SPL drops **3 dB per doubling of distance**.
*   **Plane Source:** Constant intensity in ideal far-field, SPL **does not decrease with distance** (in the ideal case).
*   The ideal models (infinite plane, isotropic point source) are approximations. Real-world sources often behave as hybrids or approximations of these models.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A small industrial fan is operating. It can be reasonably approximated as a point source. If the Sound Power Level (PWL) of the fan is 90 dB, what would be the approximate Sound Pressure Level (SPL) at a distance of 10 meters in a free field?
a) 70 dB
b) 80 dB
c) 86 dB
d) 90 dB

**Question 2:**
A long, noisy conveyor belt is the primary source of noise in a factory. It can be approximated as a line source. If the Sound Power Level per unit length (PWL/m) is 85 dB/m, what is the expected change in SPL if you move from 5 meters away to 10 meters away from the conveyor belt?
a) Increase by 3 dB
b) Decrease by 3 dB
c) Increase by 6 dB
d) Decrease by 6 dB

**Question 3:**
A large, vibrating metal sheet on a machine can be approximated as a plane source. In the far-field, how does the SPL generally change with distance for an ideal infinite plane source?
a) Decreases by 6 dB per doubling of distance.
b) Decreases by 3 dB per doubling of distance.
c) Remains constant.
d) Increases with distance.

**Question 4:**
Which of the following is a characteristic of Sound Power?
a) It is measured in decibels (dB).
b) It depends on the distance from the source.
c) It is an intrinsic property of the source.
d) It is what is directly perceived by the listener.

**Question 5:**
A small diesel generator is placed in an open field. You measure an SPL of 85 dB at 5 meters.
(a) Assuming it's a point source and the environment is a free field, what is the expected SPL at 10 meters?
(b) What is the expected SPL at 20 meters?

### 8. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
The correct answer is **c) 86 dB**.
Using the point source formula: $SPL = PWL - 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)$
Let $r_1 = 1$ meter (standard reference for PWL calculation) and $r_2 = 10$ meters.
$SPL(10m) = 90 dB - 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{10}{1}\right) = 90 dB - 20 \log_{10}(10) = 90 dB - 20(1) = 70 dB$.
*Correction*: The standard reference for PWL calculation for point sources is often implicitly assumed as the point of origin, but the formula for SPL reduction with distance is key. A common way to think about it is:
$SPL(r) = PWL_{ref} - 20 \log_{10}(r/r_{ref})$
If PWL is given as 90 dB, and we assume this is at a reference distance of 1 meter.
$SPL(10m) = 90 dB - 20 \log_{10}(10/1) = 90 dB - 20 dB = 70 dB$.
*Re-evaluation based on typical problem formulation:* Often, PWL is the absolute sound power expressed in dB relative to $10^{-12}$ W. The formula for SPL from a point source in a free field is:
$SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{Q}{4\pi r^2}\right)$.
Assuming an isotropic point source ($Q=1$):
$SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\right)$
$SPL = PWL - 10 \log_{10}(4\pi r^2)$
$SPL = PWL - (10 \log_{10}(4\pi) + 20 \log_{10}(r))$
$SPL = PWL - (10 \log_{10}(12.566) + 20 \log_{10}(r))$
$SPL = PWL - (11.0 + 20 \log_{10}(r))$
For $r=10m$:
$SPL = 90 dB - (11.0 + 20 \log_{10}(10)) = 90 dB - (11.0 + 20) = 90 dB - 31 dB = 59 dB$.

*Let's rethink the question and options.* The options suggest a simpler interpretation of the 6 dB per doubling of distance rule.
If $PWL = 90 dB$, let's assume this is the "effective" sound power level.
At 1 meter: SPL ≈ 90 dB (ignoring the spherical spreading constant for a moment, or assuming PWL is defined to make this true).
At 2 meters (doubling): SPL ≈ 90 - 6 = 84 dB
At 4 meters (doubling): SPL ≈ 84 - 6 = 78 dB
At 8 meters (doubling): SPL ≈ 78 - 6 = 72 dB
At 10 meters. This is between 8 and 16 meters.
$10 \log_{10}(10/1) = 10$
$20 \log_{10}(10/1) = 20$
$SPL(10m) = PWL - 20 \log_{10}(10/1) = 90 - 20 = 70 dB$.

Let's re-examine the standard formula $SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{Q}{4\pi r^2}\right)$.
For an isotropic point source $Q=1$.
$SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\right) = PWL - 10 \log_{10}(4\pi) - 20 \log_{10}(r)$
$SPL = PWL - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(r)$
If PWL = 90 dB and r = 10m:
$SPL = 90 - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(10) = 90 - 11 - 20 = 59 dB$.

There seems to be a discrepancy between typical textbook questions, simplified rules, and the strict formula. However, the 6 dB per doubling rule is fundamental.
Let's assume the question implies a simpler relationship where PWL represents the source strength and the options reflect the reduction.
If $PWL = 90 dB$, what is the SPL at 10m?
The reduction from 1m to 10m is $20 \log_{10}(10/1) = 20 dB$.
So, if SPL at 1m is assumed to be 90 dB (which is not strictly correct, PWL is not SPL at 1m), then at 10m it would be $90 - 20 = 70 dB$.
The options provided are: 70 dB, 80 dB, 86 dB, 90 dB.

Let's re-evaluate the options.
If the answer is 86 dB, this implies a reduction of 4 dB. This doesn't fit the 6 dB rule.
If the answer is 80 dB, this implies a reduction of 10 dB. This fits $10 \log_{10}(10) = 10 dB$. This is more related to Intensity reduction per doubling of distance ($10 \log_{10}(r_2/r_1)$).
The reduction in Sound Pressure Level for a point source is $20 \log_{10}(r_2/r_1)$.
$20 \log_{10}(10/1) = 20 dB$.
So $SPL(10m) = SPL(1m) - 20 dB$.

Let's try to interpret PWL = 90 dB. This means the total sound power $W = 10^{90/10} \times 10^{-12} W = 10^{-3} W$.
In a free field: $SPL = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{W}{A \cdot p_{ref}^2 / \rho c}\right)$ where A is the surface area of the sphere.
$A = 4\pi r^2$.
$SPL = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{W}{4\pi r^2 \cdot p_{ref}^2 / \rho c}\right) = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{W}{\rho c p_{ref}^2}\right) + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\right)$
$SPL = PWL + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\right) = PWL - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(r)$.
With PWL = 90 dB and r = 10m:
$SPL = 90 - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(10) = 90 - 11 - 20 = 59 dB$.

There might be an error in the question or options, or a very specific convention being used.
However, if we consider the directivity and spreading *only*, a doubling of distance reduces SPL by 6 dB.
$10 \log_{10}(10/1) = 10$. So moving from 1m to 10m means a factor of 10 increase in distance.
If we consider $SPL = PWL + \text{spreading term}$.
Let's assume that PWL=90dB implies $SPL \approx 90dB$ at a very close reference distance (e.g., 1m).
Then at 2m: $90 - 6 = 84 dB$.
At 4m: $84 - 6 = 78 dB$.
At 8m: $78 - 6 = 72 dB$.
At 10m: This is between 8m and 16m.
Reduction from 8m to 10m is $20 \log_{10}(10/8) = 20 \log_{10}(1.25) = 20 \times 0.0969 = 1.94 dB$.
So, $72 dB - 1.94 dB = 70.06 dB$.
Thus, **70 dB** is the most consistent answer if we apply the 6 dB per doubling rule.

**Answer 2:**
The correct answer is **b) Decrease by 3 dB**.
For a line source, the intensity decreases with distance as $1/r$. The sound pressure level decreases by $10 \log_{10}(r_2/r_1)$ for every doubling of distance.
Moving from 5 meters to 10 meters is a doubling of distance.
The change in SPL is $10 \log_{10}(10/5) = 10 \log_{10}(2) = 10 \times 0.301 \approx 3 dB$.
Since the distance is increasing, the SPL will decrease. So, it's a decrease of 3 dB.

**Answer 3:**
The correct answer is **c) Remains constant**.
For an ideal infinite plane source in the far-field, the sound intensity does not decrease with distance, and therefore the Sound Pressure Level (SPL) remains constant.

**Answer 4:**
The correct answer is **c) It is an intrinsic property of the source**.
Sound Power (W) or its logarithmic form, Sound Power Level (PWL), is a measure of the total acoustic energy emitted by a source per unit time and is independent of the surrounding environment or the listener's position. SPL, on the other hand, is measured at a specific location and is affected by distance, reflections, and absorption.

**Answer 5:**
(a) **Expected SPL at 10 meters:**
Assuming it's a point source, SPL decreases by 6 dB for every doubling of distance.
From 5 meters to 10 meters is one doubling of distance.
Therefore, SPL at 10 meters = SPL at 5 meters - 6 dB
SPL at 10 meters = 85 dB - 6 dB = **79 dB**.

(b) **Expected SPL at 20 meters:**
From 5 meters to 20 meters is two doublings of distance (5m -> 10m -> 20m).
Therefore, SPL at 20 meters = SPL at 5 meters - (2 * 6 dB)
SPL at 20 meters = 85 dB - 12 dB = **73 dB**.

*Note on the strict formula application for Q5:*
Using $SPL = PWL - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(r)$
First, we need to find PWL from the measurement at 5m.
$85 dB = PWL - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(5)$
$85 dB = PWL - 11 - 20 \times 0.699 = PWL - 11 - 13.98 = PWL - 24.98$
$PWL = 85 + 24.98 = 109.98 dB \approx 110 dB$.

Now calculate SPL at 10m:
$SPL(10m) = 110 dB - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(10) = 110 - 11 - 20 = 79 dB$.

Calculate SPL at 20m:
$SPL(20m) = 110 dB - 11 - 20 \log_{10}(20) = 110 - 11 - 20 \times 1.301 = 110 - 11 - 26.02 = 72.98 dB \approx 73 dB$.

The simplified 6 dB/doubling rule works well and gives the same results.
