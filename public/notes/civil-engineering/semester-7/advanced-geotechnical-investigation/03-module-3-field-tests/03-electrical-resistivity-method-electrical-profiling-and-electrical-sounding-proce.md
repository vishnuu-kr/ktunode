### 1. Introduction to the Electrical Resistivity Method

#### 1.1 Fundamental Principles

The Electrical Resistivity (ER) method measures the **electrical resistivity** of subsurface materials. Resistivity is an intrinsic property of a material that quantifies its opposition to the flow of electric current.

*   **Definition of Resistivity ($\rho$)**: The resistance ($R$) of a conductor of uniform cross-sectional area ($A$) and length ($L$) is given by Ohm's Law: $V = IR$. The resistivity is defined as:
    $$ \rho = \frac{RA}{L} $$
    *   Units: Ohm-meter ($\Omega \cdot m$) or Ohm-centimeter ($\Omega \cdot cm$).
*   **How it Works**:
    1.  **Current Injection**: An electric current is introduced into the ground through two current electrodes (typically designated as A and B).
    2.  **Potential Measurement**: The resulting potential difference (voltage) is measured between two potential electrodes (typically designated as M and N) placed on the ground surface.
    3.  **Apparent Resistivity ($\rho_a$)**: The measured potential difference and injected current, along with the electrode configuration and spacing, are used to calculate an "apparent resistivity." This is the resistivity of a homogeneous, isotropic half-space that would produce the same potential distribution for the given electrode array. The actual subsurface is rarely homogeneous, hence the term "apparent."
*   **Factors Influencing Resistivity**:
    *   **Moisture Content**: Higher moisture content generally leads to lower resistivity (water contains dissolved ions).
    *   **Porosity and Permeability**: Highly porous and permeable materials can hold more water, influencing resistivity.
    *   **Clay Content**: Clays, especially those with adsorbed ions, tend to have lower resistivity than granular soils due to surface conductivity.
    *   **Salinity of Pore Water**: Dissolved salts significantly decrease resistivity.
    *   **Mineralogy**: Some minerals (e.g., metallic ores) have very low resistivity.
    *   **Temperature**: Resistivity decreases with increasing temperature.

#### 1.2 Common Electrode Arrays

The arrangement of current and potential electrodes significantly impacts the depth of investigation and the sensitivity to subsurface features.

*   **Schlumberger Array**:
    *   **Configuration**: Current electrodes (A, B) are spaced far apart, and potential electrodes (M, N) are placed closer together and centrally between A and B.
    *   **Usage**: Primarily used for **electrical sounding** to determine vertical variations in resistivity. The outer current electrodes are expanded systematically.
*   **Wenner Array**:
    *   **Configuration**: All four electrodes (A, M, N, B) are equally spaced along a straight line.
    *   **Usage**: Can be used for both **profiling** (moving the entire array) and **sounding** (expanding the electrode spacing). It offers good vertical resolution but is sensitive to lateral variations.
*   **Dipole-Dipole Array**:
    *   **Configuration**: Two current dipoles and two potential dipoles, each offset from the other.
    *   **Usage**: Provides good lateral resolution and is sensitive to lateral variations and anomalies. Often used for detailed mapping.
*   **Gradient Array (Pole-Dipole)**:
    *   **Configuration**: One current electrode is very far away (effectively infinite), and the other is relatively close. Potential electrodes are moved.
    *   **Usage**: Sensitive to shallow lateral variations.

**Key Point to Remember**: The choice of electrode array is crucial and depends on the objectives of the investigation (depth vs. lateral mapping).

### 2. Electrical Profiling (Lateral Investigation)

#### 2.1 Procedure

Electrical profiling involves moving a fixed electrode array along a survey line or grid to map lateral variations in resistivity. The depth of investigation is primarily controlled by the electrode spacing, which is kept constant during profiling.

1.  **Select Electrode Array**: Typically, the Wenner or Gradient array is preferred for profiling due to their sensitivity to lateral changes.
2.  **Establish Survey Line(s)**: Mark a line or grid on the ground surface where measurements will be taken.
3.  **Position Electrode Array**: Place the electrodes at the first measurement point according to the chosen array configuration.
4.  **Inject Current**: Connect the current electrodes (A, B) to a power source and inject a known current into the ground.
5.  **Measure Potential Difference**: Record the voltage difference between the potential electrodes (M, N).
6.  **Calculate Apparent Resistivity**: Using the formula for the specific array, calculate the apparent resistivity ($\rho_a$).
7.  **Move Array**: Shift the entire electrode array along the survey line by a predetermined interval (e.g., 1 meter, 5 meters).
8.  **Repeat Steps 4-7**: Continue taking measurements at regular intervals along the survey line.
9.  **Data Presentation**: The measured apparent resistivity values are plotted against the position along the survey line. This creates a resistivity profile.

#### 2.2 Uses of Electrical Profiling

*   **Mapping Lateral Variations**: Identifying changes in subsurface materials like boundaries between different soil types, bedrock outcrops, or zones of differing moisture content.
*   **Locating Buried Objects**: Detecting metallic pipes, cables, or buried structures which have significantly different resistivities than the surrounding soil.
*   **Identifying Contamination Plumes**: Mapping zones of soil or groundwater contamination, which often have lower resistivity due to dissolved salts.
*   **Mapping Geologic Structures**: Delineating fault zones, fracture patterns, or buried channels that influence ground resistivity.
*   **Detecting Water Table Fluctuations**: Identifying areas with higher or lower moisture content associated with shallow groundwater.

#### 2.3 Examples

*   **Locating a buried metal pipe**: A survey line might show a sharp, localized drop in resistivity where the pipe is present.
*   **Mapping a clay layer**: A profile across a boundary between sand and clay might show a region of lower resistivity corresponding to the clay.
*   **Identifying a contaminated site**: A zone of spilled chemicals could result in significantly lower resistivity values in that area.

#### 2.4 Limitations of Electrical Profiling

*   **Ambiguity**: A particular resistivity anomaly might be caused by various subsurface conditions (e.g., a low resistivity could be wet clay or a conductive ore body).
*   **Depth Resolution**: Limited information about vertical variations with a fixed electrode spacing.
*   **Surface Effects**: Highly resistive surface layers (e.g., dry topsoil, asphalt) can mask deeper anomalies.
*   **Electrode Contact Resistance**: Poor contact between electrodes and the ground can introduce noise and errors, especially in dry or rocky conditions.
*   **Lateral Heterogeneity**: Complex lateral variations can make interpretation difficult.

### 3. Electrical Sounding (Vertical Investigation)

#### 3.1 Procedure

Electrical sounding aims to determine the vertical distribution of resistivity by systematically increasing the spacing between the current electrodes while keeping the potential electrodes fixed relative to the current electrodes (e.g., Schlumberger array) or by increasing the spacing of all electrodes (e.g., Wenner array).

1.  **Select Electrode Array**: The Schlumberger array is commonly used for sounding due to its efficiency in expanding current electrode separation. The Wenner array can also be used.
2.  **Establish Survey Point**: Select a single location on the ground surface for the sounding.
3.  **Position Electrode Array**: Place the electrodes in the chosen configuration with the smallest current electrode spacing.
4.  **Inject Current & Measure Potential**: Inject current and measure the potential difference.
5.  **Calculate Apparent Resistivity**: Calculate $\rho_a$.
6.  **Expand Electrode Spacing**: Systematically increase the spacing of the current electrodes (e.g., AB/2 in Schlumberger). The potential electrodes are also adjusted in a specific manner depending on the array and survey methodology (e.g., for Schlumberger, MN is usually kept fixed until it becomes too small relative to AB, then it's expanded).
7.  **Repeat Steps 4-6**: Continue taking measurements with progressively larger electrode spacings. Each measurement with a larger spacing probes deeper into the subsurface.
8.  **Data Presentation**: The measured apparent resistivity values are plotted against the electrode spacing (e.g., AB/2 for Schlumberger, a for Wenner) on a log-log graph. This plot is called a **sounding curve** or **resistivity curve**.

#### 3.2 Interpretation of Sounding Curves

*   **Homogeneous Half-Space**: A horizontal line on the log-log plot.
*   **Layered Earth**: The curve will deviate from horizontal, showing features indicative of different resistivity layers.
    *   **Increasing Resistivity with Depth**: Curve rises. Suggests transition from conductive to resistive layers (e.g., clay to bedrock).
    *   **Decreasing Resistivity with Depth**: Curve falls. Suggests transition from resistive to conductive layers (e.g., gravel to saturated clay).
    *   **Alternating Layers**: Shows more complex undulations.
*   **Inversion**: Sophisticated computer software is used to interpret sounding curves by fitting them to theoretical models of layered earth. This process is called inversion and provides estimates of layer thicknesses and resistivities.

#### 3.3 Uses of Electrical Sounding

*   **Determining Depth to Bedrock**: Identifying the boundary between overburden soils and competent bedrock.
*   **Mapping Groundwater Table Depth**: Locating the saturated zone, which often exhibits lower resistivity.
*   **Characterizing Subsurface Layers**: Identifying the resistivity contrasts between different soil or rock strata.
*   **Investigating Aquifer Properties**: Estimating the presence and extent of permeable and conductive layers.
*   **Foundation Design**: Providing information on the expected stiffness and strength of subsurface materials at various depths.
*   **Geotechnical Site Characterization**: Understanding the subsurface stratigraphy and material properties for engineering purposes.

#### 3.4 Examples

*   **A sounding curve that starts low and then rises sharply**: Could indicate a shallow, conductive clay layer overlying a more resistive layer like bedrock.
*   **A sounding curve that starts high and then drops**: Might suggest a dry, resistive topsoil layer overlying a saturated, conductive sand or gravel aquifer.
*   **Detecting saline intrusion**: A gradual decrease in resistivity with depth in coastal areas could indicate saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers.

#### 3.5 Limitations of Electrical Sounding

*   **Ambiguity**: Similar to profiling, different layering models can produce similar sounding curves.
*   **Non-Uniqueness**: The inversion process can sometimes yield multiple possible solutions for layer thicknesses and resistivities.
*   **Minimum Detectable Layer Thickness**: Very thin layers may not be resolved by the sounding.
*   **Lateral Variations**: Sounding assumes a horizontally layered earth. Significant lateral variations can distort the sounding curve and lead to misinterpretations.
*   **Electrode Contact Resistance**: Can affect low-spacing measurements.
*   **Depth of Investigation**: Limited by the practical maximum spacing of current electrodes and the signal strength.

### 4. Comparison: Profiling vs. Sounding

| Feature          | Electrical Profiling                                      | Electrical Sounding                                        |
| :--------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Objective**    | Map lateral variations in resistivity.                    | Determine vertical variations in resistivity (stratigraphy). |
| **Electrode Movement** | Entire array moves laterally at fixed spacing.          | Electrode spacing increases systematically at a fixed point. |
| **Array Spacing** | Constant.                                                 | Varies (increases).                                        |
| **Data Output**  | Resistivity values plotted against horizontal position.   | Resistivity values plotted against electrode spacing (sounding curve). |
| **Primary Uses** | Mapping anomalies, locating buried objects, contamination. | Depth to bedrock, groundwater depth, stratigraphic profiling. |
| **Sensitivity**  | High to lateral changes.                                  | High to vertical changes.                                  |

### 6. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
The Electrical Resistivity method works by injecting an electric current into the ground through two current electrodes and measuring the resulting potential difference between two potential electrodes. The apparent resistivity is then calculated using these measurements and the electrode geometry. This apparent resistivity is related to the intrinsic resistivity of the subsurface materials, which in turn depends on factors like moisture content, porosity, mineralogy, and the salinity of pore water.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Electrical Profiling**: The primary objective is to map **lateral variations** in resistivity across the subsurface. The electrode array is moved laterally along a survey line at a constant electrode spacing.
*   **Electrical Sounding**: The primary objective is to determine the **vertical distribution** of resistivity, essentially mapping the subsurface stratigraphy with depth. The electrode spacing is systematically increased at a fixed point.

**Answer 3:**
The typical procedure for Electrical Sounding using the Schlumberger array involves:
1.  Placing four electrodes (A, M, N, B) in a line on the ground surface, with A and B being the outer current electrodes and M and N being the inner potential electrodes.
2.  Injecting a current through A and B and measuring the potential difference between M and N.
3.  Calculating the apparent resistivity for the initial electrode configuration.
4.  Systematically increasing the distance between the current electrodes (AB/2) while keeping the potential electrodes (MN) relatively fixed initially.
5.  Repeating the current injection, potential measurement, and resistivity calculation for each expanded electrode spacing. The potential electrode spacing may need to be increased as the current electrode spacing becomes much larger.
6.  Plotting the apparent resistivity against the half-electrode spacing (AB/2) on a log-log scale to create a sounding curve.

**Answer 4:**
Three common uses of Electrical Profiling in geotechnical investigations are:
1.  **Locating buried metallic structures** (e.g., pipelines, foundation elements).
2.  **Mapping boundaries between different soil types** (e.g., clay-sand interfaces).
3.  **Identifying zones of soil or groundwater contamination** which often have altered resistivity.

**Answer 5:**
Key limitations of the Electrical Resistivity method include:
*   **Ambiguity and Non-Uniqueness**: Different subsurface conditions or layer combinations can produce similar measured resistivity data or sounding curves.
*   **Limited Depth of Investigation**: The depth probed is restricted by practical electrode spacing and signal strength.
*   **Sensitivity to Surface Conditions**: Highly resistive surface layers can obscure deeper anomalies.
*   **Assumption of Layering**: Sounding assumes horizontal layering, which may not always be true in complex geological settings.
*   **Electrode Contact Issues**: Poor electrode contact can introduce errors, especially in dry or rocky ground.
*   **Resolution Issues**: Very thin layers or subtle variations may not be detected.

**Answer 6:**
A resistivity sounding curve that starts with low apparent resistivity and then gradually increases with increasing electrode spacing typically represents a transition from a more **conductive layer at shallow depths to a more resistive layer at greater depths**. This scenario commonly corresponds to:
*   A shallow, saturated clay or silty clay layer overlying a more resistive unsaturated soil, weathered rock, or competent bedrock.
*   A shallow zone of groundwater contamination (with dissolved salts) overlying drier, less conductive material.
