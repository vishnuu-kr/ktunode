# ADVANCED GEOTECHNICAL INVESTIGATION

## Module 1: Planning an Investigation Programme

### Topic: Exploration Techniques

---

This module focuses on the crucial first step in any geotechnical project: effectively planning the investigation program. A well-planned investigation ensures that adequate and relevant information is gathered to support design and mitigate risks. This topic, "Exploration Techniques," delves into the various methods used to understand the subsurface conditions.

---

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **LO1:** Understand the purpose and importance of exploration in geotechnical engineering.
*   **LO2:** Identify and describe the main categories of exploration techniques.
*   **LO3:** Explain the principles behind common in-situ testing methods.
*   **LO4:** Discuss the advantages and limitations of different exploration techniques.
*   **LO5:** Select appropriate exploration techniques based on project requirements and subsurface conditions.

## 1. Purpose and Importance of Exploration in Geotechnical Engineering (LO1)

Geotechnical exploration is the systematic process of investigating the subsurface to gather information about the soil and rock strata, groundwater conditions, and other relevant geological features.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Subsurface Conditions:** Refers to the characteristics of the ground beneath the surface, including soil types, rock formations, groundwater levels, and the presence of any anomalies (e.g., buried utilities, cavities).
*   **Geotechnical Parameters:** Quantifiable properties of soil and rock that are used in engineering design, such as shear strength, compressibility, permeability, and density.
*   **Risk Mitigation:** Identifying and understanding potential geotechnical hazards (e.g., liquefaction, landslides, settlement) early in the project to design appropriate mitigation measures.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** Balancing the cost of the investigation with the potential cost savings achieved through better design and reduced construction issues.

**Importance:**

*   **Informed Design:** Provides essential data for designing foundations, retaining walls, slopes, tunnels, and other structures.
*   **Risk Assessment:** Identifies potential hazards that could impact project safety, stability, and longevity.
*   **Construction Planning:** Helps in planning excavation methods, dewatering requirements, and material sourcing.
*   **Cost Control:** Prevents costly unexpected ground conditions during construction, leading to delays and budget overruns.
*   **Environmental Considerations:** Helps assess potential impacts on groundwater resources and the environment.

**Example:**

Imagine designing a high-rise building. Without proper exploration, you might not know if the soil can support the immense load, leading to excessive settlement or even structural failure. Exploration would reveal the bearing capacity of the soil, guiding the choice of foundation type (e.g., shallow vs. deep foundations).

### 2.1. Desk Study/Record Review (LO2)

This is the initial and most cost-effective phase of exploration.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Geological Maps:** Show the distribution of rock and soil types, geological structures (faults, folds), and historical geological events.
*   **Topographical Maps:** Depict surface features like elevations, slopes, and water bodies.
*   **Aerial Photographs & Satellite Imagery:** Provide visual information about surface and near-surface features, vegetation patterns, and land use.
*   **Previous Geotechnical Reports:** Data from nearby or similar projects can provide valuable insights.
*   **Groundwater Data:** Information on aquifer depths, water quality, and historical groundwater levels.

**Process:**

1.  Gather relevant maps, reports, and data.
2.  Analyze the information to understand the general geological setting, potential soil/rock types, and suspected geological hazards.
3.  Identify areas requiring more detailed investigation.

**Advantages:**

*   Low cost and time-efficient.
*   Provides a broad understanding of the site.
*   Helps in planning the subsequent fieldwork.

**Limitations:**

*   May not be site-specific or sufficiently detailed.
*   Accuracy depends on the quality of available records.

**Example:**

Before visiting a site for a proposed bridge, reviewing geological maps might reveal that the area is underlain by expansive clays, indicating a potential risk of differential settlement.

### 2.2. Surface Exploration (LO2)

These techniques are used to observe and assess surface and near-surface conditions.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Site Reconnaissance:** Visual inspection of the site to observe topography, drainage, existing structures, vegetation, and any visible signs of instability (e.g., cracks, scarp).
*   **Geological Mapping:** Detailed mapping of surface geological features.

**Advantages:**

*   Relatively quick and inexpensive.
*   Can identify obvious surface-related problems.

**Limitations:**

*   Limited to visible conditions, cannot reveal subsurface details.

**Example:**

Observing erosion gullies on a sloping site during site reconnaissance might indicate potential slope instability issues.

### 2.3. Subsurface Exploration (LO2)

This is the core of geotechnical exploration, involving direct or indirect investigation of the ground below the surface.

#### 2.3.1. Sampling Methods (LO2)

These methods involve obtaining physical samples of soil and rock for laboratory testing.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Boreholes:** Vertical or inclined holes drilled into the ground.
*   **Sampling:** The process of collecting soil or rock specimens from boreholes.
*   **Disturbed Samples:** Samples that have been significantly altered from their natural state (e.g., during collection, transport). Useful for soil classification and index properties.
*   **Undisturbed Samples:** Samples that retain their in-situ density, moisture content, and structure as much as possible. Crucial for strength and compressibility tests.

**Common Sampling Techniques:**

*   **Open Excavations (Trenches, Pits):**
    *   **Description:** Shallow excavations allowing direct visual inspection and sampling of near-surface materials.
    *   **Advantages:** Simple, allows for extensive visual observation.
    *   **Limitations:** Limited to shallow depths, can be costly for deeper excavations.
    *   **Example:** Excavating a trial pit to examine the suitability of shallow foundation bearing strata.

*   **Boreholes with Soil Sampling:**
    *   **Auger Sampling:**
        *   **Description:** Uses augers (screw-like tools) to advance a borehole and collect disturbed samples at regular intervals.
        *   **Advantages:** Relatively simple and fast for cohesive soils.
        *   **Limitations:** Significant disturbance to the soil structure, not suitable for very dense sands or gravels.
        *   **Example:** Hand augers for shallow soil investigation.

    *   **Shell and Auger Boring:**
        *   **Description:** Uses a shell (a cylindrical tool with a flap valve) to scoop up soil as the borehole is advanced by pounding and rotating. Water is often used as a drilling fluid.
        *   **Advantages:** Can be used in most soil types, including gravels and soft rock.
        *   **Limitations:** Causes significant disturbance to the soil.
        *   **Example:** Traditional method for advancing boreholes in many countries.

    *   **Rotary Drilling (Core Drilling):**
        *   **Description:** Uses a rotating cutting bit to advance the borehole. Core barrels are used to recover continuous cylindrical rock samples (cores) or soil samples.
        *   **Advantages:** Can penetrate hard soils and rock effectively, provides continuous samples, can be used for in-situ testing.
        *   **Limitations:** Can be expensive, samples can still experience some disturbance.
        *   **Example:** Diamond drilling for obtaining rock cores to determine rock strength and fracture patterns.

    *   **Wash Boring:**
        *   **Description:** Uses a rotating cutting bit and a stream of water or drilling mud to flush out cuttings. Samples are collected from the retrieved cuttings or by using samplers.
        *   **Advantages:** Effective in a variety of soils.
        *   **Limitations:** Significant disturbance to the soil, difficult to recover intact samples.

    *   **Split Spoon Sampling (SPT - Standard Penetration Test):**
        *   **Description:** A split barrel sampler is driven into the soil at the bottom of a borehole using a standard hammer (63.5 kg) falling from a standard height (760 mm). The number of blows required to drive the sampler a specific distance (e.g., 300 mm after an initial seating) is recorded (N-value).
        *   **Advantages:** Widely used, provides a measure of soil density and strength, relatively inexpensive.
        *   **Limitations:** Highly disturbed sample, N-value is empirical and site-dependent, not suitable for very soft or very hard materials.
        *   **Example:** The most common method for estimating the bearing capacity of sands.

    *   **Thin-Walled Tube Sampling (Shelby Tube):**
        *   **Description:** A thin-walled steel tube is pushed into the soil with minimal disturbance, used to obtain relatively undisturbed samples of soft to firm cohesive soils.
        *   **Advantages:** Provides less disturbed samples compared to SPT, good for laboratory testing of clays.
        *   **Limitations:** Can be difficult in dense soils or sands, expensive.
        *   **Example:** Collecting samples for consolidation and shear strength tests in clay deposits.

    *   **Piston Samplers:**
        *   **Description:** A piston moves inside a sampling tube to create suction, reducing friction and allowing for better recovery of soft to very soft undisturbed cohesive soils.
        *   **Advantages:** Excellent for very soft clays, minimal sample disturbance.
        *   **Limitations:** Specialized equipment, can be slow.
        *   **Example:** Sampling highly sensitive marine clays.

*   **Rock Sampling:**
    *   **Rock Coring:** As described under Rotary Drilling, this is the primary method for obtaining intact rock samples.
    *   **Rock Mass Description:** Detailed logging of rock cores, including rock type, weathering, fracture frequency, joint conditions, and rock quality designation (RQD).

#### 2.3.2. In-situ Testing Methods (LO3)

These methods test the soil or rock properties directly in the ground, minimizing sample disturbance.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **In-situ:** In its original place or position.
*   **Direct Measurement:** Properties are measured without removing the material.
*   **Empirical Correlations:** Results are often correlated with laboratory-determined parameters or engineering behavior.

**Common In-situ Testing Methods:**

*   **Standard Penetration Test (SPT):** (Already covered under sampling, but also an in-situ test)
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the relative density of sands and the consistency of clays. Provides an empirical measure of soil strength.

*   **Cone Penetration Test (CPT):**
    *   **Description:** A cone-shaped probe is pushed into the ground at a constant rate. Resistance to penetration is measured electronically. A standard CPT measures tip resistance ($q_c$) and sleeve friction ($f_s$). Advanced CPTs (CPTu) also measure pore water pressure ($u$).
    *   **Advantages:** Continuous data, fast, provides detailed stratification, can be used to infer soil type and strength.
    *   **Limitations:** No physical sample retrieved for visual inspection or direct lab testing, difficult in gravelly soils or rock.
    *   **Example:** Identifying different soil layers and estimating bearing capacity and settlement for foundation design in fine-grained soils.

*   **Pressuremeter Test (PMT):**
    *   **Description:** A cylindrical probe is lowered into a borehole and expanded radially, applying pressure to the borehole walls. The resulting expansion of the probe is measured, providing data on the stress-strain behavior of the soil or rock.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides in-situ stress-strain characteristics, useful for both cohesive and granular soils, and soft rocks.
    *   **Limitations:** Requires a pre-drilled borehole, can be expensive.
    *   **Example:** Determining the stiffness of soils for settlement analysis of structures sensitive to deformation.

*   **Vane Shear Test (VST):**
    *   **Description:** A four-bladed vane is pushed into the soil (usually soft clays) and then rotated. The torque required to shear the soil is measured, which is then related to the undrained shear strength ($c_u$) of the soil.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides relatively undisturbed measurement of undrained shear strength in soft to medium clays, can be performed in boreholes or directly pushed into the ground.
    *   **Limitations:** Not suitable for stiff or sensitive clays, granular soils, or soils with stones.
    *   **Example:** Measuring the shear strength of soft marine clays for embankment stability analysis.

*   **Plate Load Test (PLT):**
    *   **Description:** A rigid plate is placed on the ground surface or at the base of an excavation and loaded incrementally. The settlement is measured, providing information on the bearing capacity and compressibility of the soil.
    *   **Advantages:** Directly measures bearing capacity and settlement for a specific load and foundation size.
    *   **Limitations:** Results are scale-dependent (plate size), only tests a limited zone of influence, expensive and time-consuming.
    *   **Example:** Evaluating the bearing capacity of a shallow foundation at the base of an excavation.

*   **Geophysical Methods (See below):** While often considered separate, some geophysical techniques provide in-situ information about subsurface layers and properties.

#### 2.3.3. Geophysical Methods (LO2, LO3)

These methods use physical principles to infer subsurface conditions without direct contact or significant disturbance.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Seismic Methods:** Measure the propagation of seismic waves through the ground. Wave velocity is related to material properties (e.g., stiffness).
*   **Electrical Resistivity Methods:** Measure the electrical resistance of the ground, which varies with soil type, moisture content, and salinity.
*   **Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR):** Uses electromagnetic waves to detect buried objects, changes in soil layers, and shallow subsurface features.

**Common Geophysical Techniques:**

*   **Seismic Refraction/Reflection:**
    *   **Description:** A seismic source (e.g., hammer blow, explosion) generates waves that travel through the ground and are detected by geophones. The travel times are used to determine seismic velocities and identify layer boundaries.
    *   **Advantages:** Can map bedrock depth, identify major geological structures, and assess soil stiffness.
    *   **Limitations:** Requires suitable ground conditions, interpretation can be complex, resolution decreases with depth.
    *   **Example:** Determining the depth to bedrock for foundation design in a large infrastructure project.

*   **Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT):**
    *   **Description:** Electrodes are placed on the ground surface, and electrical current is injected and measured. Resistivity values are computed and used to create 2D or 3D resistivity models of the subsurface.
    *   **Advantages:** Can identify variations in soil moisture, salinity, and lithology. Useful for detecting groundwater contamination or buried structures.
    *   **Limitations:** Sensitive to surface features and buried metal objects, interpretation can be challenging.
    *   **Example:** Mapping the extent of a saline intrusion into a freshwater aquifer.

*   **Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR):**
    *   **Description:** A radar pulse is transmitted into the ground, reflecting off subsurface interfaces. The reflected signals are recorded to create an image of the subsurface.
    *   **Advantages:** High resolution, non-invasive, good for detecting shallow features like utilities, voids, and shallow stratigraphy.
    *   **Limitations:** Depth penetration is limited (especially in conductive soils), resolution decreases with depth.
    *   **Example:** Locating buried utilities before excavation or identifying shallow anomalies on a construction site.

## 4. Selecting Appropriate Exploration Techniques (LO5)

The selection of exploration techniques depends on several factors:

*   **Project Type and Scope:**
    *   High-rise buildings vs. low-rise structures.
    *   Embankments vs. bridges vs. tunnels.
    *   Industrial facilities vs. residential developments.
*   **Geological Setting and Site Conditions:**
    *   Soil types (clays, sands, gravels, rock).
    *   Likelihood of problematic soils (e.g., expansive clays, collapsible soils, organic soils).
    *   Groundwater conditions (depth, permeability).
    *   Topography.
    *   Presence of existing structures or underground utilities.
*   **Information Required for Design:**
    *   Bearing capacity.
    *   Settlement characteristics.
    *   Shear strength parameters (drained and undrained).
    *   Permeability.
    *   Liquefaction potential.
    *   Slope stability.
    *   Rock mass properties.
*   **Cost and Time Constraints:**
    *   Budget for the investigation.
    *   Project schedule.
*   **Availability of Equipment and Expertise:**
    *   Access to specialized equipment (e.g., CPT rig, pressuremeter).
    *   Availability of trained personnel for specific tests.

**General Approach:**

1.  **Start with Desk Study & Site Reconnaissance:** To gain a preliminary understanding of the site.
2.  **Initial Subsurface Investigation (Low Cost):** Typically involves boreholes with SPT. This provides basic soil stratification and strength information.
3.  **Targeted Advanced Testing (If Required):**
    *   If detailed settlement analysis is critical, CPT or PMT might be used.
    *   For soft clays, VST is essential.
    *   For rock projects, rotary core drilling is paramount.
    *   If shallow anomalies are suspected, GPR might be employed.
    *   If large areas need to be mapped for bedrock or geological structure, seismic methods can be beneficial.
4.  **Consider a Phased Approach:** Start with less expensive methods and then progressively use more sophisticated or numerous tests based on the initial findings and the increasing certainty required for design.

**Example of a Phased Approach for a High-Rise Building:**

*   **Phase 1 (Preliminary):** Desk study, site reconnaissance, a few widely spaced boreholes with SPT and sampling for classification tests.
*   **Phase 2 (Detailed):** Based on Phase 1 results, more boreholes might be added, and specific tests like CPT or undisturbed sampling for consolidation and triaxial tests might be performed in critical areas or layers. If rock is encountered, rock coring and RQD measurements would be crucial.

## 5. Practice Questions and Exercises (LO1-LO5)

**Question 1 (LO1, LO2):**
Why is a thorough geotechnical exploration program essential for the successful design and construction of any civil engineering project? Name at least three categories of exploration techniques.

**Question 2 (LO3):**
Describe the Standard Penetration Test (SPT) and explain what the "N-value" represents. What are its main advantages and limitations?

**Question 3 (LO3, LO4):**
Compare and contrast the Cone Penetration Test (CPT) with the Vane Shear Test (VST). In what type of soil deposit would each of these tests be most appropriate?

**Question 4 (LO2, LO5):**
You are planning a geotechnical investigation for a new railway line crossing a broad alluvial plain with potentially soft clay layers. Briefly outline an appropriate exploration strategy, specifying at least three different techniques and the rationale for their selection.

**Question 5 (LO4):**
For each of the following scenarios, recommend the most appropriate exploration technique(s) and justify your choice:
    a) Locating buried historical artifacts beneath a proposed building foundation.
    b) Determining the depth to competent rock for a major bridge abutment.
    c) Assessing the potential for liquefaction in a coastal area for a new port facility.

## Answers to Practice Questions:

**Answer 1:**
A thorough geotechnical exploration program is essential to understand the subsurface conditions, identify potential risks (e.g., poor bearing capacity, settlement, landslides), and gather the necessary data for safe and economical design. Without it, projects can face significant cost overruns, delays, and even catastrophic failures due to unforeseen ground conditions.

Categories of exploration techniques include:
*   Desk Study/Record Review
*   Surface Exploration
*   Subsurface Exploration (Sampling, In-situ Testing, Geophysical Methods)

**Answer 2:**
The Standard Penetration Test (SPT) involves driving a split-spoon sampler into the soil at the bottom of a borehole using a standard hammer and fall height. The "N-value" is the number of blows required to drive the sampler a specific distance (typically 300 mm after initial seating). It represents an empirical measure of the soil's density and resistance to penetration, correlating with its strength and stiffness.

*   **Advantages:** Widely used, relatively inexpensive, provides a disturbed sample for visual classification and basic lab tests, provides an index of soil strength.
*   **Limitations:** Disturbs the soil sample significantly, the N-value is empirical and highly dependent on drilling procedures and equipment, not suitable for very soft or very hard materials.

**Answer 3:**
*   **CPT:** A cone-shaped probe is pushed into the ground, measuring tip resistance and sleeve friction (and pore pressure in CPTu). It provides continuous data, is fast, and good for stratigraphy in fine-grained soils. However, it yields no physical sample and is less effective in gravelly soils.
*   **VST:** A four-bladed vane is inserted into soft clays and rotated to measure the torque required to shear the soil. It directly measures undrained shear strength ($c_u$) in-situ and is ideal for soft to medium clays. It is not suitable for stiff clays or granular soils.

**Answer 4:**
An appropriate exploration strategy for a railway line across alluvial plains with soft clays could involve:

1.  **Desk Study & Site Reconnaissance:** To gather existing geological data, topographical information, and visually assess the site.
2.  **Geophysical Survey (e.g., Seismic Refraction):** To quickly map variations in the depth of competent strata or bedrock across the alignment and identify major geological anomalies.
3.  **Boreholes with SPT:** To obtain basic soil stratigraphy and strength information at regular intervals along the proposed alignment.
4.  **Undisturbed Sampling (e.g., Shelby Tubes) and Vane Shear Tests (VST):** Specifically within the identified soft clay layers to obtain undisturbed samples for laboratory consolidation and shear strength tests, and to directly measure undrained shear strength in-situ, crucial for embankment settlement and stability analysis.
5.  **CPT:** Could be used as an alternative or supplementary method to boreholes for detailed soil profiling and to estimate engineering properties, especially in finer-grained soil layers.

**Answer 5:**
    a) **Locating historical artifacts:** Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR) would be the most appropriate technique due to its high resolution and non-invasive nature, allowing for detection of shallow subsurface anomalies without significant disturbance.
    b) **Depth to competent rock for a bridge abutment:** Seismic Refraction surveys are effective for mapping bedrock depth over larger areas, followed by rotary core drilling at the specific abutment locations to obtain rock samples for detailed testing and confirmation of rock quality.
    c) **Liquefaction potential in a coastal area:** Standard Penetration Tests (SPT) are widely used to determine the N-values, which are then correlated with liquefaction susceptibility. Cone Penetration Tests (CPT) with pore pressure measurements (CPTu) are also highly effective for assessing liquefaction potential, offering more detailed and continuous data.

## Important Points to Remember:

*   **Exploration is iterative:** The findings from one stage of exploration inform the next.
*   **No single technique is perfect:** A combination of methods is often required for a comprehensive understanding.
*   **Cost vs. Benefit:** Invest enough to mitigate risks, but avoid unnecessary expenditure.
*   **Site-specific approach:** Always tailor the exploration program to the unique conditions of the site and project requirements.
*   **Professional judgment is crucial:** Interpreting the data and selecting techniques requires experienced geotechnical engineers.
*   **Safety:** Always prioritize safety during all exploration activities.
