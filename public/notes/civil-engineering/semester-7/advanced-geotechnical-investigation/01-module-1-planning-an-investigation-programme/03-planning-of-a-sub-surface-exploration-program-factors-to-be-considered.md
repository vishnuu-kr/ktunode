# ADVANCED GEOTECHNICAL INVESTIGATION: Module 1 - Planning an Investigation Programme

## Topic: Planning of a Sub-Surface Exploration Program - Factors to Be Considered

---

### **1. Introduction: The Importance of Sub-Surface Exploration**

*   **Definition:** Sub-surface exploration is the systematic process of investigating the physical and engineering properties of soil and rock formations beneath the ground surface.
*   **Purpose:** To obtain data necessary for the safe, economical, and reliable design and construction of civil engineering projects. This includes understanding the stratigraphy, material properties, groundwater conditions, and potential geological hazards.
*   **Why is it crucial?**
    *   **Foundation Design:** Determines the type, depth, and capacity of foundations (shallow or deep).
    *   **Slope Stability:** Assesses the risk of landslides and informs the design of retaining structures.
    *   **Earthworks:** Provides information for excavation, fill placement, and compaction requirements.
    *   **Underground Structures:** Crucial for tunnels, basements, and underground utilities.
    *   **Risk Mitigation:** Identifies potential problems (e.g., weak soils, groundwater ingress, seismic liquefaction) early on, preventing costly failures and safety issues.
    *   **Economic Viability:** Optimizes material usage and construction methods, leading to cost savings.

### **3. Factors to Be Considered in Planning a Sub-Surface Exploration Program**

The planning of a sub-surface exploration program is a complex process that requires careful consideration of numerous interconnected factors. These can be broadly categorized as follows:

#### **3.1. Project Requirements and Objectives**

*   **Type of Project:** The nature of the structure dictates the scale and type of exploration needed.
    *   **Example:** A low-rise residential building requires less extensive exploration than a high-rise skyscraper, a major bridge, or a large dam.
*   **Type of Foundation:**
    *   **Shallow Foundations (e.g., spread footings, mat foundations):** Exploration focuses on the strength and compressibility of near-surface soils.
    *   **Deep Foundations (e.g., piles, piers):** Exploration needs to reach significant depths to identify competent bearing strata and assess skin friction.
*   **Magnitude of Loads:** Heavier loads require deeper and more thorough investigations to ensure adequate bearing capacity and minimize settlement.
*   **Sensitivity of the Structure to Settlement:** Structures like power plants or precision industrial facilities require very strict settlement criteria, necessitating more detailed settlement analysis and exploration.
*   **Construction Methods:** The planned construction techniques can influence exploration needs. For example, tunneling requires detailed information on rock quality, groundwater, and potential for collapses.
*   **Seismic Considerations:** In earthquake-prone areas, exploration must assess liquefaction potential, soil amplification, and the presence of active faults.
*   **Groundwater Conditions:** Presence, depth, and fluctuation of groundwater significantly impact foundation design, excavation stability, and dewatering requirements.
*   **Environmental Considerations:** Potential for contamination, presence of sensitive ecosystems, and disposal of excavated materials might influence the choice of exploration methods.
*   **Time Constraints:** Project timelines can limit the duration and extent of exploration, requiring efficient planning and execution.

#### **3.2. Site Conditions**

*   **Topography:** Steep slopes may require specialized equipment for drilling or sampling, and pose stability risks. Flat sites are generally easier to access.
*   **Accessibility:** The ease of access for drilling rigs, sampling equipment, and personnel is crucial. Remote or difficult-to-access sites may require alternative or adapted methods.
    *   **Example:** A densely urbanized area might restrict the size of drilling equipment, necessitating smaller, more maneuverable rigs or even manual methods for certain investigations.
*   **Existing Structures:** Proximity to existing buildings or infrastructure requires careful consideration to avoid damage during exploration activities.
*   **Vegetation and Obstructions:** Dense vegetation or buried utilities can impede exploration and require pre-investigation clearing or careful navigation.
*   **Geological Setting:**
    *   **Soil Types:** Different soil types (clays, sands, silts, gravels, organic soils) have varying properties and require specific testing methods.
    *   **Rock Types and Structure:** The type of rock, its strength, presence of fractures, joints, bedding planes, and weathering can significantly affect excavation and foundation design.
    *   **Stratigraphy:** The layering and thickness of different soil and rock strata are fundamental to understanding the subsurface profile.
    *   **Groundwater Table:** Depth, pressure, and potential fluctuations of the groundwater table.
    *   **Potential Geological Hazards:** Presence of landslides, sinkholes, seismic faults, or expansive soils.

#### **3.3. Available Information and Reconnaissance**

*   **Desk Study:** Review of existing geological maps, soil surveys, historical aerial photographs, previous investigation reports for nearby projects, and local building codes.
    *   **Purpose:** To gain an initial understanding of the site, identify potential geological issues, and refine the exploration plan.
*   **Site Visit (Reconnaissance):** Direct observation of the site to assess topography, vegetation, drainage patterns, evidence of past landslides, existing structures, and general accessibility.
    *   **Purpose:** To confirm or contradict information from the desk study and identify any unexpected surface features.

#### **3.4. Exploration Methods and Techniques**

The selection of appropriate exploration methods is directly influenced by the project requirements and site conditions. Key methods include:

*   **Borings (Rotary Drilling, Auger Drilling):**
    *   **Purpose:** To obtain soil/rock samples, determine depth to different strata, and install groundwater monitoring wells.
    *   **Considerations:** Rig size, drilling fluid (mud vs. air), cost, speed, disturbance to samples.
*   **In-situ Testing:**
    *   **Standard Penetration Test (SPT):** Measures the resistance of soil to penetration by a standard sampler. Provides a qualitative indication of soil density and consistency.
    *   **Cone Penetration Test (CPT):** Pushes a cone-shaped probe into the ground, measuring tip resistance and sleeve friction. Provides continuous data and can be correlated with soil properties.
    *   **Vane Shear Test (VST):** Measures the undrained shear strength of soft cohesive soils in-situ.
    *   **Pressuremeter Test:** Measures the in-situ stress-strain behavior of soil or rock.
    *   **Dilatometer Test (DMT):** Measures soil stiffness and pore water pressure.
    *   **Geophysical Methods (e.g., Seismic Refraction, Electrical Resistivity, Ground Penetrating Radar - GPR):**
        *   **Purpose:** To delineate subsurface stratigraphy, locate bedrock, identify groundwater table, and detect anomalies non-destructively.
        *   **Considerations:** Resolution, depth penetration, soil type suitability, cost.
*   **Laboratory Testing:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the physical and engineering properties of collected samples (e.g., Atterberg limits, grain size distribution, shear strength, compressibility, permeability).
    *   **Considerations:** Sample disturbance, representativeness of samples, cost, time.
*   **Groundwater Monitoring:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine groundwater levels, pressures, and quality.
    *   **Methods:** Installation of piezometers, observation wells.

#### **3.5. Cost and Schedule Constraints**

*   **Budget Allocation:** Exploration programs are subject to budget limitations. The plan must be cost-effective while still meeting the project's information needs.
*   **Time Availability:** Project deadlines dictate the pace of exploration. Phased investigations can be employed to manage time constraints.
*   **Resource Availability:** Availability of specialized equipment, skilled personnel, and laboratories.

#### **3.6. Personnel Expertise**

*   **Geotechnical Engineers:** Responsible for planning, interpreting data, and making recommendations.
*   **Geologists:** Provide expertise on geological formations and processes.
*   **Drillers and Technicians:** Execute the field exploration activities.
*   **Laboratory Technicians:** Conduct laboratory tests.
*   **Quality Control/Quality Assurance (QC/QA):** Essential to ensure the reliability of the collected data.

### **4. The Exploration Program Planning Process**

A structured approach is vital for developing an effective exploration program:

1.  **Define Project Requirements and Objectives:** Clearly state what information is needed and why.
2.  **Gather Preliminary Information:** Conduct desk studies and site reconnaissance.
3.  **Identify Potential Sub-surface Conditions and Risks:** Based on preliminary information, hypothesize the likely subsurface profile and potential issues.
4.  **Select Appropriate Exploration Methods:** Choose methods that can effectively gather the required data for the anticipated conditions.
5.  **Determine the Number, Location, and Depth of Exploratory Points:** This is a critical step, guided by building codes, design requirements, and the variability of the site.
    *   **Spacing:** Closer spacing is needed in areas with high variability or critical structures.
    *   **Depth:** Exploration must extend to a depth that significantly influences the proposed structure's behavior. For foundations, this often means investigating a depth of 1.5 to 2 times the foundation width, or until a firm stratum is encountered.
6.  **Develop a Detailed Exploration Plan:** Document the proposed methods, locations, depths, sampling requirements, in-situ testing, and laboratory testing.
7.  **Estimate Costs and Schedule:** Develop a budget and timeline for the exploration program.
8.  **Obtain Necessary Permits and Approvals:** Ensure compliance with local regulations.
9.  **Execute the Exploration Program:** Carry out field and laboratory investigations according to the plan.
10. **Analyze and Interpret Data:** Process and interpret the collected information.
11. **Prepare a Geotechnical Report:** Document findings, analyses, and recommendations for design.

### **5. Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Stratigraphy:** The study of layered rock or soil deposits, their arrangement, and their historical development.
*   **Bearing Capacity:** The ability of a soil mass to support the loads transmitted by a foundation without excessive settlement or shear failure.
*   **Settlement:** The downward movement of a structure due to the compression of underlying soils.
*   **Liquefaction:** The phenomenon where saturated granular soils lose their strength and stiffness due to seismic shaking or rapid loading.
*   **Undrained Shear Strength:** The shear strength of a soil in a saturated condition where no pore water can escape during shear.
*   **Disturbed vs. Undisturbed Samples:**
    *   **Disturbed Samples:** Samples whose natural structure and stress history have been altered during sampling (e.g., from augers, SPT split-spoon sampler). Useful for classification tests.
    *   **Undisturbed Samples:** Samples that retain their in-situ structure and stress history as much as possible (e.g., from thin-walled samplers, block samples). Essential for strength and compressibility tests.
*   **In-situ:** Within the natural ground.
*   **Desk Study:** A review of all available existing information on a site.
*   **Reconnaissance:** A preliminary survey of a site.

---

### **6. Important Points to Remember**

*   **No single exploration method is universally applicable.** The choice depends on the project, site, and budget.
*   **The goal is to gather sufficient, reliable data.** This often involves a combination of methods.
*   **Phased investigations are common.** Initial reconnaissance and limited exploration can inform more detailed investigations if needed.
*   **Cost-effectiveness is important, but it should not compromise safety or design adequacy.**
*   **Clear communication between the design engineer and the geotechnical investigator is essential.**
*   **A thorough understanding of the project's performance criteria is paramount.**

### **7. Practice Questions**

**Question 1:**
You are planning a geotechnical investigation for a new commercial building. The site is relatively flat but has a history of shallow landslides in the surrounding area. The proposed building will have a basement. List five key factors you would prioritize when planning the sub-surface exploration program, and briefly explain why each is important in this specific scenario.

**Question 2:**
What is the primary difference between disturbed and undisturbed soil samples, and in which types of laboratory tests would each be most appropriately used?

**Question 3:**
A project requires the construction of a multi-story building on a site known to have a high groundwater table. Explain how the groundwater conditions would influence your planning of the sub-surface exploration program. What specific types of tests or observations might be crucial?

**Question 4:**
You have access to geological maps of the area, but the site itself is densely wooded and has no readily available information from previous investigations. Describe the initial steps you would take in planning the exploration program, emphasizing the role of desk study and reconnaissance.

---

### **8. Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**

1.  **Project Type and Foundation Requirements:** The building's commercial nature and basement construction mean we need to understand soil strength and compressibility for both shallow foundations (if applicable) and the basement walls/floor, and potential bearing capacity for deeper foundations.
2.  **Groundwater Conditions:** The high groundwater table will impact excavation stability, potential for buoyancy on the basement, and the selection of drilling methods (e.g., need for casing, dewatering considerations).
3.  **Geological Setting and Historical Landslides:** The history of landslides in the area necessitates thorough investigation of slope stability, soil layering, and the presence of weak or collapsible strata that might affect the building's foundation and the stability of any excavated slopes for the basement.
4.  **Accessibility and Site Conditions:** The dense woods will require clearing and careful planning for rig access, potentially influencing the location and number of boreholes.
5.  **Cost and Schedule:** Balancing the need for thoroughness (due to potential risks) with budget and time constraints will be critical in selecting the most efficient methods.

**Answer 2:**

*   **Disturbed Samples:** These samples have undergone significant changes to their natural structure, void ratio, and stress history during sampling.
    *   **Appropriate Tests:** Soil classification tests (e.g., sieve analysis, Atterberg limits), moisture content determination, compaction tests.
*   **Undisturbed Samples:** These samples aim to preserve the in-situ structure, density, and stress state of the soil.
    *   **Appropriate Tests:** Shear strength tests (e.g., triaxial compression, direct shear), consolidation tests (for settlement analysis), permeability tests.

**Answer 3:**

A high groundwater table necessitates:

*   **Determining the groundwater level accurately:** Installation of piezometers or observation wells during the initial phases of exploration is crucial to monitor fluctuations.
*   **Assessing groundwater pressure:** Measuring pore water pressures is vital for settlement analysis and stability assessments.
*   **Considering drilling methods:** Drilling through saturated soils requires techniques that prevent borehole collapse, such as using drilling mud, casing, or specialized drilling methods.
*   **Evaluating dewatering needs:** Understanding the groundwater inflow rates will be essential for basement construction.
*   **Assessing buoyancy effects:** The upward pressure from groundwater on the basement slab needs to be considered in the design.
*   **Groundwater contamination:** If contamination is suspected, groundwater sampling might be required.

**Answer 4:**

1.  **Desk Study:**
    *   Obtain and review geological maps, soil surveys, and any available topographic maps of the area.
    *   Search for previous geotechnical reports for nearby construction projects.
    *   Research local building codes and regulations related to site investigations.
    *   Look for any historical aerial photographs that might reveal past land use or surface features.
2.  **Site Reconnaissance (Walkover Survey):**
    *   Conduct a thorough walkover of the site, noting the topography, drainage patterns, and existing vegetation.
    *   Look for any signs of surface distress, such as cracking, settlement, or evidence of past erosion or landslides.
    *   Identify potential access routes for drilling equipment, considering any obstacles.
    *   Note the presence of any existing structures or utilities on or near the site.
    *   Take photographs to document existing site conditions.

This initial phase will help in formulating hypotheses about the subsurface conditions and planning the subsequent field exploration program more effectively.
