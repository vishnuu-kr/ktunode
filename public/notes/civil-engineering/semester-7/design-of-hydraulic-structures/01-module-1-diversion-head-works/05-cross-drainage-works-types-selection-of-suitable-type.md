# Design of Hydraulic Structures: Module 1 - Diversion Head Works

## Topic: Cross Drainage Works - Types and Selection of Suitable Type

---

### Learning Outcomes:

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Understand the necessity and purpose of cross-drainage works in canal systems.
*   Identify and describe the different types of cross-drainage works.
*   Explain the principles governing the selection of a suitable type of cross-drainage work.
*   Analyze factors influencing the choice of a specific cross-drainage structure.

---

### 1. Introduction to Cross Drainage Works

#### 1.1 Purpose and Necessity

*   **Definition:** Cross drainage works are structures built to provide a passage for one watercourse (like a natural stream, river, or another canal) to pass either below or above another watercourse, without interfering with the flow of either.
*   **Context:** In irrigation projects, canals are often planned to irrigate areas that are intersected by natural drainage channels (rivers, streams) or existing artificial channels (other canals, roads).
*   **Problem:** If a canal and a drainage channel intersect, one must be made to cross over or under the other. Simply allowing them to mix would lead to:
    *   **Undesirable mixing of waters:** Irrigation water may be contaminated by drainage water, affecting its suitability for crops.
    *   **Erosion and Sedimentation:** The mixing of flows at different velocities can cause erosion in the canal or sedimentation, obstructing the flow.
    *   **Inundation:** The drainage water could flood the canal, leading to its damage and disruption of irrigation supply.
*   **Solution:** Cross drainage works are essential hydraulic structures designed to manage these intersections efficiently and safely.

#### 1.2 Basic Principles of Design

The fundamental principle behind any cross-drainage work is to ensure:

*   **Uninterrupted flow:** Both the canal and the drainage channel must maintain their intended flow regimes without significant obstruction or loss of head.
*   **No mixing of waters:** The waters of the two channels should not mix.
*   **Structural stability:** The structure must be robust enough to withstand the hydraulic forces, soil pressures, and loads from traffic (if applicable).
*   **Economy:** The selected type should be cost-effective for the given site conditions.
*   **Maintenance:** The structure should be easy to maintain.

### 2. Types of Cross Drainage Works

Cross drainage works are primarily classified based on the relative levels of the canal and the drainage channel, and how the crossing is achieved.

#### 2.1 When Drainage Channel is **Above** the Canal

In this scenario, the drainage water flows *over* the canal. This typically happens when the drainage channel is at a higher elevation.

*   **Superpassage (or Aqueduct):**
    *   **Description:** The canal water is carried across the drainage channel in a trough or aqueduct supported by piers or abutments. The drainage channel flows *underneath* the canal.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:**
        ```
            |========= Canal Trough =========|
            ------------------------------------ (Canal Bed Level)
                           | Drainage Channel Flow |
        ```
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   The canal water flows in a watertight conduit.
        *   The drainage water passes freely below.
        *   This type is suitable when the canal bed level is *lower* than the natural bed level of the drainage channel.
    *   **When to use:** High flood level of drainage channel is below the crest of the aqueduct.

*   **Level Crossing:**
    *   **Description:** The canal and the drainage channel intersect at the same level, and the waters are allowed to mix. Gates or regulators are provided on both channels to regulate the flow and prevent excessive mixing or backflow.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:**
        ```
            |========= Canal Flow =========|
            ------------------------------------ (Canal Bed Level)
            ------------------------------------ (Drainage Channel Bed Level)
            |====== Drainage Channel Flow ======|
        ```
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   Both waters mix.
        *   Requires careful operation of regulators.
        *   Generally less preferred due to water mixing and potential for sediment deposition.
    *   **When to use:** When the discharge in the drainage channel is small and intermittent, and the mixing of water is not a major concern. Also, if the flow in the canal is not critical and can be shut off for maintenance.

*   **Inlet and Outlet (or Canal Fall with Inlet):**
    *   **Description:** A portion of the drainage water is allowed to enter the canal through a regulator (inlet) and is carried along with the canal water for a short distance, then discharged back into the drainage channel through another regulator (outlet) at a lower level. This is effectively a controlled mixing with a fall for the canal.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:**
        ```
            |========= Canal Flow =========|
            ------------------------------------ (Canal Bed Level)
            |   Inlet Regulator ---------> |
            |   (Drainage Water Enters)    |
            |----------------------------->|
            |========= Canal Flow =========| (with Drainage Water)
            |   Outlet Regulator --------> |
            |   (Drainage Water Exits)     |
            ------------------------------------ (Lower Canal Bed Level)
            |====== Drainage Channel Flow ======|
        ```
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   Controlled mixing of drainage water into the canal.
        *   Requires a drop in the canal level (fall).
        *   Used when drainage water has to be passed and some mixing is permissible, or when it is advantageous to utilize the drainage water.

#### 2.2 When Canal is **Above** the Drainage Channel

In this scenario, the canal water flows *over* the drainage channel. This typically happens when the canal is at a higher elevation.

*   **Aqueduct:**
    *   **Description:** The canal water is carried across the drainage channel in a trough or aqueduct supported by piers or abutments. The canal flows *over* the drainage channel.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:**
        ```
            |========= Canal Trough =========|
            ------------------------------------ (Canal Bed Level)
            |   Drainage Channel Flow Below  |
        ```
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   The canal water flows in a watertight conduit.
        *   The drainage channel passes freely below.
        *   This type is suitable when the canal bed level is *higher* than the natural bed level of the drainage channel.
    *   **When to use:** High flood level of drainage channel is below the crest of the aqueduct.

*   **Syphon Aqueduct:**
    *   **Description:** Similar to an aqueduct, but the canal water is carried across the drainage channel in a watertight conduit (trough) which dips down and passes *under* the drainage channel like a syphon. This is used when the drainage channel is at a higher elevation than the canal bed, and it is not feasible to construct a regular aqueduct due to the depth required for the drainage channel.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:**
        ```
            |========= Canal Flow =========| (Higher Level)
            ------------------------------------ (Canal Bed Level)
            |       /----------\           |
            |      /            \          | (Trough dips below drainage)
            |     /--------------\         |
            ------------------------------------ (Drainage Channel Bed Level)
            |====== Drainage Channel Flow ======|
        ```
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   The canal water flows under pressure due to the head created by the difference in water levels.
        *   Requires a sufficient head to overcome frictional losses and maintain flow.
        *   The trough is generally constructed with R.C.C.
    *   **When to use:** When the drainage channel is at a higher elevation and the canal bed is lower, but the depth required for the drainage channel to pass freely under the canal would be too great.

*   **Canal Syphon:**
    *   **Description:** The canal water is passed *under* the drainage channel by letting it flow through a tube or barrel constructed below the bed of the drainage channel. The drainage channel flows freely *over* the canal.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:**
        ```
            |====== Drainage Channel Flow ======|
            ------------------------------------ (Drainage Channel Bed Level)
            |       /----------\           |
            |      /            \          | (Syphon barrel)
            |     /--------------\         |
            ------------------------------------ (Canal Bed Level)
            |========= Canal Flow =========| (Lower Level)
        ```
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   The canal water flows under pressure.
        *   Requires sufficient head on the upstream side to drive the water through the syphon barrel.
        *   The syphon barrel is usually made of RCC or masonry.
    *   **When to use:** When the canal bed is at a lower level than the drainage channel, and the drainage channel needs to pass freely over the canal.

*   **Canal Fall with Syphon:**
    *   **Description:** Similar to a canal syphon, but it is combined with a fall. A portion of the drainage water is allowed to enter the canal through an inlet, and both waters are discharged back into the drainage channel through an outlet at a lower level. This is a controlled mixing with a fall for the canal.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:**
        ```
            |====== Drainage Channel Flow ======|
            ------------------------------------ (Drainage Channel Bed Level)
            |   Inlet Regulator ---------> |
            |   (Canal Water Enters)       |
            |----------------------------->|
            |========= Canal Flow =========| (with Drainage Water)
            |   Outlet Regulator --------> |
            |   (Canal Water Exits)        |
            ------------------------------------ (Lower Drainage Channel Bed Level)
            |======= Drainage Channel Flow ======|
        ```
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   Controlled mixing of canal water into the drainage channel.
        *   Requires a drop in the drainage channel level.
        *   Used when drainage water has to be passed and some mixing is permissible, or when it is advantageous to utilize the drainage water.

### 3. Selection of Suitable Type of Cross Drainage Work

The choice of the most suitable cross-drainage work depends on a careful analysis of various factors related to both the canal and the intersecting drainage channel.

#### 3.1 Key Factors for Selection

1.  **Relative Levels:**
    *   **Canal Bed Level vs. Drainage Channel Bed Level:** This is the primary determinant.
    *   **Canal Full Supply Level (FSL) vs. Drainage High Flood Level (HFL):** This dictates whether the canal can pass freely over the drainage or vice versa, or if a syphon action is needed.

2.  **Discharge:**
    *   **Canal Discharge:** The volume of water to be carried by the canal.
    *   **Drainage Discharge (especially peak flood discharge):** The volume of water to be passed from the drainage channel. High flood discharges in the drainage channel require larger spans and clearances.

3.  **Alignment:**
    *   **Perpendicularity of Intersection:** A perpendicular intersection is generally preferred for efficiency. Skewed intersections can increase the length of the structure and its complexity.

4.  **Topography and Geology:**
    *   **Ground Levels:** Affects the excavation depth required for drainage channels or the height of canal structures.
    *   **Soil Bearing Capacity:** Influences the foundation design for piers and abutments.
    *   **Permeability of Soil:** Affects the seepage control measures.

5.  **Economic Considerations:**
    *   **Cost of Construction:** Different types have varying construction costs due to materials, excavation, and complexity.
    *   **Maintenance Costs:** Some structures might be cheaper to build but more expensive to maintain.

6.  **Operational Requirements:**
    *   **Flexibility:** The ability to manage flow variations in both channels.
    *   **Requirement for Uninterrupted Flow:** If the canal supply cannot be stopped for long periods, structures that allow continuous flow are preferred.

7.  **Water Quality:**
    *   **Mixing of Waters:** If mixing is undesirable (e.g., polluted drainage water with clean irrigation water), structures that prevent mixing are essential.

#### 3.2 Selection Criteria (Summary Table)

| Condition                                                       | Suitable Cross Drainage Work                                  | Rationale                                                                                                   |
| :-------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Canal Bed Level > Drainage Channel Bed Level & Drainage HFL < Canal FSL** | **Aqueduct**                                                  | Canal flows over drainage; ample clearance for drainage flow; no mixing.                                    |
| **Canal Bed Level < Drainage Channel Bed Level & Canal FSL < Drainage HFL** | **Canal Syphon**                                              | Canal flows under drainage; syphon action requires sufficient head in canal; drainage flows freely over. |
| **Canal Bed Level > Drainage Channel Bed Level & Drainage HFL > Canal FSL** | **Syphon Aqueduct**                                           | Canal trough dips under drainage; canal water flows under pressure; requires sufficient head.               |
| **Canal Bed Level < Drainage Channel Bed Level & Canal FSL > Drainage HFL** | **Superpassage**                                              | Drainage flows over canal; sufficient height for drainage to pass under canal without syphon action.        |
| **Canal Bed Level ≈ Drainage Channel Bed Level (Small discharges)** | **Level Crossing**                                            | Allows mixing; controlled by regulators; only for non-critical canal flow and small drainage discharge. |
| **Need to pass drainage water but can tolerate some mixing & Canal has a fall** | **Inlet and Outlet / Canal Fall with Inlet**                  | Controlled mixing; utilizes drainage water; requires a fall in the canal.                                 |
| **Need to pass canal water but can tolerate some mixing & Drainage has a fall** | **Canal Fall with Syphon / Outlet and Inlet**                 | Controlled mixing; utilizes canal water; requires a fall in the drainage channel.                         |

### 4. Examples

*   **Example 1: Aqueduct**
    *   **Scenario:** A main canal is to irrigate land that is intersected by a small river. The river's bed level is 5 meters below the canal's full supply level (FSL). The highest flood level (HFL) of the river is 2 meters below the canal's FSL.
    *   **Selection:** An **Aqueduct** is suitable because the canal can be carried over the river in a trough, providing sufficient clearance for the river's flood flow.

*   **Example 2: Canal Syphon**
    *   **Scenario:** A branch canal needs to pass through an area that has a large natural stream. The stream's bed level is 3 meters above the canal's bed level. The stream's HFL is 1 meter above the canal's FSL.
    *   **Selection:** A **Canal Syphon** is appropriate. The canal water will be passed under the stream bed through a syphon barrel. The stream will flow over the syphon, and its HFL is still below the ground level of the canal bank, allowing it to pass freely.

*   **Example 3: Syphon Aqueduct**
    *   **Scenario:** A distributary canal needs to cross a canalized drainage channel. The distributary canal is at a lower elevation than the drainage channel. However, the depth required for the drainage channel to pass freely under the distributary would be excessively large (say, requiring 10 meters of excavation for the drainage channel).
    *   **Selection:** A **Syphon Aqueduct** is a good choice. The distributary canal trough will dip down and pass under the drainage channel like a syphon. This reduces the excavation depth for the drainage channel compared to a regular aqueduct.

### 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** Define cross drainage works and explain their importance in irrigation canal design.

**Answer 1:** Cross drainage works are hydraulic structures designed to facilitate the crossing of two watercourses (a canal and a natural stream or another canal) without allowing their waters to mix and with minimal interference to their respective flows. They are crucial in irrigation design because they prevent contamination of irrigation water, avoid erosion and sedimentation at the intersection, and prevent flooding of the canal by natural drainage.

**Question 2:** Under what conditions is a Level Crossing typically used? What are its disadvantages?

**Answer 2:** A Level Crossing is typically used when the canal and drainage channel intersect at approximately the same level, the discharge in the drainage channel is small and intermittent, and the mixing of water is not a major concern. Its disadvantages include:
*   Mixing of irrigation water with drainage water, potentially affecting water quality.
*   Potential for sedimentation at the intersection.
*   Requires careful operation of regulators to control flow and prevent backflow.
*   The canal supply may need to be shut off during maintenance or high flood periods of the drainage channel.

**Question 3:** A canal is to be constructed, and it intersects a natural stream. The canal bed level is 2 meters *higher* than the stream's bed level. The stream's high flood level (HFL) is 1 meter *below* the canal's full supply level (FSL). Which type of cross-drainage work would be most suitable, and why?

**Answer 3:** An **Aqueduct** would be the most suitable type.
*   **Reasoning:** The canal bed level is higher than the stream's bed level, allowing the canal to pass over the stream. The stream's HFL (1 meter below canal FSL) is well below the canal's FSL, meaning there is sufficient vertical clearance to construct an aqueduct trough for the canal to flow over the stream without interfering with the stream's flood discharge.

**Question 4:** Differentiate between a Canal Syphon and a Syphon Aqueduct.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Canal Syphon:** The canal water passes *under* the drainage channel. The drainage channel flows *over* the canal. The canal water flows under pressure through a syphon barrel. This is chosen when the canal bed is lower than the drainage channel, and the drainage channel needs to pass freely over the canal.
*   **Syphon Aqueduct:** The canal water passes *under* the drainage channel, but the canal itself is at a *higher* elevation relative to the drainage channel. The canal trough dips down and acts like a syphon to pass under the drainage channel. This is chosen when the canal is at a higher elevation than the drainage channel, but the depth required for the drainage channel to pass freely under the canal would be too great for a regular aqueduct.

**Question 5:** List four important factors that influence the selection of a suitable type of cross-drainage work.

**Answer 5:** Four important factors are:
1.  Relative levels of the canal and drainage channel.
2.  Discharge in the canal and the drainage channel (especially peak flood discharge).
3.  Topography and geology of the site.
4.  Economic considerations (cost of construction and maintenance).
    *(Other valid answers include alignment, operational requirements, and water quality considerations.)*

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   Cross drainage works are essential for ensuring the independent and unimpeded flow of intersecting watercourses.
*   The primary classification is based on whether the canal passes over the drainage (Aqueduct, Superpassage) or under the drainage (Canal Syphon, Syphon Aqueduct).
*   The relative levels of the canal FSL and drainage HFL are critical in determining the need for a syphon action.
*   Level crossings are the simplest but least desirable due to water mixing.
*   Economic viability, structural stability, and operational efficiency are key considerations in the selection process.
*   Always consider the highest flood level of the drainage channel when designing the clearance for an aqueduct or superpassage.
*   Syphon structures require sufficient head to overcome frictional losses and maintain flow.
