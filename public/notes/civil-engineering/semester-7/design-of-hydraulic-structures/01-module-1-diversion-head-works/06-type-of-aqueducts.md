# DESIGN OF HYDRAULIC STRUCTURES

## Module 1: Diversion Head Works

### Topic: Types of Aqueducts

---

### **1. Introduction to Aqueducts**

*   **Definition:** An aqueduct is a man-made channel or conduit designed to convey water over a significant distance, often across natural obstacles like valleys or rivers. In the context of diversion headworks, aqueducts are crucial for carrying canal water over natural drainage channels that intersect the canal's alignment.

*   **Purpose:**
    *   To maintain the continuous flow of canal water without interruption by natural streams or rivers.
    *   To prevent contamination of canal water by mixing with the drainage water.
    *   To ensure the canal water flows at its designed level and gradient.

*   **Key Principle:** The fundamental principle behind an aqueduct is to allow the natural drainage water to pass *below* the canal water, maintaining the integrity of the canal's flow.

---

### **2. Classification of Aqueducts**

Aqueducts are primarily classified based on the relative levels of the canal water surface and the highest flood level of the drainage channel. This classification helps in determining the structural design and type of aqueduct required.

#### **2.1. Aqueduct (or Canal Aqueduct)**

*   **Description:** This is the most common type of aqueduct. In this type, the canal water flows at a *higher* level than the highest flood level of the natural drainage channel. The drainage water passes *underneath* the canal through arches or culverts.

*   **Key Features:**
    *   Canal bed is above the highest flood level of the drainage channel.
    *   The structure consists of a trough or barrel carrying the canal water, supported by piers.
    *   The space below the trough is utilized for the drainage channel, which can be an open channel or a closed conduit (culvert).
    *   Afflux (increase in water level upstream of the obstruction) caused by the drainage opening must be considered.

*   **Structural Components:**
    *   **Trough/Barrel:** The water-tight conduit carrying the canal water.
    *   **Piers/Abutments:** Supports for the trough.
    *   **Arches/Culverts:** Structures carrying the drainage water.
    *   **Wing Walls:** To guide the drainage water and protect the abutments.
    *   **Foundations:** To support the entire structure.

*   **Example:** A canal flowing over a small river or a large stream.

#### **2.2. Syphon Aqueduct**

*   **Description:** In a syphon aqueduct, the canal water surface is *below* the highest flood level of the drainage channel. The canal water is forced to flow under pressure through a closed conduit (like a barrel or pipe) which dips below the drainage channel.

*   **Key Features:**
    *   Canal bed is below the highest flood level of the drainage channel.
    *   The canal water acts as a pressurised flow through the syphon barrel.
    *   The drainage water flows freely over the syphon barrel.
    *   The structure is designed to withstand the pressure of the canal water.
    *   It requires special attention to prevent leakage and ensure adequate head to drive the flow through the syphon.

*   **Structural Components:**
    *   **Syphon Barrel/Pipes:** The closed conduit carrying canal water under pressure.
    *   **Inlet & Outlet Chambers:** To control the flow into and out of the syphon.
    *   **Retaining Walls/Abutments:** To support the canal banks and the syphon structure.
    *   **Foundations:** Designed to support the weight of water and the structure.

*   **Example:** When a canal alignment crosses a river with a high flood discharge, and it's uneconomical to raise the canal level significantly.

#### **2.3. Level Crossing**

*   **Description:** In a level crossing, the canal and the drainage channel intersect at the *same* or approximately the same level. This arrangement is generally avoided due to the risk of mixing of waters and potential for silt deposition.

*   **Key Features:**
    *   Canal and drainage channel are at similar levels.
    *   Requires regulation gates on both the canal and the drainage channel to control flow and prevent mixing.
    *   Can be of two types:
        *   **Superpassage:** Canal water flows over the drainage channel, with the drainage channel passing through a culvert beneath the canal. This is similar to an aqueduct but the levels are closer.
        *   **True Level Crossing:** Both channels flow side-by-side or intersect directly with gates for regulation. This is the least desirable option.

*   **Structural Components:**
    *   **Regulation Gates:** Essential for controlling flow and preventing mixing.
    *   **Foundation for Gates and Piers.**
    *   **Retaining Walls:** To define the channels.

*   **Example:** Used only in specific situations where other options are not feasible or economical, often for minor streams.

### **3. Factors Governing the Choice of Aqueduct Type**

The selection of the appropriate aqueduct type depends on several crucial factors related to the canal and the drainage channel:

*   **Relative Levels:**
    *   **Canal Bed Level vs. Highest Flood Level of Drainage:** This is the primary factor.
        *   If Canal Bed > HFL (Highest Flood Level): Aqueduct (Canal Aqueduct)
        *   If Canal Bed < HFL: Syphon Aqueduct
        *   If Canal Bed ≈ HFL: Level Crossing (generally avoided)

*   **Discharge:**
    *   **Canal Discharge:** Affects the size of the trough or barrel.
    *   **Drainage Discharge:** Affects the size of the arches/culverts or syphon barrels and the afflux. High drainage discharge requires larger openings.

*   **Afflux:** The rise in water level upstream of the obstruction caused by the aqueduct structure. This must be kept within acceptable limits to avoid impacting the canal's upstream reach.

*   **Cost:** The overall construction and maintenance cost of each type. Syphon aqueducts can be more expensive due to the pressure conduits and gate mechanisms.

*   **Silt Content of Canal Water:** If canal water is highly silty, it can lead to silt deposition in the syphon barrels, increasing friction and reducing flow capacity. This makes syphon aqueducts less suitable for highly silty canals.

*   **Navigation Requirements:** If navigation is required on the canal, the free board and clearance under the aqueduct structure become important.

*   **Site Conditions:** Topography, soil conditions, and availability of construction materials.

### **4. Design Considerations for Aqueducts**

While the types of aqueducts are the focus here, a brief mention of design considerations is helpful:

*   **Trough Design:**
    *   Cross-section (rectangular, trapezoidal).
    *   Water velocity to prevent silt deposition and scour.
    *   Structural strength to carry water load and external forces.
    *   Water-tightness.

*   **Arches/Culvert Design:**
    *   Span and rise of arches.
    *   Load carrying capacity.
    *   Hydraulic design to minimize afflux.

*   **Syphon Barrel Design:**
    *   Diameter/Size of barrels.
    *   Head loss calculations.
    *   Material and jointing to withstand pressure.
    *   Gate design for flow control.

*   **Foundations:** Must be stable and capable of carrying all loads.

### **5. Practice Questions & Exercises**

**Question 1:**
An aqueduct is required to carry canal water over a natural drainage channel. The highest flood level of the drainage channel is observed to be at RL 100.0 m. The full supply level (FSL) of the canal is at RL 105.0 m. What type of aqueduct would be most suitable?

**Answer 1:**
Since the Full Supply Level (FSL) of the canal (105.0 m) is higher than the Highest Flood Level (HFL) of the drainage channel (100.0 m), a **Canal Aqueduct** would be the most suitable type. The canal water will flow above the drainage channel.

**Question 2:**
Describe the main difference between a Canal Aqueduct and a Syphon Aqueduct in terms of water levels.

**Answer 2:**
The main difference lies in the relative levels of the canal water surface and the highest flood level of the drainage channel:
*   **Canal Aqueduct:** The canal water surface is at a *higher* level than the highest flood level of the drainage channel.
*   **Syphon Aqueduct:** The canal water surface is at a *lower* level than the highest flood level of the drainage channel.

**Question 3:**
When would a Level Crossing be considered for a canal crossing a drainage channel? What are the critical components required for its operation?

**Answer 3:**
A Level Crossing is considered only when the canal and drainage channel intersect at approximately the same level. This is generally avoided due to the risk of mixing waters and siltation. The critical components required for its operation are **regulation gates** on both the canal and the drainage channel to control flow and prevent mixing. A **Superpassage** is a type of level crossing where the canal flows over the drainage channel via a culvert.

**Question 4:**
What are the potential problems associated with using a Syphon Aqueduct for a canal with a high silt content?

**Answer 4:**
A major problem with using a Syphon Aqueduct for a canal with high silt content is the potential for **silt deposition** within the syphon barrels. This deposition can:
*   Reduce the effective cross-sectional area of the barrels.
*   Increase friction losses, leading to higher head requirements for flow.
*   Require frequent desilting operations, increasing maintenance costs.
*   Potentially lead to choking of the barrels.

---

### **6. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Aqueducts carry canal water OVER a drainage channel.**
*   **Syphons (in general) carry water UNDER pressure.**
*   **The primary factor determining the type of aqueduct is the relative level of the canal's Full Supply Level (FSL) and the drainage channel's Highest Flood Level (HFL).**
*   **Canal Aqueduct:** Canal FSL > Drainage HFL. Drainage passes underneath.
*   **Syphon Aqueduct:** Canal FSL < Drainage HFL. Canal water flows under pressure through dipped barrels.
*   **Level Crossing:** Canal FSL ≈ Drainage HFL. Requires gate control.
*   **Afflux** must be considered in the design of the drainage openings.
*   **Silt load** of the canal is a critical factor when considering Syphon Aqueducts.
