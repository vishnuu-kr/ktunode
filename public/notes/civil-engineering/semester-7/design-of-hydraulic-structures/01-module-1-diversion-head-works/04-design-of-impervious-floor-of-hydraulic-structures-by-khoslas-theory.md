# DESIGN OF HYDRAULIC STRUCTURES

## Module 1: Diversion Head Works

### Topic: Design of Impervious Floor of Hydraulic Structures by Khosla’s Theory

---

### 1. Introduction to Impervious Floors and Seepage

**1.1 What is an Impervious Floor?**

*   An impervious floor (also known as a foundation floor or cutoff) is a barrier constructed beneath and extending downstream of a hydraulic structure (like a weir or barrage) to prevent or control the seepage of water through the foundation soil.
*   It aims to reduce the uplift pressure on the structure and limit the exit gradient of the seepage water.

**1.2 Why is an Impervious Floor Necessary?**

*   **Prevention of Piping:** Seepage water flowing through the soil can erode the soil particles, creating internal channels. This phenomenon, known as "piping," can lead to the collapse of the foundation and the structure itself.
*   **Reduction of Uplift Pressure:** Water seeping beneath the structure exerts an upward pressure (uplift pressure). High uplift pressures can counteract the weight of the structure, potentially causing it to float or fail.
*   **Control of Exit Gradient:** The exit gradient is the hydraulic gradient at the point where the seepage water emerges from the soil downstream of the structure. A high exit gradient indicates high velocity of seepage, which can cause erosion and piping.

**1.3 Seepage Flow through Porous Media**

*   Water percolating through the soil under a hydraulic structure follows a path known as the **seepage line** or **flow line**.
*   This flow is governed by Darcy's Law, which states that the flow rate is proportional to the hydraulic gradient and the permeability of the soil.

### 2. Khosla’s Theory: Principles and Concepts

**2.1 Introduction to Khosla’s Theory**

*   Developed by Dr. A.N. Khosla, this theory provides a method for analyzing the seepage flow and designing the impervious floor for hydraulic structures.
*   It is based on the **theory of translatory waves** and the **principle of superposition**, utilizing the concept of **flow nets**.

**2.2 Key Assumptions of Khosla’s Theory**

*   The soil is homogeneous and isotropic.
*   The flow is steady and two-dimensional.
*   The pores of the soil are completely filled with water.
*   The seepage is governed by Darcy's Law.
*   The water level on the upstream side and downstream side is constant.
*   The hydraulic gradient is finite everywhere except at the points where the seepage emerges into the air.

**2.3 Basic Concepts**

*   **Equipotential Lines:** Lines connecting points of equal hydraulic head.
*   **Streamlines:** Lines representing the path of individual water particles during seepage.
*   **Flow Net:** A graphical representation of equipotential lines and streamlines, orthogonal to each other. The number of squares formed by these lines indicates the flow rate and pressure distribution.
*   **Hydraulic Gradient:** The rate of change of hydraulic head with respect to distance. It is the slope of the equipotential lines.
*   **Exit Gradient:** The hydraulic gradient at the downstream end of the impervious floor where the water emerges from the soil. This is a critical parameter for design.

**2.4 The Principle of Superposition**

*   Khosla's theory utilizes the principle of superposition to determine the pressure distribution for complex floor configurations.
*   This involves breaking down a complex flow problem into simpler, solvable components.
*   The total hydraulic head at any point is the sum of the heads from individual simple cases.

**2.5 Correction Factors for Intermediate Piles**

*   Khosla's theory initially developed solutions for simple cases like a floor with no piles, a floor with an upstream cutoff, and a floor with a downstream cutoff.
*   However, real-world structures often have both upstream and downstream cutoffs (piles) and may also have intermediate piles.
*   For these complex scenarios, Khosla introduced **correction factors** to account for the influence of these piles on the seepage flow and uplift pressures.

**2.6 Types of Correction Factors**

*   **Correction Factor for the Effect of Depth of Piles:** Accounts for the reduction in seepage due to the presence of cutoffs of finite depth.
*   **Correction Factor for the Effect of Intermediate Galleries/Piles:** Accounts for the interference of piles located between the upstream and downstream ends of the floor.
*   **Correction Factor for the Effect of Sloping Floors:** Accounts for the variation in seepage when the floor is not horizontal.

### 3. Design of Impervious Floor using Khosla’s Theory

**3.1 Design Procedure**

The design of an impervious floor using Khosla's theory typically involves the following steps:

1.  **Determine the Head Difference (h):** This is the difference between the upstream and downstream water levels.
2.  **Assume a Floor Configuration:** Decide on the layout of the impervious floor, including its length and the location and depth of upstream and downstream cutoffs (piles).
3.  **Calculate the Exit Gradient:** This is the most crucial step. Khosla's theory provides methods to calculate the exit gradient for various floor configurations.
4.  **Compare Exit Gradient with Permissible Limit:** The calculated exit gradient must be less than the permissible exit gradient for the specific soil type to prevent piping.
5.  **Modify Floor Dimensions (if necessary):** If the calculated exit gradient exceeds the permissible limit, the length of the floor or the depth of the cutoffs needs to be increased.
6.  **Calculate Uplift Pressures:** Determine the uplift pressure at various points along the floor, especially at the junction with the structure.
7.  **Design the Thickness of the Floor:** The thickness of the impervious floor is designed to withstand the uplift pressures and the weight of the structure.

**3.2 Calculating Seepage Discharge (Q)**

The seepage discharge through the floor can be calculated using the formula:

$Q = K \times h \times (N_f / N_d)$

Where:
*   $Q$ = Seepage discharge
*   $K$ = Coefficient of permeability of the soil
*   $h$ = Head difference across the floor
*   $N_f$ = Number of flow channels (determined from the flow net)
*   $N_d$ = Number of equipotential drops (determined from the flow net)

**3.3 Calculating Exit Gradient ($G_e$)**

The exit gradient is calculated as:

$G_e = h / (L \times \alpha)$

Where:
*   $G_e$ = Exit gradient
*   $h$ = Head difference
*   $L$ = Length of the impervious floor
*   $\alpha$ = A factor that depends on the floor configuration (determined from Khosla's curves or calculations involving correction factors).

**3.4 Permissible Exit Gradient ($G_{e, permissible}$)**

The permissible exit gradient depends on the type of soil and the nature of the downstream protection:

*   **Coarse sand and gravel:** $1/4$ to $1/5$
*   **Sand:** $1/6$
*   **Silt:** $1/7$ to $1/8$
*   **Clay:** $1/9$ to $1/10$

**3.5 Effect of Upstream and Downstream Piles on Exit Gradient**

*   **No Piles:** The exit gradient is highest at the downstream edge of the floor.
*   **Upstream Pile:** Reduces the overall seepage head but has a marginal effect on the exit gradient at the downstream end.
*   **Downstream Pile:** Significantly reduces the exit gradient at the downstream edge of the floor. The deeper the downstream pile, the lower the exit gradient.
*   **Intermediate Piles:** Help to redistribute the hydraulic head and reduce the uplift pressure along the floor.

**3.6 Calculating Uplift Pressure**

Khosla's theory provides methods to calculate the uplift pressure at different points along the floor using the concept of potential drop. The uplift pressure at any point is given by:

$U = h_{wp} \times \rho_w \times g$

Where:
*   $U$ = Uplift pressure
*   $h_{wp}$ = Uplift head at that point
*   $\rho_w$ = Density of water
*   $g$ = Acceleration due to gravity

The uplift head ($h_{wp}$) at a point is the portion of the total head difference ($h$) that has been "lost" by the water up to that point, considering the streamline path and equipotential drops.

### 4. Practical Application and Examples

**4.1 Case Study: Design of an Impervious Floor for a Weir**

*   **Problem:** Design an impervious floor for a weir with a length of 100m and a head difference of 5m. The soil is sandy, with a permissible exit gradient of 1/6. The upstream cutoff is 2m deep, and the downstream cutoff is 4m deep.

*   **Solution Approach:**
    1.  Calculate the required floor length or cutoff depth to satisfy the exit gradient criteria.
    2.  Use Khosla's curves or formulas to determine the exit gradient for the assumed configuration.
    3.  If the exit gradient is too high, increase the floor length or cutoff depth.
    4.  Calculate the uplift pressures at critical points, especially at the upstream face of the weir.
    5.  Determine the thickness of the impervious floor to withstand these pressures.

**4.2 Use of Khosla's Curves**

*   Khosla developed a set of curves that simplify the calculation of seepage characteristics for various floor configurations.
*   These curves relate the ratio of floor length to cutoff depth, the ratio of cutoff depths, and the exit gradient.
*   **Important Note:** These curves are applicable for specific basic configurations and require correction factors for more complex scenarios.

**4.3 Example Calculation of Exit Gradient (Simplified)**

Let's consider a simple case of a horizontal floor of length $L$ with an upstream cutoff of depth $d_u$ and a downstream cutoff of depth $d_d$.

*   **Formula (Simplified representation, actual formulas involve correction factors):**
    $G_e \approx h / (L + \alpha \cdot d_d)$ (where $\alpha$ is a factor related to the downstream pile)

*   **Example:** If $h = 6$m, $L = 30$m, and $d_d = 4$m. Assume a factor $\alpha = 2$ (hypothetical for illustration).
    $G_e \approx 6 / (30 + 2 \times 4) = 6 / 38 \approx 0.158$
    The permissible exit gradient for sandy soil is $1/6 \approx 0.167$.
    In this hypothetical case, the exit gradient is acceptable.

**4.4 Effect of Intermediate Piles**

*   Intermediate piles are typically placed at a certain distance from the upstream pile.
*   They help to break the flow path and reduce the overall uplift pressure.
*   The design involves calculating the interference factors for intermediate piles using Khosla's methods.

### 5. Important Points to Remember

*   **Exit Gradient is Critical:** Always ensure that the calculated exit gradient is less than the permissible exit gradient for the soil to prevent piping.
*   **Uplift Pressure:** The uplift pressure is maximum at the junction of the impervious floor and the hydraulic structure.
*   **Depth of Downstream Cutoff:** The depth of the downstream cutoff has a significant impact on reducing the exit gradient.
*   **Correction Factors are Essential:** For any configuration other than the basic ones, correction factors must be applied to the calculated seepage characteristics.
*   **Khosla's Theory vs. Flow Nets:** While flow nets provide a visual understanding, Khosla's theory provides analytical solutions and correction factors for practical design.
*   **Soil Properties:** The permeability and grading of the soil are crucial inputs for the design.

### 7. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
**(b) To prevent piping and reduce uplift pressure**

**Answer 2:**
**(a) The permeability of the soil and the hydraulic gradient** (Based on Darcy's Law)

**Answer 3:**
$1/5 = 0.20$

**Answer 4:**
The exit gradient is the hydraulic gradient at the downstream end of the impervious floor where seepage emerges. A high exit gradient indicates high seepage velocity, which can cause soil erosion and "piping," leading to the structural failure of the foundation and the structure. Therefore, it is crucial to ensure the exit gradient is below a safe permissible limit.

**Answer 5:**
A downstream cutoff significantly reduces the exit gradient. By extending the impervious floor or adding a cutoff pile downstream, the path of seepage is lengthened, and the hydraulic head is dissipated over a greater distance, thereby lowering the gradient at the exit point.

**Answer 6:**
To estimate the exit gradient, we can use a simplified approach considering the downstream pile:
Exit Gradient ($G_e$) $\approx h / (L + \alpha \times d_d)$

Given:
*   $h = 6$m
*   $L = 40$m
*   $d_d = 5$m
*   $\alpha = 2$ (hypothetical factor)

$G_e \approx 6 / (40 + 2 \times 5)$
$G_e \approx 6 / (40 + 10)$
$G_e \approx 6 / 50$
$G_e \approx 0.12$

Permissible exit gradient for sand is $1/6 \approx 0.167$.

Since the estimated exit gradient (0.12) is less than the permissible exit gradient (0.167), the floor is likely to be safe in this simplified scenario. However, for actual design, Khosla's detailed formulas and correction factors for upstream and intermediate piles would be used.
