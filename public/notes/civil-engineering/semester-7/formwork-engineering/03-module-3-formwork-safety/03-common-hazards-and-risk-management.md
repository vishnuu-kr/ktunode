# FORMWORK ENGINEERING: Module 3: Formwork Safety - Common Hazards and Risk Management

This module focuses on identifying and mitigating the risks associated with formwork construction and dismantling. A safe working environment is paramount in formwork engineering to prevent injuries and project delays.

## Learning Outcomes:

Upon successful completion of this module, you will be able to:

*   Identify common hazards associated with formwork construction and dismantling.
*   Explain the principles of risk assessment and management in formwork operations.
*   Describe various control measures to mitigate identified hazards.
*   Understand the importance of proper training, communication, and supervision.
*   Recognize the role of personal protective equipment (PPE) in formwork safety.
*   Apply knowledge to practical scenarios involving formwork safety.

---

## 1. Common Hazards Associated with Formwork

Formwork, while essential for concrete construction, presents a range of hazards that can lead to serious injuries if not properly managed. These hazards can be broadly categorized as follows:

### 1.1. Structural Failures

These are catastrophic events where the formwork system collapses or fails under load.

*   **Causes:**
    *   **Overloading:** Exceeding the designed load capacity of the formwork system, including the weight of fresh concrete, reinforcement, and construction loads.
        *   *Example:* Pouring concrete in lifts that are too high, or placing excessive construction materials on the formwork deck.
    *   **Inadequate Bracing and Support:** Insufficient or improperly installed bracing, props, or shores to resist lateral and vertical loads.
        *   *Example:* Not using adequate diagonal bracing on tall formwork, or using damaged/undersized props.
    *   **Improper Assembly/Erection:** Incorrectly fitting components, loose connections, or failure to follow manufacturer’s instructions.
        *   *Example:* Not fully inserting pins in modular formwork systems, or failing to tighten bolts on ties.
    *   **Material Defects:** Using damaged, weakened, or unsuitable materials for formwork components (e.g., rotten timber, bent steel).
        *   *Example:* Using plywood sheets with large voids or delamination, or using cracked timber props.
    *   **Premature Removal of Formwork:** Removing formwork or shores before the concrete has gained sufficient strength.
        *   *Example:* Striking formwork for a beam before the concrete has reached the specified curing period and strength.
    *   **Environmental Factors:** High winds, heavy rain, or unstable ground conditions can compromise the stability of erected formwork.
        *   *Example:* Formwork collapsing due to strong winds before the concrete is adequately set.

*   **Consequences:**
    *   Crushing injuries or fatalities from falling materials and collapsing structures.
    *   Damage to surrounding infrastructure and services.
    *   Significant project delays and financial losses.

### 1.2. Falls from Height

Working on elevated formwork structures poses a significant risk of falls.

*   **Causes:**
    *   **Lack of Edge Protection:** Open edges of formwork decks without guardrails or safety nets.
        *   *Example:* Workers walking on formwork decks without any barriers to prevent them from falling.
    *   **Unsafe Access:** Using unstable ladders, unsecured scaffolding, or climbing on formwork components.
        *   *Example:* Using a damaged ladder that is not properly secured at the top or bottom.
    *   **Slippery Surfaces:** Wet or oily formwork surfaces, especially during concrete pouring or cleaning.
        *   *Example:* Spilled concrete mix or release agents creating slippery walking surfaces.
    *   **Inadequate Housekeeping:** Accumulation of materials, debris, or tools on working platforms.
        *   *Example:* Leaving tools or offcuts of timber scattered on formwork decks.
    *   **Unprotected Openings:** Uncovered holes or gaps in the formwork where workers can fall through.
        *   *Example:* Gaps in floor formwork that have not been covered during construction.

*   **Consequences:**
    *   Fractures, spinal injuries, head trauma, and fatalities.

### 1.3. Struck-by Hazards

Being hit by falling objects or moving equipment is a common risk.

*   **Causes:**
    *   **Falling Materials:** Tools, materials, or formwork components dropping from heights during erection, striking, or dismantling.
        *   *Example:* A worker dropping a hammer from an upper level of formwork.
    *   **Moving Equipment:** Contact with concrete pumps, cranes, forklifts, or other site vehicles operating near formwork areas.
        *   *Example:* A worker being struck by a concrete pump hose or truck during a pour.
    *   **Dislodging of Materials:** Loose ties, wedges, or other components becoming dislodged and falling.
        *   *Example:* A vibrating formwork panel causing wedges to loosen and fall.

*   **Consequences:**
    *   Bruises, lacerations, fractures, head injuries, and fatalities.

### 1.4. Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs) and Ergonomic Hazards

Repetitive manual handling and awkward postures can lead to long-term injuries.

*   **Causes:**
    *   **Manual Handling of Heavy Materials:** Lifting, carrying, and positioning heavy formwork components without proper lifting aids or techniques.
        *   *Example:* Manually lifting heavy steel formwork panels or large timber beams.
    *   **Repetitive Tasks:** Repeatedly performing the same motions, such as hammering nails, screwing bolts, or placing ties.
        *   *Example:* Continuous nailing of timber formwork.
    *   **Awkward Postures:** Working in confined spaces, bending, reaching, or twisting to assemble/dismantle formwork.
        *   *Example:* Working in a crouched position under a suspended formwork structure.
    *   **Vibration:** Prolonged exposure to vibration from power tools used in formwork assembly/dismantling.
        *   *Example:* Using vibrating demolition tools to remove old formwork.

*   **Consequences:**
    *   Back pain, sprains, strains, carpal tunnel syndrome, and other chronic injuries.

### 1.5. Electrical Hazards

Contact with live electrical sources can be lethal.

*   **Causes:**
    *   **Damaged or Improperly Insulated Cords:** Using extension cords with frayed insulation or exposed wires.
        *   *Example:* Using an extension cord that has been run over by a vehicle.
    *   **Proximity to Overhead Power Lines:** Erecting or dismantling formwork near energized power lines.
        *   *Example:* A crane boom or a tall formwork section coming into contact with overhead power lines.
    *   **Improper Grounding:** Using electrical tools or equipment that are not properly grounded.
        *   *Example:* Using a power tool that lacks a three-prong plug or has a damaged grounding pin.
    *   **Working in Wet Conditions:** Using electrical equipment in damp or wet environments without proper protection.
        *   *Example:* Operating power tools on a formwork deck during rain.

*   **Consequences:**
    *   Electric shock, burns, and electrocution.

### 1.6. Material Handling and Storage Hazards

Improper handling and storage of formwork materials can lead to various incidents.

*   **Causes:**
    *   **Unstable Stacking:** Stacking materials too high or without proper support, leading to collapse.
        *   *Example:* Stacking timber planks or steel props in a disorganized and unstable manner.
    *   **Poorly Secured Loads:** Loads on forklifts or trucks not properly secured, leading to items falling off.
        *   *Example:* A pallet of formwork ties falling off a forklift during transport.
    *   **Sharp Edges:** Handling materials with sharp edges (e.g., steel formwork panels, cut timber) without protection.
        *   *Example:* Handling steel formwork panels without gloves.

*   **Consequences:**
    *   Crushing injuries, cuts, lacerations, and sprains.

## 2. Risk Assessment and Management

Risk assessment is a systematic process of identifying hazards, analyzing the likelihood and severity of harm, and determining appropriate control measures. Risk management is the ongoing process of implementing and monitoring these control measures.

### 2.1. Principles of Risk Assessment

The core principles of risk assessment involve:

1.  **Hazard Identification:** Recognizing potential sources of harm.
2.  **Risk Analysis:** Estimating the likelihood of a hazardous event occurring and the severity of its consequences.
3.  **Risk Evaluation:** Deciding whether the identified risks are acceptable or require further action.
4.  **Risk Control:** Implementing measures to eliminate or reduce the risks.

### 2.2. The Risk Management Process

This is a continuous cycle:

1.  **Establish the Context:** Define the scope of the activity, identify stakeholders, and understand the legal and organizational requirements.
2.  **Hazard Identification:** As detailed in Section 1.
3.  **Risk Analysis:**
    *   **Likelihood:** The probability of the hazard causing harm (e.g., Rare, Unlikely, Possible, Likely, Almost Certain).
    *   **Severity:** The potential harm or consequence if the hazard occurs (e.g., Negligible, Minor, Moderate, Major, Catastrophic).
    *   A **Risk Matrix** is often used to combine likelihood and severity to determine the level of risk (e.g., Low, Medium, High, Extreme).

    |                 | Severity: Negligible | Severity: Minor | Severity: Moderate | Severity: Major | Severity: Catastrophic |
    | :-------------- | :------------------- | :-------------- | :----------------- | :-------------- | :--------------------- |
    | **Likelihood: Rare** | Low                  | Low             | Low                | Medium          | Medium                 |
    | **Likelihood: Unlikely**| Low                  | Low             | Medium             | Medium          | High                   |
    | **Likelihood: Possible**| Low                  | Medium          | Medium             | High            | High                   |
    | **Likelihood: Likely**  | Medium               | Medium          | High               | High            | Extreme                |
    | **Likelihood: Almost Certain**| Medium               | High            | High               | Extreme         | Extreme                |

4.  **Risk Evaluation:** Based on the risk matrix, determine which risks require immediate attention. Risks classified as High or Extreme require urgent and robust control measures.

5.  **Risk Control (Hierarchy of Controls):** The most effective way to manage risks is by applying the hierarchy of controls, starting with the most effective and moving to the least effective:

    *   **Elimination:** Removing the hazard entirely.
        *   *Example:* Using a prefabricated formwork system that requires less on-site assembly, reducing manual handling and working at height.
    *   **Substitution:** Replacing a hazardous material or process with a less hazardous one.
        *   *Example:* Using lighter, modular formwork systems instead of heavy, traditional timber formwork.
    *   **Engineering Controls:** Designing or modifying the workplace or equipment to reduce exposure to hazards.
        *   *Example:* Installing guardrails and toe boards on formwork decks, using mechanical lifting devices for heavy components.
    *   **Administrative Controls:** Implementing work procedures, training, signage, and supervision to minimize risk.
        *   *Example:* Developing a detailed formwork erection and dismantling plan, providing specific training on formwork safety, implementing strict permit-to-work systems for high-risk activities.
    *   **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Providing and ensuring the use of appropriate safety equipment as a last resort.
        *   *Example:* Hard hats, safety harnesses, safety footwear, gloves, eye protection.

6.  **Implementation:** Putting the control measures into practice.
7.  **Monitoring and Review:** Regularly checking the effectiveness of control measures and updating the risk assessment as needed, especially after incidents or changes in the work process.

## 3. Control Measures for Common Hazards

Effective control measures are crucial for mitigating the identified hazards.

### 3.1. Controlling Structural Failures

*   **Design and Planning:**
    *   **Load Calculations:** Accurately calculate all loads (dead load, live load, lateral loads from wind and concrete pressure) and ensure the formwork system can withstand them.
    *   **Formwork Drawings and Specifications:** Develop detailed drawings showing erection sequences, bracing requirements, support systems, and load capacities.
    *   **Manufacturer's Guidelines:** Strictly adhere to the manufacturer's specifications and load tables for proprietary formwork systems.
*   **Erection and Striking:**
    *   **Qualified Supervision:** Ensure formwork erection and dismantling are supervised by experienced and competent personnel.
    *   **Trained Workforce:** Employ workers who are trained in the specific formwork systems being used and understand safe erection practices.
    *   **Pre-pour Checks:** Conduct thorough pre-pour inspections by a competent person to verify correct assembly, bracing, and support.
    *   **Controlled Concrete Pours:** Plan and control the rate of concrete pouring to avoid overloading. Monitor concrete pressure.
    *   **Gradual Striking:** Remove shores and formwork in a systematic sequence, ensuring concrete has reached sufficient strength as per engineer's instructions. Use intermediate shores if necessary.
    *   **Proper Bracing:** Install adequate bracing (horizontal and diagonal) at appropriate intervals to resist lateral forces.
*   **Material Quality:**
    *   **Inspection of Materials:** Inspect all formwork materials (timber, steel, plywood, props, ties) for damage, defects, or wear before use. Discard or repair damaged items.

### 3.2. Preventing Falls from Height

*   **Working at Height (WAH) Procedures:**
    *   **Guardrails:** Install robust guardrails (top rail, mid-rail, toe board) around all open edges of formwork decks and elevated working platforms.
    *   **Safety Nets:** Install safety nets below areas where there is a risk of falling objects or personnel.
    *   **Personal Fall Arrest Systems (PFAS):** Provide and ensure the correct use of safety harnesses, lanyards, and anchor points when working at height, especially where collective protection (guardrails, nets) is not feasible.
    *   **Access:** Provide safe means of access, such as secure ladders, stair towers, or scaffolding. Ensure ladders are secured and extended a safe distance above the landing surface.
*   **Housekeeping:**
    *   **Clear Work Areas:** Maintain clear and tidy working platforms, removing all debris, tools, and materials not in immediate use.
*   **Supervision and Training:**
    *   **Awareness:** Educate workers on the risks of working at height and the correct use of fall protection equipment.
    *   **Competency:** Ensure workers involved in WAH activities are competent and trained.

### 3.3. Preventing Struck-by Hazards

*   **Exclusion Zones:** Establish and enforce exclusion zones around areas where materials are being lifted, dropped, or where there is vehicle movement.
*   **Safe Lifting and Hoisting:**
    *   **Ropes and Nets:** Use safe lifting techniques and appropriate lifting gear. Secure materials when lifting.
    *   **Communication:** Maintain clear communication between crane operators, riggers, and ground personnel.
*   **Tool and Material Storage:**
    *   **Secure Storage:** Store tools and small materials in secure containers or tool belts to prevent them from falling.
    *   **Lanyards:** Use tool lanyards for tools used at height.
*   **Vehicle Movement:**
    *   **Traffic Management:** Implement traffic management plans to control vehicle movement on site.
    *   **Spotters:** Use trained spotters to guide vehicles and warn pedestrians.

### 3.4. Controlling MSDs and Ergonomic Hazards

*   **Mechanical Aids:**
    *   **Lifting Equipment:** Utilize forklifts, cranes, pallet trucks, or formwork handling devices for moving heavy components.
    *   **Wheeled Carts:** Use trolleys for transporting smaller components.
*   **Work Methods:**
    *   **Team Lifting:** Encourage team lifting for heavy or awkward loads, ensuring good communication and coordination.
    *   **Ergonomic Design:** Design formwork assembly processes to minimize awkward postures and repetitive movements where possible.
*   **Training:**
    *   **Manual Handling Training:** Provide training on safe lifting techniques, body mechanics, and the importance of taking breaks.
*   **Tool Selection:**
    *   **Ergonomic Tools:** Use tools designed to reduce vibration and strain.

### 3.5. Preventing Electrical Hazards

*   **Inspection of Electrical Equipment:**
    *   **PAT Testing:** Regularly inspect and test all portable electrical equipment (PAT testing).
    *   **Cable Checks:** Inspect all extension cords and cables for damage before each use.
*   **Safe Work Practices:**
    *   **Wet Conditions:** Avoid using electrical equipment in wet or damp conditions unless it is specifically designed for such environments (e.g., RCD protected, waterproof).
    *   **Isolation:** Ensure electrical supplies are isolated and locked off before any work is carried out on or near them.
    *   **Grounding:** Use only properly grounded electrical equipment.
*   **Overhead Power Lines:**
    *   **Clearance:** Maintain safe working clearances from overhead power lines. If work must be performed near lines, coordinate with the power utility company to de-energize them.
*   **RCDs:**
    *   **Residual Current Devices (RCDs):** Use RCDs on all temporary electrical circuits to provide immediate protection against electric shock.

### 3.6. Safe Material Handling and Storage

*   **Storage Areas:** Designate clear and safe storage areas for formwork materials.
*   **Stacking:**
    *   **Stability:** Stack materials in stable, well-organized piles, ensuring they are not too high. Use appropriate dunnage and chocks.
    *   **Segregation:** Segregate different types of materials.
*   **Securing Loads:**
    *   **Strapping and Securing:** Ensure all loads being transported are properly secured using straps, chains, or netting.
*   **Edge Protection:**
    *   **Gloves:** Provide and ensure workers use appropriate gloves when handling materials with sharp edges.

## 4. Importance of Training, Communication, and Supervision

These are fundamental pillars of effective formwork safety.

### 4.1. Training

*   **Competency-Based Training:** Ensure all personnel involved in formwork operations receive adequate training tailored to their roles and the specific formwork systems used. This includes:
    *   **General Formwork Safety:** Understanding common hazards and control measures.
    *   **Specific System Training:** How to safely erect, use, and dismantle particular formwork systems (e.g., modular, table formwork, slipform).
    *   **Working at Height Training:** Safe work practices, use of fall protection.
    *   **Manual Handling Training:** Proper lifting techniques.
    *   **Tool Safety:** Safe operation of power tools.
*   **Refresher Training:** Conduct periodic refresher training to reinforce safety practices and update knowledge on new techniques or regulations.

### 4.2. Communication

*   **Pre-Task Briefings (Toolbox Talks):** Conduct regular toolbox talks before starting work to discuss the day's tasks, identified hazards, control measures, and any specific instructions.
*   **Clear Instructions:** Provide clear, concise, and understandable instructions for all formwork operations.
*   **Reporting:** Encourage open communication and a culture where workers feel comfortable reporting hazards, near misses, and unsafe conditions without fear of reprisal.
*   **Signage:** Use appropriate safety signage to warn of hazards (e.g., "Danger: Falling Objects," "Hard Hat Area," "Working at Height").
*   **Emergency Procedures:** Ensure all workers are aware of emergency procedures, assembly points, and first-aid provisions.

### 4.3. Supervision

*   **Competent Supervisors:** Ensure supervisors are competent, experienced, and knowledgeable in formwork operations and safety management.
*   **Active Supervision:** Supervisors should actively monitor work activities, observe safe and unsafe behaviors, and intervene promptly to correct any deviations from safety procedures.
*   **Enforcement:** Supervisors are responsible for enforcing safety rules and ensuring that control measures are implemented and effective.
*   **Promoting a Safety Culture:** Supervisors play a key role in fostering a positive safety culture where safety is prioritized by everyone on site.

## 5. Role of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

PPE is the last line of defense in the hierarchy of controls and should be used in conjunction with other measures.

### 5.1. Essential PPE for Formwork Operations

*   **Head Protection:**
    *   **Hard Hat (Helmet):** Mandatory to protect against falling objects and impact.
*   **Eye Protection:**
    *   **Safety Glasses/Goggles:** To protect against dust, debris, and flying particles.
*   **Hand Protection:**
    *   **Gloves:** Various types depending on the task:
        *   **Leather/Heavy-duty Gloves:** For handling rough materials, timber, or sharp objects.
        *   **Chemical-resistant Gloves:** For handling release agents or cleaning solvents.
*   **Foot Protection:**
    *   **Safety Boots with Steel Toecaps and Puncture-Resistant Soles:** To protect feet from falling objects and sharp objects on the ground.
*   **Body Protection:**
    *   **High-Visibility Clothing (Hi-Vis):** To ensure workers are visible to moving machinery and vehicles.
    *   **Durable Work Clothing:** To protect against abrasions and minor cuts.
*   **Fall Protection:**
    *   **Safety Harness:** Full body harness for working at height.
    *   **Lanyards:** Shock-absorbing lanyards to connect the harness to an anchor point.
*   **Respiratory Protection:**
    *   **Dust Masks/Respirators:** When working with dusty materials (e.g., cutting timber) or in environments with airborne particles.
*   **Hearing Protection:**
    *   **Earplugs/Earmuffs:** When using noisy power tools.

### 5.2. Proper Use and Maintenance of PPE

*   **Fit and Comfort:** PPE must fit correctly to be effective and comfortable.
*   **Inspection:** Regularly inspect PPE for damage, wear, or defects. Discard and replace damaged PPE.
*   **Maintenance:** Clean and maintain PPE according to manufacturer instructions.
*   **Training:** Ensure workers are trained on the correct use, limitations, and maintenance of their PPE.

## 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

### 6.1. Multiple Choice Questions

1.  Which of the following is **NOT** a common cause of structural failure in formwork?
    a) Overloading
    b) Inadequate bracing
    c) Proper striking of formwork
    d) Premature removal of shores

    **Answer:** c) Proper striking of formwork

2.  The hierarchy of controls, from most effective to least effective, is:
    a) Elimination, Substitution, Engineering Controls, Administrative Controls, PPE
    b) PPE, Administrative Controls, Engineering Controls, Substitution, Elimination
    c) Engineering Controls, Elimination, Substitution, Administrative Controls, PPE
    d) Substitution, Elimination, PPE, Administrative Controls, Engineering Controls

    **Answer:** a) Elimination, Substitution, Engineering Controls, Administrative Controls, PPE

3.  Which type of PPE is crucial for protecting against falling objects from above?
    a) Safety glasses
    b) Gloves
    c) Hard hat
    d) Safety footwear

    **Answer:** c) Hard hat

### 6.2. Scenario-Based Questions

**Scenario 1:** A crew is erecting tall vertical formwork for a concrete column. They have installed diagonal bracing on one side but not the other. The wind speed is increasing.

*   **Question:** What is the primary hazard present in this scenario, and what specific control measure is missing or inadequate?
    **Answer:** The primary hazard is **structural instability leading to collapse** due to lateral forces (wind). The missing or inadequate control measure is **adequate lateral bracing on all sides** of the tall formwork.

**Scenario 2:** Workers are dismantling floor formwork on the third level of a building. There are no guardrails around the perimeter of the formwork deck, and some debris has accumulated.

*   **Question:** Identify two significant hazards in this scenario and suggest appropriate control measures for each.
    **Answer:**
    *   **Hazard 1:** Falls from height due to lack of edge protection.
        *   **Control Measure:** Install guardrails (top rail, mid-rail, toe board) around the perimeter of the deck or use personal fall arrest systems (harnesses and lanyards).
    *   **Hazard 2:** Struck-by hazard from falling debris.
        *   **Control Measure:** Implement good housekeeping practices to clear the debris, use toe boards on guardrails to prevent small items from falling, and/or establish exclusion zones below the work area.

### 6.3. Short Answer Questions

1.  Explain the purpose of a risk assessment in formwork engineering.
    **Answer:** A risk assessment identifies potential hazards associated with formwork operations, analyzes the likelihood and severity of harm they can cause, and helps in determining and implementing appropriate control measures to eliminate or reduce those risks, thereby ensuring a safe working environment.

2.  What are two examples of engineering controls used in formwork safety?
    **Answer:**
    *   Installation of guardrails and toe boards on formwork decks.
    *   Use of mechanical lifting devices (e.g., cranes, forklifts) to handle heavy formwork components.

## Important Points to Remember:

*   **Safety is Proactive, Not Reactive:** Always anticipate hazards and implement controls *before* work begins.
*   **Hierarchy of Controls:** Prioritize elimination and substitution over PPE.
*   **Competence is Key:** Ensure all personnel are adequately trained and supervised.
*   **Communication is Vital:** Regular toolbox talks and open reporting channels are essential.
*   **Formwork is a System:** Treat formwork as a complete structural system, considering all components and their interdependencies.
*   **Never Assume:** Always verify that formwork is correctly erected, braced, and stable before loading it.
*   **Dynamic Risk Assessment:** Be prepared to re-assess risks and adapt control measures if conditions change.
*   **Manufacturer's Instructions are Paramount:** For proprietary formwork systems, always follow the manufacturer's guidelines.
*   **Proper Striking:** Never remove formwork or shores before the concrete has achieved sufficient strength as confirmed by the engineer.
