### 1. Introduction to Loads on Formwork

Formwork is a temporary structure that supports freshly poured concrete until it achieves sufficient strength to be self-supporting. Therefore, formwork must be designed to safely resist all loads that it will encounter during its service life.

**Key Concept:** The primary objective of formwork design is to ensure the stability and integrity of the structure throughout the concreting process, preventing premature failure or collapse.

---

### 2. Classification of Loads on Formwork

Loads acting on formwork can be broadly categorized as follows:

*   **Vertical Loads:** Forces acting downwards.
    *   **Dead Loads:** Permanent loads that are present throughout the life of the structure.
    *   **Live Loads:** Temporary loads that are applied during the construction phase.
*   **Lateral Loads:** Forces acting horizontally.

---

### 3. Vertical Loads

#### 3.1. Dead Loads

*   **Weight of Fresh Concrete:** This is the most significant dead load. It is calculated by multiplying the volume of concrete by its density.
    *   **Density of Concrete:** Typically ranges from 23.5 kN/m³ to 25 kN/m³ for normal-weight concrete. Reinforced concrete might have a slightly higher density.
*   **Weight of Formwork:** The self-weight of the formwork materials (plywood, timber, steel, etc.).
*   **Weight of Reinforcement:** The weight of the steel reinforcing bars embedded within the concrete.

**Calculation Example:**
Consider a concrete slab of dimensions 10m x 5m x 0.2m. The density of concrete is 24 kN/m³.
*   **Volume of Concrete:** 10m x 5m x 0.2m = 10 m³
*   **Weight of Fresh Concrete:** 10 m³ x 24 kN/m³ = 240 kN

**Important Point:** The density of concrete should be taken from relevant codes or project specifications.

#### 3.2. Live Loads

These are transient loads that occur during the construction process.

*   **Weight of Workers:** Men working on the formwork.
*   **Weight of Equipment:** Tools, vibrators, wheelbarrows, concrete buckets, etc.
*   **Impact Loads:** Forces due to dropping materials or equipment onto the formwork.
*   **Momentum of Vibrating Equipment:** The forces generated by concrete vibrators.
*   **Construction Loads:** Any other temporary loads imposed during the construction process.

**Design Consideration:** Live loads are often assumed to be uniformly distributed over a certain area or applied as concentrated loads at specific points. Codes often provide minimum live load requirements for formwork.

---

### 4. Concrete Pressure (Lateral Load)

This is the **most critical load** in formwork design for vertical elements like walls and columns. Fresh concrete exerts hydrostatic pressure on the formwork.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Hydrostatic Pressure:** The pressure exerted by a fluid at rest, proportional to the depth of the fluid.
*   **Rate of Pouring:** How quickly the concrete is placed. A faster pour rate leads to higher pressure as the concrete below does not have time to gain significant strength.
*   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures can lead to faster setting times, reducing the duration of maximum pressure. Lower temperatures prolong the plastic state and increase pressure duration.
*   **Vibration:** Excessive vibration can cause segregation and increase pressure.
*   **Retarders:** Admixtures that slow down concrete setting will increase the duration of maximum pressure.

**Pressure Distribution:**
The pressure exerted by fresh concrete on formwork is not uniform. It typically varies with depth:

*   **At the top:** Pressure is minimal.
*   **At some depth:** Pressure reaches a maximum.
*   **At the bottom:** Pressure might decrease slightly due to the concrete gaining some strength or the lower layers being supported by the base.

**Simplified Pressure Calculation:**
A common simplified approach is to treat concrete pressure as hydrostatic pressure with modifications:

*   **Maximum Pressure (P_max):** Can be estimated using formulas that account for the rate of pour, temperature, and concrete density.
    *   A common simplified formula for the **maximum pressure at the bottom of a lift** is often related to the height of the pour (h) and the density of concrete (ρg): $P = \rho g h$ (hydrostatic pressure).
    *   However, real-world pressures are often modified by factors related to the rate of pour and temperature. Codes like ACI 347 (Guide to Shoring and Reshoring of Concrete Buildings) provide more detailed guidance.
    *   **ACI 347 Recommended Pressure Formula:**
        $P = w (c + v \times R \times T)$
        Where:
        *   $P$ = maximum lateral pressure per unit area
        *   $w$ = unit weight of concrete (e.g., 24 kN/m³)
        *   $c$ = internal cohesion of concrete (typically taken as 0 or a small value for design purposes, representing the residual strength gain in the bottom layers)
        *   $v$ = a factor related to the rate of rise of concrete (typically 1.5 or higher for faster pours)
        *   $R$ = rate of rise of concrete (m/hr)
        *   $T$ = temperature of concrete in the form (degrees Celsius). The actual temperature of the concrete in the form is considered.

**Practical Considerations for Concrete Pressure:**

*   **Walls and Columns:** Pressure is applied to the face of the formwork.
*   **Slabs:** Pressure is applied downwards on the supporting formwork (decking) and upwards on the soffit formwork.
*   **Beam Sides:** Pressure acts on the vertical sides of the beam formwork.

**Example Calculation (Simplified for illustration):**
Consider a concrete wall pour with the following:
*   Unit weight of concrete (w) = 24 kN/m³
*   Rate of rise (R) = 1.5 m/hr
*   Concrete temperature (T) = 20°C
*   Assume $c=0$ and $v=1.5$ for this simplified example.

Using a simplified approach for maximum pressure (recognizing this is a simplification and detailed codes should be consulted):
*   If the pour height is 3m, the hydrostatic pressure at the bottom would be roughly $24 \text{ kN/m³} \times 3 \text{ m} = 72 \text{ kN/m²}$.
*   Using the ACI-like formula (highly simplified): $P = 24 \times (0 + 1.5 \times 1.5 \times 20) = 24 \times (45) = 1080 \text{ kN/m²}$ (This is an exaggerated result, highlighting the need for proper code application. The formula is usually for pressure at the bottom of the pour, not directly multiplied by height this way).

**Correct application of a simplified ACI-like approach for maximum pressure:**
Let's consider the pressure on the formwork of a wall with a pour rate of 1.5 m/hr and concrete temperature of 20°C. The height of the pour (h) is 3m.
The maximum pressure *at the bottom of the pour* is generally considered. The actual pressure distribution is complex, but for design purposes, a maximum pressure value is used.

A more refined calculation using ACI factors would involve determining the pressure at different depths. For a pour height of 3m:
*   At the very top (depth=0), pressure is close to 0.
*   At the bottom (depth=3m), the pressure is the maximum.
    *   Consider the rate of rise (R=1.5 m/hr). This implies the concrete is setting as it rises.
    *   If we use a simplified approach where pressure is limited by a maximum value based on the rate of pour and temperature, it's often much higher than pure hydrostatic pressure.
    *   Let's use a common rule of thumb for illustrative purposes: Maximum pressure can be approximated by $P_{max} \approx 2.5 \times \text{hydrostatic pressure at depth h}$.
    *   Hydrostatic pressure at 3m depth = $24 \text{ kN/m³} \times 3 \text{ m} = 72 \text{ kN/m²}$.
    *   Approximate maximum pressure = $2.5 \times 72 \text{ kN/m²} = 180 \text{ kN/m²}$.

**Important Point:** The actual pressure exerted by concrete depends on many factors and should always be calculated using appropriate codes (e.g., ACI 347, BS EN 13781).

### 5. Lateral Loads

These are horizontal forces acting on the formwork.

#### 5.1. Wind Loads

*   **Impact:** Significant on tall structures, unfenced formwork, or in exposed locations.
*   **Calculation:** Determined by wind speed, exposure category, and the surface area of the formwork exposed to wind.
*   **Design:** Formwork needs to be adequately braced to resist wind forces.

#### 5.2. Bracing Forces

*   **Purpose:** To stabilize formwork against lateral loads and to maintain its shape and alignment.
*   **Calculation:** Forces in braces are determined by analyzing the formwork as a structural system. They are often calculated based on the lateral loads applied and the geometry of the bracing.
*   **Types:** Diagonal braces, cross-braces, wall braces.

#### 5.3. Accidental Loads

*   **Impact:** Due to misoperation, collisions, or other unforeseen events.
*   **Design:** While not always explicitly calculated, the robustness of the formwork system should consider the possibility of such loads.

---

### 6. Load Combinations

Formwork must be designed to resist the most critical combination of loads that are likely to occur simultaneously.

**Common Load Combinations:**

*   **Vertical Loads:** Dead Load (concrete, formwork, reinforcement) + Live Load (workers, equipment).
*   **Vertical Loads + Lateral Loads:** Dead Load + Live Load + Wind Load + Bracing Forces.
*   **Concrete Pressure:** Pressure from fresh concrete is a dominant lateral load and often considered in combination with dead loads on supporting elements.

**Design Codes:** Codes provide specific load factors to be applied to each load type when considering combinations, ensuring an adequate margin of safety.

---

### 7. Load Considerations in Formwork Component Design

Understanding the loads allows for the selection and sizing of formwork components:

*   **Form Liners (Plywood, Steel Panels):** Must be strong enough to resist the lateral pressure of concrete without excessive deflection.
*   **Shoring and Propping:** Must carry the vertical loads (dead and live) and any overturning moments.
*   **Walers and Studs (Vertical Formwork):** Must resist the concrete pressure and transfer it to the ties or braces.
*   **Form Ties:** Must resist the tensile forces created by the concrete pressure trying to push the forms apart.
*   **Braces:** Must resist buckling and carry lateral forces.

### 8. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Calculate the dead load of fresh concrete for a beam with dimensions 5m length, 0.3m width, and 0.5m depth. Assume the density of concrete is 24 kN/m³.

**Answer 1:**
*   Volume of concrete = 5m x 0.3m x 0.5m = 0.75 m³
*   Dead load of concrete = 0.75 m³ x 24 kN/m³ = 18 kN

**Question 2:**
A concrete wall formwork is subjected to a maximum lateral pressure of 120 kN/m². If the formwork panel has a height of 2.5m and is supported by vertical studs spaced at 0.6m centers, what is the total lateral force acting on a single stud?

**Answer 2:**
*   Area of the formwork panel supported by one stud = height x stud spacing = 2.5m x 0.6m = 1.5 m²
*   Total lateral force on the stud = pressure x area = 120 kN/m² x 1.5 m² = 180 kN

**Question 3:**
Discuss the key factors that influence the lateral pressure exerted by fresh concrete on formwork.

**Answer 3:**
The key factors are:
*   **Height of the pour (depth):** Pressure increases with depth (hydrostatic principle).
*   **Rate of pouring:** Faster pours lead to higher pressures as the concrete has less time to gain strength.
*   **Temperature of the concrete:** Lower temperatures prolong the plastic state, increasing pressure duration and potentially magnitude.
*   **Use of admixtures:** Retarders slow down setting, increasing pressure duration.
*   **Vibration:** Excessive vibration can increase pressure.
*   **Cohesion and strength gain of concrete:** As concrete sets, its internal strength increases, reducing pressure.

---

### 9. Important Points to Remember

*   **Safety First:** Formwork design is critical for the safety of workers. Never underestimate the loads.
*   **Code Compliance:** Always refer to and follow relevant building codes and standards (e.g., ACI 347, BS EN 13781) for load calculations and design procedures.
*   **Dynamic Nature of Loads:** Recognize that loads on formwork can change throughout the concreting process (e.g., fresh concrete pressure vs. hardened concrete weight).
*   **Comprehensive Analysis:** Consider all potential loads, including dead, live, lateral, and impact loads.
*   **Load Combinations:** Design for the worst-case scenario by combining loads appropriately.
*   **Deflection:** Formwork components should not deflect excessively under load, as this can affect the shape and dimensions of the finished concrete element.
*   **Material Properties:** Understand the strength and stiffness properties of the formwork materials being used.
