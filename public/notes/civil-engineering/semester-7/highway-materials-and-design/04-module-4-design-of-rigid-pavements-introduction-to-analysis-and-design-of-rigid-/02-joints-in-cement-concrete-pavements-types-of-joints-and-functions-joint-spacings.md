# HIGHWAY MATERIALS AND DESIGN - Module 4: Design of Rigid Pavements

## Topic: Joints in Cement Concrete Pavements

### 1. Introduction to Joints in Cement Concrete Pavements

Cement concrete pavements (CCPs) are susceptible to cracking due to various factors like shrinkage during setting, thermal expansion and contraction, and external loading. Joints are strategically incorporated into concrete pavements to:

*   **Control Cracking:** Create weakened planes to induce cracking at predetermined locations, preventing random and unsightly cracking.
*   **Accommodate Movement:** Allow for expansion and contraction due to temperature changes and moisture variations.
*   **Facilitate Construction:** Divide large pavement slabs into manageable sections for construction.
*   **Ensure Load Transfer:** Provide mechanisms for transferring loads between adjacent slabs to prevent excessive differential settlement.

### 2. Types of Joints and Their Functions

There are primarily three types of joints used in cement concrete pavements:

#### 2.1. Contraction Joints (Dummy Joints)

*   **Description:** These are transverse grooves saw-cut or formed into the pavement slab, extending to a depth of approximately one-fourth to one-third of the slab thickness. They are designed to weaken the slab at specific locations.
*   **Function:**
    *   **Controlled Cracking:** They create a plane of weakness, ensuring that any cracking that occurs due to drying shrinkage and thermal contraction happens at the joint.
    *   **Reduced Restraint:** By allowing the slab to crack at the joint, they reduce the restraint forces that could otherwise build up and cause random cracking.
*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Depth:** Typically 1/4 to 1/3 of the slab thickness.
    *   **Width:** 3 mm to 10 mm.
    *   **Reinforcement:** Often filled with a flexible material (e.g., sealant) to prevent infiltration of incompressible materials. Dowel bars or tie bars are generally *not* used in simple contraction joints unless specifically designed for load transfer or to hold slabs together under specific conditions.

#### 2.2. Expansion Joints

*   **Description:** These are full-depth joints that extend through the entire slab thickness. They are designed to completely separate adjacent slabs and accommodate significant expansion.
*   **Function:**
    *   **Accommodate Expansion:** To prevent compressive stresses that could lead to pavement buckling (blow-ups) when the concrete expands due to temperature increases.
    *   **Prevent Spalling:** By providing a gap, they prevent adjacent slabs from pressing against each other.
*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Depth:** Full slab thickness.
    *   **Width:** Typically 10 mm to 25 mm.
    *   **Filler Material:** Filled with a compressible material like asphalt-impregnated fiberboard, cork, rubber, or preformed plastic to prevent infiltration and allow for expansion.
    *   **Dowel Bars:** Essential for load transfer across the joint and must be smooth and coated to allow for free longitudinal movement of the slabs.

#### 2.3. Construction Joints (Longitudinal Joints)

*   **Description:** These joints are formed when construction is stopped for the day or when paving lanes are constructed separately. They can be transverse or longitudinal.
*   **Function:**
    *   **Construction Convenience:** To divide the pavement into sections that can be constructed in manageable phases.
    *   **Load Transfer & Restraint:** Depending on the design, they may incorporate tie bars (to hold slabs together) and/or dowel bars (for load transfer).
*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Type:** Can be either "keyed" (interlocking sections) or "doweled/tied."
    *   **Tie Bars:** Used in longitudinal construction joints to hold the adjacent slabs tightly together, preventing opening due to warping stresses and traffic.
    *   **Dowel Bars:** Can be used in construction joints to provide load transfer, especially where significant traffic is expected.

#### 2.4. Tie Bars

*   **Description:** Plain round steel bars used across longitudinal joints (and sometimes transverse contraction joints) to keep the slabs tied together. They have no special coating to ensure bonding with the concrete on both sides.
*   **Function:**
    *   **Prevent Joint Opening:** Resist the opening of longitudinal joints caused by differential warping and curling stresses due to temperature and moisture gradients.
    *   **Maintain Slab Integrity:** Keep the slabs acting as a continuous unit, reducing the risk of independent movement and cracking.
*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Material:** Plain round steel bars.
    *   **Placement:** Embedded across the joint.
    *   **Bonding:** Relies on bond with the concrete.

#### 2.5. Dowel Bars

*   **Description:** Smooth, round, high-strength steel bars used across transverse joints (contraction and expansion) to facilitate load transfer between adjacent slabs.
*   **Function:**
    *   **Load Transfer:** Allow traffic loads to be shared by adjacent slabs, reducing the stress at the free edge of a slab and preventing excessive deflection.
    *   **Prevent Differential Settlement:** Minimize the vertical displacement between adjacent slabs under load.
*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Material:** Smooth, round, high-strength steel bars.
    *   **Coating:** Must be coated with a bond-breaking material (e.g., epoxy, grease, or a plastic sleeve) on one side to allow for longitudinal movement of the slab.
    *   **Placement:** Perpendicular to the joint.

### 3. Joint Spacings

The spacing of joints in rigid pavements is a critical design parameter that depends on several factors, including:

*   **Concrete Properties:** Tensile strength, modulus of rupture, coefficient of thermal expansion.
*   **Environmental Conditions:** Temperature variations, humidity.
*   **Traffic Loading:** Magnitude and frequency of wheel loads.
*   **Slab Thickness:** Thicker slabs generally require wider joint spacing.
*   **Reinforcement:** Presence and amount of steel reinforcement.

**General Guidelines for Joint Spacing (IRC Recommendations - Indian Roads Congress):**

*   **Contraction Joints (Transverse):**
    *   For plain concrete pavements: Typically **4.5 meters to 6.0 meters**.
    *   For reinforced concrete pavements: Spacing is often determined by reinforcement requirements and can be larger, but still controlled to prevent random cracking.
*   **Expansion Joints (Transverse):**
    *   Used at locations where significant expansion is anticipated, such as:
        *   Abutments of bridges.
        *   At intersections.
        *   Along the edges of rigid shoulders or rigid median barriers.
        *   At intervals of **20 to 50 meters** on continuously reinforced concrete pavements (where they are sometimes called "expansion joints" to accommodate significant expansion, although contraction joints are more common). For non-reinforced or lightly reinforced pavements, expansion joints might be placed at closer intervals, e.g., **25 to 50 meters**, to manage thermal expansion.
*   **Construction Joints (Longitudinal):**
    *   Typically placed at the edge of traffic lanes or at the centerline of divided highways.
    *   Spacing is dictated by the width of pavement lanes being constructed.

**Important Note:** The IRC provides specific guidelines in its codes (e.g., IRC: 15). These are general ranges, and detailed design should always refer to the latest IRC specifications.

### 4. Design of Tie Bars using IRC Method

Tie bars are used to hold adjacent slabs together across longitudinal joints. Their design involves determining the required diameter, spacing, and embedment length.

**IRC Method for Tie Bar Design (Based on IRC: 15):**

The primary function of tie bars is to resist the opening forces due to warping and curling. The design is typically based on resisting the tension developed at the joint due to these effects.

**1. Calculate Maximum Tensile Force (T):**

The maximum tensile force that needs to be resisted by the tie bars is often estimated based on the forces causing joint opening. A simplified approach considers the force required to overcome the frictional resistance and warping stresses.

IRC: 15 suggests considering the forces arising from warping and potential joint opening. A common approach involves calculating the force needed to pull out the tie bar from the concrete, which is related to the bond strength.

**2. Determine Required Steel Area (A_s):**

The total required steel area is calculated to provide sufficient strength to resist the tensile forces.

$$A_s = \frac{\text{Tensile Force}}{\text{Allowable Tensile Stress in Steel}}$$

*   **Allowable Tensile Stress in Steel:** This is typically taken as a fraction of the yield strength of the steel, considering safety factors. For mild steel (Fe 415), yield strength ($f_y$) is 415 N/mm². A common allowable stress is $f_y/2$ or $2/3 f_y$ depending on the code and application.

**3. Select Tie Bar Diameter (d_t):**

Based on the required steel area, an appropriate tie bar diameter is selected (e.g., 10 mm, 12 mm, 16 mm).

**4. Calculate Spacing of Tie Bars (s_t):**

The spacing of tie bars is determined by dividing the total required steel area by the area of a single tie bar at that spacing.

$$s_t = \frac{\text{Area of one tie bar} \times \text{Allowable tensile stress}}{\text{Tensile force per unit width of joint}}$$

Alternatively, and more practically, the spacing is determined by ensuring that the total strength of the tie bars within a certain length of the joint is sufficient.

**IRC Recommended Practice:**

*   **Diameter:** Typically 10 mm or 12 mm deformed bars.
*   **Spacing:** Usually **1 to 1.5 meters**.
*   **Length of Tie Bar:** The total length of the tie bar should be sufficient to provide adequate embedment on both sides of the joint.
    *   **Embedment Length (L_d):** The required embedment length into the concrete on each side of the joint is crucial for effective performance. This is calculated based on the bond strength between the steel and concrete.
    *   IRC: 15 specifies that the embedment length should be sufficient to develop the full strength of the bar.
        *   $L_d = \frac{\phi \times f_y}{k \times \tau_{bd}}$
            Where:
            *   $\phi$ = Nominal diameter of the bar (mm)
            *   $f_y$ = Characteristic yield strength of the steel (N/mm²)
            *   $\tau_{bd}$ = Design bond stress (N/mm²) for plain bars or deformed bars as per relevant IS codes (e.g., IS 456). For deformed bars, $\tau_{bd}$ can be increased by 60%.
            *   $k$ = A factor to account for the presence of adjacent bars and stress conditions.
    *   **Total Length of Tie Bar:** Typically, embedment length into each slab + the width of the joint. A common rule of thumb is embedment length of 25 to 30 times the bar diameter on each side.
        *   Total Length = $2 \times L_d + \text{Joint Width}$
        *   **Practical Example:** For a 12 mm bar and considering an embedment of 30 times the diameter on each side:
            *   $L_d = 30 \times 12 = 360$ mm
            *   Total Length = $2 \times 360$ mm = 720 mm (or 0.72 meters)
            *   If the joint width is say 10 mm, the tie bar length would be approximately 730 mm.

**Important Considerations for Tie Bars:**

*   Tie bars are typically placed at the mid-depth of the slab.
*   They should not be deformed bars unless explicitly permitted, as deformation can hinder movement in expansion joints. However, for longitudinal joints, deformed bars are preferred for better grip. IRC:15 generally recommends deformed bars for tie bars in longitudinal joints.
*   The number of tie bars per joint is determined by the width of the pavement.

### 5. Design of Dowel Bars using IRC Method

Dowel bars are used across transverse joints to facilitate load transfer. Their design involves selecting the diameter, length, and spacing.

**IRC Method for Dowel Bar Design (Based on IRC: 15):**

The design of dowel bars is primarily to allow for the free movement of slabs while transferring wheel loads effectively. The design aims to prevent excessive deflection and stress at the joint.

**1. Determine Dowel Bar Diameter (d_d):**

The diameter of the dowel bar is typically determined based on the slab thickness and expected traffic loads.

*   **IRC Recommendations:**
    *   **For roads carrying moderate to heavy traffic:** 25 mm to 38 mm diameter steel bars.
    *   The diameter of the dowel bar is often chosen such that its diameter is approximately **1/30 to 1/40 of the slab thickness**.
    *   **Example:** For a 280 mm thick slab, the dowel diameter could be around $280/30 \approx 9.3$ mm to $280/40 \approx 7$ mm. This suggests that the slab thickness influences the dowel size, but the typical range for heavy traffic is 25-38mm.

**2. Determine Dowel Bar Length (L_d_dowel):**

The length of the dowel bar must be sufficient to provide effective load transfer and allow for longitudinal movement.

*   **IRC Recommendations:**
    *   The total length of the dowel bar is usually **2 times the embedment length into the concrete** plus the width of the joint.
    *   **Embedment Length (L_e):** This is the length of the dowel bar that needs to be embedded in the concrete to effectively transfer load without excessive deflection or distress.
        *   IRC: 15 provides guidelines for calculating the required embedment length. It's crucial to ensure that the dowel bar can withstand the shear and bending stresses induced by the wheel loads.
        *   A common approach is to ensure that the bending stress in the dowel bar at the edge of the loaded slab does not exceed a specified limit, and that the deflection is within acceptable limits.
        *   **Empirical Rule/IRC Guidance:** A typical embedment length is often taken as **15 to 20 times the diameter of the dowel bar**.
            *   **Example:** For a 32 mm diameter dowel bar:
                *   Embedment Length = $15 \times 32$ mm = 480 mm
                *   Embedment Length = $20 \times 32$ mm = 640 mm
                *   Total Length = $2 \times 480$ mm to $2 \times 640$ mm = 960 mm to 1280 mm.
                *   The actual length would also include the joint width. A common practice is to use lengths like 300 mm into each slab for a total of 600 mm, plus the joint width. However, for heavier loads and thicker slabs, longer dowels are required. IRC:15 provides more detailed calculations.

**3. Determine Dowel Bar Spacing (s_d):**

Dowel bars are placed across the width of the slab at a specified spacing.

*   **IRC Recommendations:**
    *   Typically **200 mm to 300 mm**.
    *   The spacing is chosen to ensure adequate load transfer across the entire width of the slab.

**4. Coating of Dowel Bars:**

*   Dowel bars must be coated with a bond-breaking material (e.g., epoxy, grease, or fitted with a plastic sleeve) on one end (or for a certain length) to allow for free longitudinal movement of the slab. This coated portion extends into one of the adjacent slabs.
*   The uncoated portion of the dowel bar must be able to bond with the concrete in the other slab to ensure load transfer.

**Important Considerations for Dowel Bars:**

*   **Alignment:** Dowel bars must be placed perfectly horizontal and parallel to the pavement surface and the edge of the slab. Misalignment can lead to spalling or prevent movement.
*   **Coating:** Ensuring proper coating is critical to prevent restraint of movement.
*   **Placement:** Dowel bars are typically placed at mid-depth of the slab.
*   **Strength:** The dowel bars must be made of steel with adequate yield strength and modulus of elasticity to withstand bending and shear stresses.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
What is the primary function of contraction joints in cement concrete pavements?

**Answer 1:**
The primary function of contraction joints is to control cracking by creating weakened planes where shrinkage and thermal contraction can cause the concrete to crack at predetermined locations, rather than randomly across the slab.

**Question 2:**
Differentiate between tie bars and dowel bars in terms of their material, function, and placement.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Tie Bars:**
    *   **Material:** Plain or deformed round steel bars.
    *   **Function:** To hold adjacent slabs together, preventing opening of longitudinal joints due to warping and curling.
    *   **Placement:** Across longitudinal joints, embedded and bonded to both slabs.
*   **Dowel Bars:**
    *   **Material:** Smooth, round, high-strength steel bars.
    *   **Function:** To transfer loads between adjacent slabs across transverse joints, preventing differential settlement.
    *   **Placement:** Across transverse joints, embedded partially into each slab, with one side coated to allow movement.

**Question 3:**
For a 280 mm thick concrete slab carrying heavy traffic, what is a typical diameter for dowel bars according to IRC guidelines? Calculate the approximate total length of a dowel bar if the embedment into each slab is 18 times its diameter and the joint width is 10 mm.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Typical Dowel Bar Diameter:** A typical diameter for dowel bars in a 280 mm thick slab carrying heavy traffic would be between 25 mm and 38 mm. Let's assume we choose a 32 mm diameter bar.
*   **Calculation of Total Length:**
    *   Diameter ($d_d$) = 32 mm
    *   Embedment into each slab ($L_e$) = $18 \times d_d = 18 \times 32$ mm = 576 mm
    *   Joint Width = 10 mm
    *   Total Length = $2 \times L_e + \text{Joint Width}$
    *   Total Length = $2 \times 576$ mm + 10 mm = 1152 mm + 10 mm = 1162 mm (approximately 1.16 meters).

**Question 4:**
If you are designing tie bars for a longitudinal joint and have selected 12 mm diameter bars with an allowable tensile stress of 200 N/mm², and the required tensile force per meter width of the joint is 12000 N. Calculate the required spacing of the tie bars.

**Answer 4:**
*   Diameter of tie bar ($d_t$) = 12 mm
*   Allowable tensile stress in steel ($f_{sy}$) = 200 N/mm²
*   Tensile force per meter width of joint = 12000 N
*   Area of one tie bar = $\frac{\pi}{4} \times d_t^2 = \frac{\pi}{4} \times (12 \text{ mm})^2 = \frac{\pi}{4} \times 144 \text{ mm}^2 \approx 113.1 \text{ mm}^2$
*   Total allowable tensile strength of one tie bar = Area $\times$ Allowable stress
    *   Total allowable strength = $113.1 \text{ mm}^2 \times 200 \text{ N/mm}^2 = 22620 \text{ N}$
*   Let the spacing of tie bars be $s_t$.
*   The force resisted by tie bars within a spacing $s_t$ and a unit width of joint is the tensile force per meter width.
*   Alternatively, consider the force per unit length of the joint. If the tie bars are spaced at $s_t$, then for a length of $s_t$ along the joint, there is one tie bar.
*   The total load to be carried by the tie bars in a length $s_t$ of the joint is the tensile force per meter width multiplied by the spacing $s_t$.
*   Total force within spacing $s_t$ = $12000 \text{ N/m} \times s_t$
*   This force must be resisted by the tie bar.
*   Therefore, $12000 \text{ N/m} \times s_t = 22620 \text{ N}$
*   $s_t = \frac{22620 \text{ N}}{12000 \text{ N/m}} \approx 1.885$ meters.

    *   **IRC Recommendation Check:** IRC typically recommends spacing of 1 to 1.5 meters. The calculated spacing of 1.885 meters is on the higher side. This implies that either a smaller spacing might be preferred for safety, or the initial force estimation might be conservative, or a different tie bar diameter might be more suitable for this load. If we assume a spacing of 1.5 meters, the required force per tie bar would be $12000 \text{ N/m} \times 1.5 \text{ m} = 18000 \text{ N}$. The 12 mm bar can provide 22620 N, which is adequate.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Purpose of Joints:** Control cracking, accommodate movement, facilitate construction, and ensure load transfer.
*   **Types of Joints:** Contraction, Expansion, Construction.
*   **Tie Bars vs. Dowel Bars:** Tie bars tie slabs together (longitudinal joints), dowel bars transfer load across joints (transverse joints).
*   **Tie Bar Material:** Deformed bars preferred for bond.
*   **Dowel Bar Material:** Smooth bars coated on one side for free movement.
*   **IRC Guidelines:** Always refer to the latest IRC codes (e.g., IRC:15) for specific design parameters and methods.
*   **Embedment Length:** Crucial for both tie and dowel bars to develop sufficient bond strength and structural integrity.
*   **Joint Spacing:** Directly related to concrete properties, environment, and traffic.
