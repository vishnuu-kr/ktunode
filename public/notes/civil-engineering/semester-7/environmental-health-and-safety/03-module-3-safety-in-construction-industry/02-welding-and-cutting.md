# Environmental Health and Safety: Module 3 - Safety in the Construction Industry

## Topic: Welding and Cutting

### 1. Introduction to Welding and Cutting in Construction

Welding and cutting are essential processes in the construction industry, used for joining and shaping metal components. However, these activities involve inherent hazards that, if not properly managed, can lead to severe injuries or fatalities. This module focuses on understanding and mitigating these risks to ensure a safe working environment.

### 2. Hazards Associated with Welding and Cutting

Welding and cutting operations expose workers to a variety of hazards, which can be broadly categorized as:

*   **Fire and Explosion Hazards:**
    *   **Flammable Materials:** The presence of combustible materials (wood, paper, solvents, fuels, rags) near welding/cutting sparks or hot surfaces.
    *   **Ignitable Atmospheres:** Welding/cutting in confined spaces or areas where flammable gases or vapors might be present.
    *   **Hot Slag and Sparks:** Molten metal fragments ejected during the process can ignite nearby combustibles.
    *   **Oxygen Enrichment:** Improper use of oxygen cylinders can lead to an oxygen-enriched atmosphere, significantly increasing the flammability of materials.

*   **Health Hazards:**
    *   **Fumes and Gases:**
        *   **Metal Fumes:** Generated from the vaporization and condensation of base metals, filler metals, and coatings. Examples include zinc oxide fumes (metal fume fever), cadmium fumes, and iron oxide fumes.
        *   **Shielding Gases:** Inert gases like Argon and Helium, while non-toxic, can displace oxygen in confined spaces leading to asphyxiation.
        *   **Process Gases:** Gases produced from flux-cored wires (e.g., CO2, CO) or from the decomposition of coatings (e.g., phosgene from chlorinated solvents).
        *   **Ozone:** Produced by electric arc welding, which can irritate the respiratory system.
    *   **Radiation:**
        *   **Infrared (IR) Radiation:** Can cause eye injury (e.g., "welder's flash" or arc eye) and skin burns.
        *   **Ultraviolet (UV) Radiation:** The most significant radiation hazard, causing severe eye irritation (arc eye) and skin burns (similar to sunburn). UV radiation is also a known carcinogen.
        *   **Visible Light:** Intense visible light can cause temporary blindness or discomfort.
    *   **Noise:**
        *   **Grinding and Cutting:** High levels of noise from grinding, cutting, and hammering operations can lead to temporary or permanent hearing loss.
    *   **Electrical Hazards:**
        *   **Electric Shock:** From faulty welding equipment, damaged cables, or working in damp conditions.
    *   **Burns:**
        *   **Thermal Burns:** From contact with hot metal, sparks, slag, or the welding arc.
        *   **Chemical Burns:** From contact with corrosive substances used in welding processes.
    *   **Ergonomic Hazards:**
        *   **Repetitive motions, awkward postures, and heavy lifting** can lead to musculoskeletal injuries.

*   **Physical Hazards:**
    *   **Falling Objects:** Working at heights can lead to dropped tools or materials, posing a risk to those below.
    *   **Slips, Trips, and Falls:** Cluttered work areas and uneven surfaces increase the risk.
    *   **Mechanical Hazards:** From moving parts of machinery used in cutting operations (e.g., angle grinders).

### 3. Types of Welding and Cutting Processes and Their Specific Hazards

Understanding the different welding and cutting processes is crucial for implementing appropriate safety measures.

*   **Arc Welding:**
    *   **Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW/Stick Welding):** Uses a coated electrode.
        *   *Hazards:* High fume generation, UV/IR radiation, electric shock, slag chipping hazards.
    *   **Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW/MIG Welding):** Uses a consumable wire electrode and a shielding gas.
        *   *Hazards:* Moderate fume generation (depending on wire coating), UV/IR radiation, electric shock, potential for gas displacement in confined spaces.
    *   **Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW/TIG Welding):** Uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and a shielding gas.
        *   *Hazards:* Lower fume generation than MIG, but significant UV/IR radiation, electric shock, potential for gas displacement. Often requires more precise work.
    *   **Flux-Cored Arc Welding (FCAW):** Uses a tubular electrode with flux.
        *   *Hazards:* High fume generation, UV/IR radiation, electric shock, slag chipping hazards.

*   **Resistance Welding:**
    *   **Spot Welding:** Joins overlapping sheets by localized heat and pressure.
        *   *Hazards:* Electric shock (lower voltages but high currents), minor radiation, mechanical hazards from moving parts.

*   **Oxy-Fuel Welding and Cutting:**
    *   **Oxy-Acetylene Welding/Cutting:** Uses a fuel gas (acetylene) and oxygen.
        *   *Hazards:*
            *   **Fire and Explosion:** High risk due to flammable acetylene and highly oxidizing oxygen. Leaks are a major concern.
            *   **Burns:** From hot metal and sparks.
            *   **Fumes and Gases:** Carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and metal fumes.
            *   **Cylinder Handling:** Risks associated with storing and transporting compressed gas cylinders (explosion, rupture).

*   **Cutting Processes:**
    *   **Oxy-Fuel Cutting:** Similar hazards to oxy-fuel welding, with a focus on creating a kerf (cut).
    *   **Plasma Cutting:** Uses an electric arc and a gas to create a high-temperature plasma jet.
        *   *Hazards:* Intense UV/IR radiation, high noise levels, fumes, electric shock.
    *   **Grinding and Abrasive Cutting:**
        *   *Hazards:* High noise levels, flying debris (eye injury, cuts), electric shock (if powered), dust inhalation (silica, metal particles).

### 4. Safety Precautions and Control Measures

A multi-layered approach to safety is essential, following the hierarchy of controls:

**A. Elimination/Substitution (Less common for core welding/cutting, but consider alternatives):**
*   Can pre-fabricated components reduce the need for on-site welding?
*   Can less hazardous materials or coatings be used?

**B. Engineering Controls:**
*   **Ventilation:**
    *   **Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV):** Hoods, fume extractors, and extraction arms placed close to the source of fume generation to capture and remove contaminants before they reach the worker's breathing zone.
    *   **General Ventilation:** Ensuring adequate air changes in the work area, especially for large spaces.
    *   **Confined Space Ventilation:** For welding/cutting in tanks, vessels, or enclosed areas, forced ventilation is mandatory to maintain oxygen levels and remove hazardous fumes.
*   **Shielding:**
    *   **Welding Screens/Curtains:** To protect nearby workers from UV and IR radiation.
*   **Noise Control:**
    *   **Enclosures for noisy equipment.**
    *   **Using quieter tools where possible.**

**C. Administrative Controls:**
*   **Safe Work Procedures (SWPs):** Developing and enforcing detailed procedures for all welding and cutting tasks.
*   **Permit-to-Work Systems:**
    *   **Hot Work Permits:** Required for any welding, cutting, or grinding operations in areas where flammable materials are present or could become ignited. This involves a thorough hazard assessment, isolation of ignition sources, fire watch, and clearance from a designated authority.
    *   **Confined Space Entry Permits:** For any work inside confined spaces, including welding and cutting.
*   **Training and Competency:** Ensuring all personnel performing welding and cutting are properly trained and competent in their specific processes and associated safety hazards.
*   **Supervision:** Adequate supervision to ensure safety procedures are followed.
*   **Housekeeping:** Maintaining a clean and organized work area to minimize fire hazards and trip risks. Remove all combustibles from the vicinity of hot work.
*   **Fire Watch:** A trained individual responsible for monitoring the area for fires during and after hot work.
*   **Equipment Maintenance:** Regular inspection and maintenance of welding equipment, cables, gas regulators, and hoses to prevent electrical hazards and leaks.
*   **Signage:** Clearly marking areas where welding/cutting is in progress and warning of specific hazards.

**D. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):**
*   **Eye and Face Protection:**
    *   **Welding Helmets/Shields:** Equipped with the correct shade of filter lens for the specific welding process (based on light intensity and UV/IR radiation). Lenses are rated from 2 (least protective) to 14 (most protective).
    *   **Safety Glasses:** Worn under the welding helmet for protection during grinding or chipping.
    *   **Face Shields:** For grinding operations.
*   **Respiratory Protection:**
    *   **Respirators:** Depending on fume levels and specific contaminants, approved respirators (e.g., half-mask respirators with appropriate cartridges, powered air-purifying respirators - PAPRs) may be required. Fit testing and training are essential.
*   **Body Protection:**
    *   **Flame-Resistant Clothing (FRC):** Made from materials like cotton treated with flame retardants, wool, or specialized synthetics. Should cover the body fully, with no cuffs or pockets where sparks can collect. Sleeves should be worn down, and shirts tucked in.
    *   **Leather Aprons, Sleeves, Spats, and Gauntlets:** To protect against sparks, slag, and burns.
*   **Hand Protection:**
    *   **Welding Gloves:** Made of leather, providing insulation and protection from heat, sparks, and sharp edges.
*   **Foot Protection:**
    *   **Leather Safety Boots:** With steel toes and electrical hazard (EH) protection.
*   **Hearing Protection:**
    *   **Earplugs or Earmuffs:** For noisy operations like grinding or chipping.

### 5. Specific Safety Considerations

*   **Cylinder Handling and Storage (Oxy-Fuel):**
    *   Store cylinders upright and secured with chains or straps to prevent falling.
    *   Store oxygen and fuel gas cylinders at least 20 feet apart or separated by a non-combustible barrier at least 8 feet high with a fire resistance rating of at least 30 minutes.
    *   Never store cylinders in poorly ventilated areas.
    *   Use correct regulators and fittings for each gas.
    *   Never use oil or grease on cylinder valves or regulators, as this can cause an explosion.
*   **Confined Space Welding:**
    *   **Atmospheric Testing:** Before entry, test the atmosphere for oxygen content (between 19.5% and 23.5%), flammable gases (below 10% Lower Explosive Limit - LEL), and toxic gases.
    *   **Ventilation:** Continuous forced ventilation is crucial.
    *   **Rescue Plan:** A detailed rescue plan must be in place.
    *   **Attendant:** A dedicated attendant must remain outside the confined space at all times.
*   **Electrical Safety:**
    *   Inspect all welding cables and connections for damage before use.
    *   Ensure proper grounding of the welding machine.
    *   Never use welding equipment in wet or damp conditions without appropriate precautions.
    *   Wear insulating gloves and footwear.
*   **Fire Prevention and Control:**
    *   Clear the work area of all combustibles within a 35-foot radius.
    *   Cover any unavoidable combustibles with fire-resistant blankets or shields.
    *   Have fire extinguishers (appropriate class, e.g., ABC) readily available and ensure personnel are trained in their use.
    *   After hot work is completed, the area should be inspected for at least 30 minutes to ensure no smoldering fires have started.

### 8. Answers to Practice Questions

1.  The primary hazards associated with UV radiation from welding arcs are severe eye irritation (arc eye/welder's flash) and skin burns (similar to sunburn). Chronic exposure can also increase the risk of skin cancer.
2.  A Hot Work Permit is typically required for any welding, cutting, grinding, or other hot operations that could generate sparks, flames, or heat in areas where flammable materials (liquids, gases, dusts, or debris) are present or could be ignited.
3.  Three engineering controls for managing welding fumes are:
    *   Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV) systems (e.g., fume extractors, extraction arms).
    *   General ventilation of the work area.
    *   Enclosures or hoods around the welding process.
4.  A fire watch is crucial because sparks and hot slag can travel considerable distances and ignite hidden combustibles. The fire watch's role is to monitor the area for any signs of fire during the operation and for a specified period afterward, extinguishing any small fires that may occur and sounding the alarm if a larger fire breaks out.
5.  It is crucial to avoid oil or grease on oxygen cylinder valves and regulators because oxygen is a strong oxidizer. When oil or grease comes into contact with high-pressure oxygen, it can ignite spontaneously and cause a violent explosion.
6.  The recommended filter shade for welding operations varies depending on the intensity of the arc. For general arc welding (SMAW, GMAW, FCAW), shades from 10 to 13 are common. For TIG welding or arc welding with lower amperages, shades 9 to 12 might be used. A shade of 2 or 3 is typically used for gas welding or cutting with low-intensity flames.
7.  Before entering a confined space for welding, the key atmospheric tests are:
    *   **Oxygen Content:** Must be between 19.5% and 23.5%.
    *   **Flammable Gases/Vapors:** Must be below 10% of the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL).
    *   **Toxic Gases/Vapors:** Must be below their respective Permissible Exposure Limits (PELs) or Threshold Limit Values (TLVs). Common tests include for carbon monoxide (CO), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and any other known contaminants from the confined space or the welding process.
8.  Three specific types of PPE a welder would wear to protect their body from burns and sparks are:
    *   Flame-Resistant Clothing (FRC) shirt and pants.
    *   Leather welding gloves.
    *   Leather apron or sleeves.
9.  When working in an oxygen-enriched atmosphere, a welder faces an extremely high risk of fire and explosion. Materials that are normally not flammable can become highly combustible, and existing fires can burn much hotter and faster. Sparks or hot metal can ignite virtually anything.
10. Good housekeeping practices contribute to fire prevention by removing combustible materials (e.g., rags, paper, wood scraps, flammable liquids) from the vicinity of welding and cutting operations. Keeping the work area clean and organized also reduces trip hazards and ensures that fire extinguishers are accessible.
