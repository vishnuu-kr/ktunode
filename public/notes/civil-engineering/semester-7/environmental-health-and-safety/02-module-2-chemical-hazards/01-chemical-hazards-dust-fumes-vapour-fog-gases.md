# Environmental Health and Safety: Module 2 - Chemical Hazards

## Topic: Dust, Fumes, Vapour, Fog, and Gases

---

### **1. Introduction to Chemical Hazards**

*   **Definition:** Chemical hazards are risks posed by the presence of chemical substances that can cause harm to human health or the environment. These hazards can arise from the inherent properties of the chemical, the way it is used, or the conditions of exposure.
*   **Routes of Exposure:** Chemicals can enter the body through:
    *   **Inhalation:** Breathing in airborne contaminants.
    *   **Absorption:** Through the skin or eyes.
    *   **Ingestion:** Swallowing contaminated food, drink, or by hand-to-mouth transfer.
*   **Importance in EHS:** Understanding and controlling chemical hazards is a cornerstone of Environmental Health and Safety to prevent occupational illnesses, injuries, and environmental damage.

---

### **2. Forms of Airborne Chemical Hazards**

This section will explore the different physical forms that chemical hazards can take in the air, and how they are generated.

#### **2.1. Dust**

*   **Definition:** Dust refers to solid particles dispersed in the air, typically generated by mechanical processes. These particles are usually larger than fumes and are formed from the disintegration of solid materials.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Solid particles.
    *   Vary in size from sub-micron to several hundred microns.
    *   Can settle out of the air relatively quickly due to gravity.
    *   Particle size significantly influences their health effects and ability to penetrate the respiratory system.
*   **Generation:**
    *   **Mechanical Processes:** Grinding, crushing, drilling, sawing, sanding, demolition, mining, agriculture (plowing, harvesting).
    *   **Handling:** Pouring, scooping, bag emptying, sweeping.
    *   **Natural Sources:** Wind erosion, pollen, spores.
*   **Examples:**
    *   Wood dust from woodworking.
    *   Silica dust from cutting concrete or stone.
    *   Metal dust from grinding or welding.
    *   Flour dust in bakeries.
    *   Agricultural dust (soil, crop residue).
*   **Health Effects:**
    *   **Irritation:** Eyes, skin, respiratory tract.
    *   **Respiratory Diseases:** Pneumoconiosis (e.g., silicosis, asbestosis), asthma, bronchitis.
    *   **Systemic Toxicity:** If the dust contains toxic metals (e.g., lead, cadmium).
    *   **Carcinogenicity:** Certain dusts (e.g., asbestos, silica, some wood dusts) are known carcinogens.

#### **2.2. Fumes**

*   **Definition:** Fumes are solid particles formed from the condensation of vaporized solids. They are typically very fine and generated during processes involving high temperatures.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Solid particles.
    *   Extremely small, often sub-micron in size.
    *   Originate from a molten or vaporized state that then cools and condenses.
    *   Tend to remain suspended in the air for longer periods than dust.
*   **Generation:**
    *   **High-Temperature Processes:** Welding, brazing, soldering, smelting, burning or combustion of materials.
    *   **Evaporation and Condensation:** When a solid is heated to its boiling point, vaporizes, and then cools back into solid particles.
*   **Examples:**
    *   Metal fumes generated during welding (e.g., iron oxide fumes, zinc oxide fumes).
    *   Lead fumes from melting lead.
    *   Fumes from burning plastics or chemicals.
*   **Health Effects:**
    *   **Respiratory Irritation and Damage:** Can penetrate deep into the lungs.
    *   **Metal Fume Fever:** An influenza-like illness caused by inhaling metal oxide fumes (e.g., zinc oxide).
    *   **Systemic Toxicity:** Depending on the metal or chemical involved (e.g., lead poisoning from lead fumes).
    *   **Carcinogenicity:** Some fumes can contain carcinogenic components.

#### **2.3. Vapour**

*   **Definition:** Vapour refers to the gaseous state of a substance that is normally a liquid or solid at room temperature and pressure.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Gaseous state.
    *   Exists at temperatures below their critical temperature.
    *   Can mix readily with air.
    *   Invisible, unless the substance itself is colored or causes visible condensation.
*   **Generation:**
    *   **Evaporation:** From liquids at room temperature or with mild heating.
    *   **Boiling:** When a liquid is heated to its boiling point.
    *   **Sublimation:** From solids that transition directly into a gas.
*   **Examples:**
    *   Solvent vapours (e.g., from paints, glues, cleaning agents like acetone, toluene).
    *   Petrol/gasoline vapours.
    *   Ammonia vapour from cleaning products.
    *   Mercury vapour from mercury.
*   **Health Effects:**
    *   **Central Nervous System Depression:** Dizziness, headaches, nausea, unconsciousness (e.g., solvent vapours).
    *   **Organ Damage:** Liver, kidney, lung damage (depending on the specific chemical).
    *   **Irritation:** Eyes, skin, respiratory tract.
    *   **Carcinogenicity:** Some vapours are known carcinogens (e.g., benzene).

#### **2.4. Fog (Mist)**

*   **Definition:** Fog (or mist) refers to fine droplets of a liquid suspended in the air. These are distinct from vapours as they are liquid particles, not a gaseous state of a substance.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Liquid droplets.
    *   Typically larger than fumes or fine dust, but small enough to remain airborne for a time.
    *   Can be visible or invisible depending on droplet size and concentration.
*   **Generation:**
    *   **Atomization/Spraying:** Spray painting, pesticide application, industrial spraying processes.
    *   **Agitation:** Stirring or splashing of liquids.
    *   **Condensation:** Of liquid droplets from a supersaturated vapor (though true "fog" in an industrial sense is usually from mechanical dispersion).
*   **Examples:**
    *   Spray paint mists.
    *   Oil mists from machining operations.
    *   Mist from cleaning solutions.
    *   Acid mists from plating or etching processes.
*   **Health Effects:**
    *   **Irritation:** Skin, eyes, respiratory tract.
    *   **Inhalation of Toxic Liquids:** Absorption of harmful chemicals through the lungs.
    *   **Dermatitis:** From prolonged skin contact with mists.

#### **2.5. Gases**

*   **Definition:** Gases are substances that exist in a gaseous state at normal room temperature and pressure, and they fill the entire volume of their container. Unlike vapours, they are not simply the gaseous form of a liquid or solid.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Gaseous state.
    *   Expand to fill any container.
    *   Can be colorless, odorless, or have distinct properties.
    *   Often diffused by molecular motion.
*   **Generation:**
    *   **Chemical Reactions:** Combustion, decomposition, fermentation.
    *   **Industrial Processes:** Manufacturing, chemical synthesis, refrigeration.
    *   **Leakage:** From pressurized containers or systems.
    *   **Natural Processes:** Biological processes, geological activity.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Toxic Gases:** Carbon monoxide (CO), hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), chlorine (Cl₂), ammonia (NH₃), sulfur dioxide (SO₂).
    *   **Asphyxiant Gases:** Nitrogen (N₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂) (can displace oxygen).
    *   **Flammable Gases:** Methane (CH₄), propane (C₃H₈), hydrogen (H₂).
*   **Health Effects:**
    *   **Asphyxiation:** Displacement of oxygen, leading to suffocation.
    *   **Systemic Toxicity:** Interference with bodily functions (e.g., CO binding to hemoglobin).
    *   **Irritation and Corrosion:** Damage to respiratory tissues (e.g., chlorine, ammonia).
    *   **Flammability/Explosivity:** Fire and explosion hazards.

### **3. Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Particle Size:** Crucial for determining where in the respiratory tract a particle will deposit. Smaller particles (respirable dust, fumes) can reach the alveoli.
*   **Concentration:** The amount of a hazardous substance present in the air (e.g., mg/m³, ppm).
*   **Exposure Limit:** The maximum concentration of a substance to which a worker can be exposed without adverse health effects. Examples include:
    *   **Permissible Exposure Limit (PEL):** Legally enforceable limits set by regulatory bodies (e.g., OSHA in the US).
    *   **Threshold Limit Value (TLV):** Recommended exposure limits developed by organizations like the ACGIH.
*   **IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health):** A concentration of an airborne substance that poses an immediate threat to life, would cause irreversible health effects, or would impair an individual's ability to escape.
*   **Ventilation:** The process of supplying fresh air and removing contaminated air from a workspace.
    *   **Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV):** Captures contaminants at the source.
    *   **General Dilution Ventilation:** Reduces the concentration of contaminants in the general workspace.
*   **Hierarchy of Controls:** A systematic approach to controlling hazards, prioritizing methods in order of effectiveness:
    1.  **Elimination/Substitution:** Removing the hazard or replacing it with a less hazardous one.
    2.  **Engineering Controls:** Isolating people from the hazard (e.g., ventilation, enclosures).
    3.  **Administrative Controls:** Changing the way people work (e.g., job rotation, training).
    4.  **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Protecting the individual worker (e.g., respirators, gloves) - considered the last line of defense.

### **4. Health and Safety Implications**

*   **Occupational Diseases:** Many chronic and acute illnesses are linked to exposure to airborne chemical hazards.
*   **Fire and Explosion Risks:** Flammable gases and dusts (dust explosions) pose significant safety risks.
*   **Environmental Contamination:** Release of harmful airborne substances can impact the surrounding environment and community health.
*   **Regulatory Compliance:** Organizations must adhere to established exposure limits and safety regulations.

---

### **5. Control Measures**

Effective control of dust, fumes, vapour, fog, and gases involves a multi-faceted approach based on the hierarchy of controls.

*   **Engineering Controls:**
    *   **Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV):** Hoods, extractors, ducted systems to capture airborne contaminants at the source (e.g., welding booths, spray booths).
    *   **Enclosure:** Containing the process generating the hazard.
    *   **Process Modification:** Using less hazardous materials or modifying processes to reduce airborne emissions.
    *   **General Ventilation:** Diluting contaminants in the air.
    *   **Wet Methods:** Using water to suppress dust generation.
*   **Administrative Controls:**
    *   **Work Procedures:** Developing safe work practices and standard operating procedures.
    *   **Training:** Educating workers on hazards, safe handling, and emergency procedures.
    *   **Housekeeping:** Regular cleaning to prevent dust accumulation.
    *   **Exposure Monitoring:** Regularly testing air quality to ensure control measures are effective and limits are not exceeded.
    *   **Medical Surveillance:** Monitoring the health of exposed workers.
*   **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):**
    *   **Respiratory Protection:**
        *   **Dust Masks (Filtering Facepiece Respirators):** For lower concentrations of nuisance dust.
        *   **Half-Face/Full-Face Respirators with Cartridges/Filters:** Selected based on the specific contaminant (e.g., P100 filters for particulates, organic vapor cartridges for solvents).
        *   **Supplied-Air Respirators (SARs) / Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus (SCBA):** For highly toxic substances, oxygen-deficient atmospheres, or unknown environments.
    *   **Skin Protection:** Gloves, protective clothing to prevent dermal absorption.
    *   **Eye Protection:** Safety glasses, goggles, face shields.

### **6. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** What is the primary difference between dust and fumes?
    *   A) Dust is solid, fumes are gaseous.
    *   B) Dust is generated by mechanical processes, fumes are generated by condensation of vaporized solids.
    *   C) Fumes are always visible, dust is always invisible.
    *   D) Dust is always hazardous, fumes are generally not.

**Answer:** B) Dust is generated by mechanical processes, fumes are generated by condensation of vaporized solids.

**Question 2:** Which of the following is an example of a vapour hazard?
    *   A) Silica dust from cutting concrete.
    *   B) Metal fumes from welding.
    *   C) Solvent vapours from paint thinner.
    *   D) Oil mist from machining.

**Answer:** C) Solvent vapours from paint thinner.

**Question 3:** Name two engineering controls used to manage airborne chemical hazards.

**Answer:**
    1.  Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV)
    2.  Enclosure of the process

**Question 4:** A worker is painting with a solvent-based paint in a poorly ventilated area. What are the primary airborne hazards they might be exposed to, and through which route?

**Answer:**
    *   **Hazard:** Solvent vapours.
    *   **Route:** Inhalation.

**Question 5:** What is the purpose of an IDLH value?
    *   A) To indicate the acceptable daily intake of a chemical.
    *   B) To define the concentration that poses an immediate threat to life or health.
    *   C) To set the safe working limit for a chemical over a lifetime.
    *   D) To guide the selection of general ventilation rates.

**Answer:** B) To define the concentration that poses an immediate threat to life or health.

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **Distinguish between the forms:** Understanding the difference between dust, fumes, vapour, fog, and gases is crucial for effective hazard identification and control.
*   **Particle size matters:** Smaller particles are generally more hazardous as they can penetrate deeper into the respiratory system.
*   **Source control is key:** Prioritize controlling hazards at their source using engineering controls.
*   **Hierarchy of controls:** Always follow the hierarchy of controls, with PPE as the last resort.
*   **Read Safety Data Sheets (SDS):** SDSs provide critical information about chemical hazards, safe handling, and control measures.
*   **Ventilation is vital:** Proper ventilation is essential for managing airborne contaminants.
*   **Training and awareness:** Educated workers are safer workers.
*   **Never underestimate invisible hazards:** Many dangerous gases and vapours are invisible and odorless.
