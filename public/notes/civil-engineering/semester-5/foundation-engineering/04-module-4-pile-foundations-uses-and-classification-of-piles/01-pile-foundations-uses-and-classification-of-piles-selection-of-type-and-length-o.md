# FOUNDATION ENGINEERING - Module 4: Pile Foundations - Uses and Classification of Piles

## Topic: Pile Foundations - Selection of Type and Length of Piles

---

### Learning Outcomes:

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Understand the various reasons and circumstances where pile foundations are necessary.
*   Classify piles based on different criteria such as their mode of action, material, installation method, and structural form.
*   Explain the factors influencing the selection of the appropriate type of pile for a given project.
*   Discuss the methods and considerations involved in determining the required length of a pile.

---

### 1. Uses of Pile Foundations

Pile foundations are used when the shallow soil strata near the surface are inadequate to support the applied loads. They transfer the loads to deeper, stronger soil layers or bedrock.

**Key Reasons for Using Pile Foundations:**

*   **Weak Superimposed Strata:**
    *   When the upper soil layers are soft, compressible, or have low bearing capacity (e.g., silts, clays, peat).
    *   **Example:** A building foundation on a thick deposit of soft clay where a shallow foundation would settle excessively.
*   **Heavy Loads:**
    *   For structures with very heavy concentrated loads, such as tall buildings, bridges, or industrial plants.
    *   **Example:** A multi-story office building with high column loads that cannot be safely supported by spread footings.
*   **Scour:**
    *   In hydraulic structures like bridges and piers, to extend the foundation below the maximum scour depth caused by flowing water.
    *   **Example:** Bridge piers founded on piles driven deep enough to remain unaffected by erosion of the riverbed.
*   **Uplift and Overturning:**
    *   To resist uplift forces (e.g., from wind on tall structures) or overturning moments. Piles can be designed to act in tension.
    *   **Example:** The foundations for transmission towers or offshore platforms subjected to strong winds or wave action.
*   **Lateral Loads:**
    *   To resist significant horizontal forces (e.g., from wind, earthquake, or earth pressure). Piles, especially when battered or group-arranged, can provide lateral resistance.
    *   **Example:** Foundations for retaining walls or structures on slopes.
*   **Expansive and Collapsible Soils:**
    *   When soils exhibit significant volume changes due to moisture variations (expansive soils) or collapse upon wetting (collapsible soils). Piles can bypass these problematic layers.
    *   **Example:** Foundations in arid regions with collapsible soils that can cause severe settlement if wetted.
*   **Adverse Ground Conditions:**
    *   High groundwater table, presence of underground obstructions, or difficult excavation conditions.
*   **Reduction of Settlement:**
    *   To limit both total and differential settlements to acceptable values, especially for structures sensitive to settlement.

### 2. Classification of Piles

Piles can be classified based on various criteria:

#### 2.1. Based on Load Transfer Mechanism:

This is a crucial classification as it dictates how the load is resisted.

*   **End-Bearing Piles (or Bearing Piles):**
    *   Transfer the majority of the load to a strong stratum (like rock or dense sand) at the pile tip.
    *   The shaft resistance plays a secondary role.
    *   **Characteristics:** Relatively short if founded on rock, long if the bearing stratum is deep.
    *   **Analogy:** Like a column resting on a strong foundation layer.
*   **Friction Piles (or Floating Piles):**
    *   Transfer the load to the surrounding soil through skin friction (adhesion) along the shaft of the pile.
    *   The tip resistance is usually negligible.
    *   **Characteristics:** Typically long and slender, embedded in compressible soils.
    *   **Analogy:** Like a nail driven into wood, where the holding power comes from friction.
*   **Compaction Piles:**
    *   Driven into granular soils to increase their density and bearing capacity. The pile itself carries little load; it primarily improves the soil.
    *   **Example:** Used in loose sands to reduce settlement and increase bearing capacity for shallow foundations.
*   **Drivability Piles:**
    *   Driven into granular soils to improve their bearing capacity and reduce settlement. The piles themselves are designed to resist some load in addition to soil improvement.
*   **Helical Piles (Screw Piles):**
    *   Have helical plates that screw into the ground. They can act as end-bearing or friction piles depending on the depth and soil conditions.

#### 2.2. Based on Material:

*   **Concrete Piles:**
    *   **Cast-in-situ (Bored Piles):** Formed by drilling a hole and filling it with concrete. Can be plain or reinforced.
        *   **Types:** Benoto piles, Removable casing piles, Permanent casing piles, Cased bored piles, Uncased bored piles.
    *   **Pre-cast Concrete Piles:** Manufactured off-site and then driven or vibrated into the ground. Usually reinforced.
        *   **Advantages:** High quality control, can be driven rapidly.
        *   **Disadvantages:** Limited to manageable lengths for transportation, susceptible to damage during driving, require heavy equipment.
*   **Timber Piles:**
    *   Typically used for lighter loads and temporary structures or in environments where decay is not an issue (e.g., permanently submerged in water).
    *   **Advantages:** Economical, readily available.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Susceptible to decay, rot, and insect attack unless treated or permanently submerged; limited load capacity.
*   **Steel Piles:**
    *   **H-Piles:** Structural H-sections. Good for driving through dense soils and obstructions.
    *   **Pipe Piles:** Hollow steel tubes, driven open-ended or closed-ended. Can be filled with concrete for increased capacity.
    *   **Sheet Piles:** Interlocking sections forming a continuous wall. Used primarily for retaining earth or water (cofferdams, retaining walls), not typically for supporting vertical building loads.
    *   **Advantages:** High strength, can be driven to great depths, can penetrate dense layers.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Susceptible to corrosion, can buckle under heavy axial loads if not properly designed, expensive.

#### 2.3. Based on Installation Method:

*   **Driven Piles:**
    *   Piles forced into the ground by impact (hammering) or vibration.
    *   Includes pre-cast concrete, timber, and steel piles.
    *   **Advantages:** Consolidate surrounding soil, can increase soil bearing capacity.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can cause noise and vibration disturbance, may damage pile during driving, can cause heave in clay soils, difficult to place accurately.
*   **Bored Piles (or Drilled Shafts/Cased Piles):**
    *   A hole is drilled, reinforcing cage is placed, and concrete is poured.
    *   **Advantages:** Minimal noise and vibration, can be installed to great depths and large diameters, can handle very heavy loads, can be installed in noisy or vibration-sensitive areas, can be cased to prevent borehole collapse.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Slower installation, risk of borehole instability, concrete contamination, may not densify soil.
*   **Screw Piles (Helical Piles):**
    *   Piles with helical plates that are screwed into the ground.
*   **Jacked Piles:**
    *   Piles are pushed into the ground using hydraulic jacks, often used in areas with vibration restrictions.

#### 2.4. Based on Structural Form:

*   **Cylindrical Piles:** Common for bored piles and pipe piles.
*   **Prismatic Piles:** Often used for pre-cast concrete piles (square, rectangular, hexagonal).
*   **H-Piles:** Structural steel H-sections.

#### 2.5. Based on Production Method:

*   **Pre-cast:** Manufactured in a factory.
*   **Cast-in-situ:** Cast at the construction site.

### 3. Selection of Type of Pile

The choice of pile type depends on a multitude of factors related to the site conditions, structure, and economics.

**Key Factors Influencing Pile Type Selection:**

*   **Soil Profile and Properties:**
    *   **Bearing Stratum:** Depth and strength of the bearing layer (rock, dense sand, stiff clay).
    *   **Soil Compressibility:** Presence of soft, compressible layers that may cause excessive settlement.
    *   **Presence of Obstructions:** Boulders, old foundations, or debris can make driving difficult.
    *   **Groundwater Table:** High water table can affect the installation of bored piles.
    *   **Lateral Soil Conditions:** Presence of firm layers to provide lateral support.
*   **Magnitude and Type of Loads:**
    *   **Vertical Loads:** Magnitude of axial compression.
    *   **Lateral Loads:** Wind, seismic, or earth pressure forces.
    *   **Uplift Loads:** Resistance against pulling out.
*   **Environmental and Site Constraints:**
    *   **Noise and Vibration Limits:** In urban areas or near sensitive structures, driven piles may be prohibited.
    *   **Access and Space:** Site accessibility for heavy equipment.
    *   **Water Environment:** For offshore structures or marine works, specific pile types (e.g., steel pipe piles) are preferred.
*   **Cost and Availability:**
    *   Cost of materials, installation, and equipment.
    *   Availability of specialized equipment and skilled labor.
    *   Local construction practices.
*   **Desired Performance:**
    *   Required bearing capacity, settlement limits, and durability.
*   **Construction Speed:**
    *   Urgency of the project.
*   **Corrosion and Durability:**
    *   In aggressive soil or water environments, materials resistant to corrosion are necessary.

**Decision-Making Process Example:**

*   **Scenario 1:** Heavy loads on a site with soft clay overlying dense sand.
    *   **Consideration:** Need to bypass soft clay and reach dense sand.
    *   **Possible Piles:** End-bearing piles (driven steel H-piles or pipe piles, or bored piles) to reach the dense sand.
*   **Scenario 2:** Light to moderate loads on a site with loose sand.
    *   **Consideration:** Need to improve soil density and bearing capacity.
    *   **Possible Piles:** Compaction piles (driven displacement piles) or possibly driven friction piles.
*   **Scenario 3:** Urban area with vibration constraints and heavy loads.
    *   **Consideration:** Avoid noise and vibration, need high capacity.
    *   **Possible Piles:** Bored piles (large diameter shafts) are often the preferred choice.
*   **Scenario 4:** Marine environment requiring resistance to scour and uplift.
    *   **Consideration:** Durability, resistance to water forces.
    *   **Possible Piles:** Steel pipe piles driven deep and potentially filled with concrete.

### 4. Selection of Length of Piles

Determining the required pile length is critical for ensuring the foundation can safely support the loads. It involves balancing structural integrity, settlement control, and economic considerations.

**Key Factors Influencing Pile Length:**

*   **Depth to Bearing Stratum:**
    *   For end-bearing piles, the length is determined by the depth required to reach a stratum of adequate strength (rock or dense soil).
    *   **Consideration:** The bearing capacity is primarily governed by the strength of the soil at the tip.
*   **Soil Properties Along the Pile Length:**
    *   For friction piles, the length depends on the cumulative skin friction developed along the pile shaft. This is influenced by the shear strength of the soil (cohesion 'c' for clays, angle of internal friction 'φ' for sands) and the pile-soil interface friction.
    *   **Consideration:** The load capacity is developed over the entire embedded length.
*   **Magnitude and Type of Loads:**
    *   Higher loads generally require longer piles or piles of larger diameter to mobilize sufficient resistance.
    *   Lateral loads and uplift loads also influence the required length for adequate anchoring and resistance.
*   **Allowable Settlement:**
    *   The length of the pile influences settlement. Longer piles generally result in less settlement, especially if they reach a stiffer layer.
    *   **Consideration:** The settlement of the pile group is often the governing design criterion.
*   **Group Effects:**
    *   When piles are installed in a group, their load-carrying capacity and settlement behavior can be different from that of a single pile due to overlapping stress zones and soil compression. This needs to be accounted for.
*   **Economical Considerations:**
    *   Pile length is a significant cost factor. The objective is to achieve the required capacity and settlement performance at the minimum cost.
    *   **Consideration:** Cost increases with length, so finding the most economical length is crucial.
*   **Skin Friction Along the Shaft:**
    *   In cohesive soils, skin friction depends on the adhesion between the pile and the soil, often related to the undrained shear strength (cu) of the clay.
    *   In granular soils, skin friction depends on the effective vertical stress and the angle of friction between the pile and the soil.
*   **Negative Skin Friction (Downdrag):**
    *   If a compressible soil layer above the pile tip settles more than the pile, it can exert a downward drag force on the pile, reducing its net carrying capacity. This may necessitate a longer pile or specific design considerations.
    *   **Example:** A building founded on piles through a consolidating clay layer.
*   **Overburden Pressure:**
    *   Increasing depth generally means higher soil density and overburden pressure, leading to higher shaft resistance.

**Methods for Determining Pile Length:**

1.  **Experience and Local Practice:** For specific soil conditions and structures, empirical data from similar projects is often used as a starting point.
2.  **Static Load Capacity Calculation:**
    *   **End Bearing Capacity (Qp):** Calculated based on the soil properties at the pile tip.
        *   $Q_p = A_p \times q_p$, where $A_p$ is the tip area and $q_p$ is the unit bearing capacity at the tip.
    *   **Shaft Resistance (Qs):** Calculated by integrating the unit shaft friction ($f_s$) along the pile length.
        *   $Q_s = \Sigma (f_s \times A_s)$, where $A_s$ is the surface area of the pile segment.
    *   **Total Static Capacity ($Q_u$):** $Q_u = Q_p + Q_s$.
    *   **Factor of Safety (FOS):** The allowable load ($Q_{allow}$) is typically $Q_u / FOS$.
    *   **Pile length is adjusted iteratively until $Q_{allow}$ is sufficient for the applied load.**
3.  **Settlement Analysis:**
    *   Estimating the settlement of the pile or pile group under the applied load. This often involves more complex analytical or numerical methods.
    *   The pile length is adjusted to meet the allowable settlement criteria.
4.  **Dynamic Pile Driving Formulas:**
    *   Formulas like the Engineering News Record (ENR) formula or Danish formulas relate the energy delivered by a pile hammer to the pile penetration per blow.
    *   These are primarily used for driven piles and provide an estimate of the pile's ultimate capacity based on the driving resistance.
    *   **Limitations:** These formulas are empirical and can have significant inaccuracies depending on soil conditions and hammer efficiency.
5.  **Pile Load Tests:**
    *   The most reliable method. A test pile is loaded to failure or to a specified settlement in the field.
    *   **Types:** Static load tests (most common and reliable), dynamic load tests, Statnamic tests.
    *   **Purpose:** To verify the capacity and settlement characteristics of the pile in the actual soil conditions. The results from test piles are then used to confirm the design lengths for production piles.

**Practical Approach for Pile Length Selection:**

1.  **Preliminary Geotechnical Investigation:** Obtain soil profiles and properties (SPT N-values, shear strength parameters).
2.  **Estimate Preliminary Lengths:** Based on depth to bearing stratum or expected friction development.
3.  **Calculate Static Capacity:** For the estimated lengths, calculate the ultimate and allowable axial capacities.
4.  **Estimate Settlement:** Assess the expected settlement.
5.  **Compare with Design Loads:** Check if the calculated allowable capacity is sufficient. If not, increase length or diameter. Check if estimated settlement is within limits. If not, adjust length or consider pile spacing.
6.  **Consider Group Effects:** Account for reduced capacity or increased settlement in groups.
7.  **Economic Optimization:** Select the most economical length that meets all performance requirements.
8.  **Pile Load Testing:** Conduct load tests to confirm design assumptions and optimize lengths, especially for critical structures or challenging soil conditions.

### Practice Questions & Exercises:

1.  **Multiple Choice:**
    Which of the following is NOT a primary reason for using pile foundations?
    (a) Weak soil strata at shallow depths
    (b) Resistance to uplift forces
    (c) Aesthetic appeal of the structure
    (d) Heavy structural loads

    **Answer:** (c) Aesthetic appeal of the structure

2.  **Classification:**
    Classify a concrete pile that is bored at the site and filled with concrete and reinforcement as a pile based on:
    (a) Material
    (b) Installation Method
    (c) Load Transfer Mechanism

    **Answer:**
    (a) Material: Concrete
    (b) Installation Method: Bored (Cast-in-situ)
    (c) Load Transfer Mechanism: Can be either End-Bearing or Friction, depending on the soil profile and depth.

3.  **Scenario Analysis:**
    A proposed high-rise building is to be constructed on a site with a thick layer of soft, compressible clay followed by a dense sand layer at a depth of 30 meters. The column loads are very heavy. What type of pile would likely be most suitable, and why? Briefly explain the likely load transfer mechanism.

    **Answer:**
    A **pre-cast concrete end-bearing pile** or a **steel H-pile** (driven) or a **large-diameter bored pile** would likely be most suitable.
    *   **Reasoning:** The soft clay layer necessitates bypassing it to reach the stronger, denser sand layer. End-bearing piles are designed to transfer loads to such a firm stratum. Driven piles (steel or pre-cast concrete) can penetrate the soft clay effectively. Bored piles offer high capacity and can be constructed to reach the required depth reliably.
    *   **Load Transfer Mechanism:** Primarily **end-bearing**, as the majority of the load will be transferred to the dense sand layer at the pile tip. Shaft friction in the soft clay will be relatively low and might even be negative if consolidation occurs.

4.  **Pile Length Determination:**
    What are the main factors that influence the required length of a pile?

    **Answer:**
    The main factors influencing the required length of a pile are:
    *   Depth to the bearing stratum (for end-bearing piles)
    *   Soil properties along the pile shaft (shear strength, for friction piles)
    *   Magnitude and type of applied loads (axial, lateral, uplift)
    *   Allowable settlement criteria
    *   Group effects
    *   Economic considerations
    *   Presence of negative skin friction (downdrag)

---

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Pile foundations are a form of deep foundation.** They are used when shallow soils cannot support the loads.
*   **Load transfer mechanism is key:** End-bearing piles rely on tip resistance, while friction piles rely on shaft resistance.
*   **Driven piles consolidate soil**, while **bored piles can cause minimal disturbance**.
*   **Site conditions dictate pile type:** Soil profile, groundwater, adjacent structures, and environmental constraints are critical.
*   **Pile length is determined by capacity and settlement:** It's an iterative process often confirmed by load testing.
*   **Cost is a major factor:** Balancing performance with economics is essential in pile selection.
*   **Negative skin friction (downdrag)** can significantly reduce a pile's capacity and must be accounted for.
