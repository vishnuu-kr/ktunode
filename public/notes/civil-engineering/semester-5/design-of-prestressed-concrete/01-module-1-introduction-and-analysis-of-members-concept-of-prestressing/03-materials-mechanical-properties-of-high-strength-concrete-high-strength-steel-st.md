### 1. Introduction to Materials in Prestressed Concrete

Prestressed concrete relies on the use of materials with superior properties compared to those used in conventional reinforced concrete. This is primarily to achieve higher strength, reduce member sizes, and increase efficiency.

*   **High Strength Concrete (HSC):** Concrete with compressive strength generally exceeding 40-50 MPa.
*   **High Strength Steel:** Steel with high tensile strength, typically used as tendons or wires for inducing prestress.

---

### 2. Mechanical Properties of High Strength Concrete (HSC)

HSC offers several advantages over conventional concrete, making it ideal for prestressed concrete applications.

#### 2.1. Compressive Strength ($f_{ck}$ or $f_{cu}$):

*   **Definition:** The maximum stress a concrete cylinder or cube can withstand before failing in compression.
*   **Significance:** Higher compressive strength allows for smaller cross-sections, reduced self-weight, and increased load-carrying capacity.
*   **Typical Values:** HSC typically has characteristic compressive strengths ranging from 50 MPa to over 100 MPa.
*   **Factors Affecting HSC:**
    *   Low water-cement (w/c) ratio.
    *   Use of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) like silica fume, fly ash, and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS).
    *   Well-graded aggregates.
    *   Superplasticizers to maintain workability at low w/c ratios.
    *   Proper curing.

#### 2.2. Tensile Strength ($f_{ctm}$, $f_{ctk,0.05}$, $f_{ctk,0.95}$):

*   **Definition:** The stress at which concrete begins to crack under tension.
*   **Significance:** While concrete is weak in tension, its tensile strength is still important for crack control and in understanding its behaviour under service loads. HSC generally exhibits higher tensile strength than conventional concrete.
*   **Relationship to Compressive Strength:** Often related to compressive strength by empirical formulas (e.g., $f_{ctm} \approx 0.3 f_{ck}^{2/3}$ as per some codes).
*   **Important Note:** Prestressed concrete aims to keep concrete in a compressive state under service loads, minimizing tensile stresses.

#### 2.3. Modulus of Elasticity ($E_c$):

*   **Definition:** A measure of the stiffness of concrete, representing the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region.
*   **Significance:** A higher modulus of elasticity in HSC means less deformation under load and reduced losses due to elastic shortening of concrete.
*   **Typical Values:** For HSC, $E_c$ can range from 35 GPa to 50 GPa or more, depending on the compressive strength and mix design.
*   **Factors Affecting $E_c$:**
    *   Compressive strength.
    *   Aggregate type and properties.
    *   Cement content.
    *   Moisture content (concrete is generally stiffer when dry).

#### 2.4. Creep:

*   **Definition:** The gradual increase in strain over time for a material subjected to a constant stress.
*   **Significance:** Creep in concrete causes a loss of prestress. HSC generally exhibits lower creep strains than conventional concrete, especially at early ages.
*   **Factors Affecting Creep:**
    *   Stress level.
    *   Age of loading.
    *   Moisture content and environment.
    *   Mix proportions.

#### 2.5. Shrinkage:

*   **Definition:** The reduction in volume of concrete due to loss of moisture.
*   **Significance:** Shrinkage can also lead to a loss of prestress (pre-tensioned members) and induce internal stresses. HSC generally has lower drying shrinkage than conventional concrete due to its lower w/c ratio.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Drying Shrinkage:** Due to moisture loss to the atmosphere.
    *   **Autogenous Shrinkage:** Due to self-desiccation during hydration (more prominent in HSC).

#### 2.6. Durability:

*   **Definition:** The ability of concrete to resist weathering action, chemical attack, abrasion, or any other process likely to cause deterioration.
*   **Significance:** HSC, with its lower permeability (due to lower w/c ratio and use of SCMs), offers superior durability and resistance to aggressive environments. This is crucial for the long-term performance of prestressed concrete structures.

### 3. Mechanical Properties of High Strength Steel

High strength steel is essential for imparting the necessary prestressing force.

#### 3.1. Yield Strength ($f_y$):

*   **Definition:** The stress at which the steel begins to deform plastically.
*   **Significance:** High yield strength allows for greater prestressing forces to be applied, leading to more efficient designs.
*   **Typical Values:** Steel wires and strands for prestressing typically have characteristic yield strengths ranging from 1500 MPa to 1800 MPa or even higher.

#### 3.2. Ultimate Tensile Strength ($f_u$):

*   **Definition:** The maximum tensile stress the steel can withstand before fracturing.
*   **Significance:** Indicates the overall strength capacity of the prestressing steel.
*   **Typical Values:** Generally around 10-20% higher than the yield strength.

#### 3.3. Modulus of Elasticity ($E_s$):

*   **Definition:** The stiffness of steel, representing the ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region.
*   **Significance:** Crucial for calculating prestress losses due to elastic shortening of concrete.
*   **Typical Value:** For all types of steel, $E_s \approx 200$ GPa. This is a standard value and doesn't vary significantly with strength.

#### 3.4. Elongation at Break / Ductility:

*   **Definition:** The amount of strain the steel can undergo before fracturing.
*   **Significance:** While high strength is paramount, some ductility is desirable for safety and to allow for proper tensioning and anchorage. However, very high strength steels may have lower ductility.
*   **Important Note:** Prestressing steels are typically designed to be high-strength with good ductility.

#### 3.5. Relaxation of Steel:

*   **Definition:** The loss of stress in steel over time when held at a constant strain.
*   **Significance:** This is a significant source of prestress loss, especially at high stress levels. Low relaxation steels are used for prestressing to minimize this loss.
*   **Types:**
    *   **High Relaxation:** Older types, not suitable for prestressing.
    *   **Low Relaxation:** Specially manufactured to reduce stress loss at high temperatures and strains.

### 4. Stress-Strain Curve for High Strength Concrete

The stress-strain curve graphically represents the relationship between the applied stress and the resulting strain in concrete. For HSC, this curve has distinct characteristics.

#### 4.1. General Shape:

*   **Initial Linear Elastic Region:** At low stress levels, the relationship is approximately linear, similar to conventional concrete.
*   **Parabolic/Non-linear Region:** As stress increases, the concrete begins to exhibit non-linear behaviour. The curve typically rises steeply and then flattens out before failure.
*   **Peak Stress:** The maximum stress the concrete can withstand ($f_{ck}$).
*   **Post-Peak Region:** After reaching peak stress, the concrete's load-carrying capacity decreases, and it becomes more strain-softening.

#### 4.2. Key Points on the Stress-Strain Curve:

*   **$f_{ck}$:** Characteristic compressive strength.
*   **$\epsilon_{c1}$:** Strain at peak stress ($f_{ck}$). This is typically around 0.002 for HSC.
*   **$\epsilon_{c2}$:** Ultimate strain. The strain at which the concrete fails. This can vary, but for design purposes, it's often considered around 0.0035, though HSC can achieve higher strains before complete crushing.
*   **$E_c$:** Tangent modulus at any point on the curve. The initial modulus of elasticity ($E_{c,0}$) is the slope at the origin.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Conventional Concrete:

*   **Steeper Initial Slope:** HSC has a higher initial modulus of elasticity ($E_c$).
*   **Higher Peak Stress:** HSC reaches a much higher peak stress ($f_{ck}$).
*   **More Brittle Behaviour (often):** While HSC can have good ductility, some very high strength mixes might exhibit a more brittle fracture with a steeper post-peak softening. However, the use of fibres can significantly improve ductility.
*   **Higher Ultimate Strain (at peak):** The strain at peak stress ($\epsilon_{c1}$) for HSC is generally around 0.002, whereas for conventional concrete, it might be closer to 0.0025 to 0.003. However, the total ultimate strain capacity before complete failure can be comparable or even higher in some HSC due to better internal bonding.

#### 4.4. Design Models for Stress-Strain Curve:

For design purposes, simplified stress-strain relationships are used, often represented by parabolic or bilinear curves. Codes of practice provide specific models.

*   **Example (Simplified Parabolic Model):**
    The stress $\sigma_c$ at strain $\epsilon_c$ can be approximated as:
    $\sigma_c = f_{ck} \left[ 2 \left(\frac{\epsilon_c}{\epsilon_{c1}}\right) - \left(\frac{\epsilon_c}{\epsilon_{c1}}\right)^2 \right]$ for $0 \le \epsilon_c \le \epsilon_{c1}$
    $\sigma_c = f_{ck} \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{\epsilon_c - \epsilon_{c1}}{0.002}\right) \right]$ for $\epsilon_{c1} < \epsilon_c \le \epsilon_{c1} + 0.002$ (or higher, depending on code and material)

    *   **Note:** $\epsilon_{c1}$ is typically taken as 0.002 for HSC. The ultimate strain $\epsilon_{cu}$ is usually taken as 0.0035 or higher in specific design contexts.

#### 4.5. Factors Influencing the HSC Stress-Strain Curve:

*   **Water-Cementitious Material Ratio (w/cm):** Lower ratios lead to higher strengths and steeper curves.
*   **Silica Fume Content:** Silica fume improves particle packing, densifies the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), and leads to higher strength and stiffer concrete.
*   **Aggregate Properties:** Shape, size, grading, and strength of aggregates influence the curve.
*   **Admixtures:** Superplasticizers improve workability without compromising strength.
*   **Curing Conditions:** Proper curing is essential to achieve the potential strength.
*   **Age of Concrete:** Strength and stiffness increase with age.

### 5. Advantages and Limitations of Using HSC and High Strength Steel

#### 5.1. Advantages:

*   **Increased Load-Carrying Capacity:** Higher strength materials allow for greater applied forces.
*   **Reduced Member Sizes:** Lighter and more slender members can be designed, reducing dead weight and allowing for more usable space.
*   **Improved Durability:** Lower permeability leads to better resistance to environmental attack.
*   **Economic Efficiency:** In some cases, the reduction in material volume and increased span lengths can lead to overall cost savings.
*   **Aesthetics:** Slender members can offer better aesthetic appeal.
*   **Reduced Creep and Shrinkage:** Lower inherent creep and shrinkage in HSC leads to less prestress loss.

#### 5.2. Limitations:

*   **Higher Material Costs:** HSC and high strength steel are generally more expensive than conventional materials.
*   **Increased Brittleness (potential):** Very high strength mixes may exhibit less ductile
