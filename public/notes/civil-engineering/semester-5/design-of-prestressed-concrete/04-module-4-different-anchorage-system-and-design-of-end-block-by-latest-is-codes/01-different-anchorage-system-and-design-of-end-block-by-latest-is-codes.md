# DESIGN OF PRESTRESSED CONCRETE

## MODULE 4: DIFFERENT ANCHORAGE SYSTEMS AND DESIGN OF END BLOCKS BY LATEST IS CODES

### 1. DIFFERENT ANCHORAGE SYSTEMS

Anchorage systems are devices used to secure the prestressing tendons to the concrete member at the ends or at intermediate points where prestressing is terminated. The choice of anchorage system depends on several factors:

*   **Type of prestressing:** Pre-tensioning vs. Post-tensioning.
*   **Number and size of tendons:** Single wires, strands, or large cables.
*   **Force to be transferred:** Magnitude of prestressing force.
*   **Economy and ease of installation.**
*   **Durability and performance requirements.**

#### 1.1 Pre-tensioned Members

In pre-tensioned members, the prestressing force is applied to the concrete *before* it is hardened. The anchorages are typically **bond-based** and rely on the bond between the concrete and the steel.

*   **Bond Anchorage:** This is the most common method for pre-tensioning.
    *   **Mechanism:** The prestressing force is transferred to the concrete through the bond between the concrete and the steel wires/strands. This bond is developed over a certain length of embedment, known as the **transmission length**.
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   Relies on adhesion and mechanical interlock.
        *   Requires careful control of concrete mix and curing.
        *   The entire section of concrete around the tendon is put into compression.
    *   **Types of Steel Used:** High-tensile steel wires, strands, or sometimes bars.

*   **Mechanical Anchorages (Less Common in Pure Pre-tensioning):** While not the primary method, in some specialized cases or for specific applications, mechanical anchorages might be used even in pre-tensioning. These involve devices that grip the tendon externally. However, for typical pre-tensioned members, the reliance is on bond.

#### 1.2 Post-tensioned Members

In post-tensioned members, the prestressing force is applied *after* the concrete has hardened. This requires mechanical anchorages to transfer the force from the tendon to the concrete.

*   **Wedge-Type Anchorages:** These are the most widely used and effective anchorages for post-tensioning.
    *   **Mechanism:** A wedge (or a pair of wedges) is driven between the tendon and a seating or duct. The taper of the wedge forces it into the gripping device, which in turn grips the tendon.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **For Strands (Most Common):**
            *   **Wedges:** Typically conical, made of hardened steel, with internal teeth to grip the strand.
            *   **Gripping Devices:** Usually a circular "cone" with internal taper to receive the wedges and internal grooves to hold the strands.
            *   **Seating:** A circular plate or a built-in anchorage assembly that transmits the force from the wedge/cone to the concrete.
            *   **Example:** **Freyssinet Anchorage System**, **BBRV System**.
        *   **For Single Wires:** Similar principle but scaled down for individual wires.
        *   **For Tendons (Cables):** Larger anchorages designed for multi-strand or multi-wire cables, often using a single large wedge or multiple wedges.

*   **Grip-Type Anchorages:** These rely on friction or mechanical gripping to hold the tendon.
    *   **Mechanism:** Jaws or grips are clamped onto the tendon.
    *   **Less common for high forces:** Generally used for lower prestressing forces or specific applications due to potential slippage at high loads.

*   **Loop Anchorages:**
    *   **Mechanism:** The tendon is looped back on itself and anchored.
    *   **Applications:** Primarily for light pre-stressing or specific situations where the tendon is cast into the concrete member from the beginning.

*   **Specialized Anchorages:**
    *   **Bar Anchorages:** For prestressing bars, often use threaded couplers or anchor plates with nuts.

### 2. END BLOCKS: FUNCTION AND NECESSITY

#### 2.1 Definition

End blocks are localized, heavily reinforced concrete blocks cast at the ends of prestressed concrete members. They serve as the critical zone where the concentrated prestressing force from the anchorage system is transferred to the concrete member.

#### 2.2 Function

The primary functions of end blocks are:

1.  **Distribution of Concentrated Force:** To spread the concentrated prestressing force from the anchorage over a larger area of the concrete member, reducing localized stresses.
2.  **Anchoring the Tendons:** To provide a suitable seat for the anchorage devices that grip the prestressing tendons.
3.  **Resistance to Bursting Stresses:** To counteract the outward bursting forces generated by the anchorage bearing.
4.  **Resistance to Principal Tensile Stresses:** To manage the complex stress distribution in the highly stressed end region.
5.  **Protection of Anchorage:** To protect the anchorage system from environmental factors and mechanical damage.

#### 2.3 Necessity

End blocks are essential due to the following reasons:

*   **Stress Concentration:** The prestressing force is applied over a very small area at the anchorage. Without proper distribution, this would lead to very high localized compressive stresses in the concrete, potentially causing crushing.
*   **Bursting Effect:** The radial outward pressure from the anchorage system, particularly in post-tensioned members, tends to cause the concrete to split or burst at the end face.
*   **Principal Tensile Stresses:** The combination of radial and longitudinal compressive stresses, along with transverse tensile stresses from the anchorage, creates complex stress fields. The most critical is the development of principal tensile stresses, which concrete is weak against.
*   **Bond Stress (in Pre-tensioning):** In pre-tensioned members, the bond stress over the transmission length can be significant, requiring adequate concrete section to manage these stresses.

### 3. DESIGN OF END BLOCKS BY LATEST IS CODES (IS 1343:2012)

IS 1343:2012 provides guidelines for the design of anchorage zones, particularly focusing on the behaviour of concrete at the ends of prestressed members. The design is primarily governed by the stresses induced by the prestressing force.

#### 3.1 Critical Regions and Stress Analysis

The design of end blocks focuses on managing stresses in the anchorage zone. The critical stresses to consider are:

*   **Bursting Stresses:** Outward radial stresses that can cause bursting.
*   **Principal Tensile Stresses:** Tensile stresses acting in diagonal planes due to the combination of compressive and transverse stresses.
*   **Bearing Stresses:** Compressive stresses directly under the anchorage.

#### 3.2 Types of Anchorage Zones

IS 1343:2012 categorizes anchorage zones based on their geometry and the distribution of prestressing force:

*   **Type I Anchorage Zone (Prismatic):** The cross-section of the member is uniform. This is common for beams, slabs, and columns.
*   **Type II Anchorage Zone (Antiprismatic):** The cross-section changes abruptly, such as at a transition in the member's shape. This is less common for standard beams.

The design principles discussed below primarily apply to Type I prismatic anchorage zones, which are most prevalent.

#### 3.3 Design Principles and Methods

The design of end blocks aims to ensure that the stresses developed do not exceed the allowable limits for concrete.

**3.3.1 General Approach (IS 1343:2012)**

The code emphasizes that the design of anchorage zones should be based on a rational analysis of the stress distribution. It permits the use of:

*   **Elastic Analysis:** Assuming linear elastic behaviour of concrete.
*   **Limit State Design Principles:** Considering the ultimate strength and ultimate behaviour.

**3.3.2 Key Design Aspects**

**a) Bursting Stresses and Reinforcement**

*   **Cause:** Concentrated compressive forces acting radially outward cause tensile stresses in the concrete at the sides of the anchorage zone.
*   **IS Code Provision:** The code suggests that for concentrated anchorages, reinforcement should be provided to resist the bursting tensile stresses.
    *   **Amount of Reinforcement:** For anchorages transmitting a force $P$ over an area $A_a$, the amount of mild steel reinforcement ($A_s$) required is typically determined by empirical formulas or by assuming a distribution of tensile stress.
    *   **Distribution of Force:** The force $P$ is considered to be distributed over the anchorage area $A_a$.
    *   **Empirical Approach (Simplified):** For a general prismatic member, the bursting tensile force can be estimated. A common approach is to consider the force distributed within a conical or pyramidal zone.
    *   **Reinforcement Location:** This reinforcement is usually placed transversely to the direction of prestressing force, in the form of stirrups or links, encircling the prestressing tendons.

**b) Principal Tensile Stresses**

*   **Cause:** The complex interplay of radial, longitudinal, and transverse stresses leads to diagonal tensile stresses.
*   **Design Requirement:** The principal tensile stresses in the concrete should not exceed the permissible tensile strength of concrete (often considered as zero for design in cracked concrete, but for uncracked concrete, it's a fraction of compressive strength).
*   **Analysis Methods:**
    *   **1. Force Distribution or "Bursting Force" Method (Simplified by IS Code):**
        *   Consider the prestressing force $P$ applied to a concentrated area $A_a$.
        *   The force is distributed over a larger area as it moves away from the end.
        *   The tendency for bursting is related to the spread of this force.
        *   **IS 1343:2012 Clause 24.4.2:** For Type I anchorage zones (prismatic), the bursting tensile force ($F_{bt}$) can be estimated.
        *   **For a single anchor block (like Freyssinet cones):** The bursting force can be approximated. The code gives a method related to the angle of spread of stress.
        *   **For multiple anchorages:** The forces need to be considered in combination.
        *   **Reinforcement for Bursting:** The transverse reinforcement ($A_{sv}$) should be designed to resist this bursting tensile force. The minimum reinforcement is often specified.

    *   **2. Stress Distribution Methods (More Analytical):**
        *   **Trapezoidal Distribution of Stress:** Assuming the prestressing force is distributed trapezoidally over the width of the end block.
        *   **Conical Spread of Force:** Assuming the force spreads out in a cone or pyramid.
        *   **Theory of Elasticity Solutions:** For specific geometries, solutions from the theory of elasticity can be used, but these are complex for practical design.
        *   **Finite Element Analysis (FEA):** A powerful tool for analyzing complex stress distributions in anchorage zones.

    *   **3. IS 1343:2012 Specific Guidance (Simplified Practical Approach):**
        *   **For members where prestress is applied at the end face:** The force $P$ is assumed to be uniformly distributed over a smaller area $A_a$ at the end face. This area is typically the bearing area of the anchor plate.
        *   **Clause 24.4.2.1:** For members with a constant cross-section, the anchorage zone can be designed by considering the distribution of the prestressing force. The zone of distribution is often considered as extending from the end face inwards.
        *   **Design of Reinforcement:** Reinforcement is provided in the form of links or stirrups to resist the transverse tensile stresses. The total area of such steel should be $A_{st} = \frac{F_{bt}}{\sigma_{sy}}$, where $F_{bt}$ is the bursting tensile force and $\sigma_{sy}$ is the yield strength of the mild steel reinforcement.
        *   **Calculation of Bursting Force ($F_{bt}$):** The code refers to empirical methods or experimental data. A common simplified approach considers the tensile force acting on a certain width or diameter. For a single concentrated anchor, a rule of thumb or specific graphical methods (like the Magnel diagram for simple cases) might be used to estimate this force, or the stress distribution.

    *   **4. IS 1343:2012 Clause 24.4.3 - Design of Anchorage Zone for Vertical Prestress:** This clause specifically deals with the design of anchorage zones where prestress is applied vertically. It is less relevant for horizontal prestressing in beams but highlights the importance of considering the stress distribution.

**c) Bearing Stresses**

*   **Cause:** Direct compressive stress between the anchorage plate/cone and the concrete surface.
*   **Design Requirement:** The bearing stress should not exceed the permissible bearing strength of concrete.
*   **IS Code Provision:** IS 1343:2012 provides permissible bearing stresses for concrete, which depend on the stress distribution and the area ratio ($A_p / A_c$), where $A_p$ is the bearing area and $A_c$ is the area of the concrete section.
    *   For plain concrete, the permissible bearing stress is generally limited to about 0.4 to 0.48 times the characteristic compressive strength ($f_{ck}$) at transfer.
    *   With adequate reinforcement, higher bearing stresses can be allowed.

**d) Spalling Stress**

*   **Cause:** Localized tensile stress at the edges of the anchorage zone due to rapid change in stress gradient.
*   **Design:** Primarily handled by providing adequate transverse reinforcement (similar to bursting reinforcement).

**3.3.3 Reinforcement Requirements**

*   **Type of Reinforcement:** Primarily mild steel bars (stirrups/links) and sometimes mesh.
*   **Placement:** Placed concentrically with the prestressing force direction and radially outward from the anchorage.
*   **Area of Reinforcement:** Calculated based on the bursting tensile forces and yield strength of steel. Minimum reinforcement is also specified.
*   **Distribution of Reinforcement:** The reinforcement should be distributed to effectively counteract the tensile stresses. This often means closely spaced stirrups near the anchorage face.

#### 3.4 IS 1343:2012 Specific Clauses and Practical Design Steps

**Key Clauses:**

*   **Clause 24: Anchorage Zones:** Deals with the design principles and requirements for anchorage zones.
*   **Clause 24.4: Design of Anchorage Zones:** Sub-clauses on Type I and Type II zones.
*   **Clause 24.4.2: Type I Anchorage Zone:** Provides guidance for prismatic members.

**Practical Design Steps (Simplified for a common prismatic beam):**

1.  **Determine the Prestressing Force (P):** Based on the required level of prestressing.
2.  **Identify Anchorage Details:** Size and shape of the anchorage plate or cone, and the number of tendons.
3.  **Calculate Bearing Stress:**
    *   Determine the bearing area ($A_p$).
    *   Calculate the bearing stress ($\sigma_{bp} = P / A_p$).
    *   Compare with permissible bearing stress from IS 1343:2012. If insufficient, a thicker end block or higher strength concrete might be needed, or bearing reinforcement.
4.  **Estimate Bursting Tensile Force ($F_{bt}$):** This is the most critical part and often relies on empirical data or simplified analytical methods.
    *   **Simplified Method (for a single anchor distributing force P):** Consider the force $P$ applied over a diameter $D_a$. The bursting force $F_{bt}$ can be estimated as the tensile force acting on a width $W$ at a certain distance from the end.
    *   **IS 1343:2012 Example:** The code refers to distributing the force over a certain angle. For a common case of a circular anchor of diameter $D_a$, the bursting force can be related to the stress distribution in a thick cylinder or by experimental curves. A common practical approximation for estimating the maximum bursting stress ($f_{bt}$) is related to the applied force and the geometry. The bursting tensile force $F_{bt}$ is then this stress multiplied by an effective area.
    *   **IS 1343:2012 (Clause 24.4.2.1):** For prismatic members, the anchorage zone can be designed by considering the distribution of the prestressing force. The design of reinforcement is crucial to resist the bursting tensile stresses.
    *   **Practical Approach:** Many designers use established empirical formulas or software based on stress distribution diagrams for different anchorage types. A common practice is to consider the force acting over a critical cross-section within the end block where tensile stresses are maximum.
    *   **Example Calculation (Conceptual):** If we assume a certain distribution of tensile stress in the end block, we can calculate the total bursting force. For example, if the maximum bursting stress is estimated as $f_{bt}$ acting over a width $W$ and depth $D$, the force might be $F_{bt} = f_{bt} \times W \times D$. The specific formulas for $f_{bt}$ vary but are often related to $P/A_{member}$ and geometry.

5.  **Design Transverse Reinforcement for Bursting:**
    *   Calculate the required area of mild steel reinforcement ($A_{st}$) using $A_{st} = \frac{F_{bt}}{\sigma_{sy}}$, where $\sigma_{sy}$ is the yield strength of mild steel.
    *   Distribute this reinforcement as closely spaced stirrups or links around the prestressing tendons.
    *   Ensure the reinforcement is properly anchored at its ends.
    *   The code might also specify minimum reinforcement requirements for durability and crack control.

6.  **Check for Principal Tensile Stresses:** If a more detailed stress analysis is performed (e.g., using FEA), check the principal tensile stresses against the permissible limits. The transverse reinforcement is designed to handle these as well.

7.  **Consider Additional Reinforcement:**
    *   **Reinforcement to resist shear (if significant):** Although the primary concern is bursting, shear might also be a factor.
    *   **Distribution reinforcement:** To help spread the load and control cracking.

#### 3.5 Examples of Anchorage Systems and their End Block Considerations

*   **Freyssinet Anchorage:** Uses a conical wedge system gripping seven-wire strands. The anchor cone bears on a steel grill or anchor plate. The design focuses on the bursting forces generated by the radial outward thrust of the cone wedges.
*   **BBRV Anchorage:** A strand-gripping system often using wedge segments. Similar considerations for bursting and bearing stresses apply.
*   **Gifford-Ullmann Anchorage:** Uses a barrel-shaped anchor with internal wedges.
*   **Ducts (for post-tensioning):** The duct carries the prestressing force to the anchorage. The end block needs to accommodate the duct and the anchorage assembly.

### 4. PRACTICE QUESTIONS AND EXERCISES

**Question 1:**
What is the primary function of an end block in a prestressed concrete member?

**Answer 1:**
The primary function of an end block is to distribute the concentrated prestressing force from the anchorage system over a larger area of the concrete member, thereby reducing localized stresses and counteracting bursting forces.

---

**Question 2:**
Distinguish between anchorage systems used for pre-tensioned and post-tensioned members.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Pre-tensioned Members:** Rely primarily on the **bond** between the steel tendon and the concrete for anchorage. The force is transferred through adhesion and mechanical interlock over the transmission length.
*   **Post-tensioned Members:** Require **mechanical anchorages** to transfer the prestressing force after the concrete has hardened. Common types include wedge-type anchorages (for strands and wires) and loop anchorages.

---

**Question 3:**
What are the main stresses that need to be considered in the design of end blocks according to IS 1343:2012?

**Answer 3:**
The main stresses to be considered in the design of end blocks are:
1.  **Bursting Stresses:** Outward radial tensile stresses.
2.  **Principal Tensile Stresses:** Diagonal tensile stresses.
3.  **Bearing Stresses:** Localized compressive stresses under the anchorage.
4.  **Spalling Stresses:** Localized tensile stresses at edges.

---

**Question 4 (Conceptual Design):**
A post-tensioned concrete beam uses a Freyssinet anchorage system to transfer a prestressing force of 500 kN. The anchorage consists of a circular anchor plate with a diameter of 150 mm. The concrete strength is M40. Briefly describe the key design considerations for the end block.

**Answer 4:**
The key design considerations for the end block would include:

1.  **Bearing Stress:** Calculate the bearing stress under the 150 mm diameter anchor plate ($P/A_p$). Compare this with the permissible bearing stress for M40 concrete as per IS 1343:2012. The permissible stress depends on the distribution of stress and area ratio.
2.  **Bursting Stresses:** Estimate the bursting tensile force ($F_{bt}$) generated by the anchorage. This force acts radially outwards and is counteracted by transverse reinforcement (stirrups). The design of these stirrups would be based on $F_{bt}$ and the yield strength of mild steel.
3.  **Principal Tensile Stresses:** Analyze the stress distribution in the end block to ensure that principal tensile stresses do not exceed the permissible limits. The transverse reinforcement is also designed to control these stresses.
4.  **Reinforcement Detailing:** Provide adequate mild steel reinforcement (stirrups) in the end block, properly anchored, and distributed to effectively resist the tensile forces. The spacing of stirrups would be closer near the anchorage face.
