# Transportation Engineering: Module 2 - Highway Materials

## Topic: Desirable Properties and Testing of Road Aggregates, Bituminous Materials, and Subgrade Soil

---

This module delves into the fundamental materials that constitute a highway pavement structure, focusing on their essential properties and the methods used to test them. Understanding these materials is crucial for designing durable, safe, and cost-effective roadways.

---

### 1. Road Aggregates

Aggregates are granular materials, either crushed stone, gravel, sand, or slag, that form the major component of most highway pavements. They provide strength, stability, and wear resistance.

#### 1.1 Desirable Properties of Road Aggregates

Aggregates must possess specific properties to perform well in a pavement structure.

*   **Particle Shape and Texture:**
    *   **Importance:** Angular and rough-textured particles interlock better, providing higher shear strength and stability to the mix. Rounded or flaky particles lead to a less stable mix with lower strength.
    *   **Desirable:** Angular, cubical, or flaky.
    *   **Undesirable:** Rounded, elongated, flaky, or thin.

*   **Strength:**
    *   **Importance:** Aggregates must withstand the crushing forces from traffic loads without breaking down.
    *   **Key Tests:** Los Angeles Abrasion Test, Aggregate Impact Value Test, Aggregate Crushing Value Test.

*   **Hardness:**
    *   **Importance:** Resistance to abrasion and scratching, crucial for the surface course where direct contact with tires occurs.
    *   **Key Test:** Los Angeles Abrasion Test.

*   **Toughness:**
    *   **Importance:** Resistance to impact or shock, important for aggregates subjected to dynamic loading from traffic.
    *   **Key Test:** Aggregate Impact Value Test.

*   **Durability (Soundness):**
    *   **Importance:** Resistance to disintegration from weathering processes like frost action and chemical decomposition.
    *   **Key Test:** Soundness Test (using Sodium Sulfate or Magnesium Sulfate).

*   **Adhesion with Bitumen:**
    *   **Importance:** Good adhesion between aggregate and bitumen binder is essential for the structural integrity and water resistance of bituminous mixes.
    *   **Property:** Hydrophilic aggregates (like granite) tend to have poor adhesion with bitumen, especially in the presence of moisture. Hydrophobic aggregates (like basalt) generally have good adhesion.
    *   **Enhancement:** Anti-stripping agents can be added.

*   **Gradation (Particle Size Distribution):**
    *   **Importance:** A well-graded aggregate mix contains a wide range of particle sizes, filling the voids effectively. This results in a dense, stable mix with maximum aggregate interlock and minimum void content.
    *   **Desirable:** Continuous gradation. Avoidance of gap-graded mixes unless specifically designed for.

*   **Free from Clay, Silt, and Organic Matter:**
    *   **Importance:** These impurities can weaken the aggregate, reduce bitumen adhesion, and negatively impact the overall performance of the pavement.
    *   **Key Tests:** Sieve Analysis (to determine fines), Plasticity Tests (for fines).

#### 1.2 Testing of Road Aggregates

A series of standardized tests are performed to evaluate the desirable properties of aggregates.

*   **Sieve Analysis:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the particle size distribution (gradation) of the aggregate.
    *   **Procedure:** Aggregates are passed through a series of sieves of decreasing aperture size. The weight retained on each sieve is measured.
    *   **Outcome:** Gradation curve, which indicates the proportion of aggregate particles within different size ranges.

*   **Specific Gravity and Absorption:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the weight of aggregate particles relative to water and their ability to absorb water.
    *   **Key Values:** Apparent Specific Gravity, Bulk Specific Gravity (SSD - Saturated Surface Dry), Water Absorption.
    *   **Significance:** Used in volumetric calculations for mix design and in aggregate durability tests.

*   **Los Angeles Abrasion Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the aggregate's resistance to crushing, degradation, and abrasion under repeated impact and grinding action.
    *   **Procedure:** A known weight of aggregate is placed in a rotating steel drum along with steel balls. The drum rotates for a specified number of revolutions. The material finer than a 1.70 mm sieve is collected and its percentage loss is calculated.
    *   **Outcome:** Los Angeles Abrasion Value (%). Lower values indicate greater resistance to abrasion.
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Typically < 30% for surface course, < 40% for base course.

*   **Aggregate Impact Value Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the resistance of aggregate to sudden shock or impact.
    *   **Procedure:** A standard sample of aggregate is placed in a cylindrical mold and subjected to repeated blows from a hammer of a specific weight dropped from a standard height. The material passing a 2.36 mm sieve is expressed as a percentage of the total sample weight.
    *   **Outcome:** Aggregate Impact Value (%). Lower values indicate greater toughness.
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Typically < 20% for wearing course, < 30% for base course.

*   **Aggregate Crushing Value Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the resistance of aggregate to crushing under a gradually applied compressive load. Similar in concept to the impact value but with a static load.
    *   **Procedure:** A cylindrical mold is filled with a specific weight of aggregate and subjected to a hydraulic load applied at a standard rate. The percentage of material passing a 2.36 mm sieve is determined.
    *   **Outcome:** Aggregate Crushing Value (%). Lower values indicate greater strength.
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Typically < 30% for wearing course, < 40% for base course.

*   **Soundness Test (Sodium/Magnesium Sulfate Test):**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the resistance of aggregate to disintegration due to changes in temperature and moisture, simulating weathering.
    *   **Procedure:** Aggregates are immersed in a saturated solution of sodium sulfate or magnesium sulfate and then dried at an elevated temperature. This process is repeated for a specified number of cycles. The aggregate is then examined for loss in weight.
    *   **Outcome:** Percentage of loss after a certain number of cycles. Lower loss indicates better soundness.
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Typically < 12% for sodium sulfate, < 18% for magnesium sulfate.

*   **Flakiness and Elongation Index Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To quantify the presence of flaky (thin) and elongated (long) particles in the aggregate sample.
    *   **Procedure:** Uses a flakiness gauge and an elongation gauge to measure the particles.
    *   **Outcome:** Flakiness Index (%) and Elongation Index (%). Higher values indicate a higher proportion of undesirable particles.
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Typically < 15% for flakiness and < 15% for elongation.

### 2. Bituminous Materials

Bituminous materials are binders used in asphalt pavements. They bind the aggregates together to form a cohesive and waterproof layer.

#### 2.1 Types of Bituminous Materials

*   **Bitumen (Asphalt Cement):** A black, viscous, semi-solid or solid hydrocarbon residue obtained from the distillation of crude petroleum. It is the primary binder in hot mix asphalt (HMA).

*   **Cut-back Bitumen:** Bitumen mixed with volatile diluents (solvents) to reduce its viscosity for easier application at lower temperatures (e.g., for cold mix asphalt or chip seals). The solvent evaporates after application, leaving the bitumen binder.

*   **Bituminous Emulsions:** Bitumen dispersed in water in the form of fine droplets, stabilized by an emulsifying agent. They can be cationic (positively charged bitumen droplets) or anionic (negatively charged bitumen droplets), depending on the emulsifying agent. Emulsions allow for application at ambient temperatures.

#### 2.2 Desirable Properties of Bituminous Materials

*   **Viscosity:**
    *   **Importance:** The viscosity of the bitumen binder is critical for its workability during mixing and compaction at elevated temperatures (for HMA) or its flow and adhesion properties at ambient temperatures (for emulsions and cut-backs).
    *   **Property:** Must be fluid enough for proper coating and mixing but viscous enough to bind aggregates effectively.

*   **Adhesion (Affinity for Aggregates):**
    *   **Importance:** The bitumen must adhere strongly to the aggregate surfaces to create a durable and water-resistant pavement layer.
    *   **Property:** Good wetting and film formation on aggregate surfaces.

*   **Ductility:**
    *   **Importance:** The ability of the bitumen to deform or stretch under tensile stress without breaking. This is important for resisting cracking caused by traffic loads and thermal stresses.
    *   **Property:** Measured by the ductility test, which determines the distance a bitumen sample can be elongated before it breaks.

*   **Consistency (Penetration):**
    *   **Importance:** Relates to the hardness or softness of the bitumen at a standard temperature. Softer bitumens are used in colder climates, and harder bitumens in warmer climates.
    *   **Property:** Measured by the penetration test, which indicates the depth a standard needle can penetrate a bitumen sample under a standard load, temperature, and time.

*   **Solubility:**
    *   **Importance:** The bitumen should be soluble in carbon disulphide (or other specified solvents) to ensure it is a pure bitumen product and not contaminated with inert materials.
    *   **Property:** High solubility indicates purity.

*   **Softening Point:**
    *   **Importance:** Indicates the temperature at which the bitumen begins to soften and lose its rigidity. This is important for resisting rutting at high temperatures.
    *   **Property:** Measured by the Ring and Ball Softening Point Test. Higher softening points indicate greater resistance to deformation at high temperatures.

*   **Flash Point and Fire Point:**
    *   **Importance:** These are safety parameters. The flash point is the lowest temperature at which bitumen vapors ignite when exposed to an open flame. The fire point is the temperature at which ignition occurs and continues.
    *   **Property:** A high flash point is desirable to ensure safety during handling and heating.

*   **Resistance to Aging:**
    *   **Importance:** Bitumen can age due to oxidation and polymerization, leading to hardening and embrittlement. Resistance to aging is crucial for long-term pavement performance.

#### 2.3 Testing of Bituminous Materials

*   **Penetration Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To measure the consistency (hardness/softness) of bitumen.
    *   **Procedure:** A standard needle with a specific load (100g) is allowed to penetrate the bitumen sample for 5 seconds at a standard temperature (25°C). The depth of penetration is measured in tenths of a millimeter.
    *   **Outcome:** Penetration value (e.g., 80/100, meaning penetration between 80 and 100 tenths of a millimeter).

*   **Ductility Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To measure the ductility of bitumen.
    *   **Procedure:** A bitumen sample is molded into a standard briquette shape, cooled to a specific temperature (e.g., 25°C), and then pulled apart at a standard rate of speed. The distance the sample stretches before breaking is measured.
    *   **Outcome:** Ductility value (in cm).
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Typically > 50 cm for paving grade bitumen.

*   **Softening Point Test (Ring and Ball):**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the temperature at which bitumen softens and begins to flow.
    *   **Procedure:** A bitumen sample is placed in a brass ring, and a standard steel ball is placed on top. The assembly is immersed in water, and the temperature of the water is gradually increased. The temperature at which the ball touches a plate at the bottom of the ring is recorded as the softening point.
    *   **Outcome:** Softening point temperature (°C).

*   **Viscosity Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To measure the resistance to flow of bitumen at a given temperature.
    *   **Methods:** Saybolt-Furol Viscometer (for higher viscosity bitumen) or capillary viscometers.
    *   **Outcome:** Viscosity in Saybolt seconds or kinematic viscosity.

*   **Solubility Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the percentage of bitumen soluble in a specific solvent (e.g., carbon disulphide or trichloroethylene).
    *   **Procedure:** A sample of bitumen is dissolved in the solvent. The insoluble residue is filtered, dried, and weighed.
    *   **Outcome:** Percentage of soluble bitumen.
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Typically > 97%.

*   **Flash and Fire Point Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the safety parameters of bitumen.
    *   **Procedure:** The bitumen sample is heated, and a small flame is applied periodically to detect the flash point. The heating continues until the bitumen ignites and burns, indicating the fire point.
    *   **Outcome:** Flash point (°C) and Fire point (°C).
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** Flash point typically > 175°C for paving bitumen.

*   **Thin Film Oven Test (TFO Test):**
    *   **Purpose:** To simulate the aging of bitumen during mixing and compaction at elevated temperatures and to measure the loss in weight and change in penetration and ductility.
    *   **Procedure:** A thin film of bitumen is exposed to a circulating air oven at a specified temperature for a specific time.
    *   **Outcome:** Residue from the oven is then tested for properties like penetration, ductility, and viscosity.

*   **Rolling Thin Film Oven Test (RTFOT):**
    *   **Purpose:** Similar to TFO, but the bitumen sample is in a rotating glass bottle, providing a more representative simulation of aging during handling. It's used to evaluate the susceptibility of bitumen to oxidation.

*   **Stripping Test (e.g., Bitumen-Aggregate Immersion Test):**
    *   **Purpose:** To evaluate the susceptibility of a bitumen-aggregate bond to the damaging effects of water.
    *   **Procedure:** Bitumen-coated aggregates are immersed in water, and the extent of stripping (loss of bitumen film) is visually assessed.

### 3. Subgrade Soil

The subgrade is the natural soil formation on which the pavement structure is built. It is the foundation of the entire pavement system.

#### 3.1 Desirable Properties of Subgrade Soil

The subgrade soil's strength and stability are paramount for the long-term performance of the pavement.

*   **Strength and Load-Bearing Capacity:**
    *   **Importance:** The subgrade must be able to support the stresses transmitted from the overlying pavement layers under traffic loads without excessive deformation.
    *   **Property:** High modulus of elasticity and shear strength.

*   **Stability:**
    *   **Importance:** The subgrade should not undergo significant volume changes (swelling or shrinking) with fluctuations in moisture content or temperature.
    *   **Property:** Low plasticity, resistance to frost heave.

*   **Drainage:**
    *   **Importance:** Good drainage of the subgrade is essential to prevent moisture accumulation, which can significantly reduce its strength and lead to pavement distress.
    *   **Property:** Permeability, often influenced by the soil's grain size distribution.

*   **Compressibility:**
    *   **Importance:** Low compressibility is desired to prevent excessive settlement of the pavement structure.
    *   **Property:** Low void ratio and good compaction.

*   **Uniformity:**
    *   **Importance:** A uniform subgrade provides consistent support across the pavement area. Variations in soil properties can lead to differential settlements and pavement distress.

#### 3.2 Classification of Subgrade Soils

Subgrade soils are typically classified based on their engineering properties to understand their suitability. Common classification systems include:

*   **AASHTO Soil Classification System (American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials):**
    *   Divides soils into eight main groups (A-1 to A-8) based on grain size distribution, plasticity, and other properties.
    *   **Group Index (GI):** A numerical value within each group that further indicates the quality of the subgrade. Lower GI values (0-10) are generally desirable.
        *   **GI Formula:** $GI = (F-35)[0.2 + 0.005(LL-40)] + 0.01(F-15)(P_t-55)$ (where F is % passing 0.075mm sieve, LL is liquid limit, Pt is % passing 0.425mm sieve).

*   **Unified Soil Classification System (USCS):**
    *   Classifies soils into groups based on grain size (gravels, sands, silts, clays) and plasticity characteristics.
    *   **Major Divisions:** Coarse-grained soils (Gravels G, Sands S), Fine-grained soils (Silts M, Clays C), Organic soils (O), Peats (Pt).

#### 3.3 Testing of Subgrade Soil

*   **Sieve Analysis:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the grain size distribution and classify the soil (e.g., for USCS or AASHTO).
    *   **Procedure:** Same as for aggregates.

*   **Atterberg Limits Tests (Liquid Limit, Plastic Limit, Plasticity Index):**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the plasticity characteristics of fine-grained soils.
    *   **Liquid Limit (LL):** The moisture content at which the soil passes from a plastic to a liquid state.
    *   **Plastic Limit (PL):** The moisture content at which the soil passes from a plastic to a semi-solid state.
    *   **Plasticity Index (PI):** The difference between the Liquid Limit and the Plastic Limit ($PI = LL - PL$).
    *   **Significance:** High PI soils (clays) are prone to volume changes (swelling/shrinking) and can have lower strength when wet. Low PI or non-plastic soils are generally more stable.
    *   **Acceptable Limits:** For subgrades, a PI of less than 10 is generally preferred.

*   **Moisture-Density Relationship Test (Proctor Compaction Test):**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the maximum dry density ($ \rho_{d,max} $) and the optimum moisture content (OMC) for a given soil.
    *   **Procedure:** Soil is compacted in a mold using a standard rammer at different moisture contents. The dry density is calculated for each moisture content.
    *   **Outcome:** A curve plotting dry density against moisture content, showing a peak value ($ \rho_{d,max} $) at the OMC.
    *   **Significance:** Indicates the best effort to achieve a dense, stable subgrade through compaction.

*   **California Bearing Ratio (CBR) Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To measure the strength or load-bearing capacity of a compacted soil or pavement layer. It's a comparative test, relating the strength of the soil to that of a standard crushed stone.
    *   **Procedure:** A compacted soil sample is subjected to a load applied by a plunger of a specific diameter at a standard rate. The load required to cause a certain penetration is recorded. The CBR value is expressed as a percentage of the load required for a standard crushed stone.
    *   **Outcome:** CBR value (%). Higher values indicate greater strength.
    *   **Significance:** A fundamental parameter for pavement design, used to determine the required thickness of pavement layers.
    *   **Testing:** Typically performed on soil compacted to its OMC and maximum dry density, either in a soaked or unsoaked condition. Soaked CBR is usually more critical.

*   **Triaxial Compression Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the shear strength parameters (cohesion 'c' and angle of internal friction '$ \phi $' ) of the soil.
    *   **Procedure:** Cylindrical soil samples are subjected to a confining pressure and an axial load until failure. Tests are conducted at different confining pressures to establish the Mohr-Coulomb failure envelope.
    *   **Significance:** Provides fundamental strength parameters used in pavement analysis and design.

*   **Plate Load Test:**
    *   **Purpose:** To determine the modulus of elasticity or bearing capacity of the subgrade in situ.
    *   **Procedure:** A rigid plate is placed on the subgrade, and load is applied incrementally. The settlement of the plate is measured for each load increment.
    *   **Outcome:** Load-settlement curve, from which the modulus of subgrade reaction (k) can be determined.

### Key Concepts and Definitions Summary

*   **Aggregates:** Granular materials forming the bulk of pavement layers.
*   **Bitumen:** Viscous hydrocarbon binder.
*   **Subgrade:** Natural soil foundation for the pavement.
*   **Gradation:** Particle size distribution of aggregates.
*   **Plasticity Index (PI):** Measure of clay's tendency to deform.
*   **Los Angeles Abrasion Value:** Resistance to wear and degradation.
*   **Aggregate Impact Value:** Resistance to sudden shock.
*   **Ductility (Bitumen):** Ability to stretch without breaking.
*   **Penetration (Bitumen):** Measure of hardness/softness.
*   **CBR:** Measure of subgrade strength relative to a standard.
*   **OMC/Maximum Dry Density:** Optimal moisture content for maximum soil compaction.

---

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Aggregate Shape and Texture:** Angular and rough aggregates provide better interlocking and higher stability.
*   **Aggregate Durability:** Crucial for resistance to weathering and freeze-thaw cycles.
*   **Bitumen Adhesion:** Essential for pavement durability and water resistance; consider using anti-stripping agents if needed.
*   **Bitumen Consistency:** Dictates its suitability for different climate conditions.
*   **Subgrade Strength:** Directly impacts the required thickness of pavement layers. CBR is a key parameter.
*   **Subgrade Plasticity:** High PI soils are prone to volume changes and can lead to instability.
*   **Compaction:** Achieves maximum density for aggregates and subgrade, leading to higher strength and stability.
*   **Moisture:** Detrimental to subgrade strength and can cause stripping in bituminous mixes. Proper drainage is vital.

### Answers to Practice Questions

**A1.**
b) Los Angeles Abrasion Test

**A2.**
c) Stretch without breaking.

**A3.**
b) Prone to swelling and shrinking with moisture variations.

**A4.**
b) To find the optimum moisture content and maximum dry density.

**A5.**
Good adhesion ensures that the bitumen binder effectively coats and binds the aggregate particles, forming a cohesive and waterproof pavement layer. This cohesion provides the necessary tensile strength and stability to the pavement under traffic loads. Factors affecting adhesion include:
*   **Aggregate Surface Properties:** Hydrophobic aggregates (e.g., basalt) generally have better adhesion than hydrophilic aggregates (e.g., granite).
*   **Presence of Moisture:** Water can displace bitumen from the aggregate surface (stripping), weakening the bond.
*   **Bitumen Properties:** The viscosity and chemical composition of the bitumen influence its ability to wet and adhere to aggregates.
*   **Presence of Fines:** Clay and dust on aggregate surfaces can hinder proper adhesion.

**A6.**
The wearing course (surface layer) of a road is subjected to the most severe conditions, including direct contact with traffic tires, abrasion, and weathering. The Los Angeles Abrasion Value quantifies the aggregate's resistance to crushing, degradation, and abrasion. A low LA Abrasion Value indicates that the aggregate is hard and tough, and will resist wear and breakdown under traffic, ensuring the longevity and skid resistance of the road surface. Aggregates with high LA values will break down quickly, leading to a rough surface, loss of material, and reduced pavement performance.

**A7.**
The subgrade is the natural soil foundation upon which the entire pavement structure rests. Its primary role is to provide stable support for the loads transmitted from the overlying pavement layers.
Implications of a weak subgrade:
*   **Reduced Load-Bearing Capacity:** A weak subgrade cannot effectively distribute traffic loads, leading to excessive stresses on the pavement layers.
*   **Increased Pavement Thickness:** A weaker subgrade requires thicker pavement layers to adequately spread the load and prevent excessive deformation or rutting.
*   **Excessive Settlement:** The pavement may settle differentially, leading to unevenness and cracking.
*   **Premature Pavement Failure:** Weak subgrades can lead to premature failure of the pavement structure, requiring costly repairs and rehabilitation.
*   **Susceptibility to Moisture Damage:** If the subgrade has poor drainage and high plasticity, it can become saturated, significantly reducing its strength and contributing to pavement distress like pumping and stripping.
