# Applied Hydrology and Climatology: Module 2 - Causes and Effects of Climate Change

## Topic: Classification of Streams - Stream Pattern and Stream Order

---

### **Introduction**

Understanding stream morphology, specifically their patterns and orders, is crucial in hydrology. These classifications help us analyze drainage basin characteristics, predict water flow, and understand the impact of various factors, including climate change, on fluvial systems. This topic delves into how streams are categorized based on their spatial arrangement (pattern) and their hierarchical structure (order).

*   Describe and differentiate between various stream patterns.

### **1. Stream Order: Hierarchical Classification**

Stream order is a method of quantifying the size and complexity of a river network. It assigns a numerical value to each stream segment based on its position within the dendritic drainage system.

#### **1.1 Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Drainage Basin (Catchment Area):** The total area of land where precipitation collects and drains off into a common outlet, such as a river, lake, or ocean.
*   **Tributary:** A stream or river that flows into a larger stream or river.
*   **Confluence:** The point where two or more streams or rivers join.
*   **Dendritic Drainage Pattern:** A tree-like pattern where tributaries join the main stream at acute angles, resembling the branching of a tree. This is the most common type.

#### **1.2 Methods of Stream Ordering**

Several systems exist, but the most widely used are the **Strahler Order** and the **Shreve Order**. We will focus on the **Strahler Order** as it's more commonly applied.

##### **1.2.1 Strahler Order (Modified Horton Order)**

*   **Definition:** A system that assigns an order to each stream segment based on the highest order of its upstream tributaries.
*   **How it works:**
    *   **First-Order Stream:** A stream segment that has no tributaries. These are typically the smallest, uppermost streams.
    *   **Second-Order Stream:** Formed when two first-order streams merge.
    *   **Third-Order Stream:** Formed when two second-order streams merge.
    *   **General Rule:** When two stream segments of the same order merge, the resulting downstream segment is one order higher.
    *   **Important Note:** When a higher-order stream merges with a lower-order stream, the order of the resulting downstream segment remains that of the higher-order stream.

*   **Example:**
    *   Imagine a small mountain stream with no tributaries – it's a 1st-order stream.
    *   Two such 1st-order streams meet to form a 2nd-order stream.
    *   If another 1st-order stream joins this 2nd-order stream, the combined segment remains a 2nd-order stream.
    *   However, if two 2nd-order streams meet, their confluence creates a 3rd-order stream.

##### **1.2.2 Shreve Order**

*   **Definition:** A system where the order of a stream segment is the sum of the orders of all upstream contributing segments.
*   **How it works:**
    *   The order of a stream segment is the number of its upstream tributaries.
    *   At a confluence, the order of the downstream segment is the sum of the orders of the upstream segments.
*   **Example (same scenario as above):**
    *   First-order streams: Order 1.
    *   When two 1st-order streams merge: The downstream segment becomes Order 1 + 1 = 2.
    *   If a 1st-order stream joins a 2nd-order stream: The downstream segment becomes Order 2 + 1 = 3.

#### **1.3 Significance of Stream Order**

*   **Basin Size and Complexity:** Higher stream orders generally indicate larger and more complex drainage basins.
*   **Discharge and Sediment Transport:** Higher-order streams typically have greater discharge, carry more sediment, and have wider channels.
*   **Ecological Habitat:** Stream order is a significant factor in determining the types of aquatic habitats and species found in a river system.
*   **Hydrological Modeling:** Stream order is a fundamental parameter used in hydrological models for predicting flow, flood risk, and water quality.

### **2. Stream Pattern: Spatial Arrangement of Streams**

Stream pattern refers to the visual appearance of a river network across the landscape. It describes the geometric arrangement of stream channels within a drainage basin.

#### **2.1 Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Channel:** The path that a river or stream follows.
*   **Meander:** A bend or curve in a river channel.
*   **Oxbow Lake:** A crescent-shaped lake formed when a meander loop is cut off from the main river channel.

#### **2.2 Common Stream Patterns**

##### **2.2.1 Dendritic Pattern**

*   **Description:** Resembles a tree or a vein network. Tributaries join the main stream at acute angles.
*   **Causes/Influences:** Develops on relatively uniform rock types where erosion rates are consistent. The underlying geology has little influence on the drainage direction.
*   **Example:** Most river systems in gently sloping landscapes with uniform bedrock. The Mississippi River system is a classic example.

##### **2.2.2 Rectangular Pattern**

*   **Description:** Channels are characterized by a series of right-angle bends and are roughly parallel or perpendicular to each other.
*   **Causes/Influences:** Occurs in areas with prominent jointing or faulting in the bedrock, where fractures control the drainage paths.
*   **Example:** Streams flowing through areas with strongly jointed metamorphic or igneous rocks.

##### **2.2.3 Trellis Pattern**

*   **Description:** A series of long, parallel main streams with shorter tributaries joining them at right angles. It looks like a garden trellis.
*   **Causes/Influences:** Forms in regions with alternating resistant and less resistant rock layers that are folded into long, parallel anticlines and synclines. The main streams erode along the softer rock, and the tributaries drain the slopes of the resistant ridges.
*   **Example:** Streams in folded mountain belts like the Appalachian Mountains.

##### **2.2.4 Radial Pattern**

*   **Description:** Streams originate from a central elevated point and flow outward in all directions, like spokes on a wheel.
*   **Causes/Influences:** Typically found on the flanks of isolated volcanic cones, domes, or other upwarped structures.
*   **Example:** Rivers flowing from a shield volcano or a dissected dome.

##### **2.2.5 Annular Pattern**

*   **Description:** Streams form concentric rings around a central structural dome or basin.
*   **Causes/Influences:** Develops on areas of uplifted sedimentary rocks where erosion has exposed weaker underlying rock layers that form a structural basin.
*   **Example:** Streams surrounding a dissected structural dome.

##### **2.2.6 Parallel Pattern**

*   **Description:** Streams run parallel to each other over a considerable distance.
*   **Causes/Influences:** Occurs on steep, uniformly sloping land, such as the flanks of mountain ranges. The tributaries are short and join the main stream at acute angles.
*   **Example:** Streams flowing down the steep slopes of a newly uplifted mountain range.

##### **2.2.7 Deranged Pattern**

*   **Description:** An irregular, haphazard network of streams with no organized pattern. Often characterized by numerous swamps, lakes, and poorly defined channels.
*   **Causes/Influences:** Associated with areas that have been heavily glaciated. Glacial till and moraines disrupt the pre-existing drainage, creating blocked valleys, kettle lakes, and disorganized drainage.
*   **Example:** Drainage systems in areas recently covered by glaciers, such as parts of Canada and Scandinavia.

### **3. Factors Influencing Stream Patterns and Order**

Several factors interact to shape the pattern and order of a river network:

*   **Geology (Lithology and Structure):**
    *   **Uniformity:** Uniform rock types promote dendritic patterns.
    *   **Fractures/Joints:** Control rectangular and sometimes angular patterns.
    *   **Folding/Tilting:** Leads to trellis and parallel patterns.
    *   **Differential Erosion:** Creates patterns based on varying rock resistance.
*   **Topography (Slope and Relief):**
    *   **Steep Slopes:** Can lead to parallel patterns.
    *   **Gentle Slopes:** Often result in dendritic patterns.
    *   **Elevated Features (Volcanoes, Domes):** Lead to radial and annular patterns.
*   **Tectonics:**
    *   **Faulting:** Can influence channel alignment and create fault-controlled patterns.
    *   **Uplift/Subsidence:** Can create new drainage divides or alter existing ones.
*   **Climate:**
    *   **Precipitation Amount and Intensity:** Affects discharge and erosion rates.
    *   **Glaciation:** Can create deranged patterns and alter existing drainage.
*   **Vegetation Cover:**
    *   **Root Systems:** Stabilize soil, influencing erosion and channel development.
    *   **Canopy Cover:** Affects infiltration and overland flow.
*   **Time:** The longer a landscape is exposed to erosion, the more developed and characteristic the drainage pattern becomes.

---

### **4. Relating Stream Patterns and Order to Drainage Basin Characteristics**

The stream pattern and order of a drainage basin are intrinsically linked to its underlying physical characteristics.

*   **Drainage Density:** A measure of the total length of stream channels per unit area of the basin. Basins with higher stream orders and more complex patterns (like dendritic) often have higher drainage densities, indicating more efficient water collection and runoff.
*   **Stream Order Distribution:** The relative proportion of different stream orders in a basin provides insights into its age and geomorphic development.
*   **Basin Shape:** The overall shape of a basin (e.g., long and narrow vs. compact and circular) can influence its drainage network development.
*   **Gradient:** The steepness of the terrain affects the velocity of water flow and the erosive power of streams, influencing channel morphology and pattern.

### **5. Impacts of Climate Change on Stream Patterns and Orders**

Climate change is a significant driver of geomorphic change, and its effects on river systems can manifest as alterations in stream patterns and orders.

*   **Changes in Precipitation Regimes:**
    *   **Increased Intensity:** More intense rainfall events can lead to increased bank erosion, widening of channels, and potentially the formation of more anastomosing (interconnecting) patterns in some areas.
    *   **Shifts in Seasonal Rainfall:** Altered seasonality can affect streamflow variability, influencing sediment transport and channel stability.
    *   **Droughts:** Reduced streamflow can lead to channel simplification and potentially the abandonment of some tributaries, effectively reducing the stream order in affected areas.
*   **Changes in Temperature:**
    *   **Glacier and Snowmelt:** Changes in meltwater patterns can alter discharge seasonality and volume, impacting channel morphology and the persistence of higher-order streams, particularly in mountainous regions. Earlier and more intense melt can lead to flashier floods.
    *   **Permafrost Thaw:** In permafrost regions, thawing can destabilize slopes, leading to increased erosion, landslides, and changes in channel patterns (e.g., formation of thermokarst lakes and more convoluted channel networks).
*   **Sea Level Rise:**
    *   **Coastal River Systems:** Can lead to backwater effects, increased sediment deposition in estuaries, and changes in the mixing zone, potentially influencing lower-order stream confluences and channel characteristics.
*   **Increased Extreme Weather Events:**
    *   **Floods:** Severe floods can reshape channels, causing avulsion (sudden change in river course), creating new oxbow lakes, and altering the network structure, potentially leading to a temporary simplification of some patterns.
    *   **Landslides/Debris Flows:** Can dramatically alter the input of sediment and water into river systems, changing channel morphology and connectivity.

*   **Overall Impact:** While it's complex to predict precise changes, climate change can lead to:
    *   **Shifts in drainage density.**
    *   **Increased channel instability and meandering.**
    *   **Alterations in the hierarchical structure of river networks (stream orders).**
    *   **Reorganization of drainage patterns in response to new hydrological regimes and geomorphic drivers.**

---

### **Key Points to Remember**

*   **Stream order** quantifies the hierarchy of streams within a drainage basin, with the Strahler system being most common.
*   **Stream patterns** describe the spatial arrangement of channels and are controlled by geology, topography, and tectonics.
*   **Dendritic patterns** are common on uniform terrain, while **rectangular** and **trellis** patterns indicate geological control.
*   **Radial patterns** form on elevated features, and **deranged patterns** are linked to glaciation.
*   Climate change can significantly alter stream patterns and orders through changes in precipitation, temperature, and extreme weather events.

### **Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**
Stream order is a quantitative measure of the size and complexity of a river network, assigning a numerical value to each stream segment based on its position and the number of upstream tributaries. Using the Strahler system, when two stream segments of the same order merge, the resulting downstream segment is assigned an order one higher.

**Answer 2:**
    a) **Rectangular Pattern:** Characterized by channels with right-angle bends, often flowing in parallel or perpendicular directions. It typically develops in areas with prominent jointing or faulting in the bedrock, where fractures control the drainage paths.
    b) **Trellis Pattern:** Features long, parallel main streams with shorter tributaries joining at right angles. It forms in regions with alternating resistant and less resistant rock layers that are folded into parallel anticlines and synclines, with main streams eroding along softer rock and tributaries draining the resistant ridges.
    c) **Radial Pattern:** Streams originate from a central elevated point and flow outward in all directions. It is commonly found on the flanks of isolated volcanic cones, domes, or other upwarped structures.

**Answer 3:**
The stream pattern is most likely **Deranged**. A common cause for this pattern is **glaciation**, where glacial deposits like till and moraines disrupt pre-existing drainage, leading to blocked valleys, kettle lakes, and disorganized flow.

**Answer 4:**
An increase in the intensity of rainfall events due to climate change can lead to increased bank erosion, channel widening, and potentially the development of more **anastomosing** (interconnecting) channel patterns in some areas, as higher flows mobilize more sediment and reshape the riverbanks. It can also lead to more frequent and severe floods, which can cause avulsions and alter the network structure.

**Answer 5:**
Changes in glacier melt, such as earlier or more intense melt, can significantly impact stream order.
*   **Earlier/More Intense Melt:** This can lead to higher peak discharges in the spring and summer. These increased flows can cause greater erosion, potentially widening channels and strengthening lower-order tributaries. It might also lead to the temporary or permanent abandonment of some very small, ephemeral tributaries if they cannot sustain flow throughout the year, effectively lowering the effective stream order in certain segments. The overall impact could be a shift towards larger, more persistent stream orders in the long term if the increased discharge leads to channel enlargement and greater stability for higher-order streams. Conversely, extreme flash floods from rapid melt could also destabilize channels, leading to unpredictable changes.
