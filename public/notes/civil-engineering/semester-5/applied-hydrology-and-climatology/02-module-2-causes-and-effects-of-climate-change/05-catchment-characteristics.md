# APPLIED HYDROLOGY AND CLIMATOLOGY: MODULE 2 - CAUSES AND EFFECTS OF CLIMATE CHANGE

## Topic: Catchment Characteristics

---

This topic explores how the physical features and properties of a catchment (also known as a watershed or drainage basin) influence hydrological processes, and how these characteristics are being impacted by and interact with climate change. Understanding catchment characteristics is fundamental to predicting and managing water resources in a changing climate.

---

### Learning Outcomes:

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Define and explain the significance of key catchment characteristics** in hydrological analysis and their relevance to climate change.
*   **Identify and describe various methods for characterizing a catchment**, including topographic, geological, soil, vegetation, and land-use attributes.
*   **Analyze how different catchment characteristics influence hydrological responses** such as runoff, infiltration, evapotranspiration, and groundwater recharge.
*   **Discuss the implications of climate change on catchment characteristics** and how these changes, in turn, feedback into hydrological processes.
*   **Explain the importance of considering catchment characteristics** for effective water resource management and adaptation strategies under climate change.

### 1. Key Concepts and Definitions

#### 1.1 What is a Catchment?

*   **Definition:** A catchment is the total area of land where all precipitation (rain, snow, etc.) that falls within it drains to a common outlet, typically a river, lake, or ocean. It is also referred to as a watershed or drainage basin.
*   **Significance:** Understanding catchment boundaries is crucial because it defines the spatial extent of water movement and the area influenced by various hydrological processes. Actions within a catchment can impact water quality and quantity downstream.

#### 1.2 Hydrological Processes within a Catchment

*   **Precipitation:** The input of water into the catchment system.
*   **Interception:** The retention of precipitation by vegetation canopy, which may evaporate before reaching the ground.
*   **Infiltration:** The process of water seeping into the soil.
*   **Runoff:** The flow of water over the land surface (surface runoff) or through the soil (subsurface runoff/interflow) towards a stream channel.
*   **Evapotranspiration (ET):** The combined process of evaporation from surfaces (soil, water bodies) and transpiration from plants. This is a major water loss from a catchment.
*   **Percolation/Infiltration:** Deeper movement of water through the soil profile into the unsaturated and saturated zones.
*   **Groundwater Recharge:** The replenishment of groundwater aquifers.
*   **Groundwater Flow:** The movement of water through underground aquifers.
*   **Streamflow:** The total volume of water flowing in a river or stream at a specific point.

#### 1.3 Importance of Catchment Characteristics in Hydrology and Climate Change

*   **Hydrological Response:** Catchment characteristics fundamentally control how precipitation is converted into streamflow, how much water infiltrates, how much is lost to ET, and how quickly water moves through the system.
*   **Climate Change Impacts:** Climate change alters precipitation patterns (intensity, frequency, form), temperature, and potentially ET rates. These changes interact with existing catchment characteristics to produce new hydrological outcomes. For example, a catchment with a high proportion of impervious surfaces will respond more rapidly and intensely to extreme rainfall events, which are projected to increase in frequency under climate change.
*   **Water Resource Management:** Understanding these interactions is vital for predicting future water availability, managing flood and drought risks, and developing effective adaptation strategies for water infrastructure and ecosystems.

### 2. Methods for Characterizing a Catchment

Catchment characteristics can be broadly categorized and measured. Modern analysis heavily relies on Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and remote sensing.

#### 2.1 Topographic Characteristics

*   **Definition:** Features related to the shape and elevation of the land surface.
*   **Key Metrics and Their Significance:**
    *   **Catchment Area (A):** The total surface area contributing to the outlet.
        *   *Significance:* Larger catchments generally have larger water yields but may have more complex flow paths and longer response times.
    *   **Catchment Shape:**
        *   **Length (L):** The longest distance from the outlet to the furthest point in the catchment along the main drainage line.
        *   **Width (W):** Average width of the catchment.
        *   **Shape Factor (e.g., Elongation Ratio = L / sqrt(A)):** A dimensionless index describing the catchment's compactness.
        *   *Significance:* Elongated catchments tend to have lower peak flows and longer response times compared to compact (circular) catchments, as water from different parts of the catchment arrives at the outlet at different times.
    *   **Slope (S):** The average gradient of the catchment.
        *   *Significance:* Steeper slopes lead to faster surface runoff and infiltration, potentially increasing flood peaks and reducing groundwater recharge. Gentler slopes promote slower flow, more infiltration, and longer residence times.
    *   **Elevation:** The range of altitudes within the catchment.
        *   *Significance:* Influences temperature (and thus precipitation form – rain vs. snow), vegetation types, and snowmelt contributions to streamflow. Higher elevations are more vulnerable to changes in snowpack dynamics under climate change.
    *   **Drainage Density (Dd):** The total length of stream channels per unit area (Dd = Total stream length / Catchment area).
        *   *Significance:* High drainage density indicates a well-dissected catchment, leading to rapid response to rainfall and higher peak flows. Low drainage density suggests slower drainage and less surface runoff.
    *   **Stream Order (Strahler's or Shreve's):** A system for classifying stream network hierarchy.
        *   *Significance:* Higher stream orders generally correlate with larger discharge and watershed areas.

*   **Measurement Methods:**
    *   **Digital Elevation Models (DEMs):** Provide elevation data that can be used to delineate catchment boundaries, calculate slopes, derive drainage networks, and measure lengths and areas using GIS software.
    *   **Topographic Maps:** Traditional source for manual analysis.

#### 2.2 Geological Characteristics

*   **Definition:** The underlying rock types, geological structures (faults, folds), and bedrock properties.
*   **Key Aspects and Their Significance:**
    *   **Permeability/Porosity of Bedrock:**
        *   *Significance:* Highly permeable bedrock (e.g., sandstone, fractured limestone) can store and transmit large volumes of groundwater, influencing baseflow to streams and drought resilience. Impermeable bedrock (e.g., granite, shale) limits groundwater storage and flow, leading to more rapid surface runoff.
    *   **Geological Structures:** Faults and fractures can act as preferential pathways for groundwater flow.
    *   **Rock Type:** Affects weathering rates, soil formation, and the chemical composition of water.

*   **Measurement Methods:**
    *   **Geological Maps:** Show the distribution of rock types and structures.
    *   **Hydrogeological Surveys:** Investigate groundwater flow and aquifer properties.

#### 2.3 Soil Characteristics

*   **Definition:** The physical and chemical properties of the soil layer covering the catchment surface.
*   **Key Aspects and Their Significance:**
    *   **Soil Texture:** The proportion of sand, silt, and clay.
        *   *Significance:* Sandy soils are highly permeable, promoting infiltration and reducing runoff. Clayey soils are less permeable, leading to higher runoff and potential for soil saturation.
    *   **Soil Structure:** The arrangement of soil particles into aggregates.
        *   *Significance:* Good soil structure enhances infiltration and aeration. Poor structure (e.g., compacted soil) impedes water movement.
    *   **Soil Depth:** The thickness of the soil layer.
        *   *Significance:* Deeper soils can store more water, acting as a buffer and supporting more robust vegetation. Shallow soils lead to rapid runoff.
    *   **Soil Permeability/Hydraulic Conductivity:** The ease with which water moves through the soil.
        *   *Significance:* Directly influences infiltration rates and the speed of subsurface water movement.
    *   **Soil Moisture Content:** The amount of water held in the soil.
        *   *Significance:* Pre-existing soil moisture conditions determine how much rainfall can infiltrate before saturation occurs. Dry soils can absorb more initially.

*   **Measurement Methods:**
    *   **Soil Maps:** Provide information on soil types and their distribution.
    *   **Field Soil Surveys:** In-situ measurements of texture, structure, depth, and infiltration rates.
    *   **Laboratory Analysis:** Characterization of soil samples.

#### 2.4 Vegetation Characteristics (Land Cover/Land Use)

*   **Definition:** The type, density, and distribution of plants and human-modified surfaces within the catchment.
*   **Key Aspects and Their Significance:**
    *   **Vegetation Type:**
        *   *Significance:* Forests have high interception rates, significant root systems promoting infiltration, and high evapotranspiration. Grasslands have moderate interception and ET, and good infiltration. Croplands can vary greatly depending on cultivation practices. Impervious surfaces (urban areas, roads) have minimal interception, very low infiltration, and high runoff.
    *   **Vegetation Cover Density/Canopy Cover:**
        *   *Significance:* Denser vegetation cover generally leads to more interception, higher ET, and increased infiltration due to root channels.
    *   **Root Depth and Architecture:**
        *   *Significance:* Deeper roots can access water from lower soil layers, sustaining ET during dry periods. They also create macropores that enhance infiltration.
    *   **Leaf Area Index (LAI):** The total area of leaves per unit ground area.
        *   *Significance:* A measure of vegetation density, directly related to interception and ET potential.
    *   **Land Use/Land Cover (LULC):**
        *   *Significance:* Urbanization increases imperviousness, leading to flashier floods and reduced groundwater recharge. Deforestation can increase erosion, sediment transport, and runoff peaks, while also potentially decreasing ET. Afforestation can enhance infiltration and reduce flood risk.

*   **Measurement Methods:**
    *   **Remote Sensing (Satellite Imagery, Aerial Photography):** Used to map LULC patterns, vegetation indices (like NDVI), and canopy cover.
    *   **Ground-based Surveys:** Field verification and detailed vegetation assessments.

#### 2.5 Hydrological Characteristics

*   **Definition:** Properties directly related to water flow and storage within the catchment.
*   **Key Aspects and Their Significance:**
    *   **Channel Network Characteristics:**
        *   **Stream Length and Density:** As discussed under topographic characteristics.
        *   **Channel Shape and Roughness:** Affect flow velocity and flood wave propagation.
    *   **Storage Capacities:**
        *   **Soil Water Storage:** The amount of water the soil can hold.
        *   **Groundwater Storage:** The volume of water in aquifers.
        *   **Surface Water Storage:** Lakes, reservoirs, wetlands.
        *   *Significance:* Storage acts as a buffer, regulating streamflow and mitigating extremes of flood and drought.
    *   **Baseflow:** The portion of streamflow derived from groundwater.
        *   *Significance:* Indicates the contribution of groundwater reserves to sustained flow, especially during dry periods. High baseflow is often supported by permeable geology and significant soil/groundwater storage.
    *   **Lag Time:** The time between the center of mass of rainfall and the center of mass of the resulting hydrograph.
        *   *Significance:* A measure of how quickly a catchment responds to rainfall. Influenced by steepness, drainage density, soil infiltration, and storage.

*   **Measurement Methods:**
    *   **Stream Gauging:** Direct measurement of streamflow over time to develop hydrographs.
    *   **Groundwater Monitoring:** Wells to measure water levels and aquifer properties.
    *   **Hydrological Modeling:** Using input data (rainfall, catchment characteristics) to simulate streamflow and other hydrological variables.

### 4. Implications of Climate Change on Catchment Characteristics and Hydrological Processes

Climate change is not just about changing precipitation and temperature; it also directly and indirectly alters catchment characteristics, leading to complex feedback loops.

#### 4.1 Direct Impacts of Climate Change on Catchment Characteristics

*   **Vegetation Changes:**
    *   **Shifts in Species Composition:** Warmer temperatures and altered precipitation can favor some plant species over others, changing forest types, grassland composition, and potentially leading to the encroachment of invasive species or the decline of native ones.
    *   **Increased Wildfire Frequency/Intensity:** Warmer, drier conditions can lead to more frequent and severe wildfires. This can cause significant changes to vegetation cover, soil properties (e.g., hydrophobicity), and increase erosion.
    *   **Changes in Forest Health:** Drought stress, pest outbreaks (often exacerbated by climate change), and increased storm damage can alter forest structure and density.
*   **Soil Degradation:**
    *   **Increased Erosion:** More intense rainfall events and altered vegetation cover can lead to increased soil erosion, reducing soil depth and altering soil properties.
    *   **Soil Compaction:** Changes in farming practices driven by climate variability or increased heavy machinery use for disaster relief can lead to soil compaction, reducing infiltration.
*   **Changes in Snowpack and Glaciers:**
    *   **Reduced Snow Cover/Earlier Snowmelt:** Warmer temperatures cause more precipitation to fall as rain instead of snow and lead to earlier melting of existing snowpack. This shifts the timing of peak streamflow and can reduce water availability in late spring and summer.
    *   **Glacier Retreat:** Melting glaciers, a significant water source for many regions, will eventually disappear, leading to a long-term reduction in water availability and altered flow regimes.
*   **Permafrost Thaw:** In arctic and alpine regions, thawing permafrost can destabilize landscapes, alter drainage patterns, and release stored carbon.
*   **Land Use Changes (Indirectly driven):**
    *   **Increased Urbanization:** Pressure for new development to accommodate climate refugees or changes in agricultural suitability can lead to further land conversion, increasing impervious surfaces.
    *   **Changes in Agricultural Practices:** Farmers may adapt by changing crop types, irrigation demands, or land management practices, which can alter soil properties and water use.

#### 4.2 Feedback Loops: How Changed Characteristics Affect Hydrology

*   **Loss of Forest Cover (due to fire, pests, logging):**
    *   *Hydrological Effect:* Decreased interception and ET, leading to increased runoff volume and faster peak flows. Increased soil exposure leads to higher erosion and sediment load in rivers. Reduced infiltration can lead to lower groundwater recharge.
*   **Increased Impervious Surfaces (due to urbanization):**
    *   *Hydrological Effect:* Significantly increases the proportion of rainfall becoming surface runoff, leading to more frequent and severe flash floods, reduced groundwater recharge, and altered stream baseflows (often lower due to less recharge).
*   **Changes in Snowmelt Timing:**
    *   *Hydrological Effect:* Earlier snowmelt means higher flows in early spring and potentially lower flows in summer, impacting water availability for irrigation, hydropower, and ecosystems.
*   **Soil Hydrophobicity (after fires):**
    *   *Hydrological Effect:* Water is repelled by the soil, leading to increased surface runoff and erosion, even with moderate rainfall.
*   **Permafrost Thaw:**
    *   *Hydrological Effect:* Can create new wetlands, alter stream channels, and release stored water, leading to unpredictable changes in flow regimes.

### 5. Importance of Considering Catchment Characteristics for Water Resource Management and Adaptation

*   **Accurate Hydrological Modeling:** Understanding catchment characteristics is essential for calibrating and validating hydrological models that predict future water availability, flood risks, and drought impacts under climate change scenarios.
*   **Targeted Adaptation Strategies:**
    *   **Flood Management:** In catchments with high imperviousness or steep slopes, strategies like green infrastructure (e.g., permeable pavements, rain gardens) in urban areas or improved forest management in mountainous regions are crucial.
    *   **Drought Management:** Catchments with good soil depth, forest cover, and permeable geology are more resilient to drought due to better water storage and groundwater recharge. Management efforts can focus on enhancing these characteristics (e.g., reforestation, soil conservation).
    *   **Water Supply Reliability:** Understanding how snowmelt timing or glacier retreat will affect streamflow is critical for reservoir operation and planning water allocations.
*   **Ecosystem Health:** Changes in flow regimes and water availability directly impact aquatic ecosystems. Management needs to consider how catchment characteristics influence these impacts.
*   **Integrated Water Resource Management (IWRM):** A holistic approach that considers all aspects of water use and management within a catchment. This requires a thorough understanding of the catchment's physical and ecological characteristics.
*   **Catchment Restoration and Management:** Efforts to restore degraded catchments (e.g., reforestation, soil erosion control) are often most effective when tailored to specific catchment characteristics.

### 6. Practice Questions/Exercises

**Question 1: Defining Key Terms**

Define the following terms:
a) Catchment
b) Drainage Density
c) Baseflow
d) Interception

**Answer 1:**
a) **Catchment:** The total area of land where all precipitation drains to a common outlet.
b) **Drainage Density:** The total length of stream channels per unit area of the catchment.
c) **Baseflow:** The portion of streamflow that is derived from groundwater outflow.
d) **Interception:** The retention of precipitation by vegetation canopy, which may evaporate before reaching the ground.

**Question 2: Impact of Characteristics on Runoff**

Describe how each of the following catchment characteristics would influence the peak flow of a river following a heavy rainfall event:
a) High drainage density
b) Predominance of sandy soils
c) Presence of extensive forest cover
d) High proportion of impervious surfaces

**Answer 2:**
a) **High drainage density:** Would lead to a **higher peak flow** due to a more efficient and interconnected network of channels, allowing water to reach the outlet quickly.
b) **Predominance of sandy soils:** Would lead to a **lower peak flow** as sandy soils have high infiltration rates, absorbing a significant portion of the rainfall and reducing surface runoff.
c) **Presence of extensive forest cover:** Would lead to a **lower peak flow** due to interception by the canopy, increased infiltration facilitated by root systems and forest floor, and higher evapotranspiration.
d) **High proportion of impervious surfaces:** Would lead to a **significantly higher peak flow** as there is minimal infiltration and interception, resulting in most of the rainfall becoming surface runoff and reaching the outlet rapidly.

**Question 3: Climate Change and Catchment Feedback**

Explain how an increase in the frequency and intensity of wildfires in a mountainous catchment could lead to changes in its hydrological response to rainfall, considering the feedback mechanisms.

**Answer 3:**
An increase in wildfire frequency and intensity would first directly affect the vegetation and soil characteristics. The removal of forest cover reduces interception and evapotranspiration, meaning more rainfall reaches the ground. The forest floor litter, which typically enhances infiltration, is also removed. Wildfires can also bake the soil surface, making it hydrophobic (water-repellent).

These changes lead to significant hydrological feedback.
1.  **Increased Surface Runoff:** Due to reduced interception, lower ET, and soil hydrophobicity, a larger proportion of rainfall will become surface runoff.
2.  **Higher Peak Flows:** The increased surface runoff, combined with potentially altered drainage patterns (e.g., ash clogging small channels), will lead to faster and higher peak flows in rivers during rainfall events.
3.  **Increased Erosion and Sediment Transport:** Bare soil and hydrophobic surfaces are highly susceptible to erosion by rainfall. This will increase the sediment load in rivers, impacting water quality and potentially altering channel morphology.
4.  **Reduced Groundwater Recharge:** Less water infiltrating the soil means less water reaching the groundwater table, potentially leading to lower baseflows during dry periods.
5.  **Altered Flow Regimes:** The overall effect will be a shift towards more flashy and extreme flow conditions, with higher floods and potentially lower dry-season flows.

**Question 4: GIS Application**

Imagine you are given a Digital Elevation Model (DEM) of a catchment. What three key topographic characteristics can you derive from it, and how might they influence the catchment's response to climate change-induced extreme rainfall?

**Answer 4:**
From a DEM, you can derive:
1.  **Catchment Area and Shape:**
    *   *Influence:* A large, compact catchment might experience a rapid and high peak flow from extreme rainfall if other factors are conducive. A large, elongated catchment might have a more spread-out response over time, but the total volume could still be significant.
2.  **Slope (Average and Distribution):**
    *   *Influence:* Steep slopes will accelerate surface runoff, exacerbating the effects of extreme rainfall by leading to faster and higher flood peaks and increased erosion potential. Gentler slopes might allow for more infiltration if soil conditions permit, but with very intense rainfall, they can still contribute to significant runoff volume.
3.  **Drainage Density:**
    *   *Influence:* A high drainage density in a catchment with steep slopes and clayey soils will amplify the response to extreme rainfall, leading to very rapid and high peak flows. If the catchment has low drainage density, the response might be slower but still significant in volume.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Catchment characteristics are interconnected.** Changes in one characteristic (e.g., vegetation) can influence others (e.g., soil properties, infiltration).
*   **Topography sets the fundamental physical controls** on water movement within a catchment.
*   **Soil and geology govern infiltration and water storage**, acting as crucial buffers.
*   **Vegetation/Land Cover significantly modifies** how precipitation is processed, influencing ET, interception, and infiltration.
*   **Climate change directly alters some catchment characteristics** (e.g., vegetation, snowpack) and indirectly influences others (e.g., land use changes driven by climate impacts).
*   **Understanding these interactions is vital for predicting future hydrological behaviour** and developing effective water resource management and adaptation strategies.
*   **GIS and remote sensing are powerful tools** for characterizing and analyzing catchments.
