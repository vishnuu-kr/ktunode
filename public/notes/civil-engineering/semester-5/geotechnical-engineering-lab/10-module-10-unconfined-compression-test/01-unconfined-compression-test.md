# GEOTECHNICAL ENGINEERING LAB - Module 10: Unconfined Compression Test

## 1. Introduction to the Unconfined Compression Test (UCT)

The Unconfined Compression Test (UCT) is a fundamental laboratory test used in geotechnical engineering to determine the **unconfined compressive strength** ($q_u$) of a soil sample. It is one of the simplest and most common tests performed on cohesive soils, particularly clays.

### Key Concepts:

*   **Unconfined Compressive Strength ($q_u$)**: The maximum axial stress a soil sample can withstand without any lateral support before failure. It is a measure of the soil's shear strength.
*   **Cohesive Soils**: Soils that possess shear strength due to the electrochemical forces between soil particles, even in the absence of external confinement. This includes clays and silts.
*   **Shear Strength ($\tau$)**: The resistance of a soil mass to shearing stresses. In the context of UCT, it's often approximated by $c_u$ (undrained shear strength).
*   **Undrained Shear Strength ($c_u$)**: The shear strength of a cohesive soil under undrained conditions (rapid loading, where pore water pressure cannot dissipate). For saturated clays tested under unconfined conditions, $q_u \approx 2c_u$.
*   **Failure**: The point at which the soil sample can no longer sustain increasing load and exhibits a significant increase in strain with little or no increase in stress.

## 2. Purpose and Importance of the UCT

The UCT is crucial for several reasons in geotechnical engineering:

*   **Shear Strength Determination:** It provides a direct measure of the soil's shear strength, which is essential for analyzing the stability of slopes, foundations, and retaining walls.
*   **Soil Classification:** The UCT results can be used to classify cohesive soils based on their strength properties.
*   **Quality Control:** It's used in construction to verify the strength of compacted fill materials or dredged materials.
*   **Foundation Design:** The unconfined compressive strength is often used to estimate the bearing capacity of shallow foundations.
*   **Preliminary Strength Assessment:** It's a quick and simple test for an initial assessment of a soil's strength.

## 3. Equipment and Materials

The following equipment and materials are typically required for a UCT:

*   **Compression Testing Machine:** A calibrated machine capable of applying a controlled axial load at a constant rate of strain or stress. This could be:
    *   **Motor-driven loading frame:** Applies load at a constant rate of displacement.
    *   **Spring-loaded apparatus:** Applies load at a constant rate of strain (less common now).
*   **Soil Sample:** Typically a cylindrical specimen.
*   **Measuring Instruments:**
    *   **Calipers/Ruler:** For measuring the initial dimensions (diameter and height) of the sample.
    *   **Loading Platen/Pads:** Smooth, flat surfaces to ensure uniform stress application to the sample ends.
*   **Sample Trimming Tools:**
    *   **Wire saw or trimming knife:** To trim the sample to the desired dimensions.
    *   **Mold or extrusion device:** To obtain cylindrical samples from larger blocks or core samples.
*   **Balance:** For determining the mass of the sample.
*   **Data Recording Sheets/Software:** For recording load and deformation readings.

## 4. Sample Preparation

Proper sample preparation is critical for obtaining reliable UCT results.

### Types of Samples:

1.  **Undisturbed Samples:** Obtained from the field using thin-walled samplers (e.g., Shelby tubes) to minimize disturbance to the soil structure. These are preferred for accurate strength assessment.
2.  **Remolded/Compacted Samples:** Prepared in the lab by compacting soil at a specific moisture content and density to simulate field conditions.
3.  **Cylindrical Specimens:** The standard shape for the UCT. Typical dimensions are:
    *   **Height-to-Diameter Ratio:** Commonly 2:1 (e.g., 100 mm height, 50 mm diameter). Other ratios (e.g., 1:1) are also used but can affect the measured strength.

### Preparation Steps:

1.  **Trimming to Cylindrical Shape:**
    *   For undisturbed samples from tubes, carefully extrude the soil.
    *   Use a wire saw or trimming knife to cut the sample to the required length and diameter, ensuring the ends are flat and parallel.
    *   Avoid disturbing the soil structure during trimming.
2.  **Measuring Dimensions:**
    *   Measure the initial diameter of the sample at least at three equally spaced points along the height and average them.
    *   Measure the initial height of the sample.
3.  **Moisture Content Determination:**
    *   Trim a small portion from the ends of the sample (or a representative sample if multiple are tested) to determine the initial moisture content in accordance with standard methods (e.g., oven-drying). This is important for correlating strength with moisture content.
4.  **Mass Determination:**
    *   Weigh the trimmed sample accurately. This can be used to calculate the bulk density.

**Important Point:** For cohesive soils, it's essential to keep the samples moist during preparation and testing to prevent drying and changes in strength. Use a spray bottle with water or cover samples with plastic wrap.

## 5. Conducting the Unconfined Compression Test

The test involves applying a compressive load to the prepared soil sample at a controlled rate.

### Procedure:

1.  **Place the Sample:** Carefully place the prepared cylindrical soil sample between the loading platens of the compression testing machine. Ensure the sample is centered and the platens are parallel.
2.  **Set Initial Readings:**
    *   Set the deformation indicator (e.g., dial gauge or digital sensor) to zero.
    *   Set the load indicator to zero.
3.  **Apply Load at a Constant Rate:**
    *   Start the testing machine to apply load at a constant rate of strain or deformation.
    *   **Rate of Strain:** The rate should be slow enough to allow for some pore water pressure dissipation in more permeable cohesive soils, but fast enough to represent undrained conditions for most clays. A typical rate of strain is 0.5% to 2% per minute of the sample's original height. The rate is usually specified by the relevant standard (e.g., ASTM D2166, BS 1377).
4.  **Record Data:**
    *   Record the applied load (or deformation) at regular intervals of deformation (or load).
    *   Continue applying the load until the sample fails or the deformation reaches a predetermined maximum (e.g., 15-20% strain).
5.  **Observe Failure:**
    *   Monitor the sample for signs of failure, which may manifest as:
        *   A significant drop in load after reaching a peak.
        *   A barrel-like bulging or shearing along a failure plane.
        *   A rapid increase in deformation with little increase in load.

## 6. Data Analysis and Calculations

The recorded data is used to calculate the unconfined compressive strength and other properties.

### Calculations:

1.  **Calculate Corrected Load:**
    *   The raw load readings from the testing machine might need correction based on the machine's calibration.
2.  **Calculate Stress ($\sigma$)**:
    *   For each load reading, calculate the axial stress applied to the sample using the **original cross-sectional area ($A_0$)**:
        $$ \sigma = \frac{\text{Load}}{A_0} $$
    *   Where $A_0 = \frac{\pi D_0^2}{4}$, and $D_0$ is the initial diameter of the sample.
3.  **Calculate Strain ($\epsilon$)**:
    *   For each deformation reading, calculate the axial strain:
        $$ \epsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0} $$
    *   Where $\Delta L$ is the change in length (deformation) and $L_0$ is the initial height of the sample.
4.  **Determine Unconfined Compressive Strength ($q_u$)**:
    *   Plot a stress-strain curve ($\sigma$ vs. $\epsilon$).
    *   The unconfined compressive strength ($q_u$) is the **maximum stress** the sample reached on the stress-strain curve.
    *   **If a distinct peak stress is observed:** $q_u$ is the peak stress.
    *   **If no peak is observed and the stress continues to increase up to a certain strain (e.g., 15%):** $q_u$ is the stress at that maximum strain.
    *   **If the stress drops after the peak:** $q_u$ is the peak stress.

### Other Parameters:

*   **Undrained Shear Strength ($c_u$)**: For saturated clays tested rapidly (undrained conditions), the unconfined compressive strength is approximately twice the undrained shear strength:
    $$ q_u \approx 2 c_u $$
    Therefore,
    $$ c_u = \frac{q_u}{2} $$
*   **Strain at Failure ($\epsilon_f$)**: The strain corresponding to $q_u$.
*   **Bulk Density ($\rho_b$)**:
    $$ \rho_b = \frac{\text{Mass of sample}}{\text{Initial Volume of sample}} = \frac{M}{V_0} $$
    Where $V_0 = A_0 \times L_0 = \frac{\pi D_0^2}{4} \times L_0$.
*   **Moisture Content ($w$)**: Determined from sample preparation.

**Important Point:** Using the original cross-sectional area for stress calculation assumes that the sample is tested under undrained conditions and that the lateral expansion is prevented by the ends (though in reality, it bulges). This simplification is valid for the definition of $q_u$ in UCT.

## 7. Interpretation of Results

The interpretation involves analyzing the stress-strain curve and the calculated strength values.

### Stress-Strain Curve:

*   **Shape of the Curve:** The shape provides insights into the soil's behavior.
    *   **Peak then Strain Softening:** Typical for sensitive clays or normally consolidated clays. The soil reaches a peak strength and then softens as the strain increases.
    *   **Peak then Constant Stress:** Characteristic of stiffer, overconsolidated clays.
    *   **No distinct peak:** The stress continues to increase up to the maximum strain, indicating a more ductile behavior.
*   **Slope of the Initial Portion:** The initial slope of the stress-strain curve is related to the Young's modulus ($E$) of the soil, but this is rarely calculated precisely from UCT due to the non-linear nature of soil response.

### Shear Strength Interpretation:

*   The calculated $c_u$ value represents the undrained shear strength of the soil at the tested moisture content and density.
*   This value is critical for various geotechnical designs:
    *   **Slope stability:** To determine the factor of safety.
    *   **Bearing capacity:** To estimate the ultimate bearing capacity of shallow foundations.
    *   **Settlement analysis:** To assess the stiffness and deformation characteristics.

**Example Interpretation:**

If a clay sample has $q_u = 100 \text{ kPa}$, then its undrained shear strength $c_u = 50 \text{ kPa}$. If this soil is found at a depth of 2m below a proposed footing, this $c_u$ value would be used in bearing capacity calculations. If the test shows a sharp peak followed by a significant drop in stress, it suggests the clay is sensitive and its strength can be significantly reduced by disturbance.

## 8. Limitations and Applicability of the UCT

While useful, the UCT has specific limitations:

### Limitations:

*   **Applicability to Cohesive Soils Only:** The test is generally not suitable for granular soils (sands and gravels) because they cannot be prepared into stable, unconfined cylindrical samples.
*   **Undrained Conditions Assumption:** The test is considered an undrained test, meaning pore water pressure changes are not measured, and it assumes rapid loading. This is appropriate for saturated clays with low permeability.
*   **Sensitivity to Sample Disturbance:** The strength of cohesive soils is highly sensitive to disturbance. Even careful sampling can alter the soil structure and its measured strength.
*   **End Restraint Effects:** The platens provide some lateral restraint at the sample ends, which can slightly increase the measured strength compared to a perfectly unconfined condition.
*   **No Pore Water Pressure Measurement:** The test does not directly measure pore water pressures, which are crucial for understanding soil behavior under drained conditions or when pore pressure dissipation is significant.
*   **Not Representative of Field Stress Conditions:** The unconfined state does not reflect the confining pressures that soil typically experiences in the ground.

### Applicability:

*   **Saturated Clays and Silts:** Most applicable to fine-grained soils that exhibit some cohesion.
*   **Normally Consolidated and Overconsolidated Clays:** Can be used for both, but interpretations might differ.
*   **Quick Strength Assessment:** Useful for rapid field or lab assessment of clay strength.
*   **Quality Control of Embankments:** Checking the strength of compacted clay fills.

**Important Point:** For granular soils or when drained conditions are relevant, other tests like the Triaxial Compression Test (drained or undrained with pore pressure measurement) are more appropriate.

## 10. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
The primary property of a soil that the Unconfined Compression Test (UCT) determines is its **Shear Strength**. Specifically, it determines the unconfined compressive strength ($q_u$).

**Answer 2:**
The relationship between unconfined compressive strength ($q_u$) and undrained shear strength ($c_u$) for a saturated clay sample tested under undrained conditions is approximately: **B. $q_u = 2c_u$**.

**Answer 3:**
Two ways in which a soil sample's preparation can affect the results of a UCT are:
1.  **Disturbance of Soil Structure:** If the sample is obtained from the field (undisturbed sample), any disturbance during extrusion or trimming can alter the soil's natural structure, leading to a lower measured strength. For remolded samples, the compaction method, moisture content, and density significantly influence the strength.
2.  **Drying Out:** If the sample is allowed to dry out during preparation or between preparation and testing, its moisture content will decrease, leading to an artificially higher measured strength. Cohesive soils are sensitive to moisture content changes.

**Answer 4 (Calculation):**
Given:
Initial diameter, $D_0 = 38$ mm
Initial height, $L_0 = 76$ mm
Maximum applied load, $P_{max} = 650$ N

a) **Initial cross-sectional area ($A_0$)**:
$A_0 = \frac{\pi D_0^2}{4}$
$A_0 = \frac{\pi (38 \text{ mm})^2}{4}$
$A_0 = \frac{\pi (1444 \text{ mm}^2)}{4}$
$A_0 = 1130.97 \text{ mm}^2$

To express in $m^2$: $A_0 = 1130.97 \text{ mm}^2 \times \frac{(1 \text{ m})^2}{(1000 \text{ mm})^2} = 0.001131 \text{ m}^2$

b) **Unconfined compressive strength ($q_u$)**:
$q_u = \frac{P_{max}}{A_0}$
Using $A_0$ in $mm^2$ and load in N gives strength in N/mm$^2$ (MPa):
$q_u = \frac{650 \text{ N}}{1130.97 \text{ mm}^2}$
$q_u = 0.5747 \text{ N/mm}^2$
$q_u = 0.5747 \text{ MPa}$

c) **Approximate undrained shear strength ($c_u$)**:
$q_u \approx 2 c_u$
$c_u = \frac{q_u}{2}$
$c_u = \frac{0.5747 \text{ MPa}}{2}$
$c_u = 0.2874 \text{ MPa}$
Or, $c_u \approx 287.4 \text{ kPa}$

## 11. Important Points to Remember

*   **UCT is for Cohesive Soils:** Primarily for clays and silts.
*   **$q_u$ is the Maximum Stress:** Determined from the peak of the stress-strain curve.
*   **$c_u = q_u / 2$:** For saturated clays under undrained conditions.
*   **Sample Preparation is Key:** Minimize disturbance and prevent drying.
*   **Rate of Strain Matters:** Affects pore water pressure and measured strength.
*   **Use Original Area ($A_0$) for Stress:** This is a defining characteristic of UCT calculations.
*   **Limitations:** No pore pressure measurement, end restraint, not for granular soils.
*   **Field Application:** Strength data is used in slope stability, bearing capacity, and foundation design.
