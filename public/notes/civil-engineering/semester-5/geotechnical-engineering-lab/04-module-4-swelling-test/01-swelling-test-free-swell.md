# GEOTECHNICAL ENGINEERING LAB: Module 4 - Swelling Test

## Topic: Free Swell Test

---

### **1. Introduction to Swelling Test and Free Swell**

*   **Purpose:** The Free Swell test is a fundamental laboratory procedure used to determine the potential for a soil to swell when exposed to water. This is particularly important for soils containing expansive clay minerals, which absorb water and increase in volume.
*   **Significance of Swelling:**
    *   **Foundation Instability:** Swelling of soil beneath foundations can lead to significant uplift pressures, causing structural damage (cracking, differential settlement).
    *   **Pavement Distress:** Swelling in subgrade soils can cause heaving and cracking of pavements.
    *   **Slope Stability:** Swelling can alter pore water pressures, potentially reducing the shear strength of soil and impacting slope stability.
*   **Free Swell Test Definition:** The Free Swell test is a simple, qualitative test that measures the **percentage increase in volume** of a soil sample when it is immersed in water without any applied load or constraint. It's a quick indicator of a soil's swelling potential.

### **3. Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Expansive Soils:** Soils containing significant amounts of clay minerals (e.g., montmorillonite, smectite) that exhibit a large volume change upon wetting and drying cycles.
*   **Clay Minerals:** Layered silicate minerals that have a high surface area and can readily adsorb water between their layers, causing expansion.
*   **Swelling Pressure:** The pressure exerted by an expansive soil when it swells against a restraint.
*   **Free Swell:** The maximum volume increase of a soil sample when it is allowed to swell freely in water without any applied stress.
*   **Free Swell Index (FSI):** The percentage of volume increase of a soil sample in the Free Swell test. It is a measure of the soil's swelling potential.

---

### **4. Apparatus Required**

The following apparatus are typically used for the Free Swell test:

*   **Graduated Cylinder:** A clear glass cylinder with volume markings (e.g., 100 mL or 250 mL). This is the primary container for the soil and water.
*   **Scoop or Spoon:** For transferring the soil sample into the graduated cylinder.
*   **Measuring Tape or Scale:** For initial measurement of the soil sample height (if using a cylindrical sample, though typically dry powder is used).
*   **Balance:** For weighing the dry soil sample (optional but good practice for consistency).
*   **Water:** Distilled or deionized water is preferred to avoid mineral interference.
*   **Oven:** For drying the soil sample to a constant weight (typically at 105-110°C).
*   **Sieve:** A sieve of size 425 microns (or 400 microns) for preparing the soil sample.

---

### **5. Procedure for Performing the Free Swell Test**

The Free Swell test is a straightforward procedure:

1.  **Sample Preparation:**
    *   Take an air-dried soil sample.
    *   Pass the soil through a **425-micron sieve** (or a similar specified sieve size). Discard any particles retained on the sieve. This ensures that the test is sensitive to the fine-grained portion of the soil.
    *   Ensure the soil is in a friable condition (crumbles easily).

2.  **Initial Measurement:**
    *   Fill the clean, dry graduated cylinder to a **specific mark** with the sieved soil. Typically, **10 cm³ (or 10 mL)** of dry soil is used.
    *   **Important:** Do not compact the soil. The soil should be placed loosely into the cylinder.

3.  **Adding Water:**
    *   Carefully add **distilled or deionized water** to the graduated cylinder until the soil is completely submerged.
    *   Slowly add water to avoid disturbing the loosely placed soil.
    *   Fill the cylinder to a convenient level (e.g., the top).

4.  **Observation and Measurement:**
    *   Allow the soil to settle and swell. The process can take several hours.
    *   After **24 hours** (a standard observation period), gently tap the cylinder to dislodge any soil particles that might be clinging to the sides.
    *   Record the **final volume** occupied by the swollen soil in the graduated cylinder. Note the level of the top surface of the swollen soil.

---

### **6. Calculation of Free Swell Percentage (FSI)**

The Free Swell Index (FSI) is calculated as follows:

$$ \text{FSI} (\%) = \frac{(\text{Final Volume} - \text{Initial Volume})}{\text{Initial Volume}} \times 100 $$

Where:

*   **Initial Volume:** The volume of dry soil taken at the beginning of the test (e.g., 10 cm³).
*   **Final Volume:** The volume occupied by the swollen soil after 24 hours in the graduated cylinder.

**Example Calculation:**

Let's say you used **10 cm³** of dry sieved soil as your initial volume. After 24 hours, the swollen soil occupies a volume of **18 cm³** in the graduated cylinder.

$$ \text{FSI} (\%) = \frac{(18 \text{ cm}^3 - 10 \text{ cm}^3)}{10 \text{ cm}^3} \times 100 $$
$$ \text{FSI} (\%) = \frac{8 \text{ cm}^3}{10 \text{ cm}^3} \times 100 $$
$$ \text{FSI} (\%) = 0.8 \times 100 = 80\% $$

Therefore, the Free Swell Index for this soil is 80%.

---

### **7. Interpretation of Results**

The Free Swell Index (FSI) provides a qualitative indication of the soil's swelling potential. While classifications can vary slightly between different agencies and standards, a general interpretation is as follows:

| Free Swell Index (FSI) | Swelling Potential | Engineering Significance                                                                 |
| :--------------------- | :----------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| < 10%                  | Very Low           | Minimal swelling concern.                                                              |
| 10% - 20%              | Low                | Slight swelling potential, may require consideration in critical structures.         |
| 20% - 50%              | Medium             | Moderate swelling potential. Requires careful design consideration for foundations.   |
| 50% - 100%             | High               | Significant swelling potential. Foundations must be designed to mitigate uplift.      |
| > 100%                 | Very High          | Extreme swelling potential. May require specialized foundation techniques or soil modification. |

**Example Interpretation:**

In the previous example, an FSI of 80% indicates a **High** swelling potential. This means the soil is likely to cause significant uplift pressures on structures if not properly addressed.

---

### **8. Limitations of the Free Swell Test**

While useful as a screening tool, the Free Swell test has several limitations:

*   **Qualitative Nature:** It provides a percentage but doesn't directly yield swelling pressure or strain under confinement.
*   **No Applied Load:** The test is performed without any external load, which is unrealistic for most engineering applications where soil is confined.
*   **Sensitivity to Sample Preparation:** The loosely packed nature of the sample can lead to variability in results if not performed consistently.
*   **Does Not Quantify Swelling Pressure:** The test doesn't measure the force the soil exerts when it swells against a restraint, which is a critical parameter for foundation design.
*   **Effect of Water Chemistry:** The test uses distilled water. In reality, groundwater chemistry can influence the swelling behavior of clays.
*   **Particle Size Influence:** While sieved, the interaction of particles in a loose state might differ from their behavior in a compacted state.

---

### **9. Practical Applications of the Free Swell Test**

The Free Swell test is primarily used as:

*   **Preliminary Screening Test:** To quickly identify soils with potential swelling characteristics. If a soil exhibits a high FSI, further, more detailed testing (like the Oedometer Swell Test) is warranted.
*   **Classification of Expansive Soils:** Used in conjunction with other Atterberg limit tests to classify soils as potentially expansive.
*   **Field Identification:** While not a direct field test, the results can inform engineers about the likely behavior of a soil encountered in the field.

---

### **10. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Sieve Size:** Always use the specified sieve size (typically 425 microns) for sample preparation.
*   **Loose Compaction:** Do not compact the soil sample in the graduated cylinder.
*   **Distilled Water:** Use distilled or deionized water for consistency.
*   **24-Hour Observation:** Record the final volume after 24 hours.
*   **Interpretation is Relative:** FSI values are best interpreted in comparison to established guidelines and other soil properties.
*   **Complementary Testing:** The Free Swell test should ideally be complemented by more advanced tests (e.g., Oedometer Swell Test) for critical designs.

### **11. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**

A geotechnical engineer conducts a Free Swell test on a soil sample. They take 10 cm³ of air-dried soil passed through a 425-micron sieve. After 24 hours, the swollen soil occupies a volume of 25 cm³ in the graduated cylinder. Calculate the Free Swell Index (FSI) of the soil.

**Answer 1:**

*   Initial Volume = 10 cm³
*   Final Volume = 25 cm³
*   $$ \text{FSI} (\%) = \frac{(25 \text{ cm}^3 - 10 \text{ cm}^3)}{10 \text{ cm}^3} \times 100 $$
*   $$ \text{FSI} (\%) = \frac{15 \text{ cm}^3}{10 \text{ cm}^3} \times 100 $$
*   $$ \text{FSI} (\%) = 1.5 \times 100 = 150\% $$

**Question 2:**

Interpret the swelling potential of a soil sample that yields an FSI of 35%.

**Answer 2:**

An FSI of 35% falls in the range of 20% - 50%, which indicates a **Medium** swelling potential. This suggests that the soil has a moderate tendency to swell and requires careful consideration in foundation design to mitigate potential uplift.

**Question 3:**

List three limitations of the Free Swell test.

**Answer 3:**

1.  It is qualitative and does not directly measure swelling pressure or strain under confinement.
2.  The test is conducted without any applied load, which is not representative of real-world conditions.
3.  Results can be sensitive to variations in sample preparation and the loosely packed nature of the soil.

**Question 4:**

Why is it important to sieve the soil sample to 425 microns before performing the Free Swell test?

**Answer 4:**

Sieving the soil to 425 microns ensures that the test is primarily sensitive to the fine-grained portion of the soil (fines and clay), which are responsible for the swelling phenomenon. Larger particles (coarser sand and gravel) have negligible swelling potential, and their inclusion could mask the true swelling behavior of the clay fraction.
