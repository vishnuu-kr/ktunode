### 1. Necessity of Storage Structures in Irrigation

Irrigation is the artificial application of water to land to supplement rainfall and support crop growth. However, rainfall is often erratic and unevenly distributed throughout the year. This leads to periods of water scarcity even in regions with good average rainfall. Storage structures are crucial to bridge this gap.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Irrigation:** Artificial application of water to land to meet the water requirements of crops.
*   **Rainfall Variability:** The fluctuation of rainfall amounts and distribution over time and space.
*   **Water Scarcity:** The condition where available water resources are insufficient to meet the demand.
*   **Water Demand:** The amount of water required for agricultural, domestic, industrial, and environmental purposes.

**Why are Storage Structures Necessary?**

*   **Bridging Supply-Demand Gaps:** Rainfall often occurs during non-cropping seasons or in excess during monsoon periods, while crops require water during dry periods. Storage structures capture surplus water during periods of plenty and release it during periods of scarcity.
*   **Managing Erratic Rainfall:** Natural rainfall patterns are unpredictable. Storage helps to regulate water availability despite variations in rainfall.
*   **Meeting Peak Water Demands:** Crops have different water requirements at different growth stages. Peak demand often occurs during dry periods, which storage can satisfy.
*   **Flood Control:** Reservoirs can store excess floodwaters, reducing flood damage downstream.
*   **Hydropower Generation:** Stored water can be released through turbines to generate electricity.
*   **Water Supply for Other Purposes:** Reservoirs can serve as sources for domestic, industrial, and environmental water supply.
*   **Recreation and Navigation:** Large reservoirs can be used for recreational activities and improving navigation.

### 2. Benefits and Ill Effects of Irrigation (Brief Overview – as context for reservoirs)

While the focus is on reservoirs, understanding the broader impact of irrigation is important.

**Benefits of Irrigation:**

*   **Increased Crop Production:** Ensures consistent water supply, leading to higher yields and multiple cropping seasons.
*   **Food Security:** Contributes significantly to national food security by stabilizing agricultural output.
*   **Economic Development:** Supports rural economies through increased agricultural productivity and related industries.
*   **Drought Mitigation:** Protects crops from the adverse effects of droughts.
*   **Land Reclamation:** Allows cultivation of arid and semi-arid lands.

**Ill Effects of Irrigation:**

*   **Waterlogging:** Excessive irrigation or poor drainage can lead to a rise in the groundwater table, saturating the root zone and harming crops.
*   **Salinization:** In arid and semi-arid regions, the evaporation of irrigation water leaves behind dissolved salts in the soil, making it infertile.
*   **Spread of Waterborne Diseases:** Stagnant irrigation water can become breeding grounds for disease vectors like mosquitoes.
*   **Sedimentation:** Rivers carry sediment, which can accumulate in reservoirs, reducing their storage capacity over time.
*   **Environmental Impacts:**
    *   **Alteration of River Regimes:** Reduced downstream flow can affect riverine ecosystems.
    *   **Habitat Loss:** Construction of dams and reservoirs can inundate land, displacing communities and destroying habitats.
    *   **Changes in Water Quality:** Evaporation can concentrate salts and pollutants in remaining water.

### 3. Irrigation Structures – Storage Structures – Reservoirs

**Definition:**

A **reservoir** is an artificial or natural lake used for the storage of water. In the context of irrigation, it is typically created by constructing a dam across a river or stream to impound water.

**Purpose of Reservoirs in Irrigation:**

*   To store water from periods of surplus (e.g., monsoon) for use during periods of deficit (e.g., dry season).
*   To regulate river flow for consistent water supply to irrigation canals.

---

#### 3.1. Types of Reservoirs

Reservoirs can be classified based on various criteria:

**A. Based on the Purpose of Storage:**

*   **Multi-purpose Reservoirs:** Designed to serve multiple objectives simultaneously, such as irrigation, hydropower generation, flood control, domestic water supply, navigation, and recreation.
    *   *Example:* Hoover Dam and Lake Mead in the USA (hydropower, irrigation, flood control, domestic supply).
*   **Single-purpose Reservoirs:** Primarily built for a single dominant objective.
    *   *Example:* A reservoir solely for irrigating a specific command area.

**B. Based on the Nature of Flow:**

*   **Storage Reservoirs:** Designed to store water for a significant period, often seasonal, to regulate flow. These are the most common type for irrigation.
    *   *Example:* Large dams like the Bhakra Dam in India, storing monsoon runoff for rabi crops.
*   **Balancing Reservoirs (or Service Reservoirs):** Smaller reservoirs located near the point of demand (e.g., at the end of a canal) to balance the intermittent supply from the main source and provide a continuous supply to users.
    *   *Example:* Balancing reservoirs for municipal water supply or for specific agricultural sub-regions.
*   **Detention Reservoirs (or Flood Control Reservoirs):** Primarily designed to store excess floodwaters temporarily and release them gradually to reduce flood peaks downstream. They are often ungated or have restricted outlet capacity.
    *   *Example:* Reservoirs built upstream of populated areas to protect them from flash floods.
*   **Percolation Reservoirs (or Absorption Reservoirs):** Designed to allow water to seep into the ground to recharge groundwater aquifers.
    *   *Example:* Structures in areas with depleted groundwater tables.

**C. Based on the Position of the Reservoir:**

*   **On-stream Reservoirs:** Created by building a dam directly across the main river channel. The entire river flow passes through the reservoir.
    *   *Example:* Most major dam projects.
*   **Off-stream Reservoirs:** Located away from the main river channel, typically in a depression or valley. Water is diverted from the river through a canal or pipeline to fill these reservoirs.
    *   *Example:* Water diverted from a river to fill a nearby lake or an excavated reservoir for later release.

#### 3.2. Zones of a Reservoir

A reservoir's storage capacity is often conceptually divided into different zones, each serving a specific purpose. These zones help in managing the reservoir and understanding its performance.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Dead Storage:** The volume of water that cannot be drawn from the reservoir by gravity and must be pumped out if needed. It is the lowest level of storage.
*   **Live Storage (or Usable Storage):** The volume of water that can be reliably withdrawn for beneficial uses.
*   **Flood Storage:** The volume of water that can be stored temporarily to control floods.
*   **Surcharge Storage (or Spillway Storage):** The volume of water that can be stored above the top of the spillway crest during extreme flood events, allowing floodwaters to pass over the spillway.

**The Zones:**

1.  **Dead Storage Zone:**
    *   **Purpose:** To accommodate the accumulation of sediment at the bottom of the reservoir without affecting the live storage. It also provides space for the intake of pumps or the tail of the turbine for hydropower.
    *   **Characteristics:** Water in this zone is generally not available for withdrawal by gravity. It requires pumping to access.
    *   **Important:** This volume is permanently lost to storage due to sedimentation over the reservoir's life.

2.  **Live Storage Zone:**
    *   **Purpose:** This is the most crucial zone for providing water for intended uses like irrigation, domestic supply, etc.
    *   **Characteristics:** Water in this zone can be withdrawn by gravity through outlet structures (sluices, gates).
    *   **Components:**
        *   **Top of Conservation Level (or Full Reservoir Level - FRL):** The maximum water level the reservoir is designed to hold for conservation purposes. Live storage extends from the dead storage level up to the FRL.
        *   **Minimum Drawdown Level (MDDL):** The lowest level to which water can be drawn from the reservoir by gravity for its intended purposes.

3.  **Flood Storage Zone (or Flood Reserve):**
    *   **Purpose:** To impound floodwaters and reduce downstream flood peaks.
    *   **Characteristics:** Water in this zone is stored temporarily and released gradually after the flood peak has passed. This zone is typically above the FRL, up to the spillway crest level or a designed flood level.
    *   **Important:** The capacity allocated for flood storage is released after the flood to make space for future flood inflows.

4.  **Surcharge Storage Zone (or Spillway Overflow):**
    *   **Purpose:** To accommodate water that flows over the spillway crest during extreme flood events that exceed the designed flood capacity.
    *   **Characteristics:** This is a temporary storage during very severe floods, and the water is released as quickly as possible. It is above the top of the spillway.

**Diagrammatic Representation:**

Imagine a vertical cross-section of the reservoir:

```
        ^ Surcharge Storage (above spillway)
        |
  ----------------------- Spillway Crest Level
  | Flood Storage Zone    |
  ----------------------- Top of Conservation Level (FRL)
  | Live Storage Zone     |
  |                       |
  ----------------------- Minimum Drawdown Level (MDDL)
  | Dead Storage Zone     |
  ----------------------- Reservoir Bed Level (bottom)
```

#### 3.3. Yield of a Reservoir

The **yield of a reservoir** refers to the amount of water that can be reliably supplied from the reservoir over a given period, considering all the factors affecting its storage and release. It's essentially the dependable draft from the reservoir.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Draft:** The rate at which water is withdrawn from the reservoir.
*   **Dependable Draft:** The draft that can be maintained for a specified percentage of time (e.g., 75% or 90% of the time).
*   **Critical Period:** The period during which the reservoir's stored water is most severely tested due to low inflows and high demands, potentially leading to shortages.
*   **Storage Required:** The volume of storage needed to meet a given draft during the critical period, considering the inflow and demand patterns.

**Types of Yield:**

1.  **Gross Yield:** The total amount of water that can be withdrawn from the reservoir without considering any losses. This is generally not a practical measure.

2.  **Net Yield (or Dependable Yield):** The amount of water that can be reliably withdrawn from the reservoir after accounting for:
    *   **Evaporation losses:** From the free water surface.
    *   **Seepage losses:** From the reservoir bed.
    *   **Operational losses:** Due to reservoir operation rules.
    *   **Minimum releases:** For downstream environmental or other purposes.

**Factors Affecting Reservoir Yield:**

*   **Inflow Pattern:** The amount and distribution of water entering the reservoir (natural runoff, tributary inflows).
*   **Demand Pattern:** The requirement of water for various uses throughout the year.
*   **Reservoir Capacity:** The physical volume of the reservoir.
*   **Evaporation and Seepage Losses:** Significant in arid and semi-arid regions.
*   **Sedimentation:** Reduces the effective storage capacity over time.
*   **Outlet Capacity:** The maximum rate at which water can be withdrawn.
*   **Operational Rules:** Policies governing how and when water is released.

**Methods of Estimating Reservoir Yield:**

Estimating reservoir yield is a complex process. Common methods include:

*   **Mass Curve Analysis (Ripple's Method):** This is a graphical method widely used to determine the required storage for a given draft or the dependable draft for a given storage.
    *   **Process:**
        1.  Develop a mass curve of cumulative inflow (usually monthly or yearly).
        2.  Develop a mass curve of cumulative demand.
        3.  Overlay the demand curve on the inflow curve, starting the demand curve at a desired point.
        4.  To find the required storage for a given draft: Draw a horizontal line representing the draft. For each period, the demand is the draft multiplied by the period duration. Plot the cumulative demand. The maximum vertical distance between the inflow mass curve and the demand mass curve, when the demand curve is drawn optimally to minimize this distance, represents the required storage.
        5.  To find the dependable draft for a given storage: Draw a horizontal line representing the storage capacity. Draw a tangent to the inflow mass curve that is parallel to the demand line drawn from the highest point of the reservoir's storage. The slope of this tangent represents the dependable draft.
    *   **Key Observation:** The maximum deficit between cumulative inflow and cumulative demand (when demand is considered as a steady draft) dictates the required storage.

*   **Sequential Generation (or Simulation Studies):** This involves simulating the reservoir operation year by year using historical or generated inflow data, applying specific operating rules, and determining the draft that can be met under various scenarios. This is a more sophisticated approach.

**Calculation of Yield (Simplified Example using Mass Curve Concept):**

Let's consider a simplified annual basis:

*   **Annual Inflow (I):** Total water entering the reservoir in a year.
*   **Annual Demand (D):** Total water required for irrigation in a year.
*   **Annual Losses (L):** Evaporation, seepage, etc.

If the reservoir is to meet a demand 'd' every year, and the initial storage is zero:

*   **Year 1:** Inflow = I1, Demand = d, Losses = L1.
    *   End of Year 1 storage (S1) = I1 - d - L1
*   **Year 2:** Inflow = I2, Demand = d, Losses = L2.
    *   End of Year 2 storage (S2) = S1 + I2 - d - L2
*   And so on.

The **yield** is the maximum steady draft 'd' that the reservoir can supply without depleting its storage below a minimum acceptable level (or causing a deficit for a specified percentage of time).

**Example:**

Suppose over 3 years, annual inflows are 100, 120, and 90 MCM (Million Cubic Meters). Annual losses are 10 MCM each year. The irrigation demand is 80 MCM per year.

*   **Year 1:** Inflow = 100. Demand = 80. Losses = 10.
    *   End of Year 1 Storage = 100 - 80 - 10 = 10 MCM.
*   **Year 2:** Inflow = 120. Demand = 80. Losses = 10.
    *   End of Year 2 Storage = 10 (from Year 1) + 120 - 80 - 10 = 40 MCM.
*   **Year 3:** Inflow = 90. Demand = 80. Losses = 10.
    *   End of Year 3 Storage = 40 (from Year 2) + 90 - 80 - 10 = 40 MCM.

In this simplified case, a draft of 80 MCM could be met. If the demand was 90 MCM:

*   **Year 1:** Inflow = 100. Demand = 90. Losses = 10.
    *   End of Year 1 Storage = 100 - 90 - 10 = 0 MCM.
*   **Year 2:** Inflow = 120. Demand = 90. Losses = 10.
    *   End of Year 2 Storage = 0 (from Year 1) + 120 - 90 - 10 = 20 MCM.
*   **Year 3:** Inflow = 90. Demand = 90. Losses = 10.
    *   End of Year 3 Storage = 20 (from Year 2) + 90 - 90 - 10 = 10 MCM.

Here, a draft of 90 MCM seems possible. However, mass curve analysis would reveal the exact dependable draft considering sequential flow and potential deficits.

### Practice Questions / Exercises

**Question 1:** Briefly explain why storage reservoirs are essential for irrigation in regions with variable rainfall.
**Answer:** Storage reservoirs are essential to collect surplus water during periods of high rainfall (e.g., monsoon) and release it during dry periods when crop water requirements are high. This bridges the gap between water availability and demand, ensuring consistent water supply for agriculture despite erratic rainfall patterns.

**Question 2:** Classify reservoirs based on their primary purpose and provide an example for each type.
**Answer:**
*   **Multi-purpose:** Serve multiple objectives (irrigation, power, flood control). Example: Bhakra Dam.
*   **Single-purpose:** Serve one dominant objective (e.g., irrigation only). Example: A smaller reservoir specifically for command area irrigation.
*   **Balancing Reservoirs:** Located near demand points to regulate supply. Example: Service reservoirs for urban water supply or specific irrigation zones.
*   **Detention Reservoirs:** Primarily for temporary floodwater storage. Example: Upstream flood retention basins.

**Question 3:** Describe the "Dead Storage Zone" of a reservoir and explain its significance.
**Answer:** The Dead Storage Zone is the volume of water at the bottom of a reservoir that cannot be withdrawn by gravity. Its significance lies in accommodating sediment deposition over the reservoir's lifespan, preventing it from silting up the intake structures or reducing the usable live storage. It also provides space for pump intakes or turbine draft tubes.

**Question 4:** If a reservoir has a capacity of 500 MCM, annual inflows are consistently 200 MCM, and annual losses are 20 MCM, what is the maximum steady annual draft the reservoir can provide if it starts empty?
**Answer:** This is a simplified steady-state problem.
Net available water per year = Annual Inflow - Annual Losses = 200 MCM - 20 MCM = 180 MCM.
This net available water can be used as a draft. The reservoir capacity (500 MCM) indicates the buffer it can provide against minor fluctuations or if the demand temporarily exceeds the net annual availability. Assuming consistent inflow and losses, the maximum steady annual draft is 180 MCM. (Note: Real-world yield calculations are more complex and consider sequential data and critical periods.)

**Question 5:** What is the primary benefit of a "balancing reservoir"?
**Answer:** The primary benefit of a balancing reservoir is to regulate intermittent supply from a primary source and provide a continuous, steady supply to the end-users, smoothing out variations in supply and demand.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Storage is Key:** Reservoirs are fundamental for overcoming the temporal and spatial variability of rainfall in irrigation.
*   **Multi-Purpose Benefits:** Modern reservoirs often aim to serve multiple functions, maximizing resource utilization.
*   **Zone Management:** Understanding reservoir zones (dead, live, flood) is crucial for effective operation and long-term planning. Dead storage is a permanent loss to sedimentation.
*   **Yield = Dependable Draft:** Reservoir yield is not the total storage but the reliably extractable water over time, considering all losses and demands.
*   **Mass Curve Analysis:** A fundamental graphical tool for understanding storage requirements and reservoir yield.
*   **Environmental and Social Impacts:** While beneficial, reservoir construction has significant environmental and social implications that need careful consideration.
*   **Sedimentation is a Challenge:** Reservoirs lose storage capacity over time due to sediment deposition, impacting their long-term yield.
