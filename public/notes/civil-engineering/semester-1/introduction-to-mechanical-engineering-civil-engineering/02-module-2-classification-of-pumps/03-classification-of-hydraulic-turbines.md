## Module 2: Classification of Pumps, **Hydraulic Turbines**

**(Introduction to Mechanical & Civil Engineering)**

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into a fascinating area that bridges both mechanical and civil engineering: **Hydraulic Turbines**. While we might have discussed pumps last time, which are used to *add* energy to fluids, turbines are their counterparts. They *extract* energy from moving fluids, typically water, and convert it into mechanical energy, which is then usually used to generate electricity. Think of the massive hydroelectric dams you've seen – the magic happens inside them thanks to these incredible machines.

This topic is crucial because it directly relates to how we harness natural resources for power generation, a key aspect of both civil infrastructure (like dams) and mechanical systems (like power plants). Specifically, understanding hydraulic turbines helps us connect with **CO1 (Relevance of mechanical engineering and its various disciplines)** and **CO4 (Relevance of civil engineering and its various disciplines)**. We'll see how mechanical design principles are applied to these large-scale civil structures.

### What Exactly is a Hydraulic Turbine?

At its core, a hydraulic turbine is a rotary machine that converts the kinetic and potential energy of a moving fluid (usually water) into mechanical energy. This mechanical energy is then typically used to drive a generator, producing electricity. Imagine a water wheel in an old mill, but vastly more sophisticated and efficient.

The fundamental principle at play is **Newton's Third Law of Motion**: for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. As water flows through the turbine and its direction or velocity changes, it exerts a force on the turbine's rotating parts (the runner), causing them to spin.

### Why Classify Hydraulic Turbines?

You might be wondering, "Why do we need to classify them?" Well, just like there are different types of cars for different purposes (a sports car versus a truck), hydraulic turbines are designed to operate most efficiently under specific conditions. These conditions relate to:

*   **The head of water available:** This is the vertical distance the water falls from the reservoir to the turbine. Low head, medium head, high head – each requires a different turbine design.
*   **The flow rate of water:** How much water is passing through the turbine per unit time. High flow rate, low flow rate – again, different turbines are optimal.
*   **The specific speed:** This is a design parameter that relates the speed of the turbine to the power it produces and the head under which it operates. It's a crucial factor in selecting the right turbine for a given site.

By understanding these classifications, engineers can choose the most suitable turbine for a particular hydroelectric power project, maximizing efficiency and energy output. This is vital for the economical and sustainable operation of power plants, a point that links to the broader understanding of **CO1** and **CO4**.

### The Two Main Categories: Impulse vs. Reaction Turbines

The primary way we classify hydraulic turbines is based on how the fluid pressure changes as it flows through the turbine. This leads us to two main categories:

1.  **Impulse Turbines:** In impulse turbines, the fluid is discharged from the nozzle at atmospheric pressure, and the pressure remains atmospheric as it passes through the runner. The fluid's energy is primarily kinetic. Think of a jet of water hitting a series of buckets on a wheel – the impact (impulse) makes it spin.

2.  **Reaction Turbines:** In reaction turbines, the fluid is not discharged at atmospheric pressure. As the fluid passes through the runner, its pressure drops, and this pressure drop, along with the change in velocity, exerts a force on the runner. Here, both kinetic and potential energy (pressure energy) of the fluid are utilized.

Let's explore these in more detail.

---

### 1. Impulse Turbines

Impulse turbines are characterized by the fact that the high-velocity jet of water strikes the runner buckets tangentially. The casing of an impulse turbine is generally not designed to withstand pressure, and it's usually open to the atmosphere.

#### Key Characteristics of Impulse Turbines:

*   **Operate under high head and low flow rates.** This means they are used where there's a significant vertical drop for the water, but the volume of water available isn't massive.
*   **The casing is not completely filled with water.** The jet of water is directed at the runner.
*   **Pressure of the water remains atmospheric throughout its passage through the runner.**
*   **Efficiency is less sensitive to variations in head and flow rate.**

#### Types of Impulse Turbines:

The most common type of impulse turbine is the **Pelton Turbine**.

**a) Pelton Turbine:**

*   **Inventor:** Lester Allan Pelton
*   **Working Principle:** A high-velocity jet of water, issuing from a nozzle, strikes a series of specially shaped buckets mounted on the periphery of a wheel (runner). These buckets are divided into two halves by a sharp splitter. The jet strikes the splitter and is deflected into two equal streams, each entering one half of the bucket. This ensures that the impulse is balanced, and the maximum kinetic energy is transferred to the runner.
*   **Construction:**
    *   **Nozzle:** Controls the flow of water and converts the pressure energy of the water into kinetic energy by accelerating it to a high velocity jet. It often includes a spear or needle valve to regulate the flow and thus the power output.
    *   **Runner:** A wheel with a series of spoon-shaped buckets mounted around its circumference.
    *   **Buckets:** Designed to deflect the water jet by approximately 180 degrees, maximizing the transfer of momentum.
    *   **Casing:** Guides the water away from the runner after impact and prevents splashing. It's not pressure-tight.
    *   **Deflector:** A plate that can be inserted into the jet path to quickly reduce the force on the runner, providing a rapid means of braking or load control.
*   **Applications:** Pelton turbines are ideal for very high heads (typically above 300 meters) and relatively low flow rates. Think of mountainous regions where water can be channeled from high altitudes.
*   **Analogy:** Imagine a merry-go-round with a powerful water hose directed at it. The force of the water pushes the merry-go-round to spin. The shape of the buckets is like a carefully designed scoop that catches the water efficiently and redirects it smoothly.

### 2. Reaction Turbines

In reaction turbines, the runner is completely submerged in water, and the casing is designed to be pressure-tight. As the water flows through the runner passages, its pressure decreases, and this change in pressure, along with the change in velocity, creates a reaction force that rotates the runner.

#### Key Characteristics of Reaction Turbines:

*   **Operate under medium to low head and high flow rates.**
*   **The casing is completely filled with water and is pressure-tight.**
*   **Pressure of the water decreases as it flows through the runner.**
*   **They utilize both the kinetic energy and the pressure energy of the water.**

#### Types of Reaction Turbines:

Reaction turbines are further classified based on the direction of flow of water relative to the axis of the runner:

*   **Radial Flow Turbines:** Water flows radially inwards or outwards.
*   **Axial Flow Turbines:** Water flows parallel to the axis of the runner.
*   **Mixed Flow Turbines:** Water flows both radially and axially.

Let's look at the prominent types:

**a) Francis Turbine:**

*   **Inventor:** James B. Francis
*   **Working Principle:** This is a *mixed flow* turbine. Water enters the turbine radially under pressure, flows through the runner passages, and is discharged axially. The runner has curved vanes. As water passes through the runner, it expands and its pressure drops, causing the runner to rotate.
*   **Construction:**
    *   **Spiral Casing (Volute Casing):** A spiral-shaped casing that gradually decreases in cross-sectional area around the runner. It ensures that the water is distributed uniformly and with constant velocity to all the guide vanes.
    *   **Guide Vanes:** Adjustable vanes that control the direction and quantity of water flow into the runner. They also regulate the speed of the turbine.
    *   **Runner:** Consists of curved vanes. Water enters radially and exits axially.
    *   **Draft Tube:** A diverging pipe connected to the outlet of the runner. It allows the water to discharge from the runner to the tailrace at a reduced velocity, thereby recovering some of the kinetic energy that would otherwise be lost. This also helps create a suction effect, further increasing the effective head. The presence of the draft tube is a distinguishing feature of reaction turbines.
*   **Applications:** Francis turbines are the most common type and are suitable for a wide range of heads, from medium to high (typically 20 meters to 600 meters). They are versatile and widely used in hydroelectric power plants.
*   **Analogy:** Imagine a Ferris wheel where the seats are designed to catch water that's flowing from the outside towards the center. As the water flows through, it pushes the seats and makes the wheel spin. The spiral casing is like a pipe that smoothly feeds water to all the seats simultaneously.

**b) Kaplan Turbine:**

*   **Inventor:** Viktor Kaplan
*   **Working Principle:** This is an *axial flow* turbine. Water flows parallel to the axis of the turbine runner, entering radially and exiting axially. It's essentially a propeller with adjustable blades. The pitch of the blades can be adjusted to change the runner's angle to the water flow, allowing it to operate efficiently over a wide range of flow rates and heads.
*   **Construction:**
    *   **Spiral Casing:** Similar to the Francis turbine, but designed for axial flow.
    *   **Guide Vanes:** Control the direction and flow of water.
    *   **Runner:** Similar to a ship's propeller, but with adjustable blades.
    *   **Draft Tube:** Essential for recovering kinetic energy, similar to the Francis turbine.
*   **Applications:** Kaplan turbines are best suited for low heads (typically below 60 meters) and very high flow rates. They are often used in situations with significant variations in water flow, such as run-of-river plants or downstream of dams where water levels can fluctuate.
*   **Analogy:** Think of a boat propeller submerged in a river. The water flow makes the propeller spin. If you could adjust the angle of the propeller blades, you could make it spin efficiently whether the river was flowing fast or slow. That’s the essence of a Kaplan turbine.

**c) Propeller Turbine:**

*   Propeller turbines are very similar to Kaplan turbines in principle and application. The main difference is that the blades of a propeller turbine are fixed, unlike the adjustable blades of a Kaplan turbine.
*   **Advantages of Kaplan over Propeller:** The adjustability of Kaplan turbine blades allows for much better efficiency over a wider range of operating conditions, especially when flow rates vary significantly.
*   **Applications:** Propeller turbines are used for low heads and high flow rates where the flow conditions are relatively constant.

**d) Tubo-turbines (or Bulb Turbines):**

*   These are a variation of axial flow turbines. The runner, generator, and gearbox are all housed within a streamlined casing (the "bulb") that is placed directly in the water flow. This design minimizes water passages and losses. They are often used in low-head applications and can be quite compact.

### Classification Based on Other Criteria

While the impulse vs. reaction and flow direction classifications are the most fundamental, turbines can also be categorized based on:

*   **Direction of Flow:**
    *   **Tangential Flow:** Impulse turbines (Pelton).
    *   **Radial Flow:** Girard turbine (less common now).
    *   **Axial Flow:** Kaplan, Propeller.
    *   **Mixed Flow:** Francis.

*   **Action on Runner:**
    *   **Impulse:** Pelton.
    *   **Reaction:** Francis, Kaplan.

*   **Specific Speed:** This is a bit more technical but worth noting. Specific speed is an index that characterizes the turbine type:
    *   **Low Specific Speed:** Pelton turbines (suited for high head, low flow).
    *   **Medium Specific Speed:** Francis turbines (versatile for medium heads).
    *   **High Specific Speed:** Kaplan turbines (suited for low head, high flow).

### Sample Questions and Answers

**1. Question:** Classify hydraulic turbines and explain the basis for this classification.
    **Answer:** Hydraulic turbines are primarily classified based on how they utilize the energy of the water and the direction of water flow through the runner.
    *   **Based on Energy Utilization:**
        *   **Impulse Turbines:** Utilize the kinetic energy of the water jet. The pressure remains atmospheric throughout the runner. Example: Pelton Turbine.
        *   **Reaction Turbines:** Utilize both kinetic energy and pressure energy (potential energy) of the water. The pressure of the water decreases as it flows through the runner. Examples: Francis Turbine, Kaplan Turbine.
    *   **Based on Direction of Flow:**
        *   **Tangential Flow:** Water strikes the runner tangentially. (Impulse Turbines like Pelton).
        *   **Radial Flow:** Water flows radially inwards or outwards. (Less common now).
        *   **Axial Flow:** Water flows parallel to the axis of rotation. (Kaplan, Propeller Turbines).
        *   **Mixed Flow:** Water enters radially and exits axially. (Francis Turbine).

**2. Question:** Under what conditions is a Pelton turbine most suitable, and why?
    **Answer:** A Pelton turbine is most suitable for **high heads** (typically above 300 meters) and **low flow rates**.
    *   **Reasoning:** Pelton turbines operate by using a high-velocity jet of water to strike the buckets. To achieve a high velocity jet, a significant difference in water level (head) is required, as velocity is proportional to the square root of the head (v = √(2gh)). For high heads, the volume of water (flow rate) available is often limited. The impulse principle is efficient in converting the kinetic energy of a high-speed jet into rotational energy, making it ideal for these specific site conditions.

**3. Question:** What is the function of a draft tube in a reaction turbine, and why is it absent in an impulse turbine?
    **Answer:**
    *   **Function of Draft Tube in Reaction Turbines:** A draft tube is a diverging pipe connected to the outlet of the runner in reaction turbines. Its main functions are:
        1.  **Recovery of Kinetic Energy:** It allows the water to discharge into the tailrace at a reduced velocity, thereby recovering some of the kinetic energy that would otherwise be lost.
        2.  **Increase in Effective Head:** By reducing the pressure at the outlet of the runner below atmospheric pressure (creating suction), it effectively increases the head under which the turbine operates, thus increasing the power output.
    *   **Absence in Impulse Turbines:** In impulse turbines (like Pelton), the water jet is discharged at atmospheric pressure, and the casing is not pressure-tight. The velocity of the water leaving the buckets is already significantly reduced due to the impulse action. There's no significant pressure energy to recover, and the casing is designed to handle atmospheric pressure only. Therefore, a draft tube is not required or beneficial for impulse turbines.

**4. Question:** Compare a Francis turbine with a Kaplan turbine, highlighting their key differences in terms of working principle, head and flow conditions, and runner design.
    **Answer:**

    | Feature              | Francis Turbine                               | Kaplan Turbine                                   |
    | :------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------- |
    | **Classification**   | Mixed flow reaction turbine                   | Axial flow reaction turbine                      |
    | **Head Range**       | Medium to high (20 m to 600 m)                | Low (typically below 60 m)                       |
    | **Flow Rate**        | Medium to high                                | Very high                                        |
    | **Working Principle**| Water enters radially and leaves axially; pressure drops through runner. | Water flows axially; pressure drop across runner; adjustable blades. |
    | **Runner Design**    | Curved vanes, spiral shape.                   | Propeller-like, with adjustable blades.          |
    | **Efficiency Range** | Good efficiency over a moderate range of flow. | Excellent efficiency over a wide range of flow rates due to adjustable blades. |
    | **Draft Tube**       | Always used.                                  | Always used.                                     |
    | **Applications**     | Most common in hydroelectric power plants.    | Run-of-river plants, tidal power.                |

    **In summary:** Francis turbines are versatile for medium heads, while Kaplan turbines excel in low-head, high-flow situations, offering better efficiency when flow rates vary due to their adjustable blades.
