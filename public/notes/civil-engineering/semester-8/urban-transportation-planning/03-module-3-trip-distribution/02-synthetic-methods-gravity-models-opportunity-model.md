### 1. Introduction to Trip Distribution

**What is Trip Distribution?**

Trip distribution is the stage in the transportation planning process that determines the **origin-destination (O-D) pairs** for trips generated or attracted in a study area. In simpler terms, it answers the question: **"Where are people going from where?"**

**Role in Transportation Planning:**

Trip distribution follows Trip Generation (how many trips are made) and precedes Mode Choice (how trips are made) and Trip Assignment (which routes are used). It's a crucial step for understanding travel patterns and forecasting future demand.

**Synthetic Methods:**

Synthetic methods, also known as "aggregate" or "macro" methods, predict trip distribution based on broad zonal characteristics and relationships between zones. They are called "synthetic" because they synthesize or create trip interchange patterns rather than directly observing them from surveys.

### 2. Synthetic Methods: Gravity Models

**2.1 Principles and Assumptions:**

The Gravity Model is based on the principle of **analogy to Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation**. It posits that the number of trips between two zones is:

*   **Directly proportional** to the "attractiveness" of the destination zone.
*   **Inversely proportional** to some measure of "friction" or "impedance" (e.g., travel time, distance) between the two zones.

**Key Assumptions:**

*   **Spatial Inertia:** People tend to travel between zones that are closer together.
*   **Attractiveness:** Zones with more opportunities (jobs, retail, etc.) attract more trips.
*   **Homogeneity:** Travelers within a zone are assumed to be homogeneous in their travel behavior.
*   **Systematic Relationship:** Travel patterns can be predicted by observable zonal characteristics.

**2.2 Mathematical Formulation:**

The basic form of the Gravity Model is:

$T_{ij} = K \times \frac{P_i \times A_j}{f(t_{ij})}$

Where:

*   $T_{ij}$: The number of trips between origin zone *i* and destination zone *j*.
*   $P_i$: The number of trip *producers* in origin zone *i* (e.g., households, population). This represents the "mass" of the origin.
*   $A_j$: The number of trip *attractors* in destination zone *j* (e.g., jobs, retail floor space). This represents the "mass" of the destination.
*   $f(t_{ij})$: The **friction or impedance function**, which represents the difficulty of traveling between zone *i* and zone *j*. This is typically a function of travel time, distance, or cost. Common forms include:
    *   $1/t_{ij}^2$ (inverse square of travel time)
    *   $1/t_{ij}$ (inverse of travel time)
    *   $e^{-\beta t_{ij}}$ (exponential decay)
*   $K$: A **sociological or regional adjustment factor** that accounts for factors not explicitly included in the equation, such as income, car ownership, and other system-wide effects. It ensures that the sum of all predicted trips equals the total trips generated by the origin zone (or attracted by the destination zone).

**2.3 The Frictional Effect (F-Curve):**

The form of $f(t_{ij})$ is crucial and is often determined empirically by fitting a **"friction factor" curve** to observed O-D data from a survey.

*   **Process:** Plot the ratio of trips to the product of production and attraction ($T_{ij} / (P_i \times A_j)$) against the travel time ($t_{ij}$) for observed trips.
*   **Fit a curve:** A smooth curve is fitted to these points.
*   **Use for forecasting:** This fitted curve is then used to estimate the $f(t_{ij})$ values for unsampled zone pairs or future conditions.

**2.4 Calibration and Application:**

**Calibration:**

1.  **Data Collection:** Obtain observed O-D trip data for a sample of zone pairs from a home-based or other travel survey.
2.  **Define $P_i$ and $A_j$:** Determine the production and attraction measures for each zone.
3.  **Calculate Impedance:** Determine the travel time or cost between all zone pairs.
4.  **Develop F-Curve:** Plot $T_{ij} / (P_i \times A_j)$ versus $t_{ij}$ and fit a curve.
5.  **Determine $K$:** For each origin zone *i*, the sum of trips to all destinations must equal the total trips generated by *i*.
    $\sum_{j} T_{ij} = P_i$
    Using the Gravity Model equation:
    $P_i = K_i \sum_{j} \frac{P_i \times A_j}{f(t_{ij})}$
    $K_i = \frac{P_i}{\sum_{j} \frac{P_i \times A_j}{f(t_{ij})}}$
    The $K$ factor is often calculated for each origin zone to ensure the model "balances" the trips originating from that zone.

**Application (Forecasting):**

1.  **Forecast $P_i$ and $A_j$:** Project the production and attraction measures for future years.
2.  **Forecast Impedance:** Estimate future travel times or costs (e.g., based on planned infrastructure).
3.  **Apply F-Curve:** Use the calibrated F-curve to determine the friction factor $f(t_{ij})$ for all future zone pairs.
4.  **Calculate $T_{ij}$:** Apply the Gravity Model formula.
5.  **Balance:** Use the $K$ factors (often recalibrated for the future scenario) to ensure the total trips from each origin zone match the generated trips.

**2.5 Strengths of the Gravity Model:**

*   **Conceptually simple:** Easy to understand the underlying logic.
*   **Data efficient (relative to disaggregate models):** Primarily requires zonal data and a sample O-D survey for calibration.
*   **Widely used and understood:** A common tool in transportation planning.
*   **Good for simulating broad patterns:** Effective for understanding general spatial interaction.

**2.6 Weaknesses of the Gravity Model:**

*   **Empirical nature:** Relies heavily on calibration with existing data, which may not hold for future conditions.
*   **Limited behavioral realism:** Doesn't explicitly model individual decision-making.
*   **Assumes proportionality:** Assumes a direct proportionality between attractiveness and trips, which may not always be true.
*   **Difficulty in capturing complex trip-making behavior:** Struggles with multi-purpose trips, complex destination choices, or special generators.
*   **Sensitivity to F-curve form:** The choice of the friction function can significantly impact results.
*   **Potential for spatial autocorrelation issues:** Treats zones as independent entities.

### 3. Synthetic Methods: Opportunity Model

**3.1 Principles and Assumptions:**

The Opportunity Model, also known as the **"Detroit Model"** or **"Growth Factor Model"**, focuses on the **destination opportunities available to travelers from a specific origin zone**. It assumes that travelers proceed to the "nearest" available opportunity, and their probability of reaching a destination is related to the cumulative opportunities encountered up to that destination.

**Key Assumptions:**

*   **Destination Sequential Choice:** Travelers are assumed to sequentially consider destinations based on increasing impedance.
*   **Opportunity Availability:** The probability of a trip ending at a destination depends on the number of opportunities encountered *before* that destination in the impedance sequence.
*   **Limited Mobility/Search:** Travelers may not explore all possible destinations; they stop their search once a satisfactory opportunity is found.
*   **"Leapfrogging" is possible:** A traveler might skip an intermediate zone if a more attractive zone further away is considered.

**3.2 Mathematical Formulation:**

The Opportunity Model is typically applied to a specific origin zone *i* and distributes its generated trips ($T_i$) to all destination zones *j*.

$T_{ij} = T_i \times P_{ij}$

Where:

*   $T_{ij}$: The number of trips from origin zone *i* to destination zone *j*.
*   $T_i$: The total number of trips originating from zone *i* (from Trip Generation).
*   $P_{ij}$: The probability that a trip originating from zone *i* will terminate in destination zone *j*.

The probability $P_{ij}$ is calculated based on the cumulative opportunities and the "total opportunities" in the system.

$P_{ij} = \frac{A_j \times f(t_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n} A_k \times f(t_{ik})}$  **(This is a simplified version, the actual calculation is cumulative)**

The more common formulation considers the probability of a trip *not* terminating before zone *j*:

$T_{ij} = T_i \times \frac{A_j \times f(t_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n} A_k \times f(t_{ik})} \times \prod_{k=1}^{j-1} \left( 1 - \frac{A_k \times f(t_{ik})}{\sum_{m=1}^{n} A_m \times f(t_{im})} \right)$

Let's break down the probability term:

*   $P(\text{trip ends at } j | \text{ trip from } i) = P(\text{trip terminates at } j \text{ given it hasn't terminated before } j)$
*   This is calculated as: $(\frac{A_j \times f(t_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n} A_k \times f(t_{ik})}) \times (\text{Probability of not terminating before } j)$

The probability of not terminating before zone *j* is the product of the probabilities of not terminating at any preceding zone *k* (where *k* is ordered by increasing impedance from *i*):

$\prod_{k=1}^{j-1} \left( 1 - \frac{A_k \times f(t_{ik})}{\sum_{m=1}^{n} A_m \times f(t_{im})} \right)$

**Simplified Cumulative Approach:**

A more intuitive way to understand the probability calculation:

1.  **Order destinations:** For each origin zone *i*, all destination zones *j* are ordered based on increasing travel impedance $t_{ij}$.
2.  **Calculate Attraction-Impedance Product:** For each ordered zone *j*, calculate $A_j \times f(t_{ij})$.
3.  **Calculate Cumulative Probability:**
    *   The probability of a trip *not* terminating before zone *j* is the proportion of total "system attractiveness" (weighted by impedance) that lies *beyond* zone *j*.
    *   The probability of a trip terminating at zone *j* is the proportion of "system attractiveness" at zone *j* (weighted by impedance) *multiplied by* the probability of not terminating before zone *j*.

**$P_{ij} = \left[ \frac{A_j f(t_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^n A_k f(t_{ik})} \right] \times \left[ \prod_{k=1}^{j-1} \left(1 - \frac{A_k f(t_{ik})}{\sum_{m=1}^n A_m f(t_{im})} \right) \right]$**

**3.3 Calibration and Application:**

**Calibration:**

1.  **Data Collection:** Obtain observed O-D trip data.
2.  **Define $T_i$ and $A_j$:** Determine trip productions for origins and attractions for destinations.
3.  **Determine Impedance:** Establish travel times/costs between zones.
4.  **Develop F-Curve:** Similar to the Gravity Model, a friction factor curve $f(t_{ij})$ is calibrated from observed data.
5.  **Apply the Model:** Use the calibrated F-curve, $T_i$, $A_j$, and impedance values to calculate $P_{ij}$ and $T_{ij}$ for observed zone pairs.
6.  **Adjustment:** The model is often calibrated by adjusting parameters in the $f(t_{ij})$ function or by adding adjustment factors to ensure the predicted $T_{ij}$ matches the observed $T_{ij}$.

**Application (Forecasting):**

1.  **Forecast $T_i$ and $A_j$:** Project future trip productions and attractions.
2.  **Forecast Impedance:** Estimate future travel times/costs.
3.  **Apply F-Curve:** Use the calibrated F-curve to determine $f(t_{ij})$ for future conditions.
4.  **Order and Distribute:** For each origin zone, order destination zones by increasing impedance, calculate the cumulative probabilities, and distribute the predicted $T_i$ to $T_{ij}$.

**3.4 Strengths of the Opportunity Model:**

*   **Behaviorally more appealing:** The concept of travelers moving to the nearest/most accessible opportunity is more intuitive than the Gravity Model's direct proportionality.
*   **Captures limited search behavior:** Better reflects situations where travelers might not survey all possible destinations.
*   **Can be good for specific trip purposes:** Particularly effective for non-home-based trips or situations with strong destination attraction gradients.
*   **No explicit K-factor needed for balancing:** The model intrinsically distributes the total trips generated from an origin.

**3.5 Weaknesses of the Opportunity Model:**

*   **Requires precise ordering:** Highly sensitive to the accuracy of impedance values for ordering zones. Small changes can significantly alter results.
*   **Assumes homogeneity of opportunities:** Treats all opportunities within a zone as equal.
*   **Difficulty with multi-purpose trips:** Primarily designed for single-purpose trips.
*   **Data intensive for calibration:** Requires detailed O-D data to accurately calibrate the ordering and probability functions.
*   **Can be computationally intensive:** Requires sorting and cumulative calculations for each origin zone.

### 4. Comparison of Gravity and Opportunity Models

| Feature                 | Gravity Model                                         | Opportunity Model                                    |
| :---------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------- |
| **Core Principle**      | Analogy to Newton's Law of Gravitation.               | Sequential search for opportunities.                 |
| **Trip Interaction**    | Direct proportionality to origin production and destination attraction, inversely proportional to impedance. | Probability based on cumulative opportunities encountered in impedance order. |
| **Behavioral Basis**    | Less behavioral realism; simulates spatial interaction. | More behaviorally appealing; reflects limited search. |
| **Destination Choice**  | All destinations considered simultaneously based on attractiveness and impedance. | Destinations considered sequentially based on impedance. |
| **Calibration Focus**   | F-curve fitting; K-factor for balancing.              | F-curve fitting; ensuring accurate ordering and probability. |
| **Data Requirements**   | Zonal data, sample O-D survey for F-curve.            | Zonal data, sample O-D survey for F-curve and ordering. |
| **Computational Load**  | Moderate.                                             | Can be higher due to ordering and cumulative calculations. |
| **Sensitivity**         | To F-curve form and K-factor calculation.             | Highly sensitive to impedance values and ordering.     |
| **Typical Application** | General trip distribution, especially for home-based work trips. | Non-home-based trips, retail trips, where sequential choice is dominant. |

### 5. Data Requirements

Both models require:

*   **Trip Generation Data:** Number of trips produced by each origin zone ($P_i$ or $T_i$).
*   **Trip Attraction Data:** Number of trips attracted by each destination zone ($A_j$).
*   **O-D Matrix (for calibration):** Observed number of trips between sampled origin-destination pairs.
*   **Impedance Data:** Travel time, distance, or cost between all origin-destination zone pairs (e.g., from a Network Analysis or Travel Demand Model's Assignment stage).
*   **Zonal Characteristics:** Data on population, households, employment, land use, etc., for defining $P_i$ and $A_j$.

---

### 6. Limitations and Biases of Synthetic Methods

*   **"Garbage in, garbage out":** The accuracy of the output is highly dependent on the accuracy of the input data (trip generation, attractions, impedance).
*   **Assumption of Fixed Behavior:** They assume past travel behavior, as captured in the calibration data, will persist into the future, which may not be true with significant socio-economic or technological changes.
*   **System-wide Effects:** May struggle to capture the impact of specific infrastructure improvements or major policy changes that alter travel behavior in non-linear ways.
*   **Calibration Dependency:** Calibration is crucial. A poorly calibrated model will produce poor results.
*   **Aggregation Issues:** Treating zones as homogeneous units can mask important variations in behavior within zones.

**Question 1:**
The Gravity Model equation is $T_{ij} = K \times \frac{P_i \times A_j}{f(t_{ij})}$.
Which of the following statements is TRUE regarding the components of this equation?

a) $P_i$ represents the number of trips attracted to zone $i$.
b) $A_j$ represents the number of trip producers in zone $j$.
c) $f(t_{ij})$ represents a measure of the "attractiveness" of the destination zone.
d) $K$ is a sociological adjustment factor that ensures the sum of trips from an origin zone equals the generated trips.

**Answer:** d) $K$ is a sociological adjustment factor that ensures the sum of trips from an origin zone equals the generated trips.

**Question 2:**
Explain the core principle behind the Opportunity Model and how it differs from the Gravity Model's approach to trip distribution.

**Answer:**
The Opportunity Model is based on the principle that travelers sequentially consider destinations based on increasing impedance and tend to terminate their trip at the first satisfactory opportunity encountered. This differs from the Gravity Model, which assumes all destinations are considered simultaneously, with the number of trips being directly proportional to the attractiveness of the destination and inversely proportional to the impedance. The Opportunity Model emphasizes a "limited search" or "nearest opportunity" behavior, while the Gravity Model focuses on a broader spatial interaction based on mass and friction.

**Question 3:**
Consider the following simplified data for two origin zones (O1, O2) and three destination zones (D1, D2, D3):

| Zone | Production ($P_i$) | Attraction ($A_j$) |
| :--- | :----------------- | :----------------- |
| O1   | 100                |                    |
| O2   | 150                |                    |
| D1   |                    | 50                 |
| D2   |                    | 80                 |
| D3   |                    | 120                |

**Impedance Matrix (Travel Time in minutes):**

| From/To | D1 | D2 | D3 |
| :------ | :- | :- | :- |
| O1      | 5  | 10 | 15 |
| O2      | 8  | 12 | 18 |

**Friction Function:** Assume $f(t_{ij}) = 1/t_{ij}^2$ for this exercise.

**Task:**
a) For origin zone O1, calculate the weighted attraction terms ($P_{O1} \times A_j / f(t_{O1,j})$) for each destination zone.
b) Using the concept of the Gravity Model, briefly explain how you would proceed to distribute the 100 trips from O1 to D1, D2, and D3, mentioning the role of the $K$ factor.

**Answer:**
a) **Weighted Attraction Terms:**
   *   $f(t_{O1,D1}) = 1/5^2 = 1/25 = 0.04$
   *   $f(t_{O1,D2}) = 1/10^2 = 1/100 = 0.01$
   *   $f(t_{O1,D3}) = 1/15^2 = 1/225 \approx 0.0044$

   *   Term for D1: $P_{O1} \times A_{D1} / f(t_{O1,D1}) = 100 \times 50 / 0.04 = 5000 / 0.04 = 125,000$
   *   Term for D2: $P_{O1} \times A_{D2} / f(t_{O1,D2}) = 100 \times 80 / 0.01 = 8000 / 0.01 = 800,000$
   *   Term for D3: $P_{O1} \times A_{D3} / f(t_{O1,D3}) = 100 \times 120 / 0.0044 = 12000 / 0.0044 \approx 2,727,273$

b) **Distribution of trips from O1:**
   To distribute the 100 trips from O1, we would use the Gravity Model formula: $T_{O1,j} = K_{O1} \times \frac{P_{O1} \times A_j}{f(t_{O1,j})}$.
   First, the sum of the calculated weighted attraction terms ($P_i \times A_j / f(t_{ij})$) for all destinations from O1 would be calculated. Then, a $K_{O1}$ factor would be determined to ensure that $\sum_{j} T_{O1,j} = P_{O1} = 100$.
   The formula for $K_{O1}$ would be:
   $K_{O1} = \frac{P_{O1}}{\sum_{j} (P_{O1} \times A_j / f(t_{O1,j}))}$
   Once $K_{O1}$ is calculated, the trips to each destination $j$ would be:
   $T_{O1,j} = K_{O1} \times (P_{O1} \times A_j / f(t_{O1,j}))$
   This would allocate the 100 trips to D1, D2, and D3 based on their weighted attraction and impedance.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Trip Distribution bridges Trip Generation and Mode Choice/Assignment.**
*   **Gravity Model:** Focuses on **attraction** and **impedance** as primary drivers. Simple and widely used.
*   **Opportunity Model:** Focuses on **sequential choice** and **cumulative opportunities**. More behaviorally realistic for certain trip purposes.
*   **Friction Factor (F-Curve):** Crucial for calibrating both models using observed O-D data.
*   **Calibration:** Essential for both models; the quality of calibration directly impacts forecast accuracy.
*   **Data Accuracy:** The success of synthetic methods heavily relies on accurate input data for productions, attractions, and impedance.
*   **Limitations:** Both models have inherent assumptions and limitations; understanding these is key to appropriate application.
