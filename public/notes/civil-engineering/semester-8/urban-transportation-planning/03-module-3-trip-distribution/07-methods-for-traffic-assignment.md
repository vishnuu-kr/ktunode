# Urban Transportation Planning: Module 3: Trip Distribution - Methods for Traffic Assignment

## Introduction to Traffic Assignment

Traffic assignment is the process of assigning the trips generated by trip generation and distributed by trip distribution to specific routes or modes within a transportation network. It's the crucial step that translates travel demand into actual traffic volumes on individual links (roads) and paths (routes). This module focuses on the various methods used to perform this assignment, helping us understand how people choose their travel routes and what impacts these choices have on the network's performance.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Network:** A representation of the transportation system, typically consisting of:
    *   **Nodes:** Points of interest (e.g., intersections, zones, centroids).
    *   **Links:** Connections between nodes, representing travel paths (e.g., road segments, transit lines).
*   **Origin-Destination (O-D) Matrix:** A table showing the number of trips between each pair of origins and destinations.
*   **Path:** A sequence of links connecting an origin to a destination.
*   **Travel Cost/Time:** A measure of the "effort" required to traverse a link or path. This can include:
    *   **Travel Time:** The actual time taken to travel.
    *   **Distance:** The physical length of a link.
    *   **Toll/Fare:** Monetary cost of travel.
    *   **Congestion:** Delay experienced due to traffic volume.
    *   **Other factors:** Safety, comfort, convenience, etc.
*   **Traffic Assignment Method:** A systematic procedure to allocate trips from an O-D matrix to paths in a network, considering travel behavior and network conditions.

## Types of Traffic Assignment Methods

Traffic assignment methods can be broadly categorized based on how they model traveler behavior and the equilibrium conditions they seek to achieve.

### 1. All-or-Nothing (AON) Assignment

*   **Concept:** Assigns all trips between an O-D pair to the **single shortest or least-cost path** based on a fixed set of travel costs (independent of volume).
*   **Process:**
    1.  Define network links and their associated impedance (travel cost).
    2.  Calculate shortest paths for all O-D pairs using algorithms like Dijkstra's.
    3.  Assign all trips from the O-D matrix to these shortest paths.
*   **Objectives:**
    *   Simple and quick to implement.
    *   Useful for initial network screening or when congestion is minimal.
    *   Provides a baseline for comparison.
*   **Limitations:**
    *   **Unrealistic:** Ignores the fact that as traffic volume increases on a path, its travel cost also increases, diverting users to alternative paths.
    *   **Doesn't capture congestion effects.**
    *   **Doesn't reflect actual traveler behavior in congested conditions.**
*   **Example:** If the travel time on Route A from Zone 1 to Zone 2 is 10 minutes, and Route B is 15 minutes, AON will assign all trips between Zone 1 and Zone 2 to Route A, regardless of how many trips are assigned to Route A.

### 2. Capacity Restraint (or Incremental) Assignment

*   **Concept:** An iterative approach that progressively loads trips onto the network, adjusting link travel times based on capacity and flow. It attempts to incorporate congestion.
*   **Process:**
    1.  Perform an All-or-Nothing assignment based on initial travel times (no congestion).
    2.  Update link travel times using a volume-delay function (VDF) that relates travel time to traffic volume and link capacity.
    3.  Repeat step 1 and 2, assigning fractions of the total O-D matrix in each iteration, until convergence is achieved.
*   **Objectives:**
    *   More realistic than AON by accounting for capacity constraints.
    *   Can provide an approximation of congestion.
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Convergence can be slow.
    *   The order of loading trips can influence the final results.
    *   Still may not perfectly converge to equilibrium conditions.
*   **Types of Capacity Restraint:**
    *   **Incremental Assignment:** Assigns trips in a fixed number of increments.
    *   **Equilibrium Assignment (using VDFs):** Aims to reach a state where users are indifferent between available routes.

### 3. User Equilibrium (UE) Assignment

*   **Concept:** Based on **Wardrop's First Principle of Traffic Assignment**: "The travel times on all routes used between any two zones are equal and less than those which would be experienced by a traveler on any unused route." This is a state where no single user can improve their travel time by unilaterally changing their route.
*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Users are rational and seek to minimize their travel time.
    *   Users have perfect information about travel times on all routes.
    *   Travel time is a function of traffic volume on a link (using Volume-Delay Functions - VDFs).
*   **Process:** This is an iterative process, often using algorithms like the Frank-Wolfe algorithm.
    1.  **Initialization:** Perform an AON assignment based on free-flow travel times.
    2.  **Update Travel Times:** Calculate link travel times based on the assigned flows using VDFs.
    3.  **Directional Search:** Determine a set of "all-or-nothing" paths based on the current travel times.
    4.  **Line Search:** Determine the optimal proportion of trips to shift from the current assignment to the new set of paths to minimize the total travel time in the system while moving towards equilibrium.
    5.  **Update Flows:** Adjust the flow on each link according to the proportion determined in the line search.
    6.  **Convergence Check:** Repeat steps 2-5 until the difference between successive assignments is below a predefined tolerance level.
*   **Volume-Delay Functions (VDFs):** Crucial for UE assignment. These functions mathematically describe how travel time on a link increases with increasing traffic volume. Common VDFs include:
    *   **BPR (Bureau of Public Roads) Function:**
        $$t = t_0 \left[ 1 + \alpha \left( \frac{V}{C} \right)^\beta \right]$$
        Where:
        *   $t$ = travel time on the link
        *   $t_0$ = free-flow travel time
        *   $V$ = volume on the link
        *   $C$ = capacity of the link
        *   $\alpha$, $\beta$ = parameters (typically $\alpha = 0.15$, $\beta = 4$)
    *   **Akcelik Function:** A more sophisticated VDF that can better represent the sharp increase in travel time at capacity.
*   **Objectives:**
    *   Represents a realistic state of traffic flow under congestion.
    *   Widely used in transportation planning for forecasting traffic conditions.
*   **Data Requirements:**
    *   Detailed network representation (nodes, links, capacities).
    *   Free-flow travel times for each link.
    *   O-D matrix of trips.
    *   Appropriate Volume-Delay Functions.
*   **Example:** If Route A has low volume and takes 10 minutes, while Route B has high volume and takes 20 minutes, users will prefer Route A. As more users switch to Route A, its travel time will increase. Eventually, the travel times on Route A and Route B will become equal, and some users will start using Route B again, leading to a state of equilibrium.

### 4. System Optimum (SO) Assignment

*   **Concept:** Based on **Wardrop's Second Principle of Traffic Assignment**: "The journey times on all routes used between any two zones will be such that the marginal increase in the total system travel time caused by an increase in the flow on any one route is the same for all routes." This aims to minimize the **total travel time for all users in the system**.
*   **Key Difference from UE:** SO considers the **marginal cost** of adding traffic to a link, which includes the delay imposed on all other users of that link, not just the additional user. UE only considers the **average cost** experienced by a user.
*   **Process:** Similar iterative process to UE, but uses **marginal VDFs** instead of average VDFs. The marginal VDF is derived from the total travel time function.
*   **Marginal Cost Function:** If the total travel time on a link is $T(V) = V \cdot t(V)$, where $t(V)$ is the average travel time function, then the marginal cost is the derivative of the total travel time with respect to volume: $MC(V) = \frac{d(V \cdot t(V))}{dV} = t(V) + V \cdot \frac{dt(V)}{dV}$.
*   **Objectives:**
    *   Achieve maximum system efficiency by minimizing total travel time.
    *   Useful for evaluating the impact of transportation policies or investments that aim to improve overall network performance (e.g., road pricing, public transport subsidies).
*   **Data Requirements:** Same as UE, but requires the ability to derive or use marginal VDFs.
*   **Example:** Imagine a toll booth. If the toll is fixed (like UE), people will pay it if it makes their route faster on average. If the toll is variable and increases significantly as more cars use the road (like SO), it discourages additional usage more effectively, leading to lower overall congestion and potentially lower total travel time for everyone. SO would suggest a higher toll than UE to internalize the externality of congestion.

### 5. Activity-Based Travel Demand Modeling (ABM) and its Assignment Component

*   **Concept:** A more disaggregate approach that models the daily activities of individuals and households, from which travel decisions are derived. This is a more advanced method and is often considered the state-of-the-art in travel demand forecasting.
*   **Process:**
    1.  **Activity Generation and Scheduling:** Models daily schedules of individuals, including work, school, shopping, leisure, etc.
    2.  **Trip Generation:** Trips are generated as a consequence of the need to move between activities.
    3.  **Destination Choice:** Individuals choose destinations for their activities.
    4.  **Mode Choice:** Individuals choose their mode of transport.
    5.  **Route Choice (Traffic Assignment):** This is where traffic assignment principles are applied, but often with more disaggregated trip information (e.g., individual person trips, by specific mode and time of day). Assignment can still be UE or SO based on the context.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Captures complex travel behaviors and interactions between trips.
    *   Better at modeling the impact of new policies and technologies (e.g., teleworking, flexible work hours).
    *   Can provide more granular insights into travel patterns.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Data intensive and computationally complex.
    *   Requires sophisticated modeling software and expertise.

## Steps in Traffic Assignment

Regardless of the specific method, the general steps involved are:

1.  **Network Definition:**
    *   Define all relevant nodes (intersections, zone centroids, public transport stations).
    *   Define all links (road segments, transit lines, pedestrian paths) connecting these nodes.
    *   Assign attributes to each link:
        *   **Capacity:** Maximum flow a link can handle.
        *   **Free-flow Travel Time ($t_0$):** Travel time under zero traffic volume.
        *   **Length:** Physical distance.
        *   **Other factors:** Number of lanes, speed limit, type of road, toll information, etc.

2.  **O-D Matrix Preparation:**
    *   Ensure the O-D matrix is complete and represents the demand for travel between all origin-destination pairs (typically zone centroids).

3.  **Route Identification/Path Building:**
    *   For each O-D pair, identify all feasible paths in the network. This is often done using shortest path algorithms (like Dijkstra's) based on current travel costs.

4.  **Trip Loading (Assignment):**
    *   Allocate the trips from the O-D matrix onto the identified paths according to the chosen assignment method (AON, UE, SO).
    *   If using iterative methods (UE, SO, Capacity Restraint), this step is repeated until convergence.

5.  **Travel Cost Update (for iterative methods):**
    *   After assigning trips, update the travel cost (typically travel time) on each link based on the volume assigned and the appropriate Volume-Delay Function (VDF).

6.  **Convergence Check:**
    *   For iterative methods, compare the current assignment with the previous one. If the difference is within acceptable tolerance, stop. Otherwise, repeat from step 5.

7.  **Result Analysis:**
    *   Analyze the resulting link volumes, travel times, and path utilizations.
    *   Calculate network performance indicators (e.g., average travel speed, total vehicle-kilometers traveled, total vehicle-hours traveled).

## Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Which traffic assignment method assigns all trips between an O-D pair to the single shortest path, ignoring congestion?
a) User Equilibrium (UE) Assignment
b) System Optimum (SO) Assignment
c) All-or-Nothing (AON) Assignment
d) Capacity Restraint Assignment

**Question 2:**
Wardrop's First Principle states that in User Equilibrium (UE), travel times on all used routes between two points are:
a) Equal and minimized for the system
b) Equal and minimized for the individual user
c) Different and minimized for the individual user
d) Different and minimized for the system

**Question 3:**
The BPR (Bureau of Public Roads) function is commonly used in which type of traffic assignment method to relate travel time to volume and capacity?
a) All-or-Nothing (AON) Assignment
b) User Equilibrium (UE) Assignment
c) System Optimum (SO) Assignment
d) Both b and c

**Question 4:**
Explain the fundamental difference between User Equilibrium (UE) and System Optimum (SO) assignment in terms of traveler behavior and objective.

**Question 5:**
Consider a simple network with two routes between Zone A and Zone B.
*   **Route 1:** Free-flow time = 10 minutes, Capacity = 500 vehicles/hour.
*   **Route 2:** Free-flow time = 15 minutes, Capacity = 300 vehicles/hour.
The O-D matrix shows 600 trips from Zone A to Zone B. Assume the BPR function: $t = t_0 [1 + 0.15 (V/C)^4]$.

**(a)** If you were to perform an All-or-Nothing (AON) assignment, which route would all 600 trips be assigned to? What would be the travel time?

**(b)** Briefly describe how a User Equilibrium (UE) assignment would differ from the AON assignment in this scenario. What would eventually happen to the travel times on both routes?

## Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
c) All-or-Nothing (AON) Assignment

**Answer 2:**
b) Equal and minimized for the individual user

**Answer 3:**
d) Both b and c (VDFs are used in both UE and SO, though SO uses marginal VDFs).

**Answer 4:**
*   **User Equilibrium (UE):** Aims to find a state where no individual traveler can reduce their travel time by unilaterally switching to a different route. It models rational travelers seeking to minimize their own travel time.
*   **System Optimum (SO):** Aims to minimize the *total* travel time for all users in the system. It considers the marginal cost of travel, meaning it accounts for the delay an additional vehicle imposes on all other vehicles using the same link. SO is often achieved through mechanisms like road pricing or by directly modeling the system's efficiency.

**Answer 5:**

**(a)**
*   **AON Assignment:** All 600 trips would be assigned to **Route 1** because it has the lower free-flow travel time (10 minutes vs. 15 minutes).
*   **Travel Time on Route 1:** Since AON ignores congestion, the travel time would remain the **free-flow travel time of 10 minutes**.

**(b)**
*   **Difference from AON:** A UE assignment would recognize that assigning 600 trips to Route 1 (which has a capacity of 500 vph) would cause significant congestion. The travel time on Route 1 would increase substantially above its free-flow time.
*   **What would happen:** As the travel time on Route 1 increases due to congestion, it would eventually become equal to the travel time on Route 2 (which would also experience some increased traffic but less severe congestion). At this point, some travelers would switch from Route 1 to Route 2, and vice versa, until a state of equilibrium is reached where the travel times on both routes are equal for those using them, and no traveler can improve their journey by switching. The final travel time on both routes would be higher than the free-flow times, reflecting the congestion.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Traffic assignment is the bridge** between travel demand (O-D matrix) and network performance.
*   **The choice of assignment method depends on the project objectives and available data.** AON is simple but unrealistic; UE is standard for forecasting congestion; SO is for optimizing system efficiency.
*   **Volume-Delay Functions (VDFs) are critical** for modeling congestion and achieving equilibrium in UE and SO assignments.
*   **UE is a descriptive model** of how people behave; **SO is a prescriptive model** for achieving system efficiency.
*   **Activity-Based Models (ABM)** represent a more advanced approach for modeling travel behavior and assignment.
*   **Network representation must be accurate** for meaningful assignment results.
*   **Iterative assignment methods require convergence criteria** to ensure stability of results.
