# Urban Transportation Planning: Module 4 - Transportation and Land Use

## Topic: Transportation Demand Management (TDM)

---

### 1. Introduction to Transportation Demand Management (TDM)

*   **Definition:** Transportation Demand Management (TDM) is a set of strategies aimed at influencing the travel behavior of individuals and organizations to reduce single-occupancy vehicle (SOV) use, decrease traffic congestion, and promote more sustainable and efficient transportation options.
*   **Core Principle:** TDM focuses on the *demand* side of transportation, rather than solely on increasing supply (e.g., building more roads). It recognizes that how people choose to travel is a significant factor in transportation system performance.
*   **Why TDM?**
    *   **Reduce Congestion:** Alleviates traffic bottlenecks and improves travel times.
    *   **Improve Air Quality:** Less vehicle emissions contribute to cleaner air.
    *   **Promote Sustainability:** Encourages modes with lower environmental impact.
    *   **Enhance Equity:** Provides more affordable and accessible transportation options.
    *   **Optimize Infrastructure Use:** Makes better use of existing transportation networks.
    *   **Land Use Efficiency:** Supports denser, mixed-use developments by reducing parking demand.

---

### 2. Key Concepts and Objectives of TDM

*   **Shift from SOV (Single-Occupancy Vehicle) Use:** The primary goal is to encourage a modal shift away from private car use by one person.
*   **Promote Alternative Modes:** Encouraging the use of public transit, cycling, walking, carpooling, and vanpooling.
*   **Reduce Trip Generation:** Minimizing the number of vehicle trips made.
*   **Shift Trip Timing:** Spreading travel demand throughout the day to avoid peak hours.
*   **Influence Trip Destination/Route:** Encouraging travel to locations with better transit access or using less congested routes.

---

### 3. TDM Strategies and Measures

TDM strategies can be broadly categorized as:

#### 3.1. Pricing Strategies

These strategies aim to make driving more expensive, thereby encouraging alternative modes.

*   **Congestion Pricing/Road Pricing:**
    *   **Definition:** Charging drivers a fee to use specific roads or areas during peak hours or when congestion is high.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **London Congestion Charge:** A daily fee for driving in central London.
        *   **Stockholm Congestion Tax:** Similar to London, a charge for entering/exiting central Stockholm.
        *   **Fremont, California (I-880 Express Lane):** A toll lane that dynamically adjusts prices based on traffic demand.
    *   **Key Concept:** **Dynamic Pricing** - prices fluctuate based on real-time traffic conditions.
*   **Parking Pricing:**
    *   **Definition:** Charging for parking, especially in central business districts (CBDs) or areas with high demand.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Market-rate parking:** Allowing private parking operators to set prices based on demand.
        *   **Differential parking pricing:** Higher prices for prime locations or peak hours, lower prices for off-peak or less desirable locations.
        *   **Parking Maximums:** Limiting the amount of parking that can be provided in new developments, thereby increasing parking costs and encouraging other modes.
*   **Fuel Taxes:** A broad-based tax on gasoline and diesel fuel, which increases the cost of driving.

#### 3.2. Non-Pricing Strategies

These strategies focus on improving the attractiveness and convenience of alternatives to SOV travel.

*   **Public Transportation Improvements:**
    *   **Definition:** Enhancing the quality, frequency, reliability, and accessibility of public transit services.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Increased Bus/Train Frequency:** Reducing wait times.
        *   **Dedicated Bus Lanes (BRT - Bus Rapid Transit):** Improving speed and reliability.
        *   **Transit Signal Priority (TSP):** Giving transit vehicles priority at traffic signals.
        *   **Improved Station Amenities:** Shelters, real-time information, safety features.
        *   **Fare Incentives:** Subsidized passes, discounted fares for specific groups.
*   **Non-Motorized Transport Infrastructure:**
    *   **Definition:** Creating safe, convenient, and attractive infrastructure for walking and cycling.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Dedicated Bicycle Lanes and Paths:** Separated from vehicle traffic.
        *   **Pedestrian-Friendly Street Design:** Wider sidewalks, reduced crossing distances, traffic calming measures.
        *   **Bike-Sharing Programs:** Affordable access to bicycles.
        *   **Secure Bicycle Parking:** At transit stations and destinations.
*   **Carpooling and Vanpooling Programs:**
    *   **Definition:** Facilitating and encouraging shared rides.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Ride-Matching Services:** Online platforms connecting potential carpoolers.
        *   **Preferential Parking:** Designated parking spots for carpools and vanpools.
        *   **Vanpool Subsidies:** Financial assistance for vanpool operations.
*   **Telework and Flexible Work Schedules:**
    *   **Definition:** Allowing employees to work from home or adjust their work hours to avoid peak travel times.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Employer-Sponsored Telework Policies:** Encouraging remote work.
        *   **Staggered Work Hours:** Employees starting and ending work at different times.
        *   **Compressed Work Weeks:** Working full-time hours in fewer days.
*   **Travel Demand Management (TDM) Plans for Employers (EO TDM):**
    *   **Definition:** Requirements for large employers to develop and implement plans to reduce their employees' SOV use.
    *   **Components:**
        *   **Information and Education:** Promoting alternative modes.
        *   **Incentives:** Subsidized transit passes, carpool matching, preferential parking.
        *   **Disincentives:** Parking charges, limited parking supply.
        *   **Facilities:** Bicycle parking, showers, changing rooms.
*   **Land Use Planning Integration:**
    *   **Definition:** Designing communities to reduce travel distances and encourage walking, cycling, and transit use.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Mixed-Use Development:** Combining residential, commercial, and retail spaces.
        *   **Transit-Oriented Development (TOD):** High-density development around transit stations.
        *   **Pedestrian-Oriented Design:** Creating vibrant, walkable streetscapes.
        *   **Parking Lot Reductions:** Minimizing large surface parking lots.

### 4. Implementing and Evaluating TDM Strategies

*   **Key Considerations for Implementation:**
    *   **Political Will:** Support from government agencies and elected officials.
    *   **Public Acceptance:** Engaging the public and addressing concerns.
    *   **Funding:** Securing resources for infrastructure and program development.
    *   **Coordination:** Collaboration between transportation agencies, employers, and local governments.
    *   **Target Audience:** Tailoring strategies to specific user groups or geographic areas.
*   **Evaluation Metrics:**
    *   **Modal Split Changes:** Percentage of trips made by different modes.
    *   **Vehicle Miles Traveled (VMT):** Reduction in total distance traveled by private vehicles.
    *   **Congestion Levels:** Travel times, speeds, and delay.
    *   **Air Quality Improvements:** Reduction in pollutant emissions.
    *   **User Satisfaction:** Surveys of travelers' experiences.
    *   **Cost-Effectiveness:** Benefits achieved relative to program costs.

### 5. Case Studies and Examples (Briefly)

*   **Singapore:** Successful implementation of Electronic Road Pricing (ERP) and a comprehensive public transit system.
*   **Curitiba, Brazil:** Pioneering Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system integrated with land-use planning.
*   **Portland, Oregon:** Strong emphasis on public transit, cycling infrastructure, and TDM policies for employers.

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   TDM is a **demand-side** approach to transportation problems.
*   It aims to **shift modes** away from single-occupancy vehicles.
*   **Pricing strategies** make driving more expensive.
*   **Non-pricing strategies** improve the attractiveness of alternatives.
*   **Employer TDM programs** are crucial for influencing commute behavior.
*   **Integration with land-use planning** is essential for long-term TDM success.
*   **Effective implementation** requires political will, public engagement, and funding.
*   **Continuous evaluation** is necessary to assess the effectiveness of TDM measures.

### 7. Practice Questions/Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define Transportation Demand Management (TDM) and explain its primary objectives.

**Answer 1:**
TDM is a set of strategies designed to influence travel behavior to reduce single-occupancy vehicle use, decrease congestion, and promote sustainable transportation. Its primary objectives include reducing congestion, improving air quality, promoting sustainability, enhancing equity, and optimizing infrastructure use by encouraging modal shifts to public transit, walking, cycling, carpooling, and vanpooling, and reducing overall vehicle miles traveled.

---

**Question 2:**
Differentiate between pricing and non-pricing TDM strategies. Provide one example for each.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Pricing Strategies:** These strategies aim to make driving more expensive to discourage its use. **Example:** Congestion pricing, where drivers pay a fee to enter a busy urban area during peak hours.
*   **Non-Pricing Strategies:** These strategies focus on improving the attractiveness, convenience, or accessibility of alternative modes. **Example:** Enhancing public transit services by increasing bus frequency and providing dedicated bus lanes.

---

**Question 3:**
What is an Employer Transportation Demand Management (EO TDM) program, and what are its typical components?

**Answer 3:**
An EO TDM program is a set of strategies implemented by employers to encourage their employees to use alternatives to single-occupancy vehicles for their commute. Typical components include:
    *   **Information and Education:** Raising awareness about available alternatives.
    *   **Incentives:** Offering subsidized transit passes, carpool matching services, or preferential parking for carpools.
    *   **Disincentives:** Implementing parking charges or limiting parking availability.
    *   **Facilities:** Providing amenities like secure bicycle parking, showers, and changing rooms.

---

**Question 4:**
Explain the role of land use planning in supporting TDM.

**Answer 4:**
Land use planning plays a crucial supportive role in TDM by creating environments that inherently reduce the need for long car trips and encourage alternative modes. This includes:
    *   **Mixed-Use Development:** Bringing destinations closer together, making walking and cycling more feasible.
    *   **Transit-Oriented Development (TOD):** Concentrating housing, jobs, and services around transit stations to maximize transit ridership.
    *   **Pedestrian-Friendly Design:** Creating walkable streetscapes with amenities and safety features that encourage walking and cycling.
    *   **Reduced Parking Minimums:** Limiting excessive parking provision, which can drive up development costs and encourage driving.

---

**Question 5:**
Discuss one TDM strategy that aims to shift trip timing.

**Answer 5:**
**Flexible Work Schedules/Staggered Work Hours** is a TDM strategy that aims to shift trip timing. By allowing employees to adjust their start and end times for work, it spreads out the demand for transportation during the morning and evening peak hours. This reduces congestion on roadways and public transit during the absolute busiest periods.
