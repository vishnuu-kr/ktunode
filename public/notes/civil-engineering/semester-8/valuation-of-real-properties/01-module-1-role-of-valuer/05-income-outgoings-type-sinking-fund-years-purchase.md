# VALUATION OF REAL PROPERTIES: Module 1: Role of Valuer

## Topic: Income, Outgoings, Sinking Fund, Year's Purchase

This module introduces fundamental concepts in the valuation of real properties, focusing on the income-generating potential and the associated costs. Understanding these elements is crucial for a valuer to accurately determine the value of a property.

---

### **1. Learning Outcomes**

Upon successful completion of this topic, you will be able to:

*   **Identify and explain the different types of income** generated by real properties.
*   **Differentiate between various categories of outgoings** associated with property ownership and management.
*   **Define and explain the purpose of a sinking fund** in property valuation.
*   **Understand the concept of Year's Purchase (YP)** and its application in income capitalization.
*   **Apply the principles of income, outgoings, and YP** to determine the capital value of investment properties.

### **2. Key Concepts and Definitions**

#### **2.1 Income**

Income refers to the revenue generated by a property. In the context of real estate valuation, the most common form of income is **rent**.

*   **Gross Potential Rent (GPR):** This is the total rent a property could theoretically generate if all units were occupied and all tenants paid their rent in full and on time, without any vacancies or defaults.

    *   **Example:** A building has 10 apartments, each renting for $1,000 per month.
        *   GPR = 10 apartments * $1,000/month/apartment * 12 months = $120,000 per annum.

*   **Effective Gross Income (EGI):** This is the actual income expected from a property after accounting for potential losses due to vacancies and bad debts.

    *   **Formula:** EGI = Gross Potential Rent - Vacancy and Credit Losses

    *   **Example:** Continuing from the GPR example, assume a 5% vacancy and credit loss rate.
        *   Vacancy and Credit Losses = $120,000 * 5% = $6,000
        *   EGI = $120,000 - $6,000 = $114,000 per annum.

*   **Other Income:** This category includes revenue generated from sources other than rent.

    *   **Examples:**
        *   Parking fees
        *   Laundry facilities
        *   Vending machines
        *   Signage rights
        *   Common area maintenance (CAM) charges recovered from tenants (if not included in base rent)

*   **Gross Rental Income (GRI):** This is the total income from rent, which can include base rent plus any additional income recovered from tenants. For simplicity, EGI often refers to the net of potential losses from rent, but if "other income" is significant and reliable, it's added to EGI.

    *   **Formula (if applicable):** GRI = Effective Gross Income + Other Income

    *   **Example:** If the property in the previous example also generates $2,000 per year from laundry machines:
        *   GRI = $114,000 + $2,000 = $116,000 per annum.

#### **2.2 Outgoings (Operating Expenses)**

Outgoings are the costs incurred in owning and operating a property. These are essential for maintaining the property and generating income. They can be categorized as follows:

*   **Management Expenses:** Costs associated with managing the property.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   Property management fees (typically a percentage of EGI or GRI)
        *   Accounting and legal fees
        *   Marketing and advertising costs

*   **Repairs and Maintenance:** Costs for keeping the property in good condition.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   Routine maintenance (e.g., plumbing, electrical repairs)
        *   Landscaping and groundskeeping
        *   Cleaning of common areas
        *   Minor renovations and upkeep

*   **Utilities:** Costs for services consumed by the property.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   Electricity
        *   Water and sewerage
        *   Gas
        *   Garbage collection
    *   *Note:* Sometimes these are paid by tenants directly. The valuer must ascertain who is responsible for each utility.

*   **Insurance:** Costs for protecting the property against damage or loss.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   Property insurance (fire, flood, etc.)
        *   Public liability insurance

*   **Property Taxes:** Levies imposed by local government authorities.
    *   **Example:** Council rates, land tax.

*   **Vacancy and Credit Losses:** Although discussed under income, these are effectively expenses from the owner's perspective, representing lost revenue.

#### **2.3 Sinking Fund**

A sinking fund is a provision made for the eventual replacement or major repair of short-life items or components of a property. These are items that wear out or become obsolete and will need to be replaced over time.

*   **Purpose:** To accumulate funds gradually to cover future capital expenditures without requiring a large lump sum payment at the time of replacement. This ensures the property's long-term viability and value.

*   **Components requiring a sinking fund:**
    *   **Short-life items:** Carpeting, appliances, boilers, air conditioning units, elevators.
    *   **Major renovations/replacements:** Roof replacement, resurfacing driveways, facade repairs.

*   **Calculation:** The amount to be contributed to a sinking fund annually is typically calculated by dividing the estimated cost of replacement by the remaining economic life of the item. A more precise calculation would involve time value of money principles (annuities), but for practical valuation, a simple pro-rata approach is often used or estimated as a percentage of rent.

    *   **Formula (simplified):** Annual Sinking Fund Contribution = (Estimated Replacement Cost) / (Remaining Economic Life in Years)

    *   **Example:** A property's roof is estimated to cost $50,000 to replace and has a remaining economic life of 20 years.
        *   Annual Sinking Fund Contribution = $50,000 / 20 years = $2,500 per annum.

*   **Important Note:** The provision for sinking funds is an **outgoing** and must be deducted from the income to arrive at the **Net Income** or **Net Rental Income (NRI)**.

#### **2.4 Year's Purchase (YP)**

Year's Purchase (YP) is a capital sum that will produce £1 (or $1) per annum in perpetuity (or for a specified term) at a given rate of interest. It is a multiplier used to convert an annual income stream into a capital value.

*   **Concept:** It represents the number of years it would take for the annual income to recoup the initial capital investment, considering the time value of money.

*   **Calculation (Perpetuity):** For an income stream that is assumed to continue forever (perpetuity), the YP is the reciprocal of the required rate of return (or capitalization rate).

    *   **Formula:** YP (perpetuity) = 1 / Rate of Return (expressed as a decimal)

    *   **Example:** If an investor requires a 10% annual return on their investment:
        *   Rate of Return = 10% = 0.10
        *   YP = 1 / 0.10 = 10

    *   **Interpretation:** This means that for every $1 of annual income, the capital value is $10.

*   **Calculation (Fixed Term):** When the income is expected to last for a specific number of years (term certain), the YP calculation becomes more complex as it needs to account for the return of capital over that term. This involves the formula for the present value of an annuity.

    *   **Formula:** YP (term certain) = [1 - (1 + r)^-n] / r
        *   Where:
            *   `r` is the rate of return (expressed as a decimal)
            *   `n` is the number of years in the term

    *   **Example:** An investor wants a 10% return on an income stream that will last for 15 years.
        *   r = 0.10, n = 15
        *   YP = [1 - (1 + 0.10)^-15] / 0.10
        *   YP = [1 - (1.10)^-15] / 0.10
        *   YP = [1 - 0.23939] / 0.10
        *   YP = 0.76061 / 0.10 = 7.6061

    *   **Interpretation:** For every $1 of annual income, the capital value is approximately $7.61.

*   **Factors Affecting YP:**
    *   **Rate of Return:** A higher rate of return leads to a lower YP (less capital is needed to generate the same income).
    *   **Term:** For a term certain, a longer term leads to a higher YP (closer to the perpetuity YP).
    *   **Risk:** Higher perceived risk associated with the income stream leads to a higher required rate of return, thus a lower YP.

### **3. Capitalization Method**

The fundamental principle of valuing income-producing properties is that their value is derived from the income they generate. The capitalization method directly links the income stream to the property's capital value using the Year's Purchase.

*   **Formula:** Capital Value = Net Income x Year's Purchase

*   **Steps to determine Capital Value using the Income Capitalization Method:**

    1.  **Estimate Gross Potential Rent (GPR).**
    2.  **Deduct Vacancy and Credit Losses** to arrive at **Effective Gross Income (EGI)**.
    3.  **Add Other Income** to arrive at **Gross Rental Income (GRI)** (or directly work with EGI if other income is negligible or not applicable).
    4.  **Deduct all Outgoings** (including management, repairs, utilities, insurance, property taxes, and sinking fund provisions) to arrive at **Net Income (NI)** or **Net Rental Income (NRI)**.
    5.  **Determine the appropriate Year's Purchase (YP)** based on the required rate of return, the term of the income stream, and the risk profile of the investment.
    6.  **Multiply the Net Income by the Year's Purchase** to arrive at the **Capital Value**.

    *   **Example:** A property generates a Gross Potential Rent of $150,000 per annum. Vacancy and credit losses are estimated at 7%. Other income from parking is $5,000 per annum.
        *   Outgoings (excluding sinking fund) are $30,000 per annum.
        *   A sinking fund provision for replacements is estimated at $4,000 per annum.
        *   The required rate of return is 12% for a perpetuity.

        **Calculation:**

        1.  GPR = $150,000
        2.  Vacancy & Credit Losses = $150,000 * 7% = $10,500
        3.  EGI = $150,000 - $10,500 = $139,500
        4.  GRI = $139,500 (EGI) + $5,000 (Other Income) = $144,500
        5.  Total Outgoings = $30,000 (excluding sinking fund) + $4,000 (sinking fund) = $34,000
        6.  Net Income (NI) = $144,500 - $34,000 = $110,500 per annum.
        7.  YP (perpetuity at 12%) = 1 / 0.12 = 8.333
        8.  Capital Value = $110,500 * 8.333 = $921,631.50 (approx.)

### **4. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**
A commercial property has a Gross Potential Rent of $200,000 per annum. Estimated vacancy and credit losses are 6%. Other income from signage is $3,000 per annum. Annual operating expenses (excluding sinking fund) are $40,000. A sinking fund provision for replacements is calculated to be $6,000 per annum. If investors require a 10% return on investment for a perpetual income stream, what is the capital value of the property?

**Question 2:**
Explain the difference between Gross Potential Rent and Effective Gross Income, and why this distinction is important in property valuation.

**Question 3:**
A property is expected to generate a Net Income of $50,000 per annum for the next 10 years. If the investor requires an 8% rate of return, calculate the Year's Purchase for this term.

---

### **5. Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**

1.  **GPR:** $200,000
2.  **Vacancy & Credit Losses:** $200,000 * 6% = $12,000
3.  **EGI:** $200,000 - $12,000 = $188,000
4.  **GRI:** $188,000 + $3,000 = $191,000
5.  **Total Outgoings:** $40,000 + $6,000 = $46,000
6.  **Net Income (NI):** $191,000 - $46,000 = $145,000 per annum.
7.  **YP (perpetuity at 10%):** 1 / 0.10 = 10
8.  **Capital Value:** $145,000 * 10 = **$1,450,000**

**Answer 2:**
*   **Gross Potential Rent (GPR)** is the theoretical maximum rent a property could generate if fully occupied and all rent is collected.
*   **Effective Gross Income (EGI)** is the actual expected rent after accounting for potential losses due to vacancies and uncollectible rent (credit losses).

This distinction is crucial because GPR is an optimistic theoretical figure. EGI provides a more realistic and accurate assessment of the income the property is likely to produce. A valuer must use EGI for accurate valuation, as it reflects the practical realities of property ownership and the risk of income loss.

**Answer 3:**

*   **Formula:** YP (term certain) = [1 - (1 + r)^-n] / r
*   **Given:**
    *   r = 8% = 0.08
    *   n = 10 years
*   **Calculation:**
    *   YP = [1 - (1 + 0.08)^-10] / 0.08
    *   YP = [1 - (1.08)^-10] / 0.08
    *   YP = [1 - 0.46319] / 0.08
    *   YP = 0.53681 / 0.08
    *   YP = **6.710** (approximately)

### **6. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Accuracy is Key:** All estimates for income and outgoings must be based on thorough research and market evidence.
*   **Responsibility:** Clearly understand who is responsible for which outgoings (owner vs. tenant).
*   **Sinking Fund:** Do not forget to include a sinking fund provision for short-life items and major replacements. This is a crucial expense that impacts the Net Income.
*   **Rate of Return:** The rate of return used to calculate YP is subjective and depends on market conditions, property type, and investor risk appetite.
*   **Perpetuity vs. Term Certain:** The YP calculation differs significantly for perpetual income versus income for a fixed term. Ensure you use the correct formula.
*   **Net Income is the Basis:** The valuation is always based on the Net Income, not gross income.
*   **Valuer's Role:** The valuer acts as an objective assessor, analyzing the property's income-generating capacity and associated costs to arrive at an informed opinion of value.
