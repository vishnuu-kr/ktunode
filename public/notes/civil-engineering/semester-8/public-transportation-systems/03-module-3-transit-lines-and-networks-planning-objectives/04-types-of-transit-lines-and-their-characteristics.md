# Public Transportation Systems: Module 3 - Transit Lines and Networks: Planning Objectives

## Topic: Types of Transit Lines and Their Characteristics

This module focuses on the fundamental building blocks of public transportation: **transit lines**. Understanding the different types of transit lines and their unique characteristics is crucial for effective network planning and achieving desired planning objectives.

---

### Learning Outcomes:

Upon completion of this topic, you will be able to:

*   Identify and describe the primary types of transit lines.
*   Explain the key characteristics of each transit line type, including their typical roles and operational features.
*   Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of different transit line types.
*   Relate the selection of transit line types to specific planning objectives.
*   Analyze how the spatial arrangement of transit lines forms a network.

---

### 1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Transit Line:** A designated route or path along which a public transportation vehicle (bus, train, tram, etc.) operates, typically serving a sequence of stops or stations.
*   **Transit Network:** The interconnected system of transit lines and their associated infrastructure (stops, stations, depots, etc.) that collectively provide transportation service within a geographic area.
*   **Service Frequency:** The number of times a transit vehicle operates on a particular line within a given period (e.g., per hour).
*   **Headway:** The time interval between successive vehicles operating on the same line.
*   **Coverage:** The extent to which a transit line serves a particular geographic area or population.
*   **Connectivity:** The degree to which different transit lines and modes are integrated, allowing passengers to transfer seamlessly between them.
*   **Capacity:** The maximum number of passengers a transit line or vehicle can carry.
*   **Speed/Travel Time:** The time it takes for a transit vehicle to complete its route, influenced by factors like stops, traffic, and right-of-way.
*   **Directness:** The degree to which a transit line provides a straight or efficient path between origins and destinations.
*   **Span of Service:** The total time during which a transit line operates (e.g., early morning to late night).
*   **Fare Structure:** The system of charges for using the transit service.
*   **Right-of-Way (ROW):** The dedicated or exclusive path on which a transit vehicle operates, free from general traffic interference.

### 2. Types of Transit Lines and Their Characteristics

Transit lines can be broadly categorized based on their spatial configuration, operational characteristics, and the mode of transport they employ.

#### 2.1. Based on Spatial Configuration:

These classifications describe the geometric shape and extent of the route.

*   **Radial Lines:**
    *   **Definition:** Lines that connect a central core or hub (e.g., downtown, major employment center) to outer suburban or outlying areas. They often extend outwards like spokes of a wheel.
    *   **Characteristics:**
        *   **Primary Function:** Move large numbers of people between the central area and the periphery.
        *   **High Demand:** Typically experience the highest passenger volumes, especially during peak hours.
        *   **Longer Distances:** Often cover significant distances.
        *   **High Frequency:** Usually operate with the highest service frequencies in a network.
        *   **Directness:** Aim for directness to the central core.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   A bus route connecting a suburban town to a city's downtown.
        *   A subway line running from the city center to a major airport or outer residential district.
    *   **Advantages:** Efficiently move large volumes of commuters to a central point; strong connectivity to the core.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be less effective in serving cross-town travel; passengers may need to transfer at the core for travel to other outer areas.

*   **Orbital/Circumferential Lines:**
    *   **Definition:** Lines that connect different suburban areas or outer districts without passing through the central core. They typically form a loop or arc around the city.
    *   **Characteristics:**
        *   **Primary Function:** Facilitate cross-town travel and connect areas that would otherwise require a trip into the center and back out.
        *   **Growing Demand:** Increasingly important as suburban areas grow and develop their own activity centers.
        *   **Moderate to High Frequency:** May have varying frequencies depending on the demand between specific points.
        *   **Connectivity:** Crucial for connecting radial lines and providing network integration.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   A bus route that circles a major metropolitan area, connecting several suburban towns and activity centers.
        *   A light rail line that bypasses the downtown core, linking residential areas to employment hubs in different sectors of the city.
    *   **Advantages:** Improve network connectivity; reduce reliance on the central hub for many trips; serve growing suburban demand.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be more complex to plan and operate; may have lower passenger volumes on individual segments compared to radial lines.

*   **Feeder Lines:**
    *   **Definition:** Shorter lines that connect lower-density residential areas to higher-capacity trunk lines (radial or orbital) or major transfer points.
    *   **Characteristics:**
        *   **Primary Function:** Provide local access and "feed" passengers into the main transit network.
        *   **Lower Demand:** Typically serve fewer passengers per segment.
        *   **Shorter Distances:** Operate over shorter distances.
        *   **Moderate to Low Frequency:** Frequencies are often adjusted to match the demand from the trunk line.
        *   **Coverage Focus:** Prioritize serving residential areas to provide access.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   Small local buses in residential neighborhoods that connect to a major bus rapid transit (BRT) corridor.
        *   Local streetcar lines that feed into a subway station.
    *   **Advantages:** Extend the reach of the transit network; provide access to transit for residents not within walking distance of trunk lines.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Lower efficiency if not well-integrated; can experience long dwell times due to frequent stops and passenger boarding/alighting; often have lower speeds.

*   **Cross-Town Lines:**
    *   **Definition:** Lines that run through urban areas, often connecting neighborhoods or activity centers that are not necessarily tied to the central business district. They may involve a mix of urban and suburban segments.
    *   **Characteristics:**
        *   **Primary Function:** Provide direct travel between different parts of the urban fabric.
        *   **Diverse Demand:** Can serve various trip purposes (commute, shopping, social).
        *   **Variable Frequency:** Frequencies can vary greatly depending on the specific corridor and time of day.
        *   **Integration:** May integrate with radial and orbital lines.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   A bus route that connects two major suburban shopping malls directly.
        *   A light rail line that runs east-west through a city, connecting various districts.
    *   **Advantages:** Offer alternative travel options; can relieve congestion on radial lines.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be subject to traffic congestion if not on dedicated ROW; planning can be complex due to multiple origins and destinations.

*   **Local Lines:**
    *   **Definition:** Lines that operate within a specific neighborhood or small geographic area, serving local trips and often making frequent stops. These are typically the shortest and slowest lines.
    *   **Characteristics:**
        *   **Primary Function:** Serve local travel needs within a community.
        *   **Very Low Demand:** Serve the smallest number of passengers per segment.
        *   **Very Short Distances:** Operate over very short distances.
        *   **Low Frequency (often):** Frequencies can be lower, and span of service might be limited.
        *   **Maximum Coverage:** Prioritize serving as many local streets as possible.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   A neighborhood circulator bus.
        *   A dial-a-ride service in a low-density area.
    *   **Advantages:** Provide essential mobility for local trips and non-drivers; enhance accessibility within a community.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Very low operational efficiency; can be slow and may not appeal to longer-distance travelers.

#### 2.2. Based on Operational Characteristics/Mode (Brief Mention, as this can be a separate module):

While this topic focuses on line *types* by geometry, mode significantly influences characteristics.

*   **Bus Lines:** Flexible, can use existing road infrastructure, susceptible to traffic.
*   **Streetcar/Tram Lines:** Higher capacity than buses, often operate on dedicated ROW or mixed traffic, electric power.
*   **Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) Lines:** High capacity, dedicated ROW, pre-board fare payment, station-based boarding, often electric or low-emission vehicles.
*   **Light Rail Transit (LRT) Lines:** Higher capacity than BRT, often operate on dedicated ROW, electric power, greater comfort and speed than streetcars.
*   **Metro/Subway Lines:** Highest capacity, fully grade-separated (underground or elevated), electric power, high speeds, exclusive ROW.

### 3. Relation to Planning Objectives

The choice and design of transit lines are directly linked to achieving specific transportation planning objectives:

*   **Maximizing Coverage:** Feeder and local lines are essential for extending the transit network's reach into residential areas.
*   **Maximizing Ridership/Passenger Volume:** Radial lines connecting major activity centers with high frequencies are key for attracting large numbers of passengers. Orbital and cross-town lines become more important as suburban centers grow.
*   **Improving Speed and Reducing Travel Time:** Lines with dedicated rights-of-way (BRT, LRT, Metro) are crucial for improving transit speed and reliability, making transit more competitive with private vehicles. Directness and fewer stops on trunk lines also contribute.
*   **Enhancing Network Connectivity:** Orbital and cross-town lines are vital for creating a connected network, reducing the need for transfers at the central hub. Well-placed transfer stations between different line types are also critical.
*   **Providing Equity and Access:** Feeder and local lines ensure that residents in lower-density or less affluent areas have access to the transit system.
*   **Optimizing Operational Efficiency:** Matching line type and service level (frequency, vehicle size) to expected demand is crucial. High-frequency radial lines are efficient for high volumes; low-frequency feeder lines are efficient for low volumes.
*   **Environmental Sustainability:** Electrically powered modes like LRT and Metro have lower emissions per passenger-mile. BRT can also incorporate low-emission technologies.

---

### 4. Forming a Transit Network

A transit network is not just a collection of individual lines; it's how these lines interact and complement each other.

*   **Hierarchy of Lines:** Networks often feature a hierarchy, with high-capacity trunk lines (radial, major orbital) forming the backbone, and feeder/local lines connecting to them.
*   **Transfer Points:** Strategic locations where passengers can easily switch between lines. Well-designed transfer points are critical for network usability.
*   **Coverage vs. Connectivity vs. Capacity:** Planning involves balancing these competing objectives. A line might offer great coverage but poor connectivity, or high capacity but limited coverage.
*   **Network Design Principles:**
    *   **Grid Network:** Lines run in parallel and perpendicular directions, offering flexibility in travel but potentially requiring more transfers.
    *   **Radial Network:** Emphasizes travel to/from a central point, efficient for core-focused travel but weaker for cross-town trips.
    *   **Hybrid Networks:** Combine radial and orbital/grid elements to leverage the strengths of each.

---

### 5. Examples

*   **London Underground (The Tube):** Primarily a radial network with some orbital lines (e.g., Circle Line, Overground). The central "Tube" lines are high-capacity, high-frequency radial lines. Outer lines act as feeders and orbital connectors.
*   **Curitiba, Brazil (BRT):** Famous for its bus-centric network with a clear hierarchy. Trunk lines are high-capacity BRT corridors running on dedicated "tubes" (stations), with feeder bus lines collecting passengers from surrounding neighborhoods and feeding into the BRT stations.
*   **Portland, Oregon (MAX Light Rail):** Features radial light rail lines extending from the downtown core into suburban areas, complemented by bus lines acting as feeders.

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **No single line type is ideal for all situations.** The optimal mix depends on the urban form, population density, travel patterns, and planning objectives.
*   **Radial lines are the backbone of most urban transit systems.**
*   **Orbital and cross-town lines are crucial for improving network efficiency and serving suburban growth.**
*   **Feeder lines are essential for extending the reach of the transit network.**
*   **The effectiveness of a transit network depends on the seamless integration of its component lines.**
*   **Consider the trade-offs between coverage, capacity, speed, and cost when selecting line types.**
*   **Dedicated rights-of-way significantly improve the performance and attractiveness of transit lines.**

### 7. Practice Questions/Exercises

1.  **Scenario:** A rapidly growing suburban area with several residential communities and emerging employment centers, but no direct access to the city's central business district (CBD).
    *   **Question:** What types of transit lines would be most appropriate for this area, and why? How would you prioritize their development to serve the planning objectives of providing access and facilitating regional travel?
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Orbital/Circumferential Lines:** Would be crucial for connecting the different residential communities and emerging employment centers, enabling cross-town travel without requiring passengers to go to the CBD. This addresses the objective of facilitating regional travel.
        *   **Feeder Lines:** Would be needed to collect passengers from lower-density residential areas and bring them to the orbital lines or to key transfer points connecting to radial lines in the wider region. This addresses the objective of providing access.
        *   **Radial Lines (potentially):** If there is a strong desire for direct travel to the CBD, a radial line could also be considered, but the orbital and feeder lines would likely be the initial priority given the description of suburban growth.

2.  **Define "Headway" and explain its significance in determining the service level of a transit line.**
    *   **Answer:** Headway is the time interval between successive vehicles operating on the same line. It is a key determinant of service frequency. A shorter headway (e.g., 5 minutes) indicates higher frequency and often means greater passenger convenience, reduced wait times, and higher potential ridership, contributing to the planning objective of maximizing ridership. Conversely, a long headway (e.g., 30 minutes) signifies lower frequency and can deter potential passengers.

3.  **Compare and contrast Radial Lines and Orbital Lines in terms of their primary functions and typical passenger volumes.**
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Radial Lines:** Primarily connect the central core to outer areas. They typically carry the highest passenger volumes, especially during peak commuting hours, as they concentrate demand towards a single destination.
        *   **Orbital Lines:** Connect outer areas to each other, bypassing the central core. Their primary function is to facilitate cross-town travel. Passenger volumes on orbital lines are often distributed across multiple origin-destination pairs and may be lower per segment than high-demand radial lines, but they are crucial for overall network connectivity and serving distributed suburban activity.

4.  **Which type of transit line is most effective at maximizing the "coverage" of a transit system, and what are its limitations in achieving other objectives like "speed" or "capacity"?**
    *   **Answer:** **Feeder Lines and Local Lines** are most effective at maximizing coverage.
        *   **Limitations:**
            *   **Speed:** These lines typically make frequent stops and may operate on mixed traffic ROW, resulting in low average speeds and longer travel times.
            *   **Capacity:** They usually operate with smaller vehicles and serve lower passenger volumes, thus having lower capacity compared to trunk lines.
            *   **Efficiency:** While they provide coverage, their low passenger volumes and frequent stops can make them less operationally efficient per passenger-mile.
