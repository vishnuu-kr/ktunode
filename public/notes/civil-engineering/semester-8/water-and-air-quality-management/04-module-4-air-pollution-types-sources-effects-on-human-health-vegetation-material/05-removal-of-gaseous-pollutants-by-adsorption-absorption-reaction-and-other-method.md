# Water and Air Quality Management - Module 4: Air Pollution

## Topic: Removal of Gaseous Pollutants by Adsorption, Absorption, Reaction, and Other Methods

---

### **Introduction**

Air pollution control is a crucial aspect of environmental management. While understanding the types, sources, and effects of air pollutants is vital, so is knowing how to mitigate their release. This topic focuses on the various technological approaches used to remove gaseous pollutants from industrial emissions and ambient air.

---

### **Learning Outcomes**

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Describe the principles of adsorption for gaseous pollutant removal.
*   Explain the mechanisms and applications of absorption in air pollution control.
*   Discuss the role of chemical reactions in removing gaseous pollutants.
*   Identify and explain other methods used for gaseous pollutant removal.
*   Compare and contrast the different removal methods based on their principles, applications, and efficiencies.
*   Analyze the suitability of various methods for specific gaseous pollutants.

### **1. Removal by Adsorption**

Adsorption is a surface phenomenon where gaseous molecules (adsorbate) accumulate on the surface of a solid material (adsorbent). It's a physical process driven by weak intermolecular forces (van der Waals forces) or stronger chemical bonds (chemisorption).

#### **Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Adsorption:** The accumulation of gas or vapor on the surface of a solid or liquid.
*   **Adsorbent:** The solid material used to remove gaseous pollutants.
*   **Adsorbate:** The gaseous pollutant being removed.
*   **Adsorption Capacity:** The maximum amount of adsorbate that can be adsorbed by a given amount of adsorbent.
*   **Breakthrough:** The point at which the effluent concentration of the pollutant exceeds a predetermined limit, indicating the adsorbent is saturated.
*   **Regeneration:** The process of removing the adsorbed pollutant from the adsorbent so it can be reused.

#### **Principles of Adsorption:**

1.  **Physical Adsorption (Physisorption):**
    *   Involves weak van der Waals forces.
    *   Reversible and exothermic (releases heat).
    *   Low heat of adsorption (typically 20-40 kJ/mol).
    *   Occurs at low temperatures and high pressures.
    *   Multilayer adsorption can occur.

2.  **Chemical Adsorption (Chemisorption):**
    *   Involves the formation of chemical bonds between the adsorbate and adsorbent.
    *   Stronger than physisorption, irreversible or difficult to reverse.
    *   High heat of adsorption (typically 80-400 kJ/mol).
    *   Often requires specific surface sites and activation energy.
    *   Monolayer adsorption is typical.

#### **Common Adsorbents:**

*   **Activated Carbon:** Widely used due to its high surface area, porosity, and affinity for a broad range of organic compounds and some inorganic gases.
    *   *Examples:* Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), chlorine (Cl₂), sulfur dioxide (SO₂).
*   **Zeolites (Molecular Sieves):** Crystalline aluminosilicates with well-defined pore structures, selective for molecules based on size and polarity.
    *   *Examples:* Water vapor, nitrogen oxides (NOx), ammonia (NH₃).
*   **Silica Gel:** Amorphous hydrated silicon dioxide with a porous structure, primarily used for drying gases.
    *   *Examples:* Water vapor.
*   **Alumina:** Activated alumina is used for drying gases and removing polar compounds.
    *   *Examples:* Water vapor, fluorine (F₂).

#### **Adsorption Equipment:**

*   **Fixed-bed Adsorbers:** The most common type. The adsorbent is packed into a vessel, and the polluted air flows through it.
    *   *Operation:* Pollutant is adsorbed until the adsorbent bed is saturated. Regeneration or replacement is then required.
    *   *Example:* Removing VOCs from factory exhaust.
*   **Moving-bed Adsorbers:** The adsorbent moves continuously or in batches through the adsorber, allowing for continuous regeneration.
*   **Fluidized-bed Adsorbers:** The adsorbent particles are suspended in the gas stream, providing good contact and heat transfer.

#### **Regeneration Methods:**

*   **Temperature Swing Adsorption (TSA):** Heating the adsorbent to desorb the pollutant.
*   **Pressure Swing Adsorption (PSA):** Reducing the pressure to desorb the pollutant.
*   **Steam Stripping:** Using steam to desorb the pollutant.

#### **Examples:**

*   **Activated Carbon Filters:** Used in respiratory masks to remove toxic gases and in domestic air purifiers to remove odors and VOCs.
*   **Industrial Scrubbers using Activated Carbon:** To remove H₂S from biogas or SO₂ from flue gas.

#### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   Adsorption is highly effective for a wide range of pollutants, especially organic compounds.
*   The choice of adsorbent is critical and depends on the pollutant and operating conditions.
*   Regeneration is essential for cost-effective operation of fixed-bed systems.
*   Adsorbent lifetime and disposal are important considerations.

### **2. Removal by Absorption (Scrubbing)**

Absorption is a process where gaseous pollutants are dissolved into a liquid solvent. The polluted gas comes into contact with the liquid, and the pollutant transfers from the gas phase to the liquid phase.

#### **Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Absorption:** The process of transferring a gas from a gas phase to a liquid phase.
*   **Absorbent (Scrubbing Liquid):** The liquid used to absorb the gaseous pollutant.
*   **Scrubber:** A device designed for absorption.
*   **Mass Transfer:** The movement of a substance from one phase to another, driven by a concentration gradient.
*   **Driving Force:** The difference in partial pressure or concentration between the gas and liquid phases.

#### **Principles of Absorption:**

*   **Physical Absorption:** The pollutant dissolves in the liquid based on its solubility (Henry's Law).
    *   *Example:* Ammonia (NH₃) dissolving in water.
*   **Chemical Absorption:** The pollutant reacts chemically with the absorbent to form a new compound that is more soluble or less volatile. This is usually more effective than physical absorption.
    *   *Example:* Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) reacting with a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution to form sodium sulfite (Na₂SO₃).

#### **Factors Affecting Absorption Efficiency:**

*   **Solubility of the Pollutant:** Higher solubility leads to more efficient absorption.
*   **Partial Pressure of the Pollutant:** Higher partial pressure increases the driving force.
*   **Temperature:** Generally, solubility decreases with increasing temperature.
*   **Surface Area of Contact:** Larger contact area between gas and liquid enhances mass transfer.
*   **Contact Time:** Longer contact time allows for more absorption.
*   **Efficiency of the Scrubber:** Design of the scrubbing equipment.

#### **Common Absorbents:**

*   **Water:** For soluble gases like ammonia (NH₃), hydrogen chloride (HCl), hydrogen fluoride (HF).
*   **Alkaline Solutions:** Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), lime (Ca(OH)₂) for acidic gases like SO₂, HCl, HF, H₂S.
    *   *Example:* Flue gas desulfurization (FGD) using lime slurry to remove SO₂.
*   **Acidic Solutions:** For basic gases like ammonia (NH₃).
    *   *Example:* Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) for ammonia removal.
*   **Oxidizing Agents:** Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), ozone (O₃) for removal of substances like H₂S, mercaptans.

#### **Absorption Equipment (Scrubbers):**

*   **Spray Towers:** Gas flows upwards through a tower where liquid is sprayed downwards, creating droplets for contact.
*   **Packed Towers:** Gas flows upwards through a packed bed of inert material (e.g., rings, saddles) which is wetted by the liquid flowing downwards. This provides a large surface area for mass transfer.
    *   *Example:* Removing SO₂ from power plant emissions.
*   **Plate Towers (Tray Towers):** Gas bubbles up through liquid on trays, or liquid flows over trays with gas flowing across.
*   **Venturi Scrubbers:** Gas and liquid are mixed at high velocity in a Venturi throat, creating intense turbulence and high mass transfer rates. Also effective for particulate removal.
    *   *Example:* Removing HCl from industrial processes.
*   **Bubble Cap Scrubbers:** Gas is forced through openings in caps submerged in liquid.

#### **Examples:**

*   **Wet Scrubbers for Flue Gas Desulfurization (FGD):** Power plants use large packed towers or spray towers with lime or limestone slurry to remove SO₂.
*   **Venturi Scrubbers:** Used to remove acidic fumes like HCl from chemical plants.
*   **Ammonia Scrubbers:** Using acidic solutions to remove ammonia from industrial emissions.

#### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   Absorption is very effective for removing highly soluble gases and acidic/basic gases when using appropriate chemical absorbents.
*   The primary byproduct is a liquid waste stream (scrubber liquor) that requires treatment or disposal.
*   Corrosion can be an issue with some scrubbing solutions.
*   Scrubber design significantly impacts efficiency.

### **3. Removal by Reaction**

This method involves inducing a chemical reaction to convert a gaseous pollutant into a less harmful or removable substance. This can be achieved through oxidation, reduction, or other chemical transformations, often facilitated by catalysts.

#### **Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Catalytic Oxidation:** Using a catalyst to promote the reaction of a pollutant with oxygen, often converting it to CO₂ and H₂O.
*   **Catalytic Reduction:** Using a catalyst to promote the reaction of a pollutant with a reducing agent (e.g., ammonia), often converting it to N₂ and H₂O.
*   **Catalyst:** A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed in the process.
*   **Regenerative Thermal Oxidizer (RTO):** Uses ceramic media to preheat incoming polluted air and then combusts the pollutants at high temperatures. The heat is then transferred to the ceramic media to preheat the next batch of polluted air.

#### **Principles of Reaction-Based Removal:**

1.  **Oxidation:**
    *   **Thermal Oxidation (Incineration):** Direct combustion of pollutants at high temperatures (700-1200°C) to convert them into CO₂, H₂O, and other less harmful substances. Requires significant fuel input.
    *   **Catalytic Oxidation:** Similar to thermal oxidation but occurs at lower temperatures (250-500°C) in the presence of a catalyst (e.g., platinum, palladium, or rare earth oxides). More energy-efficient for some pollutants.
        *   *Example:* Catalytic converters in vehicles to oxidize CO to CO₂, unburnt hydrocarbons to CO₂ and H₂O, and reduce NOx.
        *   *Example:* Catalytic oxidizers for VOCs from industrial sources.

2.  **Reduction:**
    *   **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR):** Used primarily for NOx control. Ammonia (NH₃) is injected into the flue gas, and in the presence of a catalyst, NOx is reduced to nitrogen (N₂) and water (H₂O).
        *   *Reaction:* 4NO + 4NH₃ + O₂ → 4N₂ + 6H₂O
        *   *Example:* SCR systems in power plants and diesel engines.
    *   **Non-Selective Catalytic Reduction (NSCR):** Used in conjunction with a three-way catalyst in gasoline engines, where a rich air-fuel mixture ensures the presence of both reducing and oxidizing agents to convert CO, unburnt hydrocarbons, and NOx simultaneously.

3.  **Other Reactions:**
    *   **Ozonation:** Using ozone (O₃) as a powerful oxidant to break down complex organic molecules or oxidize inorganic pollutants.
        *   *Example:* Ozonation for removing H₂S and mercaptans from odor control.
    *   **Photocatalytic Oxidation:** Using light (often UV) in combination with a photocatalyst (e.g., TiO₂) to generate reactive oxygen species that oxidize pollutants. Still largely in research and development for large-scale applications.

#### **Catalytic Converters in Vehicles:**

*   A prime example of reaction-based removal.
*   Three-way catalysts convert CO, unburnt hydrocarbons, and NOx into less harmful substances.

#### **Examples:**

*   **Industrial Catalytic Oxidizers:** Used to control VOC emissions from painting, printing, and chemical manufacturing.
*   **SCR Systems on Trucks and Power Plants:** To reduce NOx emissions.
*   **Thermal Oxidizers:** For high concentration VOC streams.

#### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   Reaction-based methods often achieve very high destruction efficiencies for organic pollutants.
*   Catalysts can be expensive and are susceptible to deactivation (poisoning).
*   Requires specific operating conditions (temperature, fuel availability).
*   Produces byproducts like CO₂ and H₂O, which can contribute to greenhouse gases.

### **4. Other Methods**

Several other techniques are employed for removing gaseous pollutants, often complementing or being integrated with the above methods.

#### **Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Adsorption/Absorption Hybrid Systems:** Combining different technologies for enhanced removal.
*   **Biofiltration:** Using microorganisms to degrade gaseous pollutants.
*   **Plasma Treatment:** Using ionized gas (plasma) to break down pollutants.

#### **Methods:**

1.  **Biofiltration (Biotreatment):**
    *   **Princ:** Microorganisms (bacteria, fungi) are immobilized on a packing material (e.g., compost, wood chips) and use the gaseous pollutants as a source of carbon and energy for their metabolism.
    *   **Application:** Odor control, removal of VOCs, H₂S, NH₃ from low to moderate concentrations.
    *   **Advantages:** Low operating cost, environmentally friendly.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited to biodegradable pollutants, sensitive to temperature and humidity fluctuations, can be slow for high concentrations.
    *   *Example:* Treating emissions from composting facilities, wastewater treatment plants.

2.  **Plasma Treatment:**
    *   **Princ:** High-energy plasma is generated, which creates highly reactive species (electrons, ions, radicals) that can break down complex organic molecules and convert them into simpler, less harmful substances.
    *   **Application:** Decomposing VOCs, dioxins, and other hazardous air pollutants.
    *   **Advantages:** Effective for a wide range of recalcitrant pollutants, can operate at ambient temperatures.
    *   **Disadvantages:** High energy consumption, can produce ozone and NOx as byproducts, complex technology.

3.  **Condensation:**
    *   **Princ:** Cooling a gas stream below the dew point of a volatile pollutant to condense it into a liquid, which can then be collected.
    *   **Application:** Recovering valuable volatile organic compounds (VOCs) or removing high-concentration solvent vapors.
    *   **Advantages:** Can be used for pollutant recovery.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Only effective for pollutants with relatively high boiling points or at very low temperatures, energy-intensive if refrigeration is used.

4.  **Membrane Separation:**
    *   **Princ:** Using semi-permeable membranes that allow certain gases to pass through while retaining others.
    *   **Application:** Separation of specific gases (e.g., hydrogen recovery from process streams, CO₂ capture).
    *   **Advantages:** Energy efficient for specific separations, can operate at ambient temperatures.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Membrane fouling, limited range of applications for general air pollution control.

#### **Examples:**

*   **Biofilters:** Treating odors from food processing plants.
*   **Condensers:** Solvent recovery in pharmaceutical manufacturing.

#### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   These methods often address specific niche applications or are used in combination with other techniques.
*   Each method has its own set of advantages, disadvantages, and optimal operating conditions.

### **5. Comparison of Removal Methods**

| Feature          | Adsorption                                     | Absorption (Scrubbing)                            | Reaction (Oxidation/Reduction)                     | Biofiltration                                     |
| :--------------- | :--------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| **Principle**    | Surface accumulation of gas on solid           | Dissolution of gas into liquid                    | Chemical conversion of pollutant                   | Biological degradation of pollutant                 |
| **Pollutants**   | VOCs, H₂S, SO₂, Cl₂, NH₃ (broad range)         | Highly soluble gases, acidic/basic gases (SO₂, HCl, NH₃) | VOCs, CO, NOx, H₂S                                 | Biodegradable VOCs, H₂S, NH₃                      |
| **Byproducts**   | Spent adsorbent, desorbed pollutants           | Scrubber liquor, treated gas                      | CO₂, H₂O, N₂                                       | CO₂, H₂O, biomass                                 |
| **Efficiency**   | High for many organics; can be selective       | High for soluble & reactive gases                 | Very high for target pollutants                    | Moderate to high for suitable pollutants            |
| **Operating Temp.** | Ambient to elevated (for regeneration)       | Ambient to elevated                               | High (thermal), moderate (catalytic)               | Ambient                                           |
| **Energy Use**   | Moderate (regeneration can be energy-intensive) | Moderate (pumps, fans)                            | High (thermal), moderate (catalytic)               | Low                                               |
| **Advantages**   | High capacity, regenerable, good for low conc. | Cost-effective for high conc., handles high flow rates | High destruction efficiency, can eliminate pollutants | Environmentally friendly, low cost, odor control    |
| **Disadvantages** | Adsorbent cost, disposal, breakthrough       | Liquid waste, corrosion, not for all gases        | Catalyst cost/deactivation, potential byproducts | Slow, limited pollutant range, sensitive to conditions |
| **Examples**     | Activated carbon filters, PSA units            | FGD scrubbers, Venturi scrubbers                  | Catalytic converters, SCR systems, RTOs            | Odor control units in waste management            |

### **Practice Questions/Exercises**

1.  **Which method is generally preferred for removing highly soluble gases like ammonia from an industrial exhaust stream?**
    *   a) Adsorption
    *   b) Absorption
    *   c) Catalytic Oxidation
    *   d) Biofiltration

2.  **Explain the difference between physical adsorption and chemical adsorption.**

3.  **A power plant is facing strict regulations on SO₂ emissions. Which pollution control technology would be most appropriate and why?**

4.  **You are designing an air pollution control system for a facility emitting significant amounts of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from solvent evaporation. Discuss the suitability of adsorption and catalytic oxidation for this application.**

5.  **What is Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR) used for, and what are the key reactants involved?**

6.  **Under what circumstances might biofiltration be a more attractive option than thermal oxidation for VOC control?**

---

### **Answers to Practice Questions**

1.  **b) Absorption:** Ammonia is highly soluble in water and acidic solutions, making absorption a very effective and common method for its removal.
2.  **Physical Adsorption (Physisorption):** Involves weak van der Waals forces, is reversible, occurs at low temperatures, and can form multilayers. **Chemical Adsorption (Chemisorption):** Involves the formation of chemical bonds, is stronger, often irreversible, occurs at higher temperatures, and typically forms a monolayer.
3.  **Wet Scrubbing (Absorption) with alkaline solutions (like lime or limestone):** This is the most common and cost-effective method for removing SO₂ from flue gas in power plants. The SO₂ reacts with the alkaline absorbent to form sulfates or sulfites, which are then removed as sludge.
4.  **Adsorption:** Activated carbon is highly effective for VOCs, especially at lower concentrations or intermittent emissions. It can recover the solvents if regeneration is done carefully. However, it requires regeneration or replacement and can have breakthrough issues.
    **Catalytic Oxidation:** This method offers high destruction efficiency for VOCs, converting them to CO₂ and H₂O. It is suitable for continuous, higher concentration VOC streams. It is more energy-intensive than adsorption but offers complete destruction rather than transfer to another medium.
    *   **Choice depends on:** VOC concentration, flow rate, economic factors, and whether solvent recovery is desired. For low to moderate concentrations and intermittent emissions, adsorption might be preferred. For high and continuous VOC streams, catalytic oxidation or thermal oxidation might be more suitable.
5.  **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR) is used for the removal of nitrogen oxides (NOx) from flue gases, typically from combustion sources like power plants and diesel engines. The key reactants involved are NOx and ammonia (NH₃), with a catalyst facilitating the conversion to nitrogen (N₂) and water (H₂O).**
6.  **Biofiltration would be more attractive than thermal oxidation when:**
    *   **The VOCs are biodegradable:** Thermal oxidation can handle a wider range of VOCs, but biofiltration is very effective for readily biodegradable compounds.
    *   **The VOC concentrations are low to moderate:** Thermal oxidation is generally more cost-effective for high concentration streams.
    *   **Odor control is also a primary concern:** Biofiltration is excellent for odor removal due to the nature of the microbial processes.
    *   **Energy efficiency and lower operating costs are paramount:** Biofiltration has significantly lower energy requirements compared to thermal oxidation.
    *   **Environmental friendliness is a key driver:** Biofiltration is a more natural and less energy-intensive process.

### **Conclusion**

The removal of gaseous pollutants is a multifaceted field employing a range of sophisticated technologies. Understanding the principles behind adsorption, absorption, reaction-based methods, and other techniques allows for the selection and design of effective air pollution control systems, contributing significantly to improving air quality and protecting human health and the environment. Each method has its own strengths and weaknesses, and the optimal choice often depends on the specific pollutant, its concentration, flow rate, economic considerations, and environmental regulations.
