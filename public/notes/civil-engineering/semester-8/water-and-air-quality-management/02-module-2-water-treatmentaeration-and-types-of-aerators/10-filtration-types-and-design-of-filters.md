# Water and Air Quality Management

## Module 2: Water Treatment: Aeration and Types of Aerators

### Topic: Filtration: Types and Design of Filters

### 1. Introduction to Filtration in Water Treatment

Filtration is a crucial physical process in water treatment that removes suspended solids, turbidity, and other particulate matter from water. It involves passing water through a porous medium that traps these impurities. Effective filtration is essential for producing aesthetically pleasing, safe, and palatable drinking water, as well as for preparing water for downstream processes.

### 3. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Filtration:** The process of separating suspended solids from a liquid by passing the liquid through a porous medium.
*   **Porous Medium:** The material used in a filter that allows water to pass through but retains suspended solids (e.g., sand, gravel, anthracite, membranes).
*   **Suspended Solids:** Particulate matter that floats or remains dispersed in water.
*   **Turbidity:** A measure of the cloudiness or haziness of water caused by suspended solids.
*   **Head Loss:** The pressure drop that occurs as water flows through a filter medium.
*   **Loading Rate:** The volume of water applied to a filter per unit area per unit time (e.g., L/m²/h or gpm/ft²).
*   **Filtration Rate:** The speed at which water passes through the filter medium.
*   **Backwashing:** The process of reversing the flow of water through a filter to clean the filter medium and remove trapped solids.
*   **Filter Ripening:** The initial period after a filter is put into service during which its filtration efficiency improves as a stable "schmutzdecke" or filter cake forms.
*   **Schmutzdecke (Filter Cake):** A biological and particulate layer that forms on the surface of a slow sand filter, significantly contributing to its purification efficiency.

---

### 4. Purpose of Filtration in Water Treatment

Filtration serves several vital purposes:

*   **Removal of Suspended Solids and Turbidity:** This is the primary goal, leading to clearer and more aesthetically pleasing water.
*   **Reduction of Pathogens:** While not a primary disinfection method, filtration can remove a significant portion of bacteria, protozoa, and some viruses, especially when combined with pre-treatment like coagulation and flocculation.
*   **Protection of Downstream Processes:** Removing solids prevents clogging and damage to equipment in subsequent treatment stages like disinfection, ion exchange, or membrane processes.
*   **Improvement of Disinfection Efficiency:** Clearer water allows disinfectants (like chlorine or UV light) to penetrate more effectively and reach all parts of the water.
*   **Removal of Color and Odor:** Some filters can adsorb dissolved organic matter responsible for color and odor.

---

### 5. Classification of Filters

Filters can be classified based on various criteria:

**A. Based on Filtration Rate:**

*   **Slow Sand Filters (SSF):** Operate at low filtration rates, relying heavily on biological activity for purification.
*   **Rapid Sand Filters (RSF):** Operate at high filtration rates and typically require pre-treatment and more frequent backwashing.

**B. Based on the Filtration Medium:**

*   **Single-Medium Filters:** Use one type of filter material (e.g., sand).
*   **Dual-Media Filters:** Use two layers of different materials (e.g., anthracite over sand).
*   **Multimedia Filters (MMF):** Use three or more layers of different materials with decreasing grain size from top to bottom.
*   **Other Media Filters:** Utilize materials like activated carbon, diatomaceous earth, or synthetic membranes.

**C. Based on Operating Pressure:**

*   **Gravity Filters:** Water flows through the filter by gravity.
*   **Pressure Filters:** Water is forced through the filter under pressure.

### 6. Types of Filters

#### 6.1. Slow Sand Filters (SSF)

*   **Description:** These are the oldest form of modern water filtration. They consist of a watertight tank filled with a layer of fine sand (0.15-0.35 mm effective size) supported by gravel layers. Water flows through the sand bed at a very low rate (typically 0.1-0.4 m³/m²/h or 0.05-0.2 gpm/ft²).
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   **Physical Straining:** Larger particles are trapped on the surface of the sand bed.
    *   **Sedimentation:** Some particles settle out within the pores.
    *   **Biological Action (Key Feature):** A vital biological layer, the **schmutzdecke**, forms on the sand surface. This layer contains microorganisms that consume organic matter and pathogens, greatly enhancing purification.
*   **Design Aspects:**
    *   **Sand Layer:** 0.6 to 1.2 meters deep.
    *   **Gravel Support:** Layers of gravel of decreasing size (0.5-2.0 cm) at the bottom.
    *   **Underdrain System:** Perforated pipes or a porous concrete bottom.
    *   **Influent and Effluent Control:** Devices to maintain a constant water level and flow rate.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   High-quality effluent, often producing water with very low turbidity and bacterial counts.
    *   No chemical pre-treatment is usually required.
    *   Simple to operate and maintain.
    *   Effective removal of pathogens.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Large land area required due to low filtration rates.
    *   Low filtration rates mean a slower overall treatment process.
    *   Requires periodic cleaning (scraping off the top layer of sand) when flow rate decreases significantly or head loss becomes too high.
    *   Vulnerable to shock loads of organic matter or turbidity.
    *   Filter ripening period needed before optimal performance.
*   **Example:** Used in many smaller communities and developing countries where land is abundant and operational simplicity is paramount.

#### 6.2. Rapid Sand Filters (RSF)

*   **Description:** These filters operate at significantly higher rates than SSFs and are commonly used in municipal water treatment plants. They consist of a bed of coarser sand (0.45-1.0 mm effective size) or a combination of media, supported by gravel.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   **Physical Straining:** Traps particles in the pores of the filter media.
    *   **Sedimentation:** Particles settle within the filter bed.
    *   **Adsorption:** Some materials adhere to the surface of the filter grains.
    *   **Interception:** Particles following the streamlines of water flow come into contact with filter grains and are captured.
    *   **Inertial Impaction:** Larger, denser particles moving at higher velocities may not follow the water streamlines and collide with the filter grains.
    *   **Brownian Movement (Diffusion):** Very small particles move randomly and are more likely to come into contact with filter grains.
*   **Design Aspects:**
    *   **Media Layer:** Typically 0.6 to 1.0 meters deep.
    *   **Single-Media:** Primarily sand.
    *   **Dual-Media:** Anthracite (larger, lighter) over sand (smaller, heavier). This allows for deeper penetration of solids into the bed, extending filter runs.
    *   **Multimedia:** Typically anthracite (top), sand (middle), and garnet/ilmenite (bottom). Offers the deepest penetration of solids and longer filter runs.
    *   **Gravel Support:** Graded layers of gravel.
    *   **Underdrain System:** Often a porous plate or a Wheeler underdrain system (nozzles) designed to distribute wash water evenly.
    *   **Loading Rate:** Typically 5-15 m³/m²/h (2.5-7.5 gpm/ft²), sometimes higher.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   High filtration rates, allowing for smaller filter area requirements.
    *   Efficient removal of turbidity and suspended solids, especially after coagulation and flocculation.
    *   Can be easily automated.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Requires effective pre-treatment (coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation) for optimal performance.
    *   Requires frequent backwashing to maintain filtration efficiency.
    *   Less effective at removing very small pathogens compared to SSFs unless combined with other treatment processes.
    *   Higher operating costs due to backwashing and energy consumption.
*   **Example:** The most common type of filter in large-scale municipal water treatment plants worldwide.

#### 6.3. Pressure Filters

*   **Description:** Water is forced through the filter bed under pressure. They can be used for both rapid and slow filtration principles but are generally more compact due to the pressurized operation.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Gravity Filters (discussed above):** Operate under atmospheric pressure.
    *   **Pressure Leaf Filters:** Consist of vertical or horizontal cylindrical vessels containing filter cloths or screens arranged on frames (leaves). Water is pumped into the vessel, and the filtrate passes through the leaves. Solids accumulate on the leaf surfaces.
    *   **Cartridge Filters:** Use replaceable cartridges with various porous materials.
    *   **Membrane Filters:** Utilize semi-permeable membranes with very small pore sizes (microfiltration, ultrafiltration, nanofiltration, reverse osmosis).
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Compact design.
    *   Can be integrated directly into pressurized piping systems.
    *   Less susceptible to contamination from the atmosphere.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Higher energy consumption (due to pumping).
    *   More complex maintenance and operation.
    *   Potential for leaks.
*   **Example:** Leaf filters are used in industries for product clarification, while cartridge and membrane filters are common in smaller-scale applications, point-of-use systems, and advanced water treatment.

#### 6.4. Other Filtration Technologies

*   **Diatomaceous Earth (DE) Filters:** Use a fine powder of fossilized diatoms as the filter medium. Very effective for removing fine particles and some microorganisms.
*   **Bag Filters:** Water passes through a fabric bag supported by a perforated basket.
*   **Membrane Filtration:**
    *   **Microfiltration (MF):** Pore sizes typically 0.1-10 µm. Removes bacteria, suspended solids, and some larger viruses.
    *   **Ultrafiltration (UF):** Pore sizes typically 0.01-0.1 µm. Removes bacteria, viruses, and macromolecules.
    *   **Nanofiltration (NF):** Pore sizes typically 0.001-0.01 µm. Removes divalent ions, organic molecules, and some monovalent ions.
    *   **Reverse Osmosis (RO):** Smallest pore size (< 0.0001 µm). Removes most dissolved salts, ions, and virtually all contaminants.

### 7. Design Considerations for Filters

When designing or selecting a filter system, several factors must be considered:

*   **Water Quality:**
    *   **Influent Turbidity:** High turbidity requires more robust pre-treatment and potentially more frequent backwashing.
    *   **Nature of Suspended Solids:** Size, density, and stickiness of particles.
    *   **Presence of Pathogens:** Requires filtration capable of physical removal and/or promoting biological activity.
*   **Required Effluent Quality:**
    *   **Target Turbidity Level:** Dictates the type and effectiveness of the filtration required.
    *   **Specific Contaminant Removal Needs:** (e.g., Giardia, Cryptosporidium).
*   **Filtration Rate (Loading Rate):**
    *   Determines the size of the filter installation and impacts operational costs.
    *   Influenced by the type of filter and the nature of the water.
*   **Available Land Area:** Crucial for distinguishing between SSFs and RSFs.
*   **Capital and Operating Costs:**
    *   **Capital:** Cost of the filter unit, media, underdrains, backwash system, etc.
    *   **Operating:** Energy for pumping, backwashing water, labor, maintenance, chemical costs (for pre-treatment).
*   **Backwashing Requirements:**
    *   Frequency and duration of backwashing.
    *   Availability of backwash water.
    *   Disposal of backwash water.
*   **Head Loss Characteristics:** How quickly head loss builds up during a filter run.
*   **Reliability and Ease of Operation:** Simplicity of operation is often preferred, especially for smaller systems.
*   **Regulatory Standards:** Effluent quality must meet established drinking water standards.
*   **Seasonal Variations:** Changes in raw water quality (e.g., during rainfall events) might necessitate adjustments in filtration operation.

### 8. Principles of Filter Operation

*   **Depth Filtration:** Solids are trapped at various depths within the filter bed, not just on the surface. This is characteristic of RSFs.
*   **Surface Filtration:** Solids are primarily retained on the surface of the filter medium, forming a cake. This is more pronounced in SSFs (schmutzdecke) and some membrane filters.
*   **Physical Trapping:** Particles are physically blocked by the pores in the filter medium. The effectiveness depends on the pore size relative to particle size.
*   **Adsorption:** Certain substances or particles adhere to the surface of the filter media grains due to molecular forces.
*   **Biological Action:** In SSFs, the schmutzdecke plays a dominant role in removing pathogens and organic matter through biological degradation.

---

### 9. Backwashing of Filters

*   **Purpose:** To remove accumulated solids from the filter bed, restore its permeability, and maintain the filtration rate and efficiency.
*   **Process:**
    1.  **Stop Influent Flow:** The flow of raw water to the filter is stopped.
    2.  **Reverse Flow:** Clean water (or a combination of water and air) is pumped upwards through the filter bed.
    3.  **Expansion of Bed:** The upward flow fluidizes the filter media, causing it to expand. This expansion allows trapped particles to be dislodged.
    4.  **Washing:** The expanded bed is agitated by the flow, releasing the trapped solids.
    5.  **Drainage:** The backwash water, now containing the solids, is collected and sent to waste or a backwash water treatment facility.
    6.  **Rinse and Return to Service:** Once clean, the flow is stopped, the bed settles, and the filter is returned to service.
*   **Key Parameters:**
    *   **Backwash Rate:** Sufficient to expand the bed by a certain percentage (e.g., 30-50%).
    *   **Backwash Duration:** Long enough to ensure effective cleaning, typically 5-15 minutes.
    *   **Backwash Water Temperature:** Colder water is denser, requiring a higher rate for the same expansion.
    *   **Air Scouring:** Often used in conjunction with water washing to provide more vigorous agitation and improve cleaning efficiency.

### 10. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
What is the primary difference in the mechanism of purification between a Slow Sand Filter and a Rapid Sand Filter?

**Answer 1:**
The primary difference lies in the role of biological activity. Slow Sand Filters rely heavily on the **schmutzdecke** (biological layer) that forms on the sand surface for pathogen removal. Rapid Sand Filters rely more on physical straining, adsorption, and interception, and are less dependent on biological action, requiring pre-treatment like coagulation.

**Question 2:**
List three important design considerations when choosing between a Slow Sand Filter and a Rapid Sand Filter for a municipal water treatment plant.

**Answer 2:**
Three important design considerations are:
1.  **Available Land Area:** SSFs require significantly more land due to their low filtration rates, whereas RSFs are more compact.
2.  **Required Effluent Quality:** While both can produce good quality water, SSFs with a mature schmutzdecke often achieve higher pathogen removal. RSFs are more reliant on effective pre-treatment.
3.  **Capital and Operating Costs:** SSFs have lower capital costs for simpler construction but higher land costs. RSFs have higher capital costs for complex underdrains and backwash systems, but lower land costs and can be automated, potentially leading to lower labor costs.

**Question 3:**
Define "filter ripening" and explain why it is particularly important for Slow Sand Filters.

**Answer 3:**
**Filter ripening** is the initial period after a filter is put into service during which its filtration efficiency improves as a stable "schmutzdecke" or filter cake forms on the surface of the filter medium. This process is particularly important for Slow Sand Filters because the **schmutzdecke** is the primary agent responsible for the biological purification and pathogen removal. Without a developed schmutzdecke, the SSF's effluent quality is significantly lower. This ripening period can take several weeks.

**Question 4:**
If a Rapid Sand Filter's head loss becomes too high, what is the typical remedial action?

**Answer 4:**
The typical remedial action is to **backwash** the filter. This involves reversing the flow of clean water through the filter bed to dislodge and remove the accumulated solids, thereby restoring the filter's permeability and reducing head loss.

**Question 5:**
What is the main advantage of using a multimedia filter compared to a single-medium sand filter?

**Answer 5:**
The main advantage of a multimedia filter is its ability to achieve **deeper penetration of solids** into the filter bed and **longer filter runs** between backwashes. The layers of different media (e.g., anthracite, sand, garnet) have decreasing pore sizes from top to bottom, allowing larger particles to be trapped higher up and smaller particles to be trapped deeper within the bed. This leads to more efficient particle removal and a reduced frequency of backwashing.

### 11. Important Points to Remember

*   **Filtration is a critical step** in producing safe and palatable water.
*   **Slow Sand Filters (SSFs)** rely on biological action (schmutzdecke) and operate at low rates, requiring significant land.
*   **Rapid Sand Filters (RSFs)** operate at high rates, typically require pre-treatment, and need regular backwashing.
*   **Multimedia filters** offer advantages over single-medium filters by extending filter runs and improving filtration efficiency.
*   **Backwashing** is essential for maintaining filter performance by removing accumulated solids.
*   **Design considerations** are crucial for selecting the appropriate filter type and ensuring effective water treatment.
*   **Understanding the principles of filtration** (straining, adsorption, biological action) helps in optimizing the process.
*   **Membrane filtration** represents advanced technologies with very small pore sizes for specific contaminant removal.
