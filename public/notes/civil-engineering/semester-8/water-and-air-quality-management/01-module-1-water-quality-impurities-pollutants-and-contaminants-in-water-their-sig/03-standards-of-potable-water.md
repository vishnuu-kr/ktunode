# WATER AND AIR QUALITY MANAGEMENT - Module 1: Water Quality

## Topic: Standards of Potable Water

### Introduction

Potable water is water that is safe for human consumption. Ensuring its quality is paramount to public health. This topic explores the various standards and parameters that define potable water, focusing on the impurities (pollutants and contaminants) that must be controlled and the methods used to assess them.

---

### Learning Outcomes

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Understand the concept and importance of potable water standards.
*   Identify various categories of impurities found in water and their significance.
*   Explain the common parameters used to assess water quality.
*   Describe the typical standards and guidelines for potable water quality.
*   Recognize the role of regulatory bodies and their standards.
*   Discuss the general principles of water quality testing.

### 1. The Concept and Importance of Potable Water Standards

**1.1 What are Potable Water Standards?**

Potable water standards are a set of legally enforceable or recommended limits for various physical, chemical, biological, and radiological characteristics of water intended for drinking. They are designed to protect public health by preventing the transmission of waterborne diseases and minimizing exposure to harmful substances.

**1.2 Why are Potable Water Standards Important?**

*   **Public Health Protection:** Prevents diseases caused by pathogenic microorganisms (e.g., cholera, typhoid, dysentery).
*   **Prevention of Chronic Health Effects:** Limits exposure to harmful chemical contaminants that can cause long-term health issues (e.g., cancer, neurological damage).
*   **Aesthetic Appeal:** Ensures water is palatable, free from unpleasant tastes, odors, and appearances, encouraging consumption.
*   **Economic Impact:** Contaminated water can lead to significant healthcare costs and loss of productivity.
*   **Environmental Protection:** While focused on human consumption, stringent standards can indirectly contribute to better overall water resource management.

### 2. Impurities in Water: Pollutants and Contaminants

**2.1 Definitions**

*   **Contaminant:** A substance or agent present in water that is not naturally occurring or has been altered from its natural state, potentially posing a risk to human health or the environment.
*   **Pollutant:** A contaminant that causes pollution – the introduction of contaminants into the natural environment that cause adverse change. In the context of water, any substance that degrades water quality and renders it unsuitable for its intended uses.

**2.2 Categories of Impurities**

Impurities in water can be broadly categorized as follows:

*   **Physical Impurities:** Affect the appearance and feel of water.
    *   **Suspended Solids:** Insoluble particles that remain suspended in water (e.g., silt, clay, organic matter, algae).
        *   **Significance:** Can cause turbidity (cloudiness), affect disinfection efficiency, and act as carriers for pathogens.
        *   **Example:** Cloudy river water after rainfall.
    *   **Dissolved Solids:** Substances that are dissolved in water (e.g., salts, minerals, organic compounds).
        *   **Significance:** Affect taste, can cause scaling in pipes, and high levels of certain dissolved solids (like nitrates or heavy metals) can be harmful.
        *   **Example:** Salty taste of seawater.
    *   **Color:** Can be caused by dissolved organic matter, iron, manganese, or algae.
        *   **Significance:** Primarily aesthetic, but can indicate the presence of undesirable substances.
        *   **Example:** Brownish color from decaying leaves.
    *   **Turbidity:** Measure of water clarity, indicating the amount of suspended matter.
        *   **Significance:** Affects aesthetic quality and can shield microorganisms from disinfectants.
        *   **Example:** Foggy or cloudy appearance of water.
    *   **Temperature:** While not always an impurity, significant deviations can affect water's suitability for use and the solubility of other substances.
        *   **Significance:** Affects taste, promotes or inhibits biological growth.

*   **Chemical Impurities:** Substances that can affect water's chemical composition and potentially pose health risks.
    *   **Inorganic Chemicals:**
        *   **Metals:**
            *   **Heavy Metals (e.g., Lead, Mercury, Arsenic, Cadmium):** Naturally occurring or from industrial/mining activities.
                *   **Significance:** Highly toxic, can accumulate in the body, causing neurological damage, cancer, kidney problems, etc.
                *   **Example:** Lead contamination from old plumbing pipes.
            *   **Essential Minerals (e.g., Iron, Manganese, Calcium, Magnesium):** Can be beneficial in small amounts but problematic at higher concentrations.
                *   **Significance:** Iron and Manganese cause staining and metallic taste. Calcium and Magnesium cause hardness, leading to scale formation.
                *   **Example:** Reddish-brown stains on sinks from high iron content.
        *   **Non-metals (e.g., Fluoride, Nitrates, Nitrites, Arsenic, Chlorine):**
            *   **Nitrates/Nitrites:** From agricultural runoff (fertilizers) and sewage.
                *   **Significance:** Can cause methemoglobinemia ("blue baby syndrome") in infants.
                *   **Example:** Drinking water contaminated with agricultural runoff.
            *   **Fluoride:** Naturally occurring or added for dental health.
                *   **Significance:** Beneficial in low concentrations for preventing tooth decay; high concentrations can cause dental or skeletal fluorosis.
            *   **Arsenic:** Naturally occurring, mining, industrial processes.
                *   **Significance:** Carcinogenic, can cause skin lesions, cardiovascular problems.
        *   **Acids and Alkalis (pH):**
            *   **Significance:** Affects corrosivity of water, taste, and solubility of other substances.
            *   **Example:** Acidic water can corrode pipes, leaching metals.
    *   **Organic Chemicals:**
        *   **Natural Organic Matter (NOM):** Decaying plant and animal material.
            *   **Significance:** Can contribute to color, taste, and odor; can react with disinfectants to form disinfection byproducts (DBPs).
        *   **Synthetic Organic Chemicals (SOCs):** Pesticides, herbicides, industrial solvents, pharmaceuticals, personal care products.
            *   **Significance:** Many are toxic, carcinogenic, or endocrine disruptors. Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs) are particularly concerning.
            *   **Example:** Pesticide runoff from farms entering water sources.
        *   **Disinfection Byproducts (DBPs):** Formed when disinfectants like chlorine react with NOM.
            *   **Significance:** Some DBPs (e.g., trihalomethanes - THMs) are suspected carcinogens.

*   **Biological Impurities (Microbiological):** Pathogenic microorganisms that cause disease.
    *   **Bacteria:**
        *   **Fecal Coliforms/E. coli:** Indicator organisms originating from the intestines of warm-blooded animals.
            *   **Significance:** Their presence indicates potential fecal contamination and the likely presence of other harmful pathogens.
            *   **Example:** Water contaminated by sewage.
        *   **Salmonella, Shigella, Vibrio cholerae:** Specific disease-causing bacteria.
            *   **Significance:** Directly cause diseases like typhoid fever, dysentery, and cholera.
    *   **Viruses:**
        *   **Hepatitis A, Norovirus, Rotavirus:**
            *   **Significance:** Cause gastroenteritis and liver infections.
    *   **Protozoa:**
        *   **Giardia lamblia, Cryptosporidium parvum:**
            *   **Significance:** Cause gastrointestinal illnesses, resistant to chlorine disinfection.
            *   **Example:** Contamination of a water source by animal feces (e.g., from cattle).
    *   **Algae and Phytoplankton:**
        *   **Significance:** Can cause taste and odor problems, produce toxins, and deplete dissolved oxygen.

*   **Radiological Impurities:** Radioactive elements.
    *   **Radon, Uranium, Radium:** Naturally occurring or from industrial waste.
        *   **Significance:** Can increase the risk of cancer.
        *   **Example:** Water sourced from granite bedrock areas may have higher radon levels.

### 3. Common Parameters for Assessing Water Quality

Water quality is assessed by measuring various parameters, which are often included in standards.

*   **Physical Parameters:**
    *   **Turbidity:** Measured in Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU).
    *   **Color:** Measured in Platinum-Cobalt (PC) units.
    *   **Total Dissolved Solids (TDS):** Measured in milligrams per liter (mg/L) or parts per million (ppm).
    *   **Temperature:** Measured in degrees Celsius (°C) or Fahrenheit (°F).

*   **Chemical Parameters:**
    *   **pH:** Measure of acidity or alkalinity (0-14 scale).
    *   **Hardness:** Measure of calcium and magnesium ions (mg/L as CaCO₃).
    *   **Nitrates/Nitrites:** Measured in mg/L.
    *   **Heavy Metals (Lead, Arsenic, Mercury, etc.):** Measured in µg/L (micrograms per liter) or mg/L.
    *   **Chlorides, Sulfates, Iron, Manganese:** Measured in mg/L.
    *   **Total Organic Carbon (TOC):** Measure of organic matter, in mg/L.
    *   **Specific Organic Compounds (Pesticides, VOCs):** Measured in µg/L or ng/L.

*   **Microbiological Parameters:**
    *   **Total Coliforms, Fecal Coliforms, E. coli:** Measured as Most Probable Number (MPN) per 100 mL or Colony Forming Units (CFU) per 100 mL.
    *   **Enterococci:** Indicator of fecal contamination, measured as CFU/100 mL.

*   **Radiological Parameters:**
    *   **Gross Alpha/Beta Activity:** Measured in Becquerels per liter (Bq/L) or picocuries per liter (pCi/L).
    *   **Specific Radionuclides (Radium, Uranium):** Measured in Bq/L or µg/L.

### 4. Typical Standards and Guidelines for Potable Water Quality

Standards vary by country and regulatory body, but common guidelines are established by organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO) and national agencies (e.g., EPA in the USA, BIS in India). These guidelines set Maximum Contaminant Levels (MCLs) or guideline values for various parameters.

**4.1 World Health Organization (WHO) Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality**

The WHO guidelines are influential globally. Key aspects include:

*   **Microbiological Quality:** Strict limits on indicator organisms. Ideally, E. coli and fecal coliforms should be absent in 100 mL of sample.
*   **Chemical Quality:**
    *   **Inorganic:**
        *   Arsenic: 0.01 mg/L (10 µg/L)
        *   Lead: 0.01 mg/L (10 µg/L)
        *   Mercury: 0.006 mg/L (6 µg/L)
        *   Nitrate: 50 mg/L
        *   Fluoride: 1.5 mg/L (for long-term consumption, can be adjusted based on climate)
    *   **Organic:**
        *   Benzene: 0.01 mg/L (10 µg/L)
        *   Pesticides: Varies for individual pesticides, often in µg/L range.
    *   **Radiological:**
        *   Total indicative dose from radionuclides should not exceed 0.1 mSv/year.

*   **Physical Quality:**
    *   Turbidity: Generally < 1 NTU for treated water, aiming for < 5 NTU if possible for aesthetic reasons.
    *   Color: < 15 PC units.
    *   TDS: Ideally < 600 mg/L, but up to 1000 mg/L may be acceptable.

**4.2 Example: US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Standards (for context)**

The EPA sets legally enforceable MCLs under the Safe Drinking Water Act.

*   **Microbiological:**
    *   Total Coliforms: MCL is 5% of samples positive for total coliforms in a month.
    *   E. coli: If total coliforms are present, E. coli must be absent.
*   **Chemical:**
    *   Arsenic: 0.010 mg/L (10 µg/L)
    *   Lead: Action Level of 0.015 mg/L (15 µg/L) at the tap.
    *   Nitrate: 10 mg/L (as nitrogen).
    *   Fluoride: 4.0 mg/L (maximum contaminant level goal) and 2.0 mg/L (secondary MCL for aesthetics).
*   **Physical:**
    *   Turbidity: Monthly average of NTU must not exceed 1.0, and 95% of samples must be below 0.3 NTU.
    *   TDS: Secondary MCL of 500 mg/L (for aesthetic reasons).

**4.3 Indian Standards (Bureau of Indian Standards - BIS)**

*   **IS 10500: 2012 - Drinking water specification**
    *   **Acceptable Limit (primary):**
        *   Total Coliforms & E. coli: Absent in 100 mL
        *   Nitrate (as N): 45 mg/L
        *   Fluoride: 1.0 mg/L
        *   Arsenic: 0.01 mg/L (10 µg/L)
        *   Lead: 0.05 mg/L (50 µg/L)
        *   Turbidity: 1 NTU
        *   Color: 5 PC Units
    *   **Permissible Limit (in the absence of an alternate source):**
        *   Fluoride: 1.5 mg/L
        *   Lead: 0.1 mg/L (100 µg/L)

**Important Note:** These are examples. Actual standards and specific limits for various parameters can be extensive and depend on the regulatory authority.

### 5. Role of Regulatory Bodies

*   **Establish Standards:** Set legally enforceable limits for various water quality parameters.
*   **Monitor Compliance:** Oversee water treatment plants and distribution systems to ensure standards are met.
*   **Enforce Regulations:** Take action against non-compliant water systems.
*   **Public Information:** Provide public access to water quality reports and information.
*   **Research and Development:** Fund research to understand emerging contaminants and improve water treatment technologies.

**Examples:**

*   **World Health Organization (WHO):** Develops international guidelines.
*   **U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA):** Regulates drinking water in the United States.
*   **Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS):** Sets standards for drinking water in India.
*   **European Environment Agency (EEA):** Oversees environmental policies, including water quality, in the EU.

### 6. General Principles of Water Quality Testing

Water quality testing involves a systematic process to analyze water for the presence and concentration of various impurities.

**6.1 Sampling:**

*   **Representative Sampling:** Crucial to obtain samples that accurately reflect the water quality at a specific location and time.
*   **Proper Containers:** Use clean, sterile bottles, often pre-treated with preservatives depending on the parameter being tested.
*   **Field Measurements:** Some parameters (e.g., pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen) are best measured immediately in the field.
*   **Chain of Custody:** Maintaining a record of who handled the sample and when is vital for legal and quality control purposes.

**6.2 Analytical Methods:**

*   **Physical Tests:**
    *   **Turbidity:** Nephelometry (using a turbidimeter).
    *   **Color:** Spectrophotometry or visual comparison with color standards.
    *   **TDS:** Gravimetric analysis (evaporation and weighing) or conductivity measurement.
*   **Chemical Tests:**
    *   **Spectrophotometry/Colorimetry:** For measuring concentrations of metals, nutrients, etc., by assessing light absorption.
    *   **Titration:** For parameters like alkalinity, hardness.
    *   **Chromatography (GC, HPLC):** For separating and quantifying organic compounds (pesticides, VOCs).
    *   **Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS) / Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP):** For precise measurement of metals.
    *   **Ion-Selective Electrodes (ISE):** For direct measurement of specific ions like fluoride or nitrate.
*   **Microbiological Tests:**
    *   **Coliform/E. coli Tests:**
        *   **Membrane Filtration:** Water is filtered, and the filter is placed on a growth medium. Colonies are counted.
        *   **Most Probable Number (MPN):** Involves inoculating multiple tubes of culture medium with different volumes of water and observing bacterial growth.
    *   **Microscopy:** Direct observation of microorganisms.
*   **Radiological Tests:**
    *   **Scintillation Counters:** Detect alpha and beta radiation.
    *   **Gamma Spectrometry:** Identifies and quantifies specific gamma-emitting radionuclides.

**6.3 Quality Assurance/Quality Control (QA/QC):**

*   **Calibration of Instruments:** Regular calibration ensures accuracy.
*   **Blanks:** Analyzing pure water to check for contamination during analysis.
*   **Spikes/Fortified Samples:** Adding known amounts of a substance to a sample to assess recovery efficiency.
*   **Duplicate Samples:** Analyzing the same sample twice to check for reproducibility.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Potable water standards are crucial for public health.**
*   Impurities are broadly categorized as **physical, chemical, biological, and radiological.**
*   **Indicator organisms (like E. coli)** are used to infer the presence of pathogens.
*   **Key chemical contaminants to watch for include heavy metals, nitrates, and disinfection byproducts.**
*   **WHO, EPA, and BIS** are prominent bodies setting potable water standards.
*   **Representative sampling and appropriate analytical methods are vital for accurate water quality assessment.**
*   **Continuous monitoring and adherence to standards are essential for safe drinking water.**
*   **The goal is to minimize risks from both short-term health effects (pathogens) and long-term effects (chronic exposure to chemicals).**

### Answers to Practice Questions

**1. Multiple Choice Questions:**

    a) ii) E. coli
    b) iii) Nitrates
    c) ii) 0.01 mg/L
    d) iii) Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS)

**2. Short Answer Questions:**

    a) **Significance of Turbidity:** Turbidity indicates the amount of suspended matter in water. High turbidity makes water aesthetically unappealing (cloudy) and can shield microorganisms from disinfection, potentially compromising microbial safety. It can also indicate that the water source is susceptible to contamination.

    b) **SOCs:** Two examples are pesticides (e.g., DDT, atrazine) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) like benzene or trichloroethylene (TCE).

    c) **Role of Indicator Organisms:** Indicator organisms (like E. coli) are used to signal the potential presence of harmful pathogenic microorganisms originating from fecal contamination. They are easier and safer to detect than specific pathogens and their presence indicates a risk of waterborne disease transmission.

    d) **Importance of Sampling:** Proper sampling ensures that the water sample analyzed is representative of the water being consumed. This means it accurately reflects the quality at the point of use and at the time of testing. Incorrect sampling can lead to inaccurate results, either overestimating or underestimating contamination, which can have serious public health consequences.

**3. Problem-Solving/Application Question:**

    a) **Are these levels within typical acceptable limits?**
        *   **Iron:** WHO guideline is 0.3 mg/L (aesthetic). BIS acceptable limit is 0.3 mg/L. 0.5 mg/L is above these.
        *   **Manganese:** WHO guideline is 0.4 mg/L (aesthetic). BIS acceptable limit is 0.1 mg/L. 0.2 mg/L is above the BIS limit and slightly above WHO's threshold for aesthetic concerns.
        *   **Conclusion:** While not acutely toxic at these levels, the Iron and Manganese levels are above the typical aesthetic limits for potable water, affecting its quality and usability.

    b) **Potential aesthetic issues:**
        *   **Color:** Brownish color due to Iron and Manganese.
        *   **Taste:** Metallic taste.
        *   **Staining:** Staining of sinks, toilets, and laundry.
        *   **Scaling:** In pipes and appliances, although less of a primary concern at these levels compared to hardness.

    c) **Water treatment process:**
        *   Processes like **oxidation followed by filtration** are commonly used to remove Iron and Manganese. Oxidation converts soluble ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) and manganous manganese (Mn²⁺) into insoluble ferric iron (Fe³⁺) and manganic manganese (Mn⁴⁺) precipitates, which can then be removed by filtration. Other methods include aeration, ion exchange, or chemical precipitation.
