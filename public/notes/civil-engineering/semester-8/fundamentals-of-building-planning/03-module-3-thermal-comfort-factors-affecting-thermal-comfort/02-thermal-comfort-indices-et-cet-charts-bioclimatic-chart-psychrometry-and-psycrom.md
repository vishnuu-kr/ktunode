# FUNDAMENTALS OF BUILDING PLANNING - MODULE 3: THERMAL COMFORT

## Topic: Thermal Comfort Indices, ET-CET Charts, Bioclimatic Chart, Psychrometry and Psychrometric Chart

This module delves into the quantitative and graphical methods used to understand and design for thermal comfort in buildings. We will explore various indices, charting techniques, and the fundamental principles of psychrometry.

---

### 1. Thermal Comfort Indices

**Definition:** Thermal comfort is that condition of mind which expresses satisfaction with the thermal environment. It is a subjective state. Thermal comfort indices are quantitative measures that attempt to predict or assess human thermal comfort based on environmental and personal factors.

**Key Factors Affecting Thermal Comfort:**

*   **Environmental Factors:**
    *   **Air Temperature:** The temperature of the surrounding air.
    *   **Radiant Temperature:** The average temperature of all surfaces surrounding a person. This significantly impacts comfort, especially from sun-exposed surfaces or cold walls.
    *   **Air Velocity:** The speed of air movement. It can increase heat loss through convection and evaporation.
    *   **Humidity:** The amount of water vapor in the air. High humidity reduces the body's ability to cool itself through perspiration.
*   **Personal Factors:**
    *   **Metabolic Rate:** The rate at which the body generates heat, influenced by activity level (sitting vs. exercising).
    *   **Clothing Insulation:** The thermal resistance of clothing worn, measured in Clo units.

**Common Thermal Comfort Indices:**

*   **Equivalent Temperature (ET):**
    *   **Concept:** ET is a single temperature value that combines the effects of air temperature, radiant temperature, and air velocity on human comfort. It represents the temperature of a theoretical saturated air space at 50% relative humidity in which a person would experience the same degree of warmth as in the actual environment.
    *   **Calculation:** Based on empirical formulas, it's complex and not typically calculated by hand in practice. However, the concept is important.
    *   **Importance:** Accounts for the combined effect of air and radiant temperature, acknowledging that a person feels warmer with radiant heat even if the air temperature is the same.
*   **Corrected Effective Temperature (CET):**
    *   **Concept:** CET is a further refinement of ET that also accounts for humidity. It represents the temperature of a theoretical saturated air space at 50% relative humidity and negligible air movement in which a person would experience the same degree of warmth as in the actual environment.
    *   **Calculation:** More complex than ET, incorporating humidity.
    *   **Importance:** Provides a more accurate representation of thermal sensation by including the impact of humidity on the body's cooling mechanisms.

**Relationship between ET and CET:** CET is generally lower than ET, especially at higher humidity levels, because increased humidity reduces the body's evaporative cooling capacity.

**Example Scenario:**
Imagine two identical rooms with the same air temperature.
*   **Room A:** Radiant walls are at the same temperature as the air.
*   **Room B:** One wall is a large, sun-exposed window, making it warmer, and the opposite wall is cold due to poor insulation.
A person in Room B would likely feel warmer due to the warmer radiant surfaces, even if the air temperature is the same as in Room A. ET and CET would reflect this difference.

### 2. ET-CET Charts

**Definition:** ET-CET charts are graphical tools that allow designers to determine the Equivalent Temperature (ET) and Corrected Effective Temperature (CET) for a given set of environmental conditions (air temperature, globe temperature, air velocity, and humidity).

**Components of an ET-CET Chart:**

*   **Axes:** Typically, air temperature and relative humidity are on the axes.
*   **Lines:**
    *   **ET Lines:** Represent constant values of Equivalent Temperature.
    *   **CET Lines:** Represent constant values of Corrected Effective Temperature.
    *   **Humidity Lines:** Indicate relative humidity levels.
    *   **Air Velocity Lines:** Often indicated as different families of curves or as separate charts for different velocities.

**How to Use an ET-CET Chart (Conceptual):**

1.  **Identify Known Variables:** Determine the air temperature, globe temperature (or radiant temperature), air velocity, and relative humidity of the environment.
2.  **Locate Point:** Find the intersection of the known air temperature and relative humidity on the chart.
3.  **Trace Lines:** Follow the appropriate air velocity line from this point.
4.  **Read Values:** Where the air velocity line intersects with the ET and CET lines, read off the corresponding temperature values.

**Example of Chart Interpretation:**
If you have an air temperature of 25°C, relative humidity of 60%, and an air velocity of 0.5 m/s.
*   Find 25°C on the air temperature axis.
*   Find 60% on the relative humidity axis.
*   Locate the intersection.
*   Follow the line corresponding to 0.5 m/s.
*   Read the ET and CET values where this line crosses the ET and CET curves.

**Important Note:** Actual ET-CET charts are complex and vary slightly based on the source and specific conditions they are designed for (e.g., sedentary activity, light clothing). Designers often refer to standard charts provided by HVAC or building science organizations.

### 3. Bioclimatic Chart

**Definition:** The Bioclimatic Chart is a graphical tool that relates the physiological comfort zone to various climate conditions. It helps designers understand the climatic potential for achieving thermal comfort through passive design strategies.

**Key Features and Concepts:**

*   **Axes:**
    *   **X-axis:** Usually represents **Dry Bulb Temperature (DBT)** or **Air Temperature**.
    *   **Y-axis:** Usually represents **Absolute Humidity** (e.g., humidity ratio, vapor pressure) or sometimes **Relative Humidity (RH)**.
*   **Comfort Zone:** A region on the chart representing the range of temperature and humidity combinations that are generally considered comfortable for a majority of people under specific activity and clothing conditions.
*   **Climate Lines/Zones:** The chart displays typical climate data for different regions, often represented by dots or shaded areas.
*   **Physiological Limits:** Lines representing the limits of human tolerance to heat and cold (e.g., upper and lower limits of acceptable skin temperature, maximum evaporative cooling potential).
*   **Passive Design Strategies:** Overlays or regions on the chart indicate where specific passive design strategies are most effective for achieving comfort. These can include:
    *   **Evaporative Cooling:** Effective in hot, dry climates.
    *   **Shading:** Essential in hot climates, both humid and dry.
    *   **Natural Ventilation:** Effective in moderating temperatures, especially with breezes.
    *   **Thermal Mass:** Helps to buffer temperature swings.
    *   **Insulation:** Crucial in cold climates.

**How to Use a Bioclimatic Chart (Conceptual):**

1.  **Plot Climate Data:** Plot the typical dry bulb temperature and humidity ratio (or RH) for the building's location.
2.  **Locate Comfort Zone:** Identify the comfort zone for the assumed activity level and clothing.
3.  **Analyze Climate's Position:** See where the climate data falls relative to the comfort zone.
    *   If the climate is within the comfort zone, minimal intervention might be needed.
    *   If the climate is outside the comfort zone, identify the direction and magnitude of the deviation.
4.  **Identify Passive Strategies:** Determine which passive design strategies can help shift the climate data into the comfort zone. For example:
    *   If the climate is too hot and humid, evaporative cooling might not be effective, but shading and natural ventilation could help.
    *   If the climate is too cold and dry, insulation and solar heating might be needed.

**Example:**
Consider a hot, dry climate. The Bioclimatic Chart would show typical high temperatures and low humidity.
*   The comfort zone might be around 20-25°C DBT and moderate humidity.
*   The hot, dry climate might fall above the comfort zone for temperature.
*   The chart would suggest strategies like shading to reduce solar gain and potentially evaporative cooling (e.g., water features, misters) to increase humidity and lower perceived temperature.

**Key Insight:** The Bioclimatic Chart emphasizes that the "best" design strategies are climate-dependent.

### 4. Psychrometry and Psychrometric Chart

**Definition:**
*   **Psychrometry:** The study of the thermodynamic properties of moist air and the relationship between these properties. It deals with the behavior of air when it contains water vapor.
*   **Psychrometric Chart:** A graphical representation of the thermodynamic properties of moist air. It is an essential tool for HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning) design and analysis, and also for understanding thermal comfort.

**Key Properties of Moist Air on a Psychrometric Chart:**

*   **Dry Bulb Temperature (DBT):** The temperature of the air measured by a standard thermometer. (Typically on the X-axis).
*   **Wet Bulb Temperature (WBT):** The temperature read by a thermometer whose bulb is covered with a wet wick and exposed to a current of air. It reflects the cooling effect of evaporation. (Often shown as diagonal lines).
*   **Dew Point Temperature (DPT):** The temperature to which air must be cooled at constant pressure and water content to reach saturation. (Often shown as horizontal lines).
*   **Humidity Ratio (or Mixing Ratio, W):** The mass of water vapor per unit mass of dry air. (Often on the Y-axis or represented by horizontal lines).
*   **Relative Humidity (RH):** The ratio of the actual partial pressure of water vapor to the saturation partial pressure of water vapor at the same temperature, expressed as a percentage. (Often shown as curved lines).
*   **Specific Volume:** The volume occupied by a unit mass of dry air. (Often shown as sloped lines).
*   **Enthalpy (h):** The total heat content of the moist air. (Often shown as diagonal lines, parallel to Wet Bulb Temperature lines).

**How to Use a Psychrometric Chart:**

1.  **Identify Known Properties:** Determine at least two independent properties of the moist air (e.g., DBT and RH, or DBT and WBT).
2.  **Locate the State Point:** Find the intersection of these two properties on the chart. This point represents the "state" of the moist air.
3.  **Read Other Properties:** From this state point, you can trace horizontally, vertically, or along diagonal lines to read off all other properties of the moist air.

**Common Psychrometric Processes (and how they are represented on the chart):**

*   **Sensible Heating/Cooling:** Change in DBT without change in humidity ratio (horizontal lines).
*   **Humidification:** Adding moisture to the air (move up and to the right).
*   **Dehumidification:** Removing moisture from the air (move down and to the left).
*   **Adiabatic Saturation:** Cooling process involving evaporation without heat transfer (follows WBT lines).
*   **Mixing of Air Streams:** The resulting state point lies on the line connecting the state points of the individual streams, and its position depends on the proportion of each stream.

**Example:**
You have air at 25°C DBT and 50% RH.
1.  Locate 25°C on the DBT axis.
2.  Locate the 50% RH curve.
3.  The intersection is your state point.
4.  From this point, you can read:
    *   Humidity Ratio (W) by tracing horizontally to the W-axis.
    *   Wet Bulb Temperature (WBT) by tracing along the diagonal WBT line.
    *   Dew Point Temperature (DPT) by tracing horizontally to the DPT axis.
    *   Enthalpy (h) by tracing along the diagonal enthalpy line.

**Importance for Thermal Comfort:**
The psychrometric chart is fundamental for understanding how changes in temperature and humidity affect the potential for evaporative cooling and thus human comfort. It's used to:
*   Analyze the effectiveness of ventilation strategies.
*   Design humidification or dehumidification systems.
*   Understand the impact of building envelope performance on indoor air conditions.

### Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** Which of the following is NOT considered a primary environmental factor affecting thermal comfort?
    a) Air Temperature
    b) Radiant Temperature
    c) Air Velocity
    d) Metabolic Rate

**Answer 1:** d) Metabolic Rate. Metabolic rate is a *personal* factor, not an environmental one.

---

**Question 2:** Equivalent Temperature (ET) primarily accounts for the combined effects of:
    a) Air temperature and humidity
    b) Air temperature and air velocity
    c) Air temperature, radiant temperature, and air velocity
    d) Air temperature, radiant temperature, humidity, and air velocity

**Answer 2:** c) Air temperature, radiant temperature, and air velocity. CET is the one that includes humidity.

---

**Question 3:** The Bioclimatic Chart helps designers to:
    a) Calculate exact ET and CET values.
    b) Understand the potential of passive design strategies for achieving comfort in a specific climate.
    c) Determine the required HVAC system capacity.
    d) Analyze the heat transfer through building materials.

**Answer 3:** b) Understand the potential of passive design strategies for achieving comfort in a specific climate. While it relates to comfort, it's not about exact calculations of ET/CET or HVAC sizing directly, but rather the climatic context for passive design.

---

**Question 4:** On a psychrometric chart, a process that involves increasing the amount of water vapor in the air while keeping the temperature constant would be represented by moving:
    a) Vertically upwards
    b) Horizontally to the right
    c) Diagonally upwards to the right
    d) Vertically downwards

**Answer 4:** a) Vertically upwards. Increasing water vapor content at constant temperature means increasing the humidity ratio (W), which is plotted vertically.

---

**Question 5:** A person feels warmer in a room with direct sunlight on a wall compared to a room with the same air temperature but without direct sunlight. This difference is primarily due to the influence of:
    a) Humidity
    b) Air Velocity
    c) Radiant Temperature
    d) Metabolic Rate

**Answer 5:** c) Radiant Temperature. The direct sunlight warms the wall, increasing the mean radiant temperature felt by the person.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Thermal comfort is subjective:** It depends on both environmental and personal factors.
*   **Multiple factors interact:** Air temperature, radiant temperature, air velocity, and humidity all play crucial roles.
*   **Indices are tools:** ET and CET provide a way to quantify comfort by combining multiple factors into a single temperature value.
*   **Bioclimatic Chart is for strategy:** It guides the selection of passive design approaches based on climate.
*   **Psychrometric chart is fundamental:** It visualizes the properties of moist air, essential for understanding HVAC processes and their impact on comfort.
*   **Radiant temperature is often overlooked:** However, it significantly impacts comfort and can be influenced by solar gains and surface temperatures.
*   **Clothing and activity matter:** These personal factors significantly shift the comfort zone.
