# FUNDAMENTALS OF BUILDING PLANNING

## Module 3: Thermal Comfort

### Topic: Thermal Comfort: Factors Affecting Thermal Comfort - Effective Temperature

---

### **1. Introduction to Thermal Comfort**

*   **Definition:** Thermal comfort is defined as **"that condition of mind which expresses satisfaction with the thermal environment."** (ASHRAE)
*   It's not just about being too hot or too cold, but about the overall *feeling* of comfort.
*   Achieving thermal comfort in buildings is a primary goal of building planning and design.

---

### **2. Factors Affecting Thermal Comfort**

Thermal comfort is influenced by a complex interplay of **environmental factors** and **personal factors**.

#### **2.1 Environmental Factors**

These are the measurable conditions of the thermal environment.

*   **Air Temperature (Dry-bulb Temperature):**
    *   **Definition:** The temperature of the air as measured by a thermometer not affected by moisture or direct solar radiation.
    *   **Impact:** This is the most direct indicator of how hot or cold the air feels.
    *   **Example:** A room with an air temperature of 24°C will generally feel warmer than a room with 20°C.

*   **Radiant Temperature (Mean Radiant Temperature - MRT):**
    *   **Definition:** The uniform temperature of an imaginary enclosure with which the occupant would exchange the same amount of radiant heat as the occupant does with the actual enclosure surfaces.
    *   **Impact:** Our bodies exchange heat through radiation with surrounding surfaces. Warm surfaces make us feel warmer, and cool surfaces make us feel cooler, even if the air temperature is the same.
    *   **Example:** Sitting near a large, cold window in winter will make you feel cooler than if the window surface were warmer, even if the air temperature is identical. Similarly, sitting near a warm wall or a sunny window will make you feel warmer.

*   **Air Velocity (Air Speed):**
    *   **Definition:** The speed at which air is moving around the occupant.
    *   **Impact:** Moving air can increase heat loss from the body through convection and evaporation, making us feel cooler. High air velocities can cause discomfort due to drafts.
    *   **Example:** A gentle breeze from a fan on a warm day can significantly improve comfort. However, a strong, direct draft from an air conditioning vent can be unpleasant.

*   **Humidity (Absolute Humidity / Relative Humidity):**
    *   **Definition:**
        *   **Absolute Humidity:** The actual amount of water vapor present in the air.
        *   **Relative Humidity (RH):** The ratio of the actual amount of water vapor in the air to the maximum amount of water vapor the air can hold at a given temperature, expressed as a percentage.
    *   **Impact:**
        *   **High Humidity:** Reduces the body's ability to cool itself through sweating, as sweat evaporates more slowly. This can make warm temperatures feel hotter and sticky.
        *   **Low Humidity:** Can lead to dry skin, irritated respiratory passages, and faster evaporation of sweat, which can make it harder to stay cool if not enough sweat is produced.
    *   **Example:** A humid summer day at 30°C can feel much more uncomfortable than a dry day at 30°C due to reduced evaporative cooling.

*   **Clothing Insulation:**
    *   **Definition:** The resistance to heat transfer offered by the clothing worn by a person. It's typically measured in **clo** units (1 clo is the insulation of typical indoor clothing).
    *   **Impact:** Clothing acts as a barrier, reducing heat loss from the body. More clothing means higher insulation and a warmer feeling.
    *   **Example:** Wearing a thick sweater (e.g., 1.0 clo) will make you feel warmer than wearing a t-shirt (e.g., 0.3 clo) at the same environmental conditions.

*   **Metabolic Rate:**
    *   **Definition:** The rate at which the body generates heat through internal metabolic processes, primarily from physical activity. It's typically measured in **met** units (1 met is the average metabolic rate of a person at rest).
    *   **Impact:** Higher activity levels generate more body heat, requiring more cooling.
    *   **Example:** Someone sitting at a desk (e.g., 1.0 met) will produce less heat than someone walking briskly (e.g., 3.0 met).

#### **2.2 Personal Factors**

These are individual characteristics that affect how a person perceives thermal comfort.

*   **Activity Level (Metabolic Rate):** (As discussed above, but its impact is personal to the individual performing the activity).
*   **Clothing:** (As discussed above, but the choice of clothing is personal).
*   **Physiological State:**
    *   Age
    *   Health conditions (e.g., circulation issues, thyroid problems)
    *   Acclimatization (how accustomed a person is to a particular climate)
    *   Sex/Gender (can influence metabolic rate and body composition)
*   **Psychological Factors:**
    *   Expectations
    *   Perception of control over the environment
    *   Adaptation

### **3. Effective Temperature (ET)**

*   **Definition:** Effective Temperature is a **single index of the thermal environment** that combines the effect of **air temperature**, **humidity**, and **air velocity** on human comfort. It represents the temperature of saturated air at which that combination of humidity and air motion would produce the same feeling of warmth or coolness.
*   **Key Idea:** It attempts to simplify the complex interaction of these three environmental factors into one understandable number.
*   **Historical Context:** Developed by researchers like Houghten and Yaglou in the 1920s.
*   **Limitations:**
    *   **Doesn't directly account for MRT:** This is a significant limitation as radiant temperature plays a crucial role.
    *   **Doesn't directly account for clothing or activity:** The original ET was developed for a specific activity level (sedentary) and clothing level.
    *   **Assumes all humidity is equally effective:** This isn't entirely true; the effect of humidity is more pronounced at higher temperatures.
    *   **Subjectivity:** While it aims for objectivity, it's based on subjective responses.

*   **How it Works (Conceptual Understanding):**
    *   If you are in an environment with warm air and high humidity, you might feel the same as if you were in an environment with slightly cooler air but no humidity.
    *   If you are in an environment with warm air and a gentle breeze, you might feel the same as if you were in an environment with cooler air but no breeze.
    *   Effective Temperature is the temperature of *saturated air* (100% humidity) with no air movement that would feel the same as the actual environment.

*   **Example:**
    *   An environment with **24°C air temperature, 50% RH, and low air velocity** might have the same Effective Temperature as
    *   An environment with **22°C air temperature, 70% RH, and low air velocity**.
    *   Or an environment with **23°C air temperature, 50% RH, and moderate air velocity**.

*   **Graphical Representation:** Effective Temperature can be visualized using psychrometric charts or ET charts, which show lines of constant ET as a function of dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity, often with lines indicating air velocity.

### **4. Evolution and Modern Indices**

While Effective Temperature was an important early index, it has been superseded by more sophisticated indices that account for a broader range of factors.

*   **Predicted Mean Vote (PMV):** Developed by P.O. Fanger, PMV predicts the average thermal sensation vote of a large group of people on the ASHRAE six-point thermal sensation scale. It accounts for all six basic factors (air temperature, MRT, air velocity, humidity, clothing, metabolic rate).
*   **Predicted Percentage of Dissatisfied (PPD):** A direct consequence of PMV, PPD quantifies the percentage of people expected to be dissatisfied with the thermal environment.
*   **Operative Temperature:** A simpler index than PMV but more comprehensive than ET. It's defined as the uniform temperature of an enclosure in which radiant heat transfer and convection have the same effect on the heat exchange as in the actual situation. It's often approximated as the average of the air temperature and the mean radiant temperature (Op = (Ta + MRT)/2).

### **5. Importance in Building Planning**

Understanding the factors affecting thermal comfort, including the concept of Effective Temperature (and its successors), is crucial for:

*   **Designing energy-efficient buildings:** By optimizing thermal comfort, designers can reduce the reliance on mechanical heating and cooling systems.
*   **Creating healthy and productive indoor environments:** Thermal comfort directly impacts occupant well-being, cognitive function, and productivity.
*   **Selecting appropriate building materials and systems:** Knowledge of thermal properties helps in choosing materials that contribute to good thermal performance.
*   **Specifying HVAC systems:** Understanding the thermal loads required to maintain comfort is essential for sizing and selecting HVAC equipment.
*   **Developing building codes and standards:** These standards are based on principles of thermal comfort to ensure a minimum level of occupant satisfaction.

---

### **6. Key Points to Remember**

*   **Thermal comfort is subjective:** It's a feeling of satisfaction.
*   **Six key factors:** Air temperature, radiant temperature, air velocity, humidity, clothing, and metabolic rate.
*   **Effective Temperature (ET):** An early index combining air temperature, humidity, and air velocity.
*   **Limitations of ET:** Does not directly include MRT, clothing, or activity level.
*   **Modern indices (PMV/PPD, Operative Temperature):** More comprehensive and widely used in current practice.
*   **Balance is key:** Achieving comfort often involves balancing these factors.

### **8. Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**
Thermal comfort is a state of mind where a person feels satisfied with the thermal conditions of their surrounding environment. It's about feeling neither too hot nor too cold, and generally feeling content with the temperature.
The six primary factors are:
1.  Air Temperature (Dry-bulb Temperature)
2.  Radiant Temperature (Mean Radiant Temperature - MRT)
3.  Air Velocity (Air Speed)
4.  Humidity (Relative Humidity)
5.  Clothing Insulation
6.  Metabolic Rate (Activity Level)

**Answer 2:**
Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) is the average temperature of all the surrounding surfaces that exchange radiant heat with a person. It significantly impacts comfort because our bodies lose heat through radiation to cooler surfaces and gain heat from warmer surfaces.
*   **Example:** If you are sitting in a room where the air temperature is 22°C, but there is a large, uninsulated window with a surface temperature of 10°C, you will feel cooler because your body is radiating heat to the cold window. Conversely, if there is a warm wall or a sunny window, you will feel warmer even if the air temperature is the same.

**Answer 3:**
High humidity during warm weather reduces thermal comfort by hindering the body's natural cooling mechanism: sweating. When the air is already saturated with water vapor, sweat evaporates more slowly from the skin. This means the body cannot efficiently dissipate heat, leading to a feeling of stickiness, clamminess, and making the ambient temperature feel much hotter than it actually is.

**Answer 4:**
Effective Temperature (ET) is a single index that combines the effects of air temperature, humidity, and air velocity on human thermal sensation. It represents the temperature of saturated air (100% humidity) with no air movement that would produce the same thermal sensation as the actual environment.
**Factors it combines:** Air temperature, humidity, and air velocity.
**Main limitations:** It does not directly account for Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT), clothing insulation, or metabolic rate, which are also critical factors influencing thermal comfort.

**Answer 5:**
To improve thermal comfort for occupants feeling too warm in an office space, considering the factors discussed:

1.  **Increase Air Velocity (if appropriate):** Introduce gentle air movement. This could be through ceiling fans, desk fans, or by ensuring the HVAC system provides a slight, but not drafty, airflow. Increased air movement enhances convective and evaporative heat loss from the body.
2.  **Reduce Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT):** Investigate if there are sources of radiant heat making occupants uncomfortable. This could involve shading windows to block direct sun, ensuring that internal surfaces like walls or equipment are not excessively hot, or improving the insulation of cold surfaces like windows or exterior walls. Alternatively, if there are cold surfaces causing discomfort, increasing their temperature would help.
3.  **Adjust Air Temperature (if possible):** If the air temperature is high, slightly lowering it can help, but this is often the most energy-intensive solution.
4.  **Manage Humidity:** If humidity is high, using a dehumidifier or ensuring the HVAC system is properly controlling humidity can improve comfort by allowing sweat to evaporate more effectively.
5.  **Consider Clothing/Activity:** Encourage occupants to wear lighter clothing (if appropriate for the workplace) or suggest they take short breaks if they are engaged in physically demanding tasks.
