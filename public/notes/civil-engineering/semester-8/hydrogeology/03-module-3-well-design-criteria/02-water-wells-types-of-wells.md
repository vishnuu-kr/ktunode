# HYDROGEOLOGY - Module 3: Well Design Criteria

## Topic: Water Wells – Types of Wells

---

This module focuses on understanding the different types of water wells, their characteristics, and the factors that influence their selection. This knowledge is crucial for designing efficient and sustainable water extraction systems.

### Learning Outcomes:

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Identify and describe the main types of water wells.**
*   **Explain the purpose and advantages of each well type.**
*   **Discuss the disadvantages and limitations of each well type.**
*   **Relate well types to specific hydrogeological conditions and usage requirements.**
*   **Recognize the importance of well construction materials and methods for different well types.**

---

### 1. Introduction to Water Wells

Water wells are engineered structures that penetrate aquifers to extract groundwater. The design and type of well are dictated by various factors, including:

*   **Hydrogeological conditions:** Aquifer type (confined, unconfined), transmissivity, storativity, hydraulic conductivity, depth of aquifer, presence of confining layers.
*   **Water demand:** Pumping rate, volume of water required.
*   **Water quality:** Presence of sediment, dissolved minerals, contaminants.
*   **Economic considerations:** Construction cost, maintenance cost, lifespan.
*   **Environmental considerations:** Impact on aquifer drawdown, potential for contamination.

### 2. Major Types of Water Wells

We can broadly categorize water wells based on their construction, depth, and purpose.

#### 2.1. Based on Construction Method and Materials:

##### 2.1.1. Dug Wells (or Hand-Dug Wells)

*   **Description:** These are the oldest and simplest type of wells. They are typically large in diameter (often several meters) and relatively shallow, excavated by hand using manual labor. They are usually lined with stone, brick, concrete rings, or timber to prevent collapse.
*   **Purpose:** Primarily for domestic use, small-scale irrigation, or livestock watering in areas with shallow water tables and permeable shallow aquifers.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Low construction cost (especially in shallow, easily excavated formations).
    *   Can yield significant volumes of water if constructed in highly permeable materials.
    *   Can effectively collect water from the "ring of saturation" surrounding the well.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Low yield and susceptible to depletion:** Due to their large diameter and limited penetration, they can be easily depleted during dry seasons.
    *   **Vulnerable to contamination:** Their open nature and shallow depth make them highly susceptible to surface water infiltration and contamination from human and animal waste.
    *   **Limited to shallow aquifers:** Construction becomes impractical and dangerous with increasing depth.
    *   **Prone to collapse:** Requires substantial lining.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Best suited for unconfined aquifers with shallow water tables and high permeability (e.g., alluvial deposits, coarse sands).
*   **Example:** Many rural villages in developing countries still rely on dug wells for their primary water source.

##### 2.1.2. Driven Wells (or Jetted Wells)

*   **Description:** These are shallow wells constructed by driving a perforated pipe with a screened end (well point) into the ground using a maul or a hammering device. The pipe is typically of small diameter (2-5 cm).
*   **Purpose:** Domestic use, small-scale irrigation, or supplying water to construction sites.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Relatively inexpensive and quick to install.
    *   Can be installed with basic equipment.
    *   Less disruptive to the surrounding area compared to drilling.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Limited to shallow aquifers:** Only suitable for penetrating unconsolidated sediments.
    *   **Low yield:** Typically have lower yields than drilled wells due to smaller diameter and limited penetration.
    *   **Limited screening area:** The screen is usually a short section at the bottom.
    *   **Susceptible to clogging:** Well points can get clogged with fine sediment.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Unconfined aquifers with unconsolidated sediments and shallow water tables.
*   **Example:** A homeowner installing a well for landscape irrigation in an area with a shallow water table.

##### 2.1.3. Bored Wells (or Augered Wells)

*   **Description:** These wells are created by boring a hole into the ground using an auger. They are typically larger in diameter than driven wells but smaller than dug wells. The borehole is often cased with concrete or plastic pipes.
*   **Purpose:** Domestic, agricultural, and small-scale municipal water supply.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   More durable and stable than dug wells.
    *   Can be constructed to greater depths than dug wells.
    *   Can achieve higher yields than driven wells.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Requires specialized drilling equipment.
    *   Can be more expensive than dug or driven wells.
    *   Construction can be slower.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Suitable for both unconfined and confined aquifers where unconsolidated materials are dominant.
*   **Example:** A community well serving a small village, drawing water from a shallow confined aquifer.

##### 2.1.4. Drilled Wells (or Boreholes)

*   **Description:** These are the most common type of modern wells, created by drilling a hole into the ground using rotary, percussion, or other drilling methods. They can range from small diameter (e.g., 10-30 cm) for domestic use to very large diameter (over 1 meter) for municipal or industrial supply. The borehole is then typically cased and screened.
*   **Purpose:** Widely used for domestic, agricultural, municipal, industrial, and mining purposes.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   **Can reach deep aquifers:** Capable of accessing deeper, often higher-quality groundwater sources.
    *   **High yield potential:** Can achieve high pumping rates due to larger diameter and effective screen design.
    *   **Better protection from contamination:** Casing and grouting provide a barrier against surface contaminants.
    *   **Versatile:** Applicable to a wide range of hydrogeological conditions and aquifer types.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Higher construction cost:** Requires specialized drilling rigs and expertise.
    *   **More complex construction:** Involves casing, screen installation, and grouting.
    *   **Potential for drilling-induced contamination:** If not properly constructed, can create conduits for contaminants.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Applicable to virtually all aquifer types and depths, from shallow unconsolidated formations to deep bedrock aquifers.
*   **Example:** A municipal water supply well providing water to a city, a large-scale agricultural irrigation well, or a deep bedrock well for industrial process water.

#### 2.2. Based on Depth and Aquifer Type:

##### 2.2.1. Shallow Wells

*   **Description:** Wells that penetrate only the shallowest water-bearing formations, typically unconfined aquifers. The depth is generally limited by the reach of manual excavation or simpler drilling methods.
*   **Purpose:** Domestic use, small-scale irrigation, livestock watering.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Lower construction cost.
    *   Easier and faster to construct.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Susceptible to seasonal fluctuations:** Water levels can drop significantly during dry periods, leading to wells going dry.
    *   **Higher risk of contamination:** More exposed to surface influences.
    *   **Limited yield:** Generally lower yields compared to deep wells.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Unconfined aquifers with shallow water tables.
*   **Example:** Dug wells and many driven wells are considered shallow wells.

##### 2.2.2. Deep Wells (or Boreholes)

*   **Description:** Wells that penetrate deeper aquifers, often confined aquifers or significantly deeper layers of unconfined aquifers. They are constructed using drilling methods.
*   **Purpose:** Municipal water supply, large-scale agriculture, industrial use, where shallow sources are inadequate or of poor quality.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   **More stable water supply:** Less affected by seasonal fluctuations.
    *   **Often higher water quality:** Deeper aquifers are generally better protected from surface contamination.
    *   **Higher yield potential:** Can access larger groundwater volumes.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Higher construction cost:** Requires specialized drilling equipment and techniques.
    *   **Potential for artesian conditions:** In confined aquifers, water may rise above the aquifer level (or even to the surface if pressure is high enough).
    *   **Increased pumping costs:** Due to greater depth, requiring more powerful pumps.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Both confined and unconfined aquifers at significant depths.
*   **Example:** A municipal well abstracting water from a deep sandstone aquifer, or a well tapping into a confined aquifer under artesian pressure.

##### 2.2.3. Artesian Wells

*   **Description:** A specific type of deep well drilled into a confined aquifer where the groundwater is under sufficient pressure. The water level in the well will rise above the bottom of the confining layer. If the pressure is high enough, the water will flow freely to the surface without pumping (flowing artesian well).
*   **Purpose:** Water supply where artesian pressure is present and beneficial.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   **No pumping required (for flowing wells):** Significantly reduces energy costs.
    *   **Stable water supply:** Often from well-protected aquifers.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Potential for water wastage:** Flowing wells can lead to uncontrolled discharge if not properly managed.
    *   **Groundwater depletion:** Uncontrolled flow can deplete the confined aquifer.
    *   **Well casing integrity crucial:** To prevent leakage and contamination into other strata.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Confined aquifers with a recharge area at a higher elevation than the wellhead, creating hydrostatic pressure.
*   **Example:** A well in a basin where an aquifer is confined between impermeable layers, and its recharge area is on a distant mountain slope.

#### 2.3. Based on Purpose:

##### 2.3.1. Water Supply Wells

*   **Description:** The most common type of well, designed for abstracting potable or non-potable water for various uses.
*   **Sub-types:**
    *   **Domestic Wells:** For individual households.
    *   **Agricultural Wells:** For irrigation and livestock.
    *   **Municipal Wells:** For public water supply systems.
    *   **Industrial Wells:** For process water, cooling, etc.
*   **Key Considerations:** Water quality, yield, reliability, sustainability.

##### 2.3.2. Monitoring Wells (or Observation Wells)

*   **Description:** Wells drilled primarily for measuring groundwater levels, collecting water samples for quality analysis, or monitoring the movement of contaminants. They are typically of small diameter and are not intended for pumping significant volumes of water.
*   **Purpose:** Groundwater monitoring, aquifer testing, environmental studies, detecting contamination plumes.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Provide crucial data for understanding aquifer behavior and water resource management.
    *   Relatively inexpensive to construct.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Do not provide water for supply.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Can be installed in any aquifer to observe conditions.
*   **Example:** Wells installed around a landfill to monitor for leachate migration, or wells installed in an aquifer to track changes in water table due to pumping.

##### 2.3.3. Recharge Wells (or Injection Wells)

*   **Description:** Wells designed to inject water back into an aquifer. This can be done to replenish depleted aquifers, manage groundwater levels, or treat contaminated groundwater.
*   **Purpose:** Aquifer recharge, managed aquifer recharge (MAR), groundwater management, aquifer remediation.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Helps maintain aquifer levels and prevent land subsidence.
    *   Can improve water quality by dilution or treatment.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Requires careful design to prevent clogging and contamination of the aquifer.
    *   Water used for recharge must meet quality standards.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Aquifers that can accept injected water without excessive clogging or negative impacts.
*   **Example:** Injecting treated wastewater or stormwater into an aquifer to augment supply, or injecting fresh water to push back a saltwater intrusion front.

##### 2.3.4. Dewatering Wells

*   **Description:** Wells used to lower the groundwater table in a specific area, typically for construction purposes (e.g., basements, tunnels) or to manage groundwater inflow into mines.
*   **Purpose:** Lowering groundwater table for construction, dewatering mines, controlling groundwater seepage.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Allows for dry and stable construction conditions.
    *   Enables mining operations in saturated ground.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Temporary solution, requires continuous operation.
    *   Can cause ground settlement in surrounding areas.
    *   Disposal of pumped water needs to be managed.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Any formation with significant groundwater saturation that needs to be temporarily dewatered.
*   **Example:** Installing a ring of wellpoints around a building excavation to keep the site dry during construction.

##### 2.3.5. Disposal Wells (or Sinkholes)

*   **Description:** Wells used to dispose of treated wastewater or industrial byproducts by injecting them into permeable subsurface formations. **Note: This practice is highly regulated and often discouraged due to the risk of groundwater contamination.**
*   **Purpose:** Wastewater disposal (historically and in some specific contexts).
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Can be a cost-effective disposal method if properly managed.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **High risk of groundwater contamination:** If the injected fluid is not sufficiently treated or the receiving formation is not suitable.
    *   Requires extensive hydrogeological and geochemical assessment.
*   **Hydrogeological Suitability:** Deep, permeable formations that are isolated from usable groundwater resources.
*   **Example:** Deep well injection of brines from oil and gas production, or industrial liquid waste disposal (with strict regulatory oversight).

### 3. Key Components of a Drilled Well

While we've focused on types, it's important to briefly mention the common components of a drilled well, as they influence well performance and longevity.

*   **Wellhead:** The visible structure at the surface, including the pump, piping, and protective cap.
*   **Casing:** The outer pipe that lines the borehole, preventing collapse and sealing off undesirable water-bearing zones or contaminants. Common materials include steel, PVC, or concrete.
*   **Screen (or Perforated Casing):** The section of the casing at the bottom of the well that is open to the aquifer, allowing water to enter while preventing sand and gravel from entering the well. Screens can be slotted, perforated, or made of wire-wrapped materials.
*   **Gravel Pack (or Screen Pack):** A layer of clean, rounded gravel surrounding the screen. It acts as a filter, preventing fine sediments from entering the well and improving hydraulic efficiency.
*   **Grout Seal (or Annular Seal):** Cement or bentonite grout pumped into the annular space between the casing and the borehole wall, typically from the bottom of the casing up to the surface. This seals off the aquifer and prevents contamination from shallower zones.
*   **Pump:** The device used to lift water from the well to the surface.
*   **Piping:** Connects the pump to the surface discharge.

### 4. Factors Influencing Well Type Selection

*   **Depth of Water Table:** Determines whether shallow or deep well methods are appropriate.
*   **Aquifer Characteristics:** Permeability, thickness, confining conditions, and yield potential dictate the well's design and the likely effectiveness of different types.
*   **Water Quality Requirements:** Shallower sources may have poorer quality, necessitating deeper wells or specific treatment.
*   **Intended Use and Demand:** Small domestic use vs. large municipal supply will influence required yield and well diameter.
*   **Available Resources:** Construction budget, equipment availability, and skilled labor.
*   **Environmental Regulations:** Restrictions on well placement, construction, and water abstraction.

---

### 5. Important Points to Remember:

*   **No single well type is universally best.** The choice depends on a thorough assessment of hydrogeological conditions, intended use, and economic feasibility.
*   **Dug wells offer simplicity and low cost but are prone to contamination and shallow aquifer limitations.**
*   **Drilled wells are versatile, capable of reaching deep aquifers and providing high yields, but are more expensive.**
*   **Artesian wells provide water without pumping (if flowing) but require careful management to prevent wastage.**
*   **Monitoring wells are crucial for understanding groundwater dynamics and environmental protection.**
*   **Proper well construction, including casing, screening, and grouting, is vital for well performance, longevity, and preventing contamination.**
*   **Always consider the long-term sustainability and environmental impact of well selection and operation.**

### Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:** A community needs a reliable water supply from an aquifer located at a depth of 100 meters. The aquifer is confined and has a moderate yield potential. Which type of well would generally be most suitable, and why?

**Answer 1:** A **drilled well (borehole)** would be most suitable. It can effectively penetrate the confined aquifer to a depth of 100 meters, allowing for the installation of appropriate casing and screens to maximize yield and ensure water quality. The ability to control the borehole diameter and install a grout seal is crucial for confining a confined aquifer and preventing contamination.

**Question 2:** You are designing a water supply for a small village in an area with a shallow water table (5 meters below ground surface) in alluvial sands. The demand is for domestic use. Discuss the potential advantages and disadvantages of using a dug well versus a driven well in this scenario.

**Answer 2:**

*   **Dug Well:**
    *   **Advantages:** Low construction cost, can potentially have a large diameter yielding significant water from the "ring of saturation."
    *   **Disadvantages:** High risk of contamination from surface sources due to its open nature and shallow depth; susceptible to drying out during dry seasons if the water table drops significantly.
*   **Driven Well:**
    *   **Advantages:** Relatively inexpensive and quick to install; provides better protection from surface contamination than a dug well due to the casing.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited yield due to smaller diameter and short screen; can be clogged by fine sediment; still susceptible to seasonal water table fluctuations, though less so than a very shallow dug well.

**Question 3:** What is the primary purpose of a monitoring well, and how does its construction typically differ from a water supply well?

**Answer 3:** The primary purpose of a monitoring well is to **measure groundwater levels, collect water samples for quality analysis, and observe changes in the aquifer.** Its construction typically differs from a water supply well in that it is usually of **smaller diameter** and is not designed for significant water abstraction. While it will have casing and potentially a screen, the screen length and well development might focus more on accurate water level measurement and representative sampling rather than maximizing flow.

**Question 4:** Explain the concept of an artesian well and the conditions required for its formation.

**Answer 4:** An artesian well is a well drilled into a **confined aquifer** where the groundwater is **under pressure**. This pressure, known as hydrostatic pressure, is created when the aquifer is recharged in an area at a higher elevation than the wellhead. The water in the well will rise above the bottom of the confining layer. If the pressure is sufficient, water will flow freely to the surface without pumping, creating a **flowing artesian well**.

**Question 5:** Imagine you are advising a farmer who needs a well for large-scale irrigation in a region with bedrock aquifers that are several hundred meters deep. What type of well construction would you recommend, and why?

**Answer 5:** For large-scale irrigation from deep bedrock aquifers, a **drilled well (borehole)** is the standard recommendation. This is because:
*   **Depth Capability:** Drilling rigs can reach significant depths to access bedrock aquifers.
*   **Yield Potential:** Properly designed drilled wells with appropriate casing, screens (or open boreholes in bedrock), and development can achieve the high yields required for irrigation.
*   **Durability:** Bedrock wells are generally stable and less prone to collapse once constructed.
*   **Water Quality:** Deep bedrock aquifers often provide good quality water, protected from surface contamination.

The specific drilling method (rotary, percussion) and completion strategy (e.g., open borehole with fracture zones, or gravel-packed screen in weathered bedrock) would depend on the specific geological characteristics of the bedrock formation.
