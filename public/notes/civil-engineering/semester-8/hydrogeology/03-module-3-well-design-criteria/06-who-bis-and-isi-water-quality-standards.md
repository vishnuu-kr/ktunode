# Hydrogeology: Module 3 - Well Design Criteria
## Topic: WHO, BIS and ISI Water Quality Standards

---

### Introduction to Water Quality Standards

Water quality standards are crucial for ensuring that water, particularly groundwater used for drinking, is safe for human consumption and suitable for its intended purpose. They set limits on the concentration of various physical, chemical, biological, and radiological constituents in water. Different organizations and countries establish these standards based on scientific research, health impacts, and available technologies. This topic focuses on the standards set by the World Health Organization (WHO), the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), and the Indian Standards Institution (ISI), which is now largely superseded by BIS.

### 1. Role of Water Quality Standards in Well Design

Water quality is a fundamental consideration in well design for several critical reasons:

*   **Public Health Protection:** The primary goal is to prevent waterborne diseases and health issues arising from consuming contaminated water. Standards define safe limits for harmful contaminants.
*   **Suitability for Use:** Water quality determines its suitability for various uses, including drinking, irrigation, industrial processes, and even construction.
*   **Well Construction and Material Selection:** Certain water chemistry (e.g., high acidity, dissolved solids) can corrode well casing materials (steel, PVC). Understanding water quality helps select appropriate and durable materials.
*   **Pumping Equipment Longevity:** Aggressive water can damage pumps and other downhole equipment.
*   **Treatment Requirements:** Water quality data dictates the type and extent of treatment required to make the water potable or suitable for its intended use. This influences the design of treatment facilities.
*   **Aquifer Protection:** Standards indirectly guide practices that protect aquifers from contamination, as the goal is to abstract water that meets these standards.

### 2. Key Organizations Setting Water Quality Standards

*   **World Health Organization (WHO):**
    *   An international public health agency.
    *   Publishes guidelines for drinking water quality (GDWQ) that are widely recognized globally.
    *   These are not legally binding but serve as a basis for national standards.
    *   Focuses on health-based guideline values.

*   **Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS):**
    *   The national standards body of India.
    *   Develops and publishes Indian Standards (IS).
    *   **IS 10500: "Drinking Water – Specification"** is the primary standard for drinking water quality in India. This standard is legally enforceable under various acts like the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
    *   BIS standards are often more prescriptive and include limits for parameters considered important in the Indian context.

*   **Indian Standards Institution (ISI):**
    *   The predecessor to BIS.
    *   Many older standards may still refer to ISI.
    *   BIS has taken over and updated most of these standards. For drinking water, IS 10500 is the current and relevant standard.

### 3. General Principles and Categories of Parameters in Water Quality Standards

Water quality standards typically cover parameters categorized as follows:

*   **A. Organoleptic and General Parameters:**
    *   Relate to the aesthetic qualities of water that affect consumer acceptability.
    *   *Examples:* Colour, Odour, Taste, Turbidity, Electrical Conductivity (EC).

*   **B. Chemical Parameters:**
    *   Inorganic constituents, organic matter, and nutrients.
    *   Can have health impacts or affect suitability for use.
    *   **Sub-categories:**
        *   **Essential Minerals (Beneficial):** Calcium, Magnesium, Potassium (in optimal ranges).
        *   **Undesirable/Harmful Inorganic Compounds:** Fluoride, Nitrate, Arsenic, Lead, Mercury, Cadmium, Chromium, Iron, Manganese.
        *   **General Chemical Properties:** pH, Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), Total Hardness, Chloride, Sulphate, Total Alkalinity.
        *   **Organic Compounds:** Detergents, Pesticides, Phenols (often with very low limits due to toxicity).

*   **C. Microbiological Parameters:**
    *   Presence of pathogenic microorganisms.
    *   **Key Indicators:**
        *   *Total Coliforms:* Bacteria commonly found in soil and water, indicates fecal contamination if present.
        *   *Faecal Coliforms/E. coli:* Specific bacteria found in the intestines of warm-blooded animals, strong indicator of fecal pollution and potential presence of pathogens.

*   **D. Radiological Parameters:**
    *   Presence of radioactive substances.
    *   *Examples:* Gross Alpha activity, Gross Beta activity, Radium, Uranium.

### 4. WHO Guidelines for Drinking Water Quality (GDWQ)

WHO GDWQ provides guideline values for various parameters. These are based on extensive scientific review and are designed to be protective of public health over a lifetime of consumption.

**Key Principles:**

*   **Health-Based Guideline Values:** Based on toxicological data and acceptable daily intake (ADI).
*   **Achievable Limits:** Takes into account what can be practically achieved through treatment and monitoring.
*   **Risk Assessment:** Incorporates risk assessment principles for some contaminants.

### 5. BIS Standards for Drinking Water (IS 10500:2012)

IS 10500:2012 is the current Indian Standard for drinking water. It specifies limits for various parameters and distinguishes between **"Acceptable Limit"** and **"Permissible Limit in absence of alternate source."**

**Key Features of IS 10500:**

*   **Two Tiers of Limits:**
    *   **Acceptable Limit:** The ideal limit for drinking water.
    *   **Permissible Limit in Absence of Alternate Source:** A higher limit allowed when no other source is available, and the water is still considered safe with potential risks. This is crucial for rural and underserved areas.
*   **Focus on Indian Context:** Includes parameters and limits relevant to India's water quality issues.
*   **Legal Enforceability:** Compliance is mandatory under various Indian laws.

### 6. Comparison of WHO and BIS Standards

| Feature               | WHO GDWQ                                      | BIS IS 10500:2012                                 |
| :-------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| **Nature**            | Guidelines, non-binding                       | National Standard, legally enforceable            |
| **Scope**             | Global, health-focused                        | India-specific, includes aesthetic and health.    |
| **Limits**            | Single guideline values (GV)                  | Acceptable Limit and Permissible Limit            |
| **Enforceability**    | No direct legal enforceability                | Mandatory under Indian law                        |
| **Flexibility**       | Provides a basis for national standards       | More prescriptive with two tiers of limits        |
| **Parameter Focus**   | Primarily health-based                        | Comprehensive, including aesthetic and practical concerns for India. |
| **Example Discrepancy** | TDS GV: 1000 mg/L; BIS Acceptable: 500 mg/L   | Fluoride GV: 1.5 mg/L; BIS Acceptable: 1.0 mg/L   |

**Important:** While WHO GDWQ is influential, national standards like BIS IS 10500 must be followed within their respective countries. BIS standards are often stricter for parameters considered critical in the Indian context.

### 7. Historical Context of ISI Standards

The Indian Standards Institution (ISI) was established in 1947. It was responsible for developing various Indian Standards, including those related to water. In 1986, ISI was converted into the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) under an Act of Parliament. The BIS continues the work of ISI, revising and updating standards regularly. For drinking water, the evolution from earlier ISI specifications to the current BIS IS 10500:2012 reflects advancements in scientific understanding, monitoring capabilities, and evolving health concerns.

### 8. Impact on Well Design and Water Treatment

Water quality data is a direct input for designing a well and its associated treatment system:

*   **Well Depth and Location:** If parameters like nitrate or arsenic are high in shallow groundwater, it suggests the need to look for deeper, potentially less contaminated aquifers or reconsider the well location.
*   **Casing and Screen Material:** High dissolved solids, chlorides, or acidity might necessitate the use of corrosion-resistant materials like stainless steel or specialized plastics for casing and screens.
*   **Pumping Equipment:** Corrosive or abrasive water can influence the type and material of pumps and drop pipes.
*   **Water Treatment System Design:**
    *   **High Turbidity:** Requires pre-treatment like sedimentation or filtration.
    *   **High Hardness:** Requires water softening (e.g., ion exchange).
    *   **High Iron/Manganese:** Requires aeration and filtration, or catalytic media.
    *   **High Fluoride:** Requires defluoridation techniques (e.g., activated alumina, Nalgonda technique).
    *   **High Arsenic/Nitrate:** Requires specific removal processes (e.g., adsorption, reverse osmosis, ion exchange).
    *   **Microbiological Contamination:** Necessitates disinfection (e.g., chlorination, UV treatment, ozonation).
    *   **High TDS:** May require reverse osmosis or distillation.

### Practice Questions & Exercises

1.  **Question:** Explain why understanding water quality standards is crucial for designing a drinking water well.
    **Answer:** Water quality standards are vital for protecting public health by setting limits on contaminants. They also guide the selection of well construction materials to prevent corrosion, influence the choice of pumping equipment, and dictate the necessary water treatment processes to ensure the water is safe and palatable for consumption.

2.  **Question:** What is the main difference between WHO guidelines and BIS standards for drinking water?
    **Answer:** WHO guidelines are international, health-focused, and non-binding recommendations. BIS standards, like IS 10500, are national, legally enforceable standards specific to India, incorporating acceptable and permissible limits in absence of alternate sources, reflecting local conditions and priorities.

3.  **Question:** List three parameters that have different acceptable limits in BIS IS 10500:2012 compared to WHO GDWQ.
    **Answer:** Several parameters show differences. For example:
    *   **Total Dissolved Solids (TDS):** WHO GV = 1000 mg/L; BIS Acceptable = 500 mg/L, BIS Permissible = 1000 mg/L.
    *   **Fluoride (F⁻):** WHO GV = 1.5 mg/L; BIS Acceptable = 1.0 mg/L, BIS Permissible = 1.5 mg/L.
    *   **Turbidity:** WHO GV = 5 NTU (Max 25); BIS Acceptable = 1 NTU, BIS Permissible = 5 NTU.

4.  **Question:** A groundwater sample from a proposed well site shows a nitrate concentration of 70 mg/L (as NO₃). According to BIS IS 10500:2012, is this water acceptable for drinking? If not, what does the "Permissible Limit" suggest?
    **Answer:** No, 70 mg/L of nitrate is not acceptable as it exceeds the BIS acceptable limit of 45 mg/L (as NO₃). However, it falls within the permissible limit of 90 mg/L (as NO₃) in the absence of an alternate source. This means it might be used cautiously with appropriate risk assessment and potential treatment considerations, but it is not ideal.

5.  **Question:** If a well's water quality report indicates high turbidity and the presence of E. coli, what are the immediate implications for the well's usability and what type of treatment would likely be required?
    **Answer:** High turbidity and the presence of E. coli indicate fecal contamination and pose a significant health risk. The water is not safe for drinking without treatment. Immediate treatment would involve disinfection (e.g., chlorination or UV treatment) to kill the bacteria and likely filtration or sedimentation to reduce turbidity, which can interfere with disinfection efficiency.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Health is Paramount:** Water quality standards are primarily designed to protect human health.
*   **BIS IS 10500 is Key in India:** Always refer to the latest BIS standard for drinking water in India.
*   **Two-Tiered Limits in BIS:** Understand the significance of "Acceptable" vs. "Permissible" limits.
*   **Holistic Approach:** Consider all parameter categories (organoleptic, chemical, microbiological, radiological).
*   **Data-Driven Design:** Water quality data directly impacts well construction material, depth, pumping equipment, and treatment system design.
*   **Monitoring is Continuous:** Water quality can change over time, so regular monitoring is essential, especially for public water supply wells.
*   **Local Conditions Matter:** While WHO guidelines are universal, national standards are tailored to specific regional issues.
