# Hydrogeology: Module 4 - Saline Water Intrusion & Groundwater in Construction

## Topic: Problems Created by Groundwater in the Construction Phase of Mega Civil Engineering Projects

This module section focuses on the challenges posed by groundwater during the construction of large-scale civil engineering projects, particularly in coastal areas where saline intrusion is a concern.

---

### 1. Introduction to Groundwater Issues in Mega Projects

Mega civil engineering projects, such as tunnels, bridges, dams, high-rise buildings, and underground infrastructure, often involve significant excavation and interaction with subsurface geological formations. Groundwater is a ubiquitous subsurface resource that can present substantial challenges during these construction phases. Understanding and managing groundwater is crucial for project success, safety, and environmental sustainability.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Groundwater:** Water held underground in the soil or in pores and crevices in rock.
*   **Aquifer:** A body of permeable rock which can contain or transmit groundwater.
*   **Water Table:** The upper level of the saturated zone of groundwater.
*   **Pore Water Pressure:** The pressure exerted by groundwater in the pore spaces of soil or rock.
*   **Hydrostatic Pressure:** The pressure exerted by a fluid at equilibrium at a given point within the fluid, due to the force of gravity.

### 2. Common Groundwater Problems Encountered During Construction

Groundwater can cause a variety of issues, impacting stability, accessibility, cost, and safety.

#### 2.1. Excavation Stability and Support

*   **Loss of Shear Strength:** Saturated soils and rocks have reduced shear strength compared to dry conditions. The presence of pore water pressure can effectively reduce the confining pressure, leading to potential instability of excavated slopes and trenches.
    *   **Example:** A deep excavation for a building foundation in saturated clay can become unstable and lead to landslides if groundwater is not controlled.
*   **Boiling and Sand Blows:** In fine-grained, permeable soils below the water table, rapid dewatering can create a significant upward hydraulic gradient. If this gradient exceeds the effective stress of the soil, it can lead to liquefaction, where soil particles become suspended in water, causing upward expulsion of soil and water (boiling or sand blows).
    *   **Example:** Dewatering a deep excavation in a sandy aquifer without proper controls can result in localized ground failure due to boiling.
*   **Ground Settlement and Subsidence:** Excessive groundwater extraction for dewatering can lead to a significant drop in the water table. This can cause consolidation of compressible soils, resulting in ground settlement and potential subsidence of adjacent structures or infrastructure.
    *   **Example:** Dewatering for a tunnel construction might cause settlement of nearby historic buildings if not managed carefully.
*   **Seepage and Inflow:** Groundwater naturally flows from areas of higher hydraulic head to areas of lower hydraulic head. During construction, excavations create a localized area of low head, leading to continuous seepage into the excavation. This inflow can impede construction progress, create working hazards, and necessitate continuous pumping.
    *   **Example:** A tunnel bored through a saturated rock formation will experience continuous groundwater inflow, requiring extensive pumping systems.

#### 2.2. Impact on Construction Materials and Processes

*   **Weakening of Excavation Sides:** Constant saturation from seepage can weaken the soil or rock mass, increasing the risk of collapses.
*   **Difficulties in Piling and Foundation Works:** Groundwater can interfere with the driving of piles, the casting of concrete in situ (e.g., diaphragm walls), and the installation of foundation elements.
    *   **Example:** Driving piles into saturated, silty soils can be difficult due to increased resistance and potential for lateral spreading of the soil.
*   **Buoyancy Effects:** Underground structures, especially those that are lightweight or partially completed, can be susceptible to uplift forces (buoyancy) from hydrostatic pressure if not adequately anchored or designed.
    *   **Example:** A basement slab that is constructed before significant waterproofing is in place can be lifted by buoyant forces if the groundwater table rises.

#### 2.3. Environmental and Geochemical Impacts

*   **Contamination of Groundwater Resources:** Dewatering operations can alter groundwater flow patterns, potentially drawing contaminated surface water or shallow groundwater into deeper, cleaner aquifers. This is particularly relevant in areas with existing pollution.
*   **Mobilization of Soluble Contaminants:** Changes in groundwater chemistry and flow can mobilize naturally occurring or anthropogenic contaminants within the aquifer, posing risks to downstream water users.
*   **Interaction with Saline Water (Coastal Areas):** This is a critical consideration for mega projects in coastal zones. Groundwater extraction for dewatering can lower the freshwater table, reducing the hydraulic barrier that prevents saline water from intruding into freshwater aquifers. This leads to increased salinity in the groundwater, impacting its usability.
    *   **Example:** Extensive dewatering for a coastal tunnel construction could lead to saline intrusion into nearby potable groundwater sources.

### 3. Groundwater Management and Control Strategies

Effective management and control of groundwater are essential for successful mega project construction.

#### 3.1. Dewatering Techniques

Dewatering involves lowering the groundwater table within the construction zone to create dry working conditions.

*   **Wellpoints:** A system of closely spaced, small-diameter wells connected to a common header pipe and a vacuum pump. Suitable for shallow to medium depths.
    *   **Advantages:** Relatively inexpensive, flexible.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited depth, susceptible to clogging.
*   **Deep Wells:** Larger diameter wells with submersible pumps. Used for deeper excavations and larger dewatering volumes.
    *   **Advantages:** Can dewater to greater depths, higher flow rates.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Higher cost, requires more powerful pumps.
*   **Drains (e.g., French Drains, Sump Pumping):** Open ditches or trenches with perforated pipes, leading to a sump where water is pumped out.
    *   **Advantages:** Simple, effective for managing seepage at the bottom of excavations.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be slow, may not significantly lower the overall water table.
*   **Ejectors:** Use a high-velocity jet of water to create a vacuum, drawing groundwater from fine-grained soils where wellpoints may clog.

#### 3.2. Groundwater Control and Exclusion

These methods aim to prevent groundwater from entering the excavation.

*   **Sheet Piling:** Driving interlocking steel sheets into the ground to form a continuous barrier.
    *   **Advantages:** Effective for retaining soil and preventing water inflow in many conditions.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be expensive, potential for leakage through interlocks, challenging in hard ground.
*   **Contiguous Bored Piles:** Large diameter concrete piles installed adjacent to each other, creating a barrier.
    *   **Advantages:** Creates a robust barrier, good for deep excavations.
    *   **Disadvantages:** May still have some leakage, requires specialized equipment.
*   **Diaphragm Walls (Slurry Walls):** Excavating trenches under a bentonite slurry, which prevents collapse. The trench is then filled with reinforced concrete.
    *   **Advantages:** Provides excellent structural support and a very effective groundwater barrier.
    *   **Disadvantages:** High cost, technically complex, requires careful slurry management.
*   **Grouting and Injection:** Injecting cementitious or chemical grouts into the soil or rock mass to reduce permeability and seal off water ingress.
    *   **Advantages:** Can be very effective in sealing fissures and fine pores.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Effectiveness depends heavily on ground conditions, can be expensive.
*   **Waterproofing Membranes:** Applying impermeable barriers to the external surfaces of permanent underground structures.
    *   **Advantages:** Protects the structure from water ingress after construction.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Primarily for the final structure, not for managing water during construction excavation.

#### 3.3. Groundwater Abstraction and Management in Coastal Areas

When dewatering in coastal areas, careful consideration must be given to the potential for saline intrusion.

*   **Controlled Dewatering:** Pumping at rates that minimize drawdown and the resulting hydraulic gradient that drives saline intrusion.
*   **Recharge/Injection Wells:** Installing wells to inject freshwater into the aquifer near the coast to create a hydraulic barrier, pushing back the saltwater interface. This is often called a "freshwater lens" or "barrier well system."
    *   **Example:** For a coastal tunnel, injection wells could be used to maintain a positive hydraulic head in the aquifer, preventing saline intrusion caused by dewatering.
*   **Monitoring:** Continuous monitoring of groundwater levels and salinity in observation wells is crucial to detect and manage saline intrusion.
*   **Aquifer Modelling:** Using numerical models to predict the impact of dewatering on groundwater flow and saline intrusion before construction begins.

### 4. Planning and Investigation for Groundwater Issues

Thorough hydrogeological investigation is paramount before commencing any mega project.

*   **Desk Studies:** Reviewing existing geological maps, boreholes, well records, and previous hydrogeological studies.
*   **Geophysical Surveys:** Using techniques like electrical resistivity and seismic refraction to infer subsurface conditions and groundwater presence.
*   **Field Investigations:**
    *   **Borehole Drilling:** Essential for obtaining soil/rock samples and installing piezometers.
    *   **Piezometer Installation:** Measuring groundwater levels and pressures at various depths.
    *   **Pumping Tests:** Determining aquifer properties such as transmissivity, storativity, and hydraulic conductivity, and assessing potential for saline intrusion.
    *   **Water Quality Sampling:** Assessing existing groundwater chemistry, including salinity.
*   **Hydrogeological Modelling:** Developing predictive models to simulate groundwater behavior under different dewatering or construction scenarios.

### 6. Practice Questions/Exercises

**Question 1:** Describe three major problems that groundwater can cause during the construction of a deep subway tunnel. For each problem, suggest a primary control measure.

**Question 2:** A large, multi-story building is planned in a coastal area with a shallow water table. What are the specific risks associated with groundwater during excavation and foundation construction, and how might saline intrusion be a particular concern in this scenario?

**Question 3:** You are involved in the dewatering for a large bridge foundation in a sandy aquifer. You observe rising salinity in your observation wells located closer to the sea. What is the most likely cause, and what management strategy would you recommend to mitigate this?

---

### 7. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**

1.  **Excavation Instability (Slope Failure):** Groundwater saturation reduces the shear strength of soil/rock, making excavated slopes prone to collapse.
    *   **Control Measure:** Dewatering (e.g., wellpoints or deep wells) to lower the water table, or installation of retaining structures like sheet piles or diaphragm walls.
2.  **Groundwater Inflow:** Continuous flow of groundwater into the excavation requires constant pumping, slowing down work and increasing costs.
    *   **Control Measure:** Effective dewatering systems (wellpoints, deep wells) and potentially impermeable barriers (e.g., slurry walls) if inflow is very high.
3.  **Ground Settlement/Subsidence:** Excessive groundwater abstraction can lead to consolidation of underlying compressible soils, causing the ground surface to sink.
    *   **Control Measure:** Managed dewatering rates, monitoring settlement, and potentially managed recharge if settlement becomes critical.

**Answer 2:**

*   **Risks during Excavation and Foundation:**
    *   **Excavation Stability:** Saturated soils lose strength, increasing risk of collapse in trenches and pits.
    *   **Groundwater Inflow:** Continuous pumping will be required, potentially slowing down progress and increasing costs.
    *   **Buoyancy:** Excavated areas and partially constructed basements can be subject to uplift forces from the groundwater, potentially causing flotation.
    *   **Foundation Constructability:** Driving piles or casting concrete in wet conditions can be compromised.
*   **Saline Intrusion Concern:**
    *   Lowering the freshwater table through dewatering creates a hydraulic gradient that can draw saline seawater inland from the coast. This can contaminate the freshwater aquifer, making the groundwater unsuitable for use and potentially affecting nearby infrastructure.

**Answer 3:**

*   **Most Likely Cause:** The dewatering operation is creating a significant cone of depression in the freshwater table. This lowers the hydraulic head, allowing the denser, more saline seawater to move inland and intrude into the aquifer, replacing the depleted freshwater.
*   **Recommended Management Strategy:**
    1.  **Reduce Pumping Rate:** Lower the overall abstraction rate from the dewatering wells to minimize the drawdown and the driving force for saline intrusion.
    2.  **Install Freshwater Injection Wells:** Establish a barrier well system. Inject freshwater into wells located between the coast and the construction site. This creates an outward hydraulic gradient, pushing back the saltwater interface and preventing it from reaching the main dewatering zone or other freshwater sources.
    3.  **Intensify Monitoring:** Increase the frequency of groundwater level and salinity measurements in existing observation wells and install new ones to track the saltwater interface more closely.
    4.  **Aquifer Modelling:** Refine the hydrogeological model to better predict the impact of dewatering and injection strategies on saline intrusion.

---

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Proactive Investigation:** Thorough hydrogeological studies *before* construction are essential to anticipate and plan for groundwater challenges.
*   **Dewatering is a Double-Edged Sword:** While necessary for dry working conditions, it can induce settlement and, critically in coastal areas, saline intrusion.
*   **Managed Approach is Key:** Dewatering rates and drawdown should be carefully managed, especially in sensitive environments.
*   **Barriers are Crucial:** For deep excavations and in areas with high water inflow or saline intrusion risk, physical barriers (sheet piles, slurry walls) or injection barriers are often required.
*   **Monitoring is Continuous:** Groundwater levels and quality must be monitored throughout the construction process to assess the effectiveness of control measures and detect unexpected issues.
*   **Environmental Responsibility:** Groundwater management must consider downstream users and the broader environmental impact, particularly concerning water quality and aquifer integrity.
