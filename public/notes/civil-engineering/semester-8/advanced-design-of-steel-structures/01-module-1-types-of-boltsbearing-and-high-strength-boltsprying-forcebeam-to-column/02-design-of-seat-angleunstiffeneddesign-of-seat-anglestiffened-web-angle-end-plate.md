### 1. Introduction to Beam-to-Column Connections

Beam-to-column connections are critical components in steel structures, transferring loads from beams to columns. The type of connection significantly influences the structural behavior, rigidity, and load-carrying capacity of the overall frame.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Connectivity:** How a beam is joined to a column.
*   **Load Transfer:** The mechanism by which forces (shear, moment, axial) are transmitted.
*   **Rigidity:** The degree to which a connection resists deformation under load.
    *   **Simple Connections (Pinned):** Primarily transfer shear forces, allowing rotation.
    *   **Moment Connections (Rigid):** Transfer both shear and moment, resisting rotation.
    *   **Partial Moment Connections:** Offer some rotational restraint, between simple and rigid.
*   **Stiffness:** The resistance to deformation.
*   **Ductility:** The ability of a connection to deform plastically before failure.

### 2. Types of Bolts

Bolts are widely used fasteners in steel structures. Understanding their types is crucial for designing reliable connections.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Bearing Bolts (Friction Bolts):** Rely on bearing pressure between the bolt and the hole edge and friction between the connected members to transfer load. Typically used in applications where slip is acceptable.
    *   **Material Grades:** Commonly Fe 410, Fe 500.
    *   **Hole Types:** Normal, close tolerance, precision.
*   **High Strength Bolts (HSFG Bolts - High Strength Friction Grip):** Rely on the clamping force generated by tightening the bolts to create friction between the connected members. This friction is the primary mechanism for load transfer, making them generally preferred for more critical connections where slip must be avoided.
    *   **Material Grades:** 8.8, 10.9 (higher strength than bearing bolts).
    *   **Components:** Bolt, nut, and washer.
    *   **Tightening:** Applied through pre-tensioning (e.g., using calibrated torque wrenches, turn-of-nut method, or direct tension indicators).
    *   **Advantages:** Higher slip resistance, less dependent on bearing strength, can act as bearing bolts if slip occurs, better fatigue performance.

### 3. Prying Force

Prying force is an additional tensile force that can be induced in the bolts or connection elements due to the flexibility of the connected members, especially when subjected to axial or moment loads.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Definition:** An additional tension in fasteners (bolts) that arises from the flexibility of the connected plates or members, when subjected to an applied external tensile force.
*   **Mechanism:** When a tensile load is applied to a connection, the connected plates (e.g., flange plates, web cleats) tend to bend. This bending causes a lever arm effect, leading to an increased tension in the outermost bolts or fasteners.
*   **Impact:** Prying force can significantly increase the stress in the bolts, potentially leading to premature failure if not accounted for in the design.
*   **Factors Influencing Prying Force:**
    *   **Stiffness of Connected Plates:** Thicker, stiffer plates experience less bending and thus lower prying forces.
    *   **Edge Distance:** Larger edge distances can increase the lever arm, leading to higher prying forces.
    *   **Bolt Location:** Bolts further from the connection's neutral axis are more susceptible.
    *   **Applied Load Magnitude and Type:** Higher tensile loads increase prying forces.

**Calculation (Simplified Approach - IS 800:2007):**

IS 800:2007 provides methods to check the prying force. A common approach involves calculating the "effective" tensile load on the bolts by considering the prying action.

*   **Design Tensile Load ($T_{db}$):** The design tensile load on a bolt is considered as $T_{db} = (P_s + P_p) / n$, where:
    *   $P_s$ is the applied tensile force per bolt.
    *   $P_p$ is the prying force.
    *   $n$ is the number of bolts sharing the tensile load.

*   **Checking for Prying Force (IS 800:2007, Clause 10.3.4):**
    The design tensile strength of a bolt in a connection subject to prying force shall be checked. The maximum force per bolt due to prying action shall be calculated.
    Let $P$ be the total applied tensile load. Let $a$ be the distance from the edge of the loaded member to the center of the fastener. Let $b$ be the distance from the center of the fastener to the furthest point of support. Let $t$ be the thickness of the connected plate.

    If $\frac{a}{t} \ge 1.2 \frac{b}{t} \sqrt{\frac{t}{t+w}}$, where $w$ is the width of the flange or web at the point of applied load, then prying force needs to be considered.
    The prying force ($P_p$) can be calculated as:
    $P_p = \frac{P(b - e)}{L - e}$ where:
    *   $P$ is the total applied tensile load.
    *   $b$ is the distance from the edge of the flange/web to the center of the furthest bolt.
    *   $e$ is the distance from the edge of the flange/web to the center of the nearest bolt.
    *   $L$ is the distance between the centers of the extreme bolts.

    The total tension on the outer bolt is then $T_{outer} = \frac{P}{n} + P_p$. This must be less than the design tensile strength of the bolt.

**Example (Conceptual):**

Imagine a cleat connection where a beam flange is attached to a column flange. If a significant upward force (tension) is applied to the beam, the cleat might bend. This bending can cause the outer bolt to experience a greater tension than just the uniformly distributed tensile load.

### 4. Design of Seat Angle Connections

Seat angle connections are commonly used to support the beam on the column, primarily transferring vertical shear forces. They can also provide some moment resistance depending on their configuration.

#### 4.1 Unstiffened Seat Angle Connections

These connections consist of a single angle (the seat angle) bolted to the column web and beam flange. They are generally used for lighter loads and when significant moment transfer is not required.

**Components:**

*   **Seat Angle:** An angle section (e.g., ISA) bolted to the column flange.
*   **Beam:** The beam being supported.
*   **Bolts:** Connecting the seat angle to the column and potentially to the beam flange.
*   **Shop vs. Field:** Often, the seat angle is shop-fitted to the column, and the beam is bolted to it in the field.

**Design Considerations:**

1.  **Bearing Strength of Beam Web:** The beam web must have adequate bearing strength to support the load transferred by the seat angle.
2.  **Shear Strength of Bolts:** Bolts connecting the seat angle to the column must have sufficient shear strength to resist the applied shear load.
3.  **Bearing Strength of Column Web/Flange:** The column web or flange where the seat angle is attached must have adequate bearing strength.
4.  **Tensile Strength of Bolts:** If any uplift or net tension is expected, the bolts must be checked for tension.
5.  **Bending Strength of Seat Angle:** The outstanding leg of the seat angle must have sufficient strength to resist bending induced by the load from the beam.
6.  **Web Crippling/Buckling:** The beam web should be checked for crippling and buckling.

**Design Steps (Simplified):**

1.  **Determine Loads:** Identify the factored shear load ($V_u$) and any potential moment ($M_u$) or tension ($T_u$) acting on the connection.
2.  **Select Seat Angle Size:** Choose an angle section that can accommodate the required number of bolts and has sufficient stiffness.
3.  **Check Beam Web Bearing:**
    *   The bearing length of the beam flange on the seat angle should be adequate.
    *   The bearing area on the column flange/web needs to be sufficient.
4.  **Design Bolts for Shear (Connecting Seat Angle to Column):**
    *   Calculate the shear force per bolt. For simple shear connections, the load is distributed evenly. However, if there's a moment, the distribution is not uniform.
    *   Determine the required bolt shear strength ($V_{db}$).
    *   Select bolt size and grade based on shear and tensile strength requirements.
    *   Check edge distances and pitch for bolt shear and bearing.
5.  **Design the Seat Angle Leg (Outstanding Leg):**
    *   This leg acts as a cantilever supporting the beam web. The critical section for bending is at the root of the angle.
    *   The load from the beam on the seat angle is assumed to be distributed over a certain length of the beam's flange.
    *   Calculate the bending moment on the outstanding leg.
    *   Check the bending stress in the outstanding leg against its yield and ultimate strength.
6.  **Check for Prying Force (if applicable):** If there are tensile loads or significant moments, prying force in the bolts connecting the seat angle to the column needs to be considered.

**Example:**

Consider a beam carrying a factored shear load of 150 kN. The beam rests on a seat angle bolted to a column flange. The seat angle is an ISA 100x100x10.

*   **Load:** $V_u = 150$ kN.
*   **Connection:** Seat angle to column flange. Assume 4 bolts (M20, 4.6 grade) in the outstanding leg of the seat angle, connecting it to the column.
*   **Design Shear Strength of Bolt:**
    *   $V_{dsb}$ for M20, 4.6 grade = $f_{yb} A_{nb} / \sqrt{3} \gamma_{mb}$.
    *   $f_{yb} = 0.4 \times 400 = 160$ N/mm².
    *   $A_{nb}$ (Nominal area of bolt) = $\pi/4 \times 20^2 = 314.16$ mm².
    *   $\gamma_{mb} = 1.25$.
    *   $V_{dsb} = 160 \times 314.16 / (\sqrt{3} \times 1.25) = 23258$ N ≈ 23.26 kN.
*   **Total Shear Resistance of 4 bolts:** $4 \times 23.26 = 93.04$ kN.

This calculation shows that 4 bolts might be insufficient. More bolts would be needed, or a larger diameter/higher grade bolt. The design would continue to check bearing, bending, etc.

**Important Points to Remember (Unstiffened Seat Angle):**

*   Primarily for shear.
*   Limited moment transfer.
*   Beam web stiffness is important for bearing.
*   Consider prying force if tensions exist.

#### 4.2 Stiffened Seat Angle Connections

These connections are similar to unstiffened seat angles but include stiffeners (usually web cleats) to enhance their load-carrying capacity, particularly for larger moments or shear loads.

**Components:**

*   **Seat Angle:** As in unstiffened connections.
*   **Stiffeners (Web Cleats):** Angles or plates attached to the web of the beam and the outstanding leg of the seat angle.
*   **Bolts:** For all connections.

**Purpose of Stiffeners:**

*   **Reduce Prying Force:** By providing a stiffer connection between the beam web and the seat angle, stiffeners reduce the lever arm for prying action, thereby reducing the tension in the main supporting bolts.
*   **Enhance Moment Capacity:** Stiffeners help in transferring moments by providing a more rigid connection.
*   **Increase Load Capacity:** They allow for greater shear and moment transfer through the connection.

**Design Considerations:**

1.  **All considerations for unstiffened seat angles apply.**
2.  **Design of Stiffeners:**
    *   **Size and Thickness:** Should be adequate to resist the forces they transmit.
    *   **Bolting:** Stiffeners need to be bolted to the beam web and the seat angle. These bolts must be designed for shear and tension.
3.  **Analysis of Load Distribution:** The load is distributed between the seat angle leg in contact with the beam flange and the stiffeners connecting to the beam web.
4.  **Prying Force Calculation:** With stiffeners, the prying force calculation needs to consider the reduced flexibility of the system. IS 800:2007 provides specific clauses for connections with stiffeners. The prying force is generally reduced, and the load distribution between bolts can be more complex.

**Design Steps (Simplified):**

1.  **Determine Loads:** Factored shear ($V_u$), moment ($M_u$), and axial forces.
2.  **Select Seat Angle and Stiffeners:** Choose suitable sections.
3.  **Check Beam Web Bearing:** Similar to unstiffened.
4.  **Design Bolts (Seat Angle to Column):**
    *   **Shear:** Total shear is distributed among bolts.
    *   **Tension & Prying:** Calculate tension per bolt, considering the stiffening effect of cleats. The load distribution to bolts can be analyzed using a rigid-body assumption for the connected parts or by considering flexibility. For practical design, often the critical bolts are checked for combined shear and tension, and prying force is reduced.
5.  **Design Stiffener Bolts:**
    *   **To Beam Web:** These bolts transfer forces from the stiffener to the beam web.
    *   **To Seat Angle:** These bolts transfer forces from the stiffener to the seat angle.
6.  **Check Stiffener Strength:** Stiffeners themselves need to have adequate strength to transmit forces without buckling or yielding.

**Example Scenario:**

A heavier beam requiring a larger moment transfer might use stiffened seat angles. The stiffeners would be bolted to the beam web and the seat angle's outstanding leg, effectively creating a more robust connection.

**Important Points to Remember (Stiffened Seat Angle):**

*   Used for heavier loads and significant moment transfer.
*   Stiffeners reduce prying force and increase rigidity.
*   Design of stiffeners and their bolting is critical.

### 5. Design of Web Angle Connections

Web angle connections, also known as cleat connections, are used to connect beams to columns or other beams. They primarily transfer shear forces and can also provide some rotational restraint.

**Components:**

*   **Web Angles (Cleats):** Angles bolted to the beam web and the supporting member (column flange or web, or another beam).
*   **Bolts:** Connecting the web angles to both members.

**Types:**

*   **Single Web Angle:** One angle on one side of the beam web.
*   **Double Web Angle:** Angles on both sides of the beam web for greater stiffness and load capacity.

**Design Considerations:**

1.  **Shear Strength of Bolts:** Bolts are critical for shear transfer.
2.  **Bearing Strength of Bolts and Holes:**
3.  **Tensile Strength of Bolts:** If there are any uplift forces or secondary bending moments causing tension.
4.  **Bearing Strength of Beam Web and Column:**
5.  **Buckling of Beam Web:** The beam web can buckle under concentrated loads from the web angles.
6.  **Prying Force:** Can occur in the bolts connecting to the column if the web angle flexes significantly.

**Design Steps (Simplified - Double Web Angle):**

1.  **Determine Loads:** Factored shear load ($V_u$).
2.  **Select Web Angle Size:** Typically ISA sections.
3.  **Determine Bolt Layout:** Number and arrangement of bolts on each side of the beam web, connecting to the column.
4.  **Design Bolts for Shear:**
    *   The shear force $V_u$ is distributed among the bolts connecting the web angles to the column.
    *   For a symmetric connection (double web angles), the shear is distributed evenly.
    *   Calculate the shear force per bolt and check against the design shear strength of the bolt.
5.  **Check Bearing:**
    *   Bearing of bolts on holes in web angles and beam web.
    *   Bearing of web angles on the column flange/web.
6.  **Check Web Cleat Strength:** The web angles themselves must be strong enough to resist bending and shear.
7.  **Check Beam Web:** Ensure the beam web can handle the bearing stress from the web angles and is checked for crippling or buckling.
8.  **Prying Force:** If there is a moment transfer component or asymmetric loading, prying force in the column bolts should be checked.

**Example:**

A beam with a factored shear of 200 kN is to be connected to a column flange using double web angles. Let's assume M20 bolts of 4.6 grade are used. The connection uses two angles (e.g., ISA 75x75x8) on each side, each with 4 bolts in a column.

*   **Total Bolts:** 4 bolts/angle * 2 angles * 2 sides = 16 bolts.
*   **Shear per Bolt (assuming uniform distribution):** $200 \text{ kN} / 16 \text{ bolts} = 12.5$ kN/bolt.
*   **Design Shear Strength ($V_{dsb}$):** As calculated earlier for M20, 4.6 grade is approx. 23.26 kN.
*   **Check:** $12.5 \text{ kN} < 23.26 \text{ kN}$. The bolts have sufficient shear capacity.
*   The design would continue to check bearing, tension, and the strength of the web angles and beam web.

**Important Points to Remember (Web Angle Connections):**

*   Primarily for shear.
*   Versatile and common.
*   Double web angles provide better stiffness.
*   Beam web capacity is crucial.

### 6. Design of End Plate Connections

End plate connections offer a more rigid connection compared to cleat connections and are often used where moment transfer is required. The end plate is welded to the beam flange and web and then bolted to the column.

**Components:**

*   **End Plate:** A steel plate welded to the end of the beam.
*   **Bolts:** Connect the end plate to the column flange.
*   **Welds:** Connect the beam to the end plate.

**Types:**

*   **Flush End Plate:** The end plate is flush with the beam flange.
*   **Extended End Plate:** The end plate extends beyond the beam flange, typically used for moment connections.
*   **Column Flush vs. Extended:** The end plate can be connected to the column flange or a column stub.

**Design Considerations for Moment Connections (Extended End Plate):**

1.  **Beam-to-End Plate Welds:** Welds must be strong enough to transfer the moment and shear from the beam to the end plate. Fillet welds and full penetration groove welds are common.
2.  **End Plate Strength:**
    *   **Bending Strength:** The end plate acts as a cantilever, bending about the face of the column. Critical sections are at the face of the column. The bolts are arranged in tension and compression zones.
    *   **Shear Strength:** The end plate must resist shear forces.
    *   **Web Cleats/Stiffeners:** Often, stiffeners are used between the beam web and the end plate to increase stiffness and reduce prying force in the bolts.
3.  **Bolt Strength (End Plate to Column):**
    *   **Tension:** Bolts are primarily subjected to tension due to the applied moment.
    *   **Shear:** Bolts also resist shear forces.
    *   **Combined Forces:** Bolts must be checked for combined tension and shear.
    *   **Prying Force:** Highly susceptible to prying force due to the lever arm between the column face and the outer tension bolts.
4.  **Column Flange Strength:** The column flange must be checked for:
    *   **Bearing Resistance:** Against the bolts.
    *   **Web Local Yielding & Crippling:** Due to forces transmitted by the bolts.
    *   **Column Flange Buckling:**

**Design Steps (Simplified - Extended End Plate Moment Connection):**

1.  **Determine Loads:** Factored shear ($V_u$) and moment ($M_u$).
2.  **Select Beam and End Plate Size:** Choose the beam and an appropriate end plate thickness and dimensions. Extended end plates are common for moment resistance.
3.  **Design Beam-to-End Plate Welds:** Ensure welds have adequate strength for shear and moment transfer.
4.  **Design Bolts (End Plate to Column):**
    *   **Force Calculation:** Calculate the tension and shear forces on each bolt due to $M_u$ and $V_u$. Use a rigid connection analysis or elastic distribution method.
    *   **Tension per Bolt:** $T_b = (M_u / \Sigma y_i^2) \times y_i + V_u/n$, where $y_i$ is the distance from the neutral axis of the bolt group to the bolt, and $n$ is the total number of bolts.
    *   **Prying Force:** Calculate prying force on the tension bolts based on the flexibility of the end plate and the bolt locations. This often requires iterative checks or using empirical methods from design codes. IS 800:2007 provides guidance for checking bolt tension, including prying action for end plate connections.
    *   **Bolt Strength Check:** Check design tensile strength ($T_{db}$) and design shear strength ($V_{db}$) of the selected bolts.
    *   **Combined Strength Check:** Ensure the combined shear and tension on bolts is within limits.
5.  **Design End Plate:**
    *   **Bending Check:** Check the bending stress in the end plate at critical sections (usually at the column face) against its yield and ultimate strength. The plate acts as a beam supporting the bolt forces.
    *   **Shear Check:** Check shear capacity of the end plate.
6.  **Design Column Flange Resistance:** Check the column flange for local yielding, web crippling, and flange yielding/buckling due to the bolt forces.
7.  **Consider Stiffeners:** If prying forces are high or moment is large, stiffeners between the beam web and the end plate can be added to increase stiffness and reduce prying.

**Example Scenario:**

A beam subjected to a significant moment might be connected to a column using an extended end plate. This connection aims to achieve a rigid or semi-rigid behavior. The end plate, welded to the beam, is then bolted to the column, with the bolts carrying significant tension and shear.

**Important Points to Remember (End Plate Connections):**

*   Used for moment transfer, achieving rigidity.
*   End plate thickness and weld design are crucial.
*   Bolts are critical for tension and shear.
*   Prying force is a significant concern.
*   Column flange capacity must be checked.

### 9. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**

*   **Bearing Bolts:** Transfer load through bearing pressure between the bolt shank and the edge of the hole in the connected members, and friction between the connected surfaces. Slip is generally allowed.
*   **High-Strength Friction Grip (HSFG) Bolts:** Transfer load primarily through the high clamping force generated by pre-tensioning, creating friction between the connected members. Slip is generally avoided.
*   **Preference for HSFG Bolts:** HSFG bolts are preferred when slip must be prevented, such as in connections subjected to fatigue loading, connections in seismic zones, or when precise alignment is critical. They offer higher slip resistance and better fatigue performance.

**Answer 2:**

*   **Prying Force:** Prying force is an additional tensile force induced in fasteners (bolts) due to the flexibility of the connected plates or members when subjected to an external tensile load. It arises from the bending of the connected elements.
*   **Factors Influencing Prying Force:**
    *   Stiffness of connected plates/members (thicker/stiffer plates reduce prying).
    *   Edge distance (larger edge distances increase lever arm).
    *   Bolt location (bolts further from the load application point are more affected).
    *   Magnitude and type of applied load.
    *   Clearance in holes.
*   **Illustration:**
    Imagine a beam flange connected to a column by a cleat. If an upward tension is applied to the beam's flange, the cleat might bend. This bending can cause the outer bolt in the cleat-to-column connection to carry a load greater than the average tension, due to the lever arm created by the bending.

    ```
    Column Face
       |
       |  Bolt 1  Bolt 2  Bolt 3  Bolt 4
       +--------+--------+--------+--------+
       |        |        |        |        |
       | <----->| <----->| <----->| <----->|
       |  Dist. |  Dist. |  Dist. |  Dist. |
       |  'e'   |        |        |  'b'   |
       +--------+--------+--------+--------+
             Beam Web/Flange
    ```
    If the cleat bends outwards due to tension, Bolt 4 will experience a higher tension than Bolt 1.

**Answer 3:**

*   **Load:** $V_u = 120$ kN.
*   **Bolt:** M16, 4.6 grade.
*   **Design Shear Strength ($V_{dsb}$) of M16, 4.6 bolt:**
    *   $f_{yb} = 0.4 \times 400 = 160$ N/mm².
    *   $A_{nb}$ (Nominal area) = $\pi/4 \times 16^2 = 201.06$ mm².
    *   $\gamma_{mb} = 1.25$.
    *   $V_{dsb} = (f_{yb} \times A_{nb}) / (\sqrt{3} \times \gamma_{mb}) = (160 \times 201.06) / (\sqrt{3} \times 1.25) = 14806$ N ≈ 14.81 kN.
*   **Number of Bolts Required:**
    *   Total shear resistance required = 120 kN.
    *   Shear resistance per bolt = 14.81 kN.
    *   Number of bolts = Total Shear Load / Shear Resistance per Bolt
    *   Number of bolts = 120 kN / 14.81 kN ≈ 8.1.
*   **Conclusion:** You would need **9 bolts** (rounding up to the nearest whole number) to safely carry the shear load. These bolts would be connecting the seat angle to the column.

**Answer 4:**

*   **Primary Advantage of Stiffeners:** The primary advantage of using stiffeners in a seat angle connection is to **increase the connection's stiffness and its capacity to transfer bending moments**. They also significantly **reduce the magnitude of prying forces** acting on the main bolts that connect the seat angle to the column.
*   **Role of Stiffeners in Reducing Prying Forces:** Stiffeners provide a more rigid connection between the beam web and the outstanding leg of the seat angle. This reduces the bending or deformation of the outstanding leg, thereby reducing the lever arm and the resulting prying force on the main supporting bolts. They effectively stiffen the cantilever action of the seat angle's outstanding leg.

**Answer 5:**

*   **Key Components:**
    *   **Beam:** The member being supported and whose end is being connected.
    *   **End Plate:** A steel plate, usually thicker than the beam flanges and webs, welded to the end of the beam. It extends beyond the beam's profile (extended end plate) to provide lever arms for moment transfer.
    *   **Bolts:** High-strength bolts used to connect the end plate to the column flange. These bolts are typically arranged in a pattern to resist both tension and shear.
    *   **Welds:** Fillet welds or full penetration groove welds connecting the beam flanges and web to the end plate.
    *   **Column Flange:** The face of the column to which the end plate is bolted.
    *   **Stiffeners (Optional but common):** Angles or plates connecting the beam web to the end plate to increase stiffness and reduce prying force.

*   **Critical Design Considerations:**
    1.  **Beam-to-End Plate Welds:** The welds must be designed to transfer the full moment and shear from the beam to the end plate. This often involves using high-strength welds.
    2.  **Bolt Tension and Shear:** Calculate the tensile and shear forces on each bolt in the end plate-to-column connection due to the applied moment and shear. This requires analyzing the bolt group as resisting a moment.
    3.  **Prying Force:** Extended end plates are prone to significant prying forces in the tension bolts. These forces must be calculated and checked against the design tensile strength of the bolts. IS 800:2007 provides methods to account for prying action.
    4.  **Bolt Design:** Select bolts (usually high strength, e.g., 8.8 or 10.9) that can safely resist the calculated tension, shear, and combined forces, including prying.
    5.  **End Plate Bending:** The end plate itself acts as a cantilever and must be designed to resist bending stresses at critical sections (typically at the column face). Its thickness is crucial.
    6.  **Column Flange Resistance:** The column flange must be checked for:
        *   **Local Flange Yielding:** Due to bearing pressure from the bolts.
        *   **Web Local Yielding:** Due to forces transmitted through the flange.
        *   **Web Crippling:** Due to concentrated loads.
        *   **Flange Buckling:** Particularly important for slender column flanges.
    7.  **Stiffener Design (if used):** If stiffeners are employed, they must be designed to transfer forces effectively between the beam web and the end plate, and their bolting must also be checked.
