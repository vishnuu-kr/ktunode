# Engineering Graphics and Computer Aided Drawing

## Module 1: Introduction - Relevance of Technical Drawing in Engineering

### Topic: BIS Code of Practice for Technical Drawing

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into a crucial aspect of technical drawing, one that ensures clarity, consistency, and universality in engineering communication: the **Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) Codes of Practice for Technical Drawing**. Think of these codes as the common language that engineers worldwide use to "speak" through drawings. Without them, a drawing from one engineer could be completely unintelligible to another, even within the same project!

You've all seen engineering drawings, perhaps in your textbooks or online. They’re not just random lines and shapes; they are precise blueprints that convey detailed information about how to manufacture, assemble, and maintain a product. This precision is paramount. Imagine building a bridge or a complex machine based on ambiguous drawings – the consequences could be disastrous, wouldn't they? This is where the BIS codes step in, establishing standardized rules and guidelines for every element of a technical drawing.

**Why are these standards so important?**

*   **Universality:** They ensure that a drawing produced in Chennai can be understood by an engineer in Delhi, or even in London or Tokyo, provided they follow the same standards. This is vital for global collaboration and trade.
*   **Clarity and Unambiguity:** They eliminate guesswork. Every line type, every dimensioning convention, every symbol has a defined meaning, leaving no room for misinterpretation.
*   **Efficiency:** By providing clear guidelines, they streamline the drawing process, making it faster and more error-free.
*   **Interchangeability:** For manufactured parts, adherence to standards ensures that components made by different manufacturers will fit together correctly.

This topic directly supports our **Course Outcomes (COs)** by laying the foundational understanding of how technical drawings are structured and communicated effectively. While CO1 to CO5 deal with the *application* of drawing principles (projection, orthographic views, pictorial drawings, CAD), understanding the *standards* that govern these applications is critical for producing drawings that are not just technically correct but also universally acceptable and professional. Without knowing *how* to represent something according to the standards, our ability to prepare multiview orthographic projections (CO2) or sketch using CAD tools (CO5) would be incomplete.

Let's explore the key areas covered by BIS codes in technical drawing. Our textbooks, like Bhatt's "Engineering Drawing" and Varghese's "Engineering Graphics," provide excellent coverage of these standards, often referencing the specific BIS codes.

### Key Areas Governed by BIS Codes

BIS has a comprehensive set of codes covering almost every aspect of technical drawing. While there are numerous specific codes, we'll focus on the most fundamental ones that form the backbone of our understanding.

#### 1. Lines and Line Types (Related to CO1, CO2)

The very first thing you learn about technical drawings is about lines – not just any lines, but specific types of lines used for different purposes. BIS standards, like **IS 10714:1983 (currently superseded by IS 10714:2021 for some aspects, but the core principles remain)**, define these line types and their applications.

*   **Continuous Thick Line:** Used for visible outlines and edges. This is the "boundary" of what you can see. Think of the outer silhouette of a machine part.
*   **Continuous Thin Line:** Used for dimension lines, extension lines, projection lines, hatching, and leader lines. These lines guide the eye and provide measurements without being mistaken for an actual edge.
*   **Dotted Thin Line:** Used for hidden outlines and edges. When you're looking at an object, some parts might be hidden from view. These lines show you where those hidden edges are. This is directly relevant to **CO1** (projection of points and lines in different quadrants) and **CO2** (visualizing hidden features in orthographic projections). Imagine looking at a cuboid from the front – you see three faces. The edges of the back faces are hidden and would be represented by dotted thin lines.
*   **Chain Thick-Thin Line:** Used for center lines, lines of symmetry, and pitch lines of screw threads. These lines indicate axes of rotation or lines around which an object is symmetrical.
*   **Chain Thin-Short Dash Line:** Used for limiting lines of interchangeable parts and positions.
*   **Dashed Thin Line:** Used for short intermittent visible outlines.

**Why is this important for exams?** You'll often be asked to identify line types or use the correct line type when drawing. Misusing a dotted line for a visible edge, for instance, is a common error and will cost you marks.

#### 2. Lettering (Related to CO5)

The ability to add clear, legible text to a drawing is essential for dimensions, notes, and titles. BIS standards, such as **IS 14994:1999 (related to ISO 3098-1)**, specify guidelines for lettering.

*   **Height and Style:** Standards define recommended letter heights and styles to ensure readability. Generally, capital letters are preferred.
*   **Clarity:** The aim is to make all text on the drawing easy to read, even when reproduced at different scales.

When you're sketching in CAD (**CO5**), using the correct font styles and sizes according to standards ensures your drawings maintain a professional and understandable appearance.

#### 3. Dimensioning (Related to CO2, CO3)

Dimensioning is arguably the most critical aspect of a technical drawing, as it dictates the exact size and location of features. BIS codes, often aligned with **ISO standards (like ISO 129 series)**, provide a comprehensive framework.

*   **Elements of Dimensioning:**
    *   **Dimension Line:** Shows the extent of the dimension. It's a continuous thin line with arrowheads or other terminal marks at the ends.
    *   **Extension Line:** Lines that project from the feature to show the extent of the dimension. They are continuous thin lines, usually extending slightly beyond the dimension line.
    *   **Leader Line:** A line drawn from a note or dimension to a feature, ending with an arrowhead or dot.
    *   **Arrowheads/Terminal Marks:** These indicate the ends of the dimension line and touch the extension lines. BIS codes specify their shape and proportions.
    *   **Dimension Value:** The numerical value indicating the size. It's placed above the dimension line or in a break within it.

*   **Methods of Dimensioning:**
    *   **Chained Dimensioning:** Dimensions are placed in a series along a straight line. This is efficient but can lead to accumulation of errors if not carefully managed.
    *   **Baseline Dimensioning:** All dimensions originate from a common reference point or datum. This is highly recommended for accuracy and easy manufacturing. Imagine measuring all parts of a component from a single, precisely machined surface – that’s baseline dimensioning in action.
    *   **Coordinate Dimensioning:** Used in CAD, where features are located by their X, Y (and Z) coordinates relative to a datum.

*   **General Rules:**
    *   Avoid redundant dimensions.
    *   Dimensions should be placed outside the object whenever possible.
    *   Extension lines should not cross other extension lines or visible outlines if it can be avoided.
    *   Maintain uniform spacing.

Proper dimensioning is directly linked to our ability to create accurate orthographic projections (**CO2**) and understand the sizes required for sectional views or development of surfaces (**CO3**). If you dimension a part incorrectly, the resulting sectional view or development might not be accurate.

#### 4. Scales (Related to CO2, CO4, CO5)

To represent objects that are too large or too small to be drawn at their actual size, we use scales. BIS standards, like **IS 962:1993 (related to ISO 1047),** define standard scales.

*   **Full Size (1:1):** When the drawing represents the object at its actual size.
*   **Enlarged Scales (e.g., 2:1, 5:1, 10:1):** Used when the object is small, like a microchip or a watch component. The drawing is larger than the object.
*   **Reduced Scales (e.g., 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, 1:100, 1:200, 1:500, 1:1000):** Used when the object is large, like a building plan, a machine bed, or a map. The drawing is smaller than the object.

The scale must always be clearly indicated on the drawing, typically in the title block. When you're creating isometric drawings (**CO4**) or using CAD software (**CO5**), understanding and applying the correct scale is crucial for accurate representation. Imagine trying to draw the floor plan of a building using a scale of 1:100 – each meter on the actual building becomes 1 centimeter on your paper.

#### 5. Sheet Sizes and Layout (Related to CO5)

The format of the drawing itself is standardized. BIS codes, aligning with **ISO 216**, define standard paper sizes (A0, A1, A2, A3, A4, etc.) and how the drawing should be laid out on these sheets.

*   **Title Block:** A standardized rectangular area, usually at the bottom right corner, containing essential information such as the drawing title, drawing number, scale, date, designer’s name, checker’s name, and approval signatures.
*   **Border Lines:** A border around the drawing area, with specific line types and widths.
*   **Zones:** Similar to a map, the drawing area can be divided into zones using grid lines to facilitate referencing specific parts of a large drawing.

When you are working with CAD (**CO5**), setting up your drawing sheet with the correct border and title block is a fundamental step.

#### 6. Projection Methods (Related to CO1, CO2)

While not exclusively a BIS code, the standards underpin how projection methods are represented. BIS standards primarily follow the **First Angle Projection** and **Third Angle Projection** methods, consistent with international practices.

*   **First Angle Projection:** The object is placed in the first quadrant. The views are projected onto planes behind the object. The front view is at the top, and the plan (top view) is below it. The side view (usually left) is to the right of the front view.
*   **Third Angle Projection:** The object is placed in the third quadrant. The views are projected onto planes in front of the object. The front view is at the bottom, and the plan (top view) is above it. The side view (usually right) is to the left of the front view.

The projection method used must be clearly indicated on the drawing, usually with a standard symbol in the title block. Understanding these methods is core to **CO1** and **CO2**. The choice of projection method affects where your views are placed on the drawing sheet.

#### 7. Representation of Different Engineering Features (Related to CO3, CO4)

BIS codes also provide guidance on how specific engineering elements should be represented:

*   **Threads:** Various types of screw threads (metric, unified, etc.) have standardized graphical representations, including their profiles and crests/roots.
*   **Welds:** Symbols for different types of welds (fillet, butt, etc.) and their specifications are standardized.
*   **Tolerances and Surface Finish:** While often detailed in separate standards, the graphical representation of these on drawings is guided by general drafting principles.
*   **Sectioning:** BIS codes dictate how section lines should be drawn and how different materials are represented by hatching patterns in section views (**CO3**).

### Connecting BIS Codes to Your Learning Journey

Remember, understanding BIS codes is not just about memorizing numbers. It's about understanding the *why* behind the rules.

*   For **CO1 (Projection of Points and Lines)** and **CO2 (Multiview Orthographic Projections)**: The line types (dotted for hidden), projection methods (first/third angle), and dimensioning rules are all dictated by BIS standards. Without them, your projections would be inconsistent.
*   For **CO3 (Sectional Views and Development of Surfaces)**: The hatching patterns for different materials in sections, dimensioning that defines the shapes to be developed, and the lines used to show fold lines are all subject to standardization.
*   For **CO4 (Pictorial Drawings - Isometric)**: While isometric drawing itself is a projection method, the standards for dimensioning and adding notes to these pictorial views are still crucial. The scale used for isometric drawings also adheres to BIS.
*   For **CO5 (Sketching Simple Drawing using CAD)**: Everything we've discussed – lines, lettering, dimensioning, scales, sheet layout – is implemented directly within CAD software. Following BIS standards ensures your CAD output is professional and compliant.

Let's take an example from Bhatt or Varghese. When you are asked to draw the orthographic views of a simple object, the dimensions you add must follow the dimensioning rules: continuous thin lines for extension and dimension lines, arrowheads of specific proportions, and the dimension value clearly readable. If you use dotted lines for visible edges, it's incorrect. If you clutter the drawing with overlapping dimension lines, it's poor practice according to the standards.

Think of it like learning a language. You need to know the alphabet (lines), the grammar (dimensioning rules), and the vocabulary (symbols) to construct meaningful sentences (drawings). BIS codes provide this essential framework.

### Practical Advice for Exams

*   **Know Your Line Types:** Be able to draw and identify all the common line types and their specific uses. This is a foundational concept.
*   **Dimensioning Rules:** Understand how to dimension clearly and unambiguously. Pay attention to arrowheads, extension lines, and the placement of dimension values.
*   **Projection Symbols:** Be familiar with the symbols used to indicate First Angle or Third Angle projection.
*   **Scale Indication:** Always remember to write the scale on your drawing.
*   **Title Block:** While you might not draw a complete title block from scratch in every exam, understanding its components is important for context.

In essence, BIS codes are the unwritten (but written in standards documents!) rules of the road for technical drawing. They ensure that the visual language of engineering is clear, accurate, and universally understood. As we progress through this course, always keep these standards in mind – they are the bedrock upon which all your drawing skills are built.

### Sample Questions with Answers

**Q1. What is the primary purpose of adhering to BIS codes of practice in technical drawing?**

**Answer:** The primary purpose is to ensure **universality, clarity, and unambiguity** in engineering drawings. This means that a drawing produced according to BIS standards can be understood by any engineer, regardless of their location or background, facilitating effective communication, collaboration, and accurate manufacturing.

**Reasoning:** This question tests the fundamental understanding of why standards are necessary. The answer highlights the key benefits of standardization in engineering communication.

**Q2. If you are drawing an object with internal holes that cannot be seen from the outside in a particular view, which type of line, according to BIS standards, should you use to represent these hidden edges?**

**Answer:** You should use a **dotted thin line** to represent hidden outlines and edges.

**Reasoning:** This question directly assesses knowledge of line types and their application in orthographic projection, relating to CO1 and CO2. The dotted thin line is the standard for representing features not visible in a particular view.

**Q3. Briefly explain the difference between chained dimensioning and baseline dimensioning, and state which is generally preferred for accuracy in manufacturing.**

**Answer:**
*   **Chained Dimensioning:** Dimensions are placed consecutively along a single line or feature. For example, if measuring a length of 100mm, you might dimension 30mm, then 40mm, then 30mm consecutively.
*   **Baseline Dimensioning:** All dimensions are referenced from a single common datum or reference point. For example, from a corner of the object, you would dimension the locations of all features along that axis (e.g., 0, 30, 70, 100).

**Baseline dimensioning** is generally preferred for accuracy in manufacturing because it minimizes the accumulation of errors from multiple chain dimensions and ensures that features are located precisely relative to a fixed reference.

**Reasoning:** This question tests understanding of dimensioning techniques, a core component of BIS standards related to CO2 and CO3. It also requires the student to evaluate the practical implications of these techniques for manufacturing accuracy.

**Q4. You are designing a new gear mechanism. The overall size of the mechanism is quite large. Which type of scale, according to BIS standards, would you most likely use for its technical drawing? Give an example of such a scale.**

**Answer:** You would use a **reduced scale**. An example of such a scale would be **1:5** or **1:10**.

**Reasoning:** This question links the concept of scales (related to CO4 and CO5) to a practical engineering scenario. It requires the student to understand when to use reduced scales (for large objects) and to provide a valid example.

**Q5. What is the significance of the title block in a technical drawing, as per BIS guidelines?**

**Answer:** The title block is a standardized area on a technical drawing that contains essential metadata about the drawing. Its significance lies in providing crucial information for identification, traceability, and context, such as the drawing title, drawing number, scale, date of creation, designer's name, material specifications, and revision history. This ensures that the drawing can be correctly filed, referenced, and understood throughout its lifecycle.

**Reasoning:** This question focuses on the layout and essential information conveyed by the title block, a key element of standardization covered by BIS codes and relevant to the overall professional presentation of drawings, including those made with CAD (CO5).
