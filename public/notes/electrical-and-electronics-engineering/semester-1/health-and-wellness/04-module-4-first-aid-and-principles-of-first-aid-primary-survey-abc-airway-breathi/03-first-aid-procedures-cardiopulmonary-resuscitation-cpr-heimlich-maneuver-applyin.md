# HEALTH AND WELLNESS - Module 4: First Aid and Principles of First Aid

## Introduction to First Aid and the Role of a First Aider

Welcome to Module 4! Today, we're diving into a crucial aspect of health and wellness: **First Aid**. Think of first aid as the immediate, on-the-spot assistance given to someone who has suddenly fallen ill or been injured. It's about providing care until professional medical help arrives or the person recovers. This isn't about performing surgery or complex medical procedures; it's about stabilizing the situation and preventing the injury or illness from worsening. It's the bridge between the moment an emergency occurs and when professional medical care takes over.

This module directly connects with **Course Outcome 4 (CO4): Explain the basics of first aid and describe common sports injuries.** Understanding these procedures is fundamental to being prepared for unexpected health crises.

### The Primary Survey: The ABCs of Emergency Care

When you encounter an emergency, the very first thing you need to do is assess the situation safely. This is where the **Primary Survey** comes in. It's a systematic approach to quickly identify and address life-threatening conditions. The most critical part of the primary survey is remembered by the acronym **ABC**:

*   **A - Airway:** Is the person's airway open and clear?
*   **B - Breathing:** Is the person breathing? If so, how effectively?
*   **C - Circulation:** Are there signs of circulation, such as a pulse? Is there severe bleeding?

Let's break down each of these components. Imagine you're at a park, and someone suddenly collapses. Your first instinct might be to panic, but by following the ABCs, you can remain calm and effective.

#### A: Airway

The **Airway** refers to the passage through which air travels into and out of the lungs. If this passage is blocked, the person cannot breathe. Common causes of airway obstruction include the tongue falling back into the throat (especially in an unconscious person), foreign objects (like food or vomit), or swelling due to an allergic reaction.

**How to check the Airway:**
1.  **Look:** Is there anything visibly blocking the airway?
2.  **Listen:** Can you hear air moving in and out of the nose or mouth?
3.  **Feel:** Can you feel air on your cheek if you place it near their mouth and nose?

If the airway is obstructed, and the person is unconscious, you need to **open the airway**. The most common technique is the **Head-Tilt/Chin-Lift**. This involves gently tilting the head back and lifting the chin upwards. This action moves the tongue away from the back of the throat, clearing the passage for air. *Remember this: The tongue is the most common cause of airway obstruction in an unconscious person.* This relates to **CO4** as maintaining an open airway is the very first step in providing any form of first aid.

#### B: Breathing

Once you've ensured the airway is open, you need to check if the person is **Breathing**.

**How to check Breathing:**
You can use the **"Look, Listen, and Feel"** method for about 10 seconds:
*   **Look:** Observe the rise and fall of their chest.
*   **Listen:** Listen for breathing sounds at their mouth and nose.
*   **Feel:** Feel for air movement on your cheek.

If the person is not breathing or is only gasping irregularly, this is a critical situation. They need immediate assistance, which leads us to the next critical component. This connects to **CO4** as assessing breathing is a fundamental first aid skill.

#### C: Circulation

The final part of the primary survey is **Circulation**. This involves checking for signs of life and the presence of severe bleeding.

**How to check Circulation:**
1.  **Pulse:** Check for a pulse. For adults and children, the carotid artery in the neck is usually the easiest place to feel for a pulse. For infants, the brachial artery in the upper arm is preferred. You should feel for the pulse for at least 5-10 seconds.
2.  **Severe Bleeding:** Look for any obvious, life-threatening bleeding. If there is severe bleeding, it needs to be controlled immediately.

If there is no pulse and no normal breathing, the person is likely in cardiac arrest, and **Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)** is required. Severe bleeding also needs immediate attention to prevent hypovolemic shock, which is a decrease in blood volume that can lead to organ failure. This aspect of checking circulation directly supports **CO4** by addressing critical conditions that might arise.

### Qualities of a Good First Aider

Being a first aider isn't just about knowing the procedures; it's also about possessing certain personal qualities that enable you to act effectively in a crisis. Think about what makes someone a reliable person to have around in an emergency.

*   **Calmness and Composure:** In a high-stress situation, remaining calm is paramount. Panic can hinder clear thinking and action. A good first aider stays focused on what needs to be done.
*   **Confidence:** Having the knowledge and training builds confidence, allowing you to act decisively without hesitation.
*   **Responsibility:** A good first aider takes responsibility for the situation and acts to help.
*   **Resourcefulness:** Being able to improvise and use available resources effectively is a key skill. You might not have all the perfect equipment, but you can make do with what's at hand.
*   **Empathy and Reassurance:** While providing physical care, it's also important to offer emotional support and reassurance to the casualty and any bystanders. A kind word can go a long way.
*   **Respect for Life:** This underpins everything. A good first aider prioritizes saving a life and preventing further harm.
*   **Knowledge and Skill:** Continuously updating your first aid knowledge and practicing your skills ensures you are prepared.

These qualities are crucial for effective application of first aid techniques, aligning with the practical nature of **CO4**.

## First Aid Procedures: Essential Life-Saving Techniques

Now that we've covered the foundational principles, let's delve into some specific, vital first aid procedures. These are techniques that can literally mean the difference between life and death.

### 1. Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

**CPR** is a life-saving technique used when a person's breathing or heartbeat has stopped. This is often the case in cardiac arrest. The goal of CPR is to manually keep oxygenated blood circulating to the brain and other vital organs until professional medical help can restore a normal heart rhythm.

*Remember this: CPR is initiated when a person is unresponsive, not breathing normally, but has no pulse.*

The process for CPR typically involves a combination of chest compressions and rescue breaths. While guidelines can be updated, the core principles remain consistent. (Refer to current local guidelines for the most up-to-date procedures).

**Chest Compressions:**
1.  **Positioning:** Place the person on a firm, flat surface. Kneel beside them.
2.  **Hand Placement:** Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone (sternum). Place the heel of your other hand on top of the first hand. Interlock your fingers and keep them off the chest.
3.  **Body Mechanics:** Position yourself so your shoulders are directly over your hands. Keep your arms straight.
4.  **Depth and Rate:** Push hard and fast. For adults, compress the chest at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep and at a rate of 100-120 compressions per minute. This is about the tempo of the song "Stayin' Alive." The chest should be allowed to recoil fully between compressions.
5.  **Cycles:** Continue compressions in cycles. For example, 30 compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths.

**Rescue Breaths:**
1.  **Open Airway:** After 30 compressions, open the person's airway using the head-tilt/chin-lift maneuver.
2.  **Seal:** Pinch the person's nostrils shut. Take a normal breath and place your mouth completely over their mouth, creating a seal.
3.  **Deliver Breaths:** Give two breaths, each lasting about 1 second, watching for the chest to rise. If the chest doesn't rise, reposition the airway and try again.
4.  **Continue Cycles:** Immediately resume chest compressions.

**Important Considerations for CPR:**
*   **AED (Automated External Defibrillator):** If an AED is available, use it as soon as possible. It can analyze the heart rhythm and deliver an electrical shock if needed. Follow the voice prompts from the AED.
*   **Compression-Only CPR:** If you are unwilling or unable to give rescue breaths, you can perform "Hands-Only CPR" by continuously performing chest compressions. This is still highly effective, especially for cardiac arrest caused by heart problems.
*   **Children and Infants:** CPR techniques differ slightly for children and infants in terms of compression depth, hand placement, and breath volume. Always get specific training for these age groups.

CPR is a direct application of the ABCs, addressing a lack of breathing and circulation. It's a critical skill that aligns with **CO4** and, by extension, **CO2** (maintaining health and wellness through emergency preparedness).

### 2. The Heimlich Maneuver (Abdominal Thrusts)

The **Heimlich Maneuver**, also known as abdominal thrusts, is a first aid procedure used to treat **choking**. Choking occurs when a person's airway is blocked by food or another object, preventing them from breathing. This is a common scenario that anyone can encounter.

*Analogy:* Imagine a traffic jam in your airway. The Heimlich maneuver is like an emergency detour to clear that blockage.

**When to Perform the Heimlich Maneuver:**
You should perform the Heimlich maneuver if a person is:
*   Consciously choking.
*   Unable to cough, speak, or breathe.
*   Clutching their throat (the universal sign for choking).
*   Making high-pitched noises or unable to make any sound.
*   Their skin may be turning blue.

**How to Perform the Heimlich Maneuver on an Adult or Child (over 1 year old):**
1.  **Assess the Situation:** Ask, "Are you choking?" If they nod or cannot respond, proceed.
2.  **Position Yourself:** Stand behind the choking person. Wrap your arms around their waist.
3.  **Hand Placement:** Make a fist with one hand. Place the thumb side of your fist slightly above the person's navel (belly button) and well below the ribcage.
4.  **Grasp Your Fist:** Grasp your fist with your other hand.
5.  **Thrust:** Give quick, upward thrusts into the abdomen. Each thrust should be forceful and distinct, aiming to dislodge the object.
6.  **Repeat:** Continue thrusts until the object is expelled, or the person starts to breathe or cough effectively.

**What if the Person Becomes Unconscious?**
If the choking person becomes unconscious, carefully lower them to the ground and begin CPR. After each set of compressions, look for the object in the mouth before giving rescue breaths. If you see the object, try to remove it.

**Heimlich Maneuver on Infants (under 1 year old):**
This is different and crucial to know.
1.  **Position:** Sit down and hold the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting their head with your hand. Their head should be lower than their chest.
2.  **Back Blows:** Give up to 5 sharp blows between the infant's shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
3.  **Chest Thrusts:** If back blows don't work, turn the infant over so they are face-up on your forearm, supporting their head. Place two fingers in the center of their chest, just below the nipple line. Give up to 5 quick chest thrusts.
4.  **Repeat:** Alternate between back blows and chest thrusts until the object is expelled or the infant becomes unresponsive. If unresponsive, begin infant CPR.

The Heimlich maneuver is a vital intervention for a blocked airway, directly impacting **CO4** by addressing a common life-threatening emergency.

### 3. Applying a Sling

A **sling** is a bandage or material used to support an injured limb, such as an arm or collarbone fracture, or a dislocated shoulder. Its primary purpose is to immobilize the injured part, reduce pain, prevent further injury, and control swelling.

*Relatable Scenario:* Imagine playing a sport like basketball or football, and a player falls awkwardly and suspects a broken arm or a dislocated shoulder. Applying a sling is a quick way to provide immediate support and comfort.

**Types of Slings and How to Apply Them:**

While commercial slings are available, you can often improvise using a triangular bandage or even a piece of cloth.

#### Applying a Triangular Bandage Sling (Standard Sling)

This is the most common type of sling and is used to support the arm and hand.

**When to Use:**
*   Fractures of the arm, forearm, or wrist.
*   Dislocated shoulder.
*   Severe sprains of the arm or elbow.

**How to Apply:**
1.  **Position the Arm:** Gently support the injured arm. If it's a fracture, try to support it in the position you found it.
2.  **Place the Bandage:** Unfold the triangular bandage. Place the apex (point) of the bandage underneath the injured arm, close to the armpit.
3.  **Wrap Around the Shoulder:** Bring the two ends of the base of the bandage up and over the uninjured shoulder.
4.  **Cross and Secure:** Cross the ends of the bandage at the back of the neck or over the opposite shoulder. Tie them securely, ensuring it's not too tight to restrict breathing or circulation.
5.  **Secure the Hand:** Bring the apex of the bandage up towards the elbow and tuck it in or tie it securely to the main bandage. This supports the hand and wrist and prevents them from drooping.
6.  **Check for Comfort and Stability:** Ensure the injured arm is supported, the sling is snug but not constricting, and there is no severe pain. You should be able to get at least two fingers between the sling and the person's neck and wrist. This ensures good circulation, a key aspect of the 'C' in ABC.

#### Creating an Improvised Sling

If you don't have a triangular bandage:
*   **Use a Shirt:** A shirt can be used. Take the sleeve of a shirt and pass the injured arm through it. Tie the ends of the sleeve securely behind the neck or around the body, depending on the injury and available material.
*   **Use a Scarf or Piece of Cloth:** Any long, sturdy piece of fabric can be used similarly to a triangular bandage.

**Important Considerations when Applying a Sling:**
*   **Check Circulation:** After applying any sling, always check for signs of circulation in the fingers of the injured limb. This includes checking for warmth, good color, and sensation. If the fingers are cold, blue, or numb, the sling is too tight and needs to be loosened immediately. This directly relates back to monitoring **Circulation (C)**.
*   **Support the Hand:** The hand should be supported at a height that is comfortable for the casualty, usually slightly higher than the elbow, to help reduce swelling.
*   **Comfort:** The sling should be comfortable and not cause additional pain.
*   **Seek Medical Attention:** A sling is a temporary measure. Always advise the person to seek professional medical attention for proper diagnosis and treatment of the injury.

Applying a sling is a practical skill that directly relates to **CO4** by managing common injuries, particularly those seen in sports contexts, and it reinforces the importance of ensuring proper **Circulation**.

## Sample Questions with Answers

Here are some questions to test your understanding of this module:

**Question 1 (Conceptual):** Explain the significance of the "Airway" check in the primary survey. Why is it considered the first priority?

**Answer:** The "Airway" check is the first priority in the primary survey because without an open airway, a person cannot breathe, and oxygen cannot reach the brain and other vital organs. If the airway is blocked, even if breathing and circulation systems are otherwise functional, the person will quickly succumb to asphyxiation. Therefore, ensuring an open airway, often through the head-tilt/chin-lift maneuver, is the absolute first step to sustain life. This directly addresses the 'A' in ABC.

**Question 2 (Exam-Oriented):** A colleague collapses at their desk. You approach them, check for responsiveness, and find they are unconscious but breathing normally. What is your next immediate action?

**a) Start CPR immediately.**
**b) Check for severe bleeding.**
**c) Place them in the recovery position.**
**d) Call for emergency medical services.**

**Answer:** **d) Call for emergency medical services.**
**Reasoning:** The scenario states the person is unconscious but breathing normally. This means their airway and breathing are currently functioning. While you would eventually want to position them appropriately and monitor them, the most critical immediate action in an unconscious but breathing casualty is to summon professional medical help. They may have an underlying condition that requires immediate advanced care, and getting emergency services en route is paramount. If they stop breathing while waiting, then CPR would commence. This question tests the understanding of immediate priorities in a primary survey.

**Question 3 (Procedural):** Describe the correct hand placement for performing chest compressions on an adult during CPR.

**Answer:** For adult CPR, place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone (sternum). Place the heel of your other hand on top of the first hand. Interlock your fingers and keep them off the chest. Your shoulders should be directly over your hands, and your arms should be kept straight. This technique ensures effective compression depth and force on the sternum.

**Question 4 (Application):** You are assisting a friend who has fallen and is complaining of severe pain in their arm, which appears to be bent at an unnatural angle. What is the primary purpose of applying a sling in this situation, and what is one crucial check you must perform after applying it?

**Answer:** The primary purpose of applying a sling is to immobilize the injured arm, which helps to reduce pain, prevent further damage to the surrounding tissues (like nerves and blood vessels), and minimize swelling. It also provides support and comfort. A crucial check after applying the sling is to ensure good **circulation** in the fingers of the injured hand. This involves checking that the fingers are warm, have a normal color (not blue or pale), and have sensation. If circulation is compromised (fingers are cold, blue, or numb), the sling is too tight and must be loosened immediately. This reinforces the importance of the 'C' in ABC.

**Question 5 (Conceptual & Link to CO):** How does the Heimlich maneuver relate to the "Airway" component of the primary survey and to the course outcome CO4?

**Answer:** The Heimlich maneuver directly addresses the "Airway" component of the primary survey. It is used when the airway is blocked by an obstruction, preventing air from entering the lungs. By performing abdominal thrusts, the goal is to create a forceful expulsion of air from the lungs, which can dislodge the obstruction and clear the airway, allowing the person to breathe again. This relates to **CO4** because choking is a common emergency scenario, and the Heimlich maneuver is a fundamental first aid skill for managing such situations, especially in contexts where sports or eating might lead to a blockage.
