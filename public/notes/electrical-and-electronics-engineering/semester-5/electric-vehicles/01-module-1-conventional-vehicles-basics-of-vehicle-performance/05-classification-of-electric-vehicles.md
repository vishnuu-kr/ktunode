### 1. Introduction to Electric Vehicles (EVs)

Electric vehicles are vehicles that use one or more electric motors for propulsion. They differ significantly from conventional internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles in their energy source, powertrain, and operational characteristics.

**Key Concept:** The core differentiator of an EV is its reliance on electricity as the primary energy source for propulsion, typically stored in batteries.

---

### 2. Historical Context and Evolution

EVs are not a new invention. Early automobiles in the late 19th and early 20th centuries included electric variants, which were popular for their quiet operation and ease of use compared to early gasoline cars. However, the limitations in battery technology and the rise of the internal combustion engine led to their decline. The recent resurgence of EVs is driven by advancements in battery technology, environmental concerns, and government regulations.

**Reference:** Chapter 1 of "Electric Vehicles Machines and Drives- Design, Analysis and Application" by K. T. Chau provides a good overview of the historical development of EVs.

### 3. Classification of Electric Vehicles

Electric vehicles can be classified based on various criteria, primarily related to their powertrain architecture and the source of their onboard electrical energy.

#### 3.1. Classification based on Electric Power Source

This is the most common and fundamental classification.

*   **Battery Electric Vehicles (BEVs):**
    *   **Description:** BEVs are powered solely by electricity stored in an onboard battery pack. They have no internal combustion engine and produce zero tailpipe emissions.
    *   **Key Components:** Electric motor, battery pack, power electronics (inverter, converter), onboard charger.
    *   **Charging:** Recharged by plugging into an external electricity source (e.g., home charging, public charging stations).
    *   **Examples:** Tesla Model 3, Nissan Leaf, Chevrolet Bolt EV.
    *   **CO1 Alignment:** Understanding BEVs is essential for comparing their performance to conventional vehicles.

*   **Fuel Cell Electric Vehicles (FCEVs):**
    *   **Description:** FCEVs use hydrogen as fuel. A fuel cell converts hydrogen and oxygen into electricity, which then powers the electric motor. A small battery pack or supercapacitor may be used to store regenerative braking energy and provide peak power.
    *   **Key Components:** Fuel cell stack, hydrogen storage tank, electric motor, power electronics, battery/supercapacitor.
    *   **How they work:** Hydrogen is fed into the fuel cell, where it reacts with oxygen (from the air) to produce electricity, water, and heat.
    *   **Advantages:** Zero tailpipe emissions (only water), longer range and faster refueling than BEVs (similar to gasoline cars).
    *   **Disadvantages:** Infrastructure for hydrogen production and refueling is limited, higher cost of fuel cells.
    *   **Examples:** Toyota Mirai, Hyundai Nexo.
    *   **CO1 Alignment:** FCEVs represent an alternative electrified powertrain, distinct from BEVs and ICEs, allowing for performance comparison.

*   **Hybrid Electric Vehicles (HEVs):**
    *   **Description:** HEVs combine an internal combustion engine (ICE) with an electric motor and a battery pack. The electric motor can assist the ICE, power the vehicle alone for short distances, or act as a generator to charge the battery.
    *   **Key Concept:** HEVs aim to improve fuel efficiency and reduce emissions by leveraging electric propulsion, but they still rely on gasoline or diesel.
    *   **Sub-classification of HEVs:**

        *   **Series Hybrid Electric Vehicles (SHEVs):**
            *   **Description:** The ICE is used solely as a generator to produce electricity, which charges the battery or directly powers the electric motor. The ICE is *not* mechanically connected to the wheels.
            *   **How they work:** ICE -> Generator -> Battery/Electric Motor -> Wheels.
            *   **Advantages:** ICE can operate at its most efficient speed, simpler powertrain.
            *   **Disadvantages:** Lower overall efficiency due to double energy conversion (ICE to electricity, then electricity to mechanical power).
            *   **Example:** BMW i3 (Range Extender version).
            *   **CO2 Alignment:** Understanding SHEVs is crucial for CO2 as it involves powertrain analysis.

        *   **Parallel Hybrid Electric Vehicles (PHEVs):**
            *   **Description:** Both the ICE and the electric motor can directly drive the wheels, either independently or together.
            *   **How they work:** ICE -> Transmission -> Wheels AND/OR Electric Motor -> Transmission -> Wheels.
            *   **Advantages:** Higher overall efficiency, more responsive performance.
            *   **Disadvantages:** More complex powertrain.
            *   **Example:** Toyota Prius (most common configuration), Honda Insight.
            *   **CO2 Alignment:** PHEVs involve direct comparison of ICE and electric propulsion for drivetrain analysis.

        *   **Series-Parallel Hybrid Electric Vehicles (SPHEVs) / Power-Split Hybrids:**
            *   **Description:** Combine features of both series and parallel hybrids. They use a power-split device (e.g., planetary gear set) to blend power from the ICE and electric motor in various combinations.
            *   **How they work:** The power-split device allows for flexible distribution of power, enabling pure electric, pure ICE, or combined operation.
            *   **Advantages:** High efficiency, smooth operation, versatility.
            *   **Disadvantages:** Highly complex powertrain.
            *   **Example:** Toyota Prius (modern generations), Ford Escape Hybrid.
            *   **Reference:** "Propulsion Systems for Hybrid Vehicles" by John M. Miller extensively covers HEV topologies.

*   **Plug-in Hybrid Electric Vehicles (PHEVs):**
    *   **Description:** PHEVs are a type of hybrid vehicle that can be charged from an external electricity source, allowing them to travel a significant distance on electric power alone before the ICE needs to engage. They typically have a larger battery pack than conventional HEVs.
    *   **Key Concept:** PHEVs offer the flexibility of electric-only driving for shorter trips and the extended range of an ICE for longer journeys.
    *   **How they work:** Can operate in pure electric mode, hybrid mode (ICE + electric motor), or ICE-only mode.
    *   **Examples:** Chevrolet Volt, Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV, Ford Fusion Energi.
    *   **CO1 Alignment:** PHEVs represent a transitional technology, important for comparing to both conventional and pure EVs.
    *   **Reference:** "Hybrid Electric Vehicles – Principles and applications with practical perspectives" by Chris Mi, M A Masrur, D W Gao provides a detailed look at PHEV operation.

#### 3.2. Classification based on the Electric Motor Type (Often discussed in later modules, but relevant for understanding EV types)

While not the primary classification, the type of electric motor used significantly impacts EV performance. Common types include:

*   **DC Motors (Brushed and Brushless):** Historically used, but less common in modern high-performance EVs.
*   **AC Motors:**
    *   **Induction Motors (ACIM):** Robust, cost-effective, and widely used.
    *   **Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSM):** High efficiency, high power density, often used in premium EVs.
    *   **Synchronous Reluctance Motors (SynRM):** Emerging technology, good efficiency, no rare-earth magnets.

**Reference:** "Permanent Magnet Synchronous and Brushless DC Motors Drives" by R. Krishnan is a key resource for understanding motor types.

#### 3.3. Classification based on the Degree of Electrification

This classification focuses on the extent to which electric power contributes to the vehicle's propulsion.

*   **Micro Hybrids:**
    *   **Description:** Primarily conventional ICE vehicles with basic start-stop systems and some energy regeneration capabilities. Minimal electrical assistance.
    *   **Features:** Engine start-stop, regenerative braking (often just to recharge the battery).
    *   **Examples:** Many modern ICE vehicles with start-stop functionality.

*   **Mild Hybrids (MHEVs):**
    *   **Description:** Feature a larger battery and a more powerful electric motor/generator than micro hybrids. The electric motor can assist the ICE, but cannot propel the vehicle solely on electric power for any significant distance.
    *   **Features:** Engine start-stop, regenerative braking, electric assist during acceleration, electric-only coasting (sometimes).
    *   **Examples:** Many 48V mild-hybrid systems found in various manufacturers' lineups.

*   **Full Hybrids (HEVs):**
    *   **Description:** Can drive on electric power alone for short distances at lower speeds. The ICE and electric motor can work together.
    *   **Features:** Electric-only driving, ICE-assisted driving, regenerative braking.
    *   **Examples:** Toyota Prius.

*   **Plug-in Hybrids (PHEVs):**
    *   **Description:** Larger battery pack, can be plugged in to charge, and can travel a significant range on electric power alone.
    *   **Features:** Extended electric-only range, ICE backup, charging from external source.
    *   **Examples:** Chevrolet Volt.

*   **Battery Electric Vehicles (BEVs):**
    *   **Description:** Fully electric, powered solely by a battery.
    *   **Features:** Zero tailpipe emissions, electric-only driving.
    *   **Examples:** Tesla Model 3.

*   **Fuel Cell Electric Vehicles (FCEVs):**
    *   **Description:** Electric vehicles powered by a fuel cell using hydrogen.
    *   **Features:** Zero tailpipe emissions (water), hydrogen as fuel.
    *   **Examples:** Toyota Mirai.

**Reference:** "Electric and Hybrid Vehicles: Design Fundamentals" by Iqbal Hussein provides comprehensive details on the different levels of hybridization.

### 4. Key Considerations When Classifying EVs

*   **Powertrain Architecture:** How the ICE (if present) and electric motor(s) are integrated and transmit power to the wheels.
*   **Energy Storage:** The type and capacity of the battery or other energy storage devices.
*   **Energy Source:** Whether the electricity is stored onboard (battery) or generated from an external source (fuel cell).
*   **Charging Capability:** Whether the vehicle can be recharged from an external power grid.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   The most fundamental classification of EVs is based on their **primary onboard energy source**.
*   **BEVs** are the purest form of EVs, relying solely on batteries.
*   **HEVs** bridge the gap between conventional and electric vehicles, using both ICEs and electric motors.
*   **PHEVs** offer a longer electric-only driving range compared to standard HEVs by allowing external charging.
*   **FCEVs** represent a hydrogen-based electric propulsion system with zero tailpipe emissions.
*   Understanding these classifications is crucial for discussing and comparing vehicle performance and environmental impact.

### 8. Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **BEV:** Powered solely by electricity stored in an onboard battery pack, recharged by plugging into an external source. **FCEV:** Powered by electricity generated from a fuel cell, which converts hydrogen and oxygen into electricity. They refuel with hydrogen.
2.  In a SHEV, the internal combustion engine (ICE) functions only as a generator to produce electricity. This electricity can either charge the battery or directly power the electric motor that drives the wheels. The ICE is not mechanically linked to the drivetrain.
3.  A PHEV is a type of HEV that has a larger battery pack and can be plugged into an external electricity source for charging. This allows it to travel a significant distance on electric power alone before the ICE is required, unlike a conventional HEV which relies solely on regenerative braking and the ICE to charge its smaller battery.
4.  Mild Hybrid Electric Vehicles (MHEVs) offer:
    *   Improved fuel efficiency compared to conventional ICE vehicles.
    *   Reduced emissions.
    *   Enhanced performance through electric torque assist during acceleration.
    *   Quieter operation at low speeds or during start-stop events.
5.  *   **Tesla Model S:** Battery Electric Vehicle (BEV)
    *   **Toyota Prius:** Full Hybrid Electric Vehicle (HEV) - often a Series-Parallel or Power-Split configuration.
    *   **Hyundai Nexo:** Fuel Cell Electric Vehicle (FCEV)
    *   **Chevrolet Volt:** Plug-in Hybrid Electric Vehicle (PHEV)
