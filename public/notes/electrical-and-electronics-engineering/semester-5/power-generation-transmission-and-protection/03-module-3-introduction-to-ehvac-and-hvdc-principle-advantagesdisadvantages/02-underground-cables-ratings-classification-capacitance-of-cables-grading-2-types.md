# Module 3: Introduction to EHVAC and HVDC: Principle, Advantages/Disadvantages

## Topic: Underground Cables – Ratings, Classification, Capacitance, and Grading

This topic delves into the fundamental aspects of underground power cables, which are crucial for modern power transmission and distribution systems, especially in urban environments where overhead lines are impractical or undesirable. We will explore their characteristics, how they are rated, how they are classified, the challenges posed by their capacitance, and methods to mitigate these effects.

### 1. Cable Ratings

Cable ratings define the maximum amount of electrical power a cable can safely transmit under specified operating conditions without exceeding its temperature limits. Exceeding these limits can lead to insulation degradation, reduced lifespan, and potential failure.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Ampacity (Current Rating):** The maximum continuous current a cable can carry without exceeding its rated temperature. This is the most common rating.
*   **Voltage Rating:** The maximum voltage at which the cable can safely operate. This is determined by the insulation strength.
*   **Temperature Rating:** The maximum permissible temperature for the conductor and insulation.
*   **Ambient Temperature:** The temperature of the surrounding medium (soil, air).
*   **Installation Method:** How the cable is installed (e.g., buried directly, in ducts, in air) significantly affects heat dissipation.
*   **Grouping:** Cables installed in groups require derating factors due to mutual heating.

**Factors Affecting Ampacity:**

*   **Conductor Resistance:** Higher resistance leads to more heat generation (I²R loss).
*   **Dielectric Losses:** Losses in the insulation material, especially significant at higher voltages and frequencies.
*   **Heat Dissipation:** How effectively heat can escape from the cable to the surroundings. This is influenced by:
    *   **Ambient temperature:** Higher ambient temperature reduces ampacity.
    *   **Thermal resistivity of the surrounding medium:** Soil resistivity, duct material, etc.
    *   **Installation depth and spacing:** Cables buried deeper or closer together will have reduced ampacity.
    *   **Heat generated by adjacent cables:** Mutual heating effect.

**Reference:**

*   **Wadhwa C. L., Electrical Power Systems, 8th ed., Chapter 7: Underground Cables (Ratings and Characteristics).** Wadhwa provides detailed discussions on ampacity calculations considering various installation conditions and derating factors.
*   **Mehta V. K. & Mehta R., Principles of Power System, 4th ed., Chapter 16: Transmission Line and Distribution System (Cable Ratings).** This textbook also covers the fundamental factors influencing cable ratings.

**Example:**

A cable rated for 1000 A at 30°C ambient temperature might have its ampacity reduced to 800 A if installed in a group of three cables or if the ambient temperature rises to 40°C.

**Important Point to Remember:**

Cable ratings are not static; they are heavily dependent on the installation environment and operating conditions. Always consult relevant standards (e.g., ICEA, NEC) for accurate derating factors.

### 2. Classification of Underground Cables

Underground cables can be classified based on several criteria, allowing for a better understanding of their construction, application, and voltage levels.

**A. Based on Insulation Type:**

This is a primary classification, as the insulation material determines the cable's voltage capability and performance.

*   **Paper Insulated Cables (PIC):**
    *   **Description:** Use paper impregnated with insulating oil as the primary dielectric. Historically significant, especially for low and medium voltages.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Mass-Impregnated Non-Draining (MIND):** Oil is viscous and does not flow readily. Suitable for laying in any position.
        *   **Mass-Impregnated Draining (MID):** Oil is less viscous and tends to drain at higher temperatures or on inclines, leading to voids and potential ionization. Not suitable for inclined runs.
        *   **Oil-Filled (OF):** Use a low-viscosity insulating oil under pressure to prevent voids. Suitable for high voltages and long distances.
            *   *Internal Oil-Filled:* Oil flows within ducts or channels in the insulation.
            *   *External Oil-Filled:* Oil is contained in a pipe or sheath surrounding the cable.
    *   **Pros:** Relatively inexpensive, good dielectric strength.
    *   **Cons:** Susceptible to moisture ingress, limited to lower voltages for reliable long-term operation, potential for oil leakage.
    *   **Reference:** Wadhwa C. L., Chapter 7.

*   **Rubber Insulated Cables:**
    *   **Description:** Use natural or synthetic rubber compounds for insulation.
    *   **Pros:** Flexible, good mechanical properties.
    *   **Cons:** Limited voltage and temperature ratings, susceptible to ozone and oil. Primarily used for low-voltage applications and flexible connections.

*   **Plastic/Polymer Insulated Cables (e.g., PVC, XLPE, EPR):**
    *   **Description:** Use thermoplastic or thermosetting polymers as insulation.
    *   **Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC):**
        *   **Pros:** Inexpensive, good chemical resistance, flame retardant.
        *   **Cons:** Limited temperature rating, brittle at low temperatures, releases toxic fumes when burned. Used for low-voltage power and control cables.
    *   **Cross-Linked Polyethylene (XLPE):**
        *   **Pros:** Excellent dielectric properties, high temperature rating (allowing for higher ampacity), good mechanical strength, resistance to moisture and chemicals. Widely used for medium, high, and extra-high voltages.
        *   **Cons:** More expensive than PVC.
    *   **Ethylene Propylene Rubber (EPR):**
        *   **Pros:** Good flexibility, excellent resistance to moisture, heat, and chemicals, good thermal stability. Often used for medium-voltage applications.
        *   **Cons:** Generally lower dielectric strength than XLPE for very high voltages.
    *   **Reference:** Wadhwa C. L., Chapter 7. Mehta V. K. & Mehta R., Chapter 16.

**B. Based on Voltage Level:**

*   **Low Voltage Cables:** Up to 1.1 kV (e.g., 400 V, 440 V). Typically use PVC or rubber insulation.
*   **Medium Voltage Cables:** 1.1 kV to 33 kV (e.g., 11 kV, 22 kV, 33 kV). Often use XLPE or EPR insulation.
*   **High Voltage Cables:** 33 kV to 132 kV (e.g., 66 kV, 110 kV, 132 kV). Primarily use XLPE or oil-filled paper insulation.
*   **Extra-High Voltage (EHV) Cables:** Above 132 kV (e.g., 220 kV, 400 kV). Almost exclusively use specialized oil-filled paper or advanced polymer insulation systems designed for extremely high dielectric stress.

**C. Based on Number of Conductors:**

*   **Single-Core Cables:** One conductor, typically used for DC or single-phase AC circuits, or for three-phase systems where multiple single-core cables are used per phase (especially in EHV systems to reduce capacitance and inductive effects).
*   **Two-Core Cables:** Two conductors, used for single-phase AC circuits or DC circuits.
*   **Three-Core Cables:** Three conductors, most common for three-phase AC power distribution. Conductors are usually twisted together.
*   **Four-Core Cables:** Three conductors plus a neutral conductor. Used in systems with a neutral requirement.

**D. Based on Construction/Armouring:**

*   **Unarmoured Cables:** Lack metallic armouring. Used where mechanical protection is not a significant concern (e.g., within conduits or trays).
*   **Armoured Cables:** Include a layer of metallic armouring (e.g., steel tape or wires) over the insulation and sheath for mechanical protection against damage (e.g., during excavation).
    *   **SWA (Steel Wire Armour):** Common for medium voltage.
    *   **SSTA (Steel Tape Armour):** Often used for protection against rodents and crushing.

**Reference:**

*   **Wadhwa C. L., Chapter 7.**
*   **Mehta V. K. & Mehta R., Chapter 16.**

**Important Point to Remember:**

The choice of cable type depends on the voltage level, current carrying capacity required, environmental conditions, and cost considerations. XLPE insulated cables have largely replaced paper-insulated cables for most applications due to their superior performance and ease of installation at higher voltages.

### 3. Capacitance of Cables

The capacitance of an underground cable arises from the presence of multiple conductors separated by an insulating dielectric, forming a capacitor. At AC frequencies, this capacitance draws a charging current, which can be significant, especially for long cable runs and at higher voltages.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Dielectric:** The insulating material between conductors.
*   **Charging Current (Ic):** The current drawn by the cable capacitance to charge it. It leads the voltage by 90°.
*   **Capacitance per unit length (C):** The ability of the cable to store charge per unit length.
*   **Total Capacitance (C_total):** The total capacitance of the cable, calculated as capacitance per unit length multiplied by the cable length.
*   **Dielectric Loss:** Energy dissipated in the dielectric material due to hysteresis and polarization effects, proportional to the square of the voltage and frequency.

**Factors Affecting Cable Capacitance:**

1.  **Dimensions of Conductors:** Larger conductor diameter increases capacitance.
2.  **Distance between Conductors:** Closer conductors increase capacitance.
3.  **Dielectric Constant (Permittivity, ε) of the Insulating Material:** Higher dielectric constant leads to higher capacitance.

**Capacitance of a Single-Core Cable:**

Consider a single-core cable with a conductor radius 'r' and the inner radius of the insulation (outer radius of the conductor) 'r', and the outer radius of the insulation 'R'. The insulation is surrounded by a metallic sheath.

The capacitance per unit length (C) of a single-core cable can be calculated using the formula for a cylindrical capacitor:

$C = \frac{2 \pi \epsilon}{ln(R/r)}$

Where:
*   $\epsilon$ is the permittivity of the dielectric material ($\epsilon = \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r$, where $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space and $\epsilon_r$ is the relative permittivity or dielectric constant of the insulating material).
*   $R$ is the outer radius of the insulation.
*   $r$ is the radius of the conductor.

**Capacitance of a Three-Core Cable:**

In a three-core cable, the conductors are typically insulated individually and then bunched together. The presence of other conductors and the metallic sheath significantly influences the capacitance of each conductor to the sheath and to other conductors.

*   **Capacitance between each core and the sheath (C_sheath):** This is the primary capacitance considered for charging current calculations. The formula is similar to the single-core cable, but R is the radius to the sheath and r is the effective radius of the core bundle.
*   **Capacitance between cores (C_inter):** This is the capacitance between any two cores.

For practical purposes, especially for charging current calculations, we often consider the capacitance between each core and the sheath, assuming the sheath is at ground potential.

**Implications of High Cable Capacitance:**

*   **Charging Current:** At AC, a capacitive reactance ($X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$) exists. The charging current ($I_c = V/X_C = V \omega C$) is significant, especially for long transmission lines.
    *   **Reactive Power Draw:** Charging current leads to a capacitive power factor, causing the voltage at the receiving end to be higher than at the sending end (Ferranti Effect), particularly under light load conditions.
    *   **Increased Line Losses:** Although reactive, charging current contributes to total current and thus to $I^2R$ losses.
    *   **Switching Surges:** Can lead to high transient voltages during switching operations.
*   **Dielectric Losses:** These losses increase with voltage and frequency, contributing to overall power loss and heat generation.

**Reference:**

*   **Wadhwa C. L., Chapter 7.** Wadhwa provides derivations for single and three-core cable capacitances and discusses their impact.
*   **Mehta V. K. & Mehta R., Chapter 16.**

**Example:**

A 10 km long single-core cable operating at 132 kV has a capacitance of 0.15 µF/km.
Total Capacitance = 0.15 µF/km * 10 km = 1.5 µF.
If the line voltage is 132 kV (phase voltage $V_p = 132/\sqrt{3}$ kV), the charging current can be calculated.

$X_C = \frac{1}{2 \pi f C_{total}} = \frac{1}{2 \pi \times 50 \times 1.5 \times 10^{-6}} \approx 2122 \Omega$
$I_c = \frac{V_p}{X_C} = \frac{132 \times 10^3 / \sqrt{3}}{2122} \approx 36.1 \text{ A}$ (This is a significant current).

**Important Point to Remember:**

Cable capacitance is a major limiting factor for the length of AC power transmission using underground cables. It necessitates methods like grading and the use of series capacitors (though less common in underground systems compared to overhead) to compensate for its effects.

### 4. Cable Grading

Cable grading is a technique used to reduce the maximum dielectric stress at the conductor surface and distribute the stress more uniformly across the insulation thickness. This is crucial for high-voltage cables where the dielectric stress can become very high, potentially leading to insulation breakdown.

**Necessity of Grading:**

In a single-core cable (or each core of a multi-core cable), the electric field strength (E) is inversely proportional to the distance from the conductor's center.
For a cylindrical conductor of radius 'r' and insulation of outer radius 'R', the electric field strength at a distance 'x' from the centre is:

$E(x) = \frac{V}{x \ln(R/r)}$

Where:
*   V is the voltage across the insulation.
*   x is the radial distance from the conductor's center.

This formula shows that the electric field is maximum at the conductor surface (x = r) and minimum at the outer sheath (x = R).

$E_{max} = \frac{V}{r \ln(R/r)}$
$E_{min} = \frac{V}{R \ln(R/r)}$

Since $r < R$, $E_{max} > E_{min}$. This high stress at the conductor surface can lead to:

*   **Ionization:** Free electrons in the dielectric can be accelerated by the high electric field, colliding with molecules and producing more free electrons, leading to ionization and a conducting path.
*   **Dielectric Breakdown:** If the electric field exceeds the dielectric strength of the material, a breakdown path can form.

Grading aims to make the electric field strength more uniform, thereby utilizing the dielectric material more efficiently and increasing the cable's voltage rating.

**Reference:**

*   **Wadhwa C. L., Chapter 7.** Provides a detailed explanation of the electric field distribution and the need for grading.
*   **Mehta V. K. & Mehta R., Chapter 16.**

**Important Point to Remember:**

The primary goal of grading is to prevent ionization and dielectric breakdown by ensuring the electric field stress is within the safe limits across the entire insulation thickness.

### 5. Types of Cable Grading

There are two main methods for grading underground cables:

**A. GradING (or DIELECTRIC GRADING)**

This method involves using multiple layers of insulating materials with different dielectric constants (relative permittivities, $\epsilon_r$) and/or different thicknesses, arranged in such a way that the electric field is made uniform.

**Principle:**

To achieve a uniform electric field ($E = \text{constant}$), the product of electric field strength and radial distance ($E \cdot x$) must be constant.
We know that $E(x) = \frac{V(x)}{dx}$ and $E(x) \propto 1/x$.
For uniform field, we need $E(x) = K/x$. The voltage drop across an elemental thickness $dx$ is $dV = E(x) dx = (K/x) dx$.

To make the field uniform, we arrange layers of materials such that the drop across each layer is proportional to the logarithm of the radii of its boundaries.

Consider a cable with two layers of dielectric materials:
*   Inner layer: Dielectric constant $\epsilon_{r1}$, thickness from $r$ to $r_1$.
*   Outer layer: Dielectric constant $\epsilon_{r2}$, thickness from $r_1$ to $R$.

For the electric field to be uniform throughout the insulation, the stress at the boundary between the two materials must be equal:

$E_{max} = \frac{V}{r \ln(r_1/r) / \epsilon_{r1} + r \ln(R/r_1) / \epsilon_{r2}}$ (This is not the simplest form for calculation but conceptually illustrates the goal)

A more direct approach to achieving uniform stress involves specific radial thicknesses for each dielectric layer. The condition for uniform stress requires:

$\frac{V_1}{ln(r_1/r)} = \frac{V_2}{ln(r_2/r_1)} = \dots = \frac{V_n}{ln(R/r_{n-1})}$

And for grading, the voltage drop across each layer is made inversely proportional to the dielectric constant of that layer's material.

To achieve uniform field stress, the voltage drop across each layer should be:

$V_1 = V \frac{\ln(r_1/r)}{\ln(R/r)}$
$V_2 = V \frac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{\ln(R/r)}$
...
$V_n = V \frac{\ln(R/r_{n-1})}{\ln(R/r)}$

Where $V_1, V_2, ..., V_n$ are the voltage drops across successive layers.
The dielectric stress $E_i$ in layer $i$ is given by: $E_i = \frac{V_i}{x \ln(\text{outer}/\text{inner})}$

For uniform stress ($E_1 = E_2 = ... = E$), we need:
$\frac{V_1}{\epsilon_{r1} \ln(r_1/r)} = \frac{V_2}{\epsilon_{r2} \ln(r_2/r_1)} = ...$

This implies that the voltage drop across each layer is proportional to the product of its dielectric constant and the logarithm of the ratio of its outer to inner radius:
$V_1 \propto \epsilon_{r1} \ln(r_1/r)$
$V_2 \propto \epsilon_{r2} \ln(r_2/r_1)$

And for uniform stress, we need $V_1 / \ln(r_1/r) = V_2 / \ln(r_2/r_1)$ if $\epsilon_{r1} = \epsilon_{r2}$.

With graded dielectrics, the condition for uniform stress is achieved when:
$\epsilon_{r1} \ln(r_1/r) = \epsilon_{r2} \ln(r_2/r_1) = \dots = \epsilon_{rn} \ln(R/r_{n-1})$

This ensures that the electric field strength in each layer is the same at the conductor surface and outer boundary, making the field uniform throughout.

**Example:**

A 3-phase, 132 kV cable uses two layers of insulation.
*   Inner layer: $\epsilon_{r1} = 3.5$, thickness up to radius $r_1$.
*   Outer layer: $\epsilon_{r2} = 2.5$, thickness from $r_1$ to $R$.

To grade for uniform stress, we choose $r_1$ such that:
$3.5 \ln(r_1/r) = 2.5 \ln(R/r_1)$

This equation allows us to determine the intermediate radius $r_1$.

**Types of Dielectric Grading:**

*   **Single Grading:** Uses two dielectric materials with different permittivities.
*   **Compound Grading:** Uses three or more dielectric materials.

**Pros:**
*   Effective in reducing maximum dielectric stress.
*   Allows for higher operating voltages for a given insulation thickness.
*   More efficient use of dielectric material.

**Cons:**
*   Manufacturing is complex and expensive due to the need for precise application of different dielectric layers.
*   Difficulty in maintaining uniform interface between layers.

**Reference:**

*   **Wadhwa C. L., Chapter 7.** Detailed equations and diagrams for dielectric grading.
*   **Mehta V. K. & Mehta R., Chapter 16.**

**B. GRADED (or PRESSURE GRADING)**

This method is primarily applicable to oil-filled cables. It involves maintaining the insulating oil under pressure, with the pressure being higher near the conductor and decreasing towards the sheath. This pressure gradient helps to prevent the formation of voids within the insulation.

**Principle:**

In oil-filled cables, the oil circulates through ducts in the insulation. If the cable is subjected to voltage stress, ionization can occur in any voids or weak spots. By maintaining the oil under pressure, the dielectric strength of the oil is increased, and any small voids are kept filled with oil, preventing ionization.

*   **Without pressure:** Ionization starts at the conductor where the stress is highest.
*   **With pressure grading:** The pressure of the oil is maintained higher at the conductor surface. This prevents the formation of any voids where ionization could begin, even at the point of highest stress.

**Methods of Pressure Grading:**

1.  **Internal Pressure:**
    *   **Self-Contained Oil-Filled (SCOF) Cables:** The oil is contained within the cable's sheath. Pressure is maintained by reservoirs or accumulators connected to the cable system. The pressure can be varied along the cable length, with higher pressure at the lower end to counteract gravity.
    *   **Pipe-Type Cables:** The cable is drawn into a pipe which is then filled with oil under pressure.

2.  **External Pressure:** The cable is immersed in an oil tank, and the oil in the tank is kept under pressure.

**Reference:**

*   **Wadhwa C. L., Chapter 7.** Discusses oil-filled cables and pressure grading.

**Pros:**
*   Very effective for very high voltage and extra-high voltage cables.
*   Prevents ionization by filling any potential voids with pressurized oil.
*   Allows for lighter and more compact cable designs.

**Cons:**
*   Requires a complex oil circulation and pressurization system.
*   Susceptible to oil leaks, which can lead to loss of pressure and potential cable failure.
*   More expensive to manufacture and maintain.

**Important Point to Remember:**

Pressure grading is a more advanced technique primarily used for high-voltage applications where the dielectric stress is very high. It ensures that the insulating oil itself, under pressure, acts as the dielectric medium, preventing breakdown.

### Practice Questions

1.  **Define Ampacity and list three factors that influence it.**
    *   **Answer:** Ampacity is the maximum continuous current a cable can carry without exceeding its rated temperature. Factors include ambient temperature, thermal resistivity of the surrounding medium, installation method (depth, spacing), and conductor resistance.

2.  **Classify underground cables based on their insulation material, mentioning one advantage and one disadvantage for each type.**
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Paper Insulated Cables:**
            *   Advantage: Relatively inexpensive.
            *   Disadvantage: Susceptible to moisture ingress, limited voltage capability.
        *   **XLPE Insulated Cables:**
            *   Advantage: Excellent dielectric properties, high temperature rating.
            *   Disadvantage: More expensive than PVC.
        *   **PVC Insulated Cables:**
            *   Advantage: Inexpensive, flame retardant.
            *   Disadvantage: Limited temperature rating, brittle at low temperatures.

3.  **Calculate the capacitance per unit length of a single-core cable with a conductor radius of 1 cm and an insulation outer radius of 3 cm. The dielectric constant of the insulation is 3.0. Assume the permittivity of free space $\epsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \, F/m$.**
    *   **Answer:**
        $C = \frac{2 \pi \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r}{ln(R/r)}$
        $C = \frac{2 \pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 3.0}{ln(3/1)}$
        $C = \frac{166.8 \times 10^{-12}}{1.0986}$
        $C \approx 151.8 \times 10^{-12} \, F/m = 0.1518 \, \mu F/km$

4.  **Why is cable grading necessary in high-voltage cables?**
    *   **Answer:** Cable grading is necessary to reduce the maximum dielectric stress at the conductor surface. In a standard cable, the electric field is highest at the conductor and decreases radially outwards. This high stress can lead to ionization and dielectric breakdown. Grading aims to distribute the stress more uniformly, utilizing the dielectric material efficiently and preventing premature failure.

5.  **Describe the basic principle of Dielectric Grading.**
    *   **Answer:** Dielectric grading uses layers of insulating materials with different dielectric constants and/or thicknesses. The materials are chosen and arranged such that the electric field stress is made uniform across the entire insulation thickness, preventing high stress concentrations at the conductor. The condition for uniform stress is $\epsilon_{r1} \ln(r_1/r) = \epsilon_{r2} \ln(r_2/r_1) = \dots$, ensuring equal radial stress distribution.

6.  **What is the primary difference between Dielectric Grading and Pressure Grading?**
    *   **Answer:** Dielectric grading uses different insulating materials to distribute the electric field stress. Pressure grading, primarily used in oil-filled cables, involves maintaining the insulating oil under pressure, with higher pressure near the conductor, to increase the dielectric strength of the oil and prevent ionization in any voids.

### Important Points to Remember for this Topic:

*   **Ratings are critical:** Always consider ambient conditions and installation methods for correct cable ampacity.
*   **XLPE is dominant:** For modern medium and high-voltage applications, XLPE cables are the standard.
*   **Capacitance is a challenge for AC transmission:** It leads to reactive power draw and voltage rise (Ferranti effect).
*   **Grading is essential for HV:** To prevent dielectric breakdown and ionization.
*   **Dielectric grading uses material properties:** By layering materials with different permittivities.
*   **Pressure grading uses physical properties:** By pressurizing the dielectric medium (oil) to increase its strength.
