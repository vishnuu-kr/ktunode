# Module 3: Introduction to EHVAC and HVDC

## 3.1 Introduction to EHVAC and HVDC

This module introduces the fundamental principles, advantages, and disadvantages of Extra High Voltage Alternating Current (EHVAC) and High Voltage Direct Current (HVDC) transmission systems. Understanding these concepts is crucial for evaluating the performance and characteristics of modern power transmission networks.

---

### 3.1.1 What are EHVAC and HVDC?

*   **Extra High Voltage AC (EHVAC):** Refers to AC power transmission systems operating at voltages above 230 kV. As transmission distances and power requirements increase, higher voltages are used to reduce current and thereby minimize losses and conductor material.
    *   **Voltage Levels:** Common EHVAC levels include 345 kV, 400 kV, 500 kV, 765 kV, and beyond.
    *   **Purpose:** To transmit large amounts of power over long distances more efficiently than lower voltage AC systems.

*   **High Voltage DC (HVDC):** Refers to DC power transmission systems operating at high voltages. Unlike AC, DC transmission has unique characteristics that make it advantageous for specific applications.
    *   **Voltage Levels:** Can range from $\pm 100$ kV to $\pm 600$ kV and even higher for bulk power transfer.
    *   **Purpose:** Primarily used for transmitting large blocks of power over very long distances, interconnecting asynchronous AC systems, and transmitting power through submarine or underground cables.

---

### 3.1.2 Principle of EHVAC Transmission

The principle of EHVAC transmission is based on the fundamental laws of electromagnetism. For AC power transmission, the goal is to transmit power ($P$) with minimal losses ($I^2R$) over long distances.

*   **Power Equation:** $P = V \times I \times \cos\phi$, where:
    *   $P$ is the power transmitted.
    *   $V$ is the voltage.
    *   $I$ is the current.
    *   $\cos\phi$ is the power factor.

*   **Reducing Losses:** To transmit a given amount of power ($P$) at a lower current ($I$), the voltage ($V$) must be increased. This is because the losses are proportional to the square of the current ($I^2R$).

*   **Example:** To transmit 1000 MW:
    *   At 400 kV (assuming $\cos\phi = 0.9$):
        $I = \frac{P}{V \times \cos\phi} = \frac{1000 \times 10^6 \text{ W}}{400 \times 10^3 \text{ V} \times 0.9} \approx 2778 \text{ A}$
    *   At 765 kV (assuming $\cos\phi = 0.9$):
        $I = \frac{1000 \times 10^6 \text{ W}}{765 \times 10^3 \text{ V} \times 0.9} \approx 1450 \text{ A}$

    As seen, increasing the voltage significantly reduces the current, leading to lower $I^2R$ losses.

*   **Components:** EHVAC transmission lines consist of:
    *   **Conductors:** Typically made of aluminum conductor steel-reinforced (ACSR) or all-aluminum alloy (AAAC) to provide good conductivity and mechanical strength. Bundled conductors are used to reduce the effective diameter, thus reducing corona discharge and increasing surge impedance.
    *   **Insulators:** Used to support the conductors and prevent current leakage to the ground or tower. String insulators made of porcelain or composite materials are common.
    *   **Towers:** Provide mechanical support for conductors at a safe height.

### 3.1.3 Principle of HVDC Transmission

HVDC transmission involves converting AC power to DC at the sending end, transmitting it as DC, and then converting it back to AC at the receiving end. This conversion is done using high-power semiconductor devices like thyristors (SCRs) and GTOs.

*   **Key Components:**
    *   **AC-DC Converter (Rectifier):** Converts AC to DC. This is typically done using a phase-controlled converter.
    *   **DC Transmission Line:** The medium for DC power transfer.
    *   **DC-AC Converter (Inverter):** Converts DC back to AC. This is also typically a phase-controlled converter.
    *   **Smoothing Reactors & Filters:** Used to reduce ripple in the DC and AC sides, respectively, and to suppress harmonics generated by the converters.

*   **Types of Converters:**
    *   **Line-Commutated Converters (LCC):** The most common type, where the switching of semiconductor devices is controlled by the AC system voltage. They require a reactive power source for commutation.
    *   **Voltage Source Converters (VSC):** These converters use self-commutated devices (like IGBTs) and can control both active and reactive power. They are more flexible but typically more expensive for bulk power transfer.

*   **Basic Principle of Rectification (LCC):** In a simple 3-phase bridge rectifier, the AC voltage is rectified to produce a pulsating DC voltage. By controlling the firing angle ($\alpha$) of the thyristors, the average DC voltage can be regulated.
    *   The average DC voltage of a 6-pulse converter is given by $V_{dc} = \frac{3 V_{ml}}{\pi} \cos\alpha$, where $V_{ml}$ is the peak value of the line-to-line voltage.

*   **Basic Principle of Inversion (LCC):** Inversion is essentially rectification in reverse. The DC power is fed into the converter, and AC power is extracted. This requires a slightly larger firing angle ($\beta$ or $180^\circ - \alpha$) to ensure proper commutation from the AC side.

### 3.1.4 Advantages and Disadvantages of EHVAC Transmission

**Advantages:**

1.  **Mature Technology:** AC transmission technology has been in use for over a century, and its components and operating principles are well-established and understood.
2.  **Easy Voltage Transformation:** AC voltage can be easily stepped up or down using transformers, facilitating efficient transmission at high voltages and safe utilization at lower voltages. This is a significant advantage for distributing power from generating stations to load centers.
3.  **Availability of AC Equipment:** A wide range of AC equipment, including generators, transformers, switchgear, and protective devices, is readily available and cost-effective compared to their DC counterparts.
4.  **Reactive Power Compensation:** AC systems can generate and absorb reactive power (e.g., using synchronous condensers or FACTS devices), which is essential for voltage control.
5.  **Interconnection of Networks:** AC networks can be easily interconnected to form a robust grid, allowing for power sharing and improved reliability.
6.  **Ground Return is not Practical:** While ground return can be used in HVDC, it's not practical for AC due to eddy currents and inductive effects. However, a neutral wire is always available for load connections and fault current paths.

**Disadvantages:**

1.  **Higher Line Costs:** For very long distances, EHVAC lines require more conductors (due to bundled conductors for higher voltages) and larger towers, leading to higher capital costs.
2.  **Higher Losses:** AC transmission suffers from increased losses due to:
    *   **Skin Effect:** Current tends to flow on the outer surface of a conductor, increasing its effective resistance.
    *   **Proximity Effect:** Magnetic fields from adjacent conductors cause uneven current distribution.
    *   **Dielectric Losses:** Energy loss in the insulating material surrounding the conductors.
3.  **Charging Current:** AC lines have a capacitive nature, drawing a significant charging current, especially over long distances. This charging current does not contribute to useful power transfer but increases line current and losses.
4.  **Surge Impedance Loading (SIL):** The power transmitted at SIL results in no reactive power flow and unit power factor. For transmission lines significantly shorter or longer than SIL, reactive power compensation is required, adding complexity and cost.
5.  **Short Circuit Currents:** AC systems are prone to higher short-circuit currents compared to DC systems, requiring more robust and expensive switchgear for protection.
6.  **Stability Issues:** AC systems are susceptible to transient stability problems, especially during faults or sudden load changes.
7.  **Corona Discharge:** At EHV levels, corona discharge (ionization of air around conductors) can occur, leading to power loss, radio interference, and audible noise. This necessitates larger conductor spacing and bundled conductors.

### 3.1.5 Advantages and Disadvantages of HVDC Transmission

**Advantages:**

1.  **Lower Line Costs for Long Distances:** For very long distances (typically > 600 km for overhead lines and > 50 km for underground/submarine cables), HVDC transmission is more economical due to fewer conductors (only two or three) and simpler tower structures.
2.  **Lower Transmission Losses:** HVDC lines have lower losses because they avoid skin effect and dielectric losses. The resistance of the conductor is the primary factor, and there are no reactive power losses.
3.  **No Charging Current:** Unlike AC lines, DC lines do not have charging current, making them ideal for long cables where charging current in AC would be prohibitively high.
4.  **Higher Power Transfer Capability:** For the same right-of-way, HVDC can transmit more power than AC.
5.  **Interconnection of Asynchronous AC Systems:** HVDC links can connect AC systems that operate at different frequencies or are not synchronized, allowing for power exchange between independent grids (e.g., connecting two countries with different grid frequencies).
6.  **Better Stability:** HVDC links are inherently stable and do not contribute to the instability of the connected AC systems. They can also be used to improve the stability of AC networks.
7.  **Lower Short Circuit Currents:** HVDC systems have lower fault current levels, simplifying the design of DC switchgear.
8.  **Environmental Advantages:** HVDC towers and conductors can be smaller, leading to a smaller environmental footprint. Corona effects are generally less problematic, reducing radio interference.

**Disadvantages:**

1.  **High Capital Cost of Converter Stations:** The AC-DC and DC-AC converter stations are very expensive due to the complex power electronic equipment (thyristors, transformers, filters). This makes HVDC uneconomical for short distances.
2.  **Complexity of Converter Stations:** The converter stations are complex to design, operate, and maintain.
3.  **No Voltage Transformation:** DC voltage cannot be easily transformed using transformers. This means that the AC voltage at the sending and receiving substations must be suitable for the converter station, and transformers are only used on the AC side.
4.  **Reactive Power Consumption:** Line-commutated converter stations consume significant reactive power, which needs to be supplied by capacitor banks or synchronous condensers at the substations.
5.  **Harmonic Generation:** Converters generate harmonics on both the AC and DC sides, which require harmonic filters to prevent them from affecting connected AC systems and causing interference.
6.  **Limited Short-Circuit Current Control:** While fault currents are lower, interrupting a DC fault can be challenging because there is no natural zero-crossing of current for switching, unlike AC.
7.  **Lack of DC Switchgear:** Reliable and economical DC circuit breakers for very high voltages are still under development, making fault interruption a significant challenge.

### 3.1.6 Applications of EHVAC and HVDC

*   **EHVAC Applications:**
    *   Bulk power transmission over moderate to long distances within interconnected AC grids.
    *   Connecting major generating stations to load centers.
    *   Establishing regional and national transmission networks.

*   **HVDC Applications:**
    *   **Very Long Distance Transmission:** Transmitting large blocks of power over distances where AC transmission becomes uneconomical or technically challenging (e.g., 600-800 km and beyond).
    *   **Underground and Submarine Cables:** Transmitting power through long underground or submarine cables where the high capacitance of AC cables leads to excessive charging current.
    *   **Interconnection of Asynchronous Networks:** Connecting AC systems with different frequencies or without phase synchronization.
    *   **Renewable Energy Integration:** Connecting offshore wind farms or remote solar farms to the mainland AC grid.
    *   **Asynchronous Connection of Two AC Systems:** Allowing power flow between two AC systems that are not synchronized.

### 3.1.8 Key Concepts and Definitions to Remember

*   **EHVAC:** AC transmission at voltages above 230 kV.
*   **HVDC:** DC transmission at high voltages.
*   **Line Losses ($I^2R$):** Reduced by increasing voltage in AC, and by eliminating reactive components and skin/proximity effects in DC.
*   **Charging Current:** A significant factor in AC transmission over long lines and cables, absent in DC.
*   **Voltage Transformation:** Easy with AC (transformers), not with DC.
*   **Converter Stations:** Essential, expensive, and complex components of HVDC systems.
*   **Asynchronous Interconnection:** A key application of HVDC.
*   **Skin Effect/Proximity Effect:** Contribute to AC line resistance.
*   **Dielectric Losses:** Occur in AC transmission due to insulation.
*   **Harmonics:** A byproduct of DC-AC conversion in HVDC.

### 3.1.9 Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Explain the fundamental principle behind increasing the voltage level for efficient AC power transmission. Illustrate with an example for transmitting 1000 MW over a distance.

**Answer:**
The principle is to reduce the current for a given power transmission. Power ($P$) is related to voltage ($V$) and current ($I$) by $P = V \times I \times \cos\phi$. Transmission line losses are primarily $I^2R$. By increasing $V$, the current $I$ can be significantly reduced, thereby minimizing $I^2R$ losses and the amount of conductor material required for a given transmission capacity.

*   **Example:** Transmitting 1000 MW at 0.9 power factor.
    *   At 400 kV: $I \approx 2778$ A
    *   At 765 kV: $I \approx 1450$ A
    The current at 765 kV is almost half that at 400 kV, leading to approximately $(1/2)^2 = 1/4$ of the resistive losses.

**Question 2:**
What are the main advantages of HVDC transmission for long-distance overhead lines compared to EHVAC transmission?

**Answer:**
The main advantages are:
1.  **Lower Line Costs:** Fewer conductors and simpler towers.
2.  **Lower Transmission Losses:** Avoids skin effect, proximity effect, and dielectric losses.
3.  **Higher Power Transfer Capability:** For the same right-of-way.
4.  **No Charging Current:** Crucial for long lines, eliminates reactive power compensation needs related to capacitance.
5.  **Stability:** HVDC links do not destabilize the connected AC grids.

**Question 3:**
List three major disadvantages of HVDC transmission.

**Answer:**
1.  **High Capital Cost of Converter Stations:** This is the primary economic hurdle.
2.  **Complexity of Converter Stations:** Requires sophisticated power electronics and control systems.
3.  **Harmonic Generation:** Requires extensive filtering.
4.  **Lack of DC Switchgear:** Difficult to interrupt DC faults.
5.  **Reactive Power Consumption:** LCC stations require external reactive power sources.

**Question 4:**
A 400 kV AC transmission line and a $\pm 400$ kV DC transmission line are to transmit 1000 MW of power.
(a) Calculate the approximate current in each line (assume power factor of 0.9 for AC).
(b) Briefly discuss which line might be preferred for a distance of 50 km and for 1000 km.

**Answer:**
(a)
*   **AC Line (400 kV):**
    $I_{AC} = \frac{P}{V_{AC} \times \cos\phi} = \frac{1000 \times 10^6 \text{ W}}{400 \times 10^3 \text{ V} \times 0.9} \approx 2778 \text{ A}$

*   **DC Line ($\pm 400$ kV):**
    The voltage is $\pm 400$ kV, meaning the potential difference is 800 kV between conductors (or 400 kV from conductor to ground if single pole). For bulk power transmission with two poles, we consider the total voltage, or more precisely, the power is $P = V_{dc} \times I_{dc}$ where $V_{dc}$ is the DC voltage and $I_{dc}$ is the DC current per pole. If we assume $\pm 400$ kV refers to the pole voltage with respect to ground, then the total voltage for calculation of current might be interpreted as the line-to-line equivalent if it were AC, or more correctly $V_{dc} = 2 \times 400$ kV = 800 kV if considering the total voltage difference for power calculation. However, typically, the voltage rating for HVDC refers to the pole-to-ground voltage. Let's assume $V_{dc} = 400$ kV per pole and we are calculating current per pole. If two poles are used, total power would be $2 \times V_{dc} \times I_{dc}$. Or, if $V_{dc}$ refers to the total bipolar voltage difference, then $V_{dc} = 800$ kV. Let's assume the question implies total power through bipolar configuration, so total voltage is 800kV.
    $I_{DC} = \frac{P}{V_{dc} (\text{total})} = \frac{1000 \times 10^6 \text{ W}}{800 \times 10^3 \text{ V}} = 1250 \text{ A}$
    *Note: If $\pm 400$ kV means 400 kV to ground on each pole, then the voltage difference is 800 kV. If it means 400 kV difference between poles, the total voltage is 400 kV. Standard HVDC ratings like $\pm 400$ kV usually mean $\pm 400$ kV to ground, giving a bipolar voltage of 800 kV.*
    Let's use the common interpretation: $\pm 400$ kV means 400 kV to ground, so total voltage $V_{dc} = 800$ kV for bipolar operation.
    $I_{DC} = \frac{1000 \times 10^6 \text{ W}}{800 \times 10^3 \text{ V}} = 1250 \text{ A}$

(b)
*   **For 50 km:** AC transmission is generally preferred. The capital cost of converter stations for HVDC would be too high for this short distance, outweighing the minor savings in line materials and losses. AC's ease of voltage transformation at substations is highly advantageous for distribution networks.
*   **For 1000 km:** HVDC transmission is likely preferred. The cost savings in conductors and towers for HVDC become significant over such a long distance, and the reduced losses contribute to better overall efficiency, offsetting the high initial cost of converter stations. The absence of charging current is also a major benefit for long distances.

### 3.1.10 Important Points to Remember

*   **Economics is Key:** The choice between EHVAC and HVDC depends heavily on economic considerations, primarily distance and power transfer capacity.
*   **Converter Stations Define HVDC:** The cost and complexity of converter stations are the main drivers for HVDC economics.
*   **No Simple DC Transformer:** This is a fundamental limitation of HVDC.
*   **Asynchronous Links:** HVDC is the only practical solution for interconnecting non-synchronous AC systems.
*   **Renewable Integration:** HVDC is increasingly important for connecting remote renewable energy sources.
