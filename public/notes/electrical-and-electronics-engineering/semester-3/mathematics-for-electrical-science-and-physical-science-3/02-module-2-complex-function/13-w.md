### **1. Introduction to the Transformation $w = \frac{1}{z}$**

This transformation is a fundamental example of a **Möbius transformation** (or linear fractional transformation). It plays a crucial role in understanding conformal mapping and has significant applications in various fields of science and engineering, including electrical circuits, fluid dynamics, and signal processing.

**Key Concept:** A transformation that maps geometric shapes and regions in the complex plane ($z$-plane) to other shapes and regions in another complex plane ($w$-plane).

**Reference:** Kreyszig, Chapter 7.4 (Conformal Mapping). This topic is a foundational example within the broader study of conformal mappings.

---

### **2. Properties of the Transformation $w = \frac{1}{z}$**

Let $z = x + iy$ and $w = u + iv$. We can express the transformation in terms of real and imaginary parts.

**Derivation:**
Given $w = \frac{1}{z}$, substitute $z = x + iy$:
$w = \frac{1}{x + iy}$

To separate the real and imaginary parts, multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator:
$w = \frac{1}{x + iy} \times \frac{x - iy}{x - iy} = \frac{x - iy}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2} - i \frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}$

Therefore,
$u = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}$
$v = -\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}$

**Important Points:**

*   **Singularity:** The transformation is undefined at $z = 0$.
*   **Behavior near the origin:** As $z \to 0$, $|w| \to \infty$. This means the origin in the $z$-plane is mapped to the "point at infinity" in the $w$-plane.
*   **Behavior at infinity:** As $z \to \infty$, $w = \frac{1}{z} \to 0$. This means the "point at infinity" in the $z$-plane is mapped to the origin in the $w$-plane.

**Reference:** Zill & Shanahan, Chapter 6.1 (Möbius Transformations). This section discusses the properties and general behavior of such transformations.

---

### **3. Mapping of Geometric Objects**

The transformation $w = \frac{1}{z}$ maps lines and circles in the $z$-plane to lines and circles in the $w$-plane.

**3.1 Mapping of Lines**

Consider a line in the $z$-plane.

*   **Lines through the origin:**
    Let $z = r e^{i\theta}$. Then $w = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{1}{r e^{i\theta}} = \frac{1}{r} e^{-i\theta}$.
    If $z$ lies on a line through the origin (constant $\theta$), then $w$ lies on a ray from the origin in the $w$-plane with angle $-\theta$.
    *   **Example:** The positive real axis ($y=0, x>0$, $\theta=0$) is mapped to the positive real axis in the $w$-plane ($v=0, u>0$). The negative real axis ($y=0, x<0$, $\theta=\pi$) is mapped to the negative real axis in the $w$-plane ($v=0, u<0$).

*   **Lines not through the origin:**
    A general line in the $z$-plane can be represented by $Ax + By + C = 0$, where $C \neq 0$.
    Substitute $x = u(x^2 + y^2)$ and $y = -v(x^2 + y^2)$:
    $A[u(x^2 + y^2)] + B[-v(x^2 + y^2)] + C = 0$
    $(Au - Bv)(x^2 + y^2) + C = 0$

    From $w = \frac{1}{z}$, we have $|w|^2 = \frac{1}{|z|^2}$, so $u^2 + v^2 = \frac{1}{x^2 + y^2}$, which means $x^2 + y^2 = \frac{1}{u^2 + v^2}$.
    Substitute this back:
    $(Au - Bv) \left(\frac{1}{u^2 + v^2}\right) + C = 0$
    $Au - Bv + C(u^2 + v^2) = 0$
    $u^2 + v^2 - \frac{A}{C}u + \frac{B}{C}v = 0$

    This is the equation of a circle in the $w$-plane. If $C=0$, it's a line through the origin.
    *   **Conclusion:** Lines not passing through the origin in the $z$-plane are mapped to circles passing through the origin in the $w$-plane. Conversely, circles passing through the origin in the $z$-plane are mapped to lines in the $w$-plane.

**Reference:** Ramana, Chapter 35 (Complex Variables and Functions) or Grewal, Chapter 14 (Complex Integration). These chapters often cover transformations and their geometric interpretations.

**3.2 Mapping of Circles**

Consider a circle in the $z$-plane. The general equation of a circle or a line is $A(x^2 + y^2) + Dx + Ey + F = 0$.

*   **Circles passing through the origin ($F=0$):**
    If $F=0$, the equation is $A(x^2 + y^2) + Dx + Ey = 0$.
    If $A=0$, this is a line through the origin ($Dx + Ey = 0$). As seen above, this maps to a line through the origin.
    If $A \neq 0$, divide by $A$: $x^2 + y^2 + \frac{D}{A}x + \frac{E}{A}y = 0$.
    Substitute $x = u(x^2 + y^2)$ and $y = -v(x^2 + y^2)$:
    $x^2 + y^2 + \frac{D}{A}u(x^2 + y^2) + \frac{E}{A}(-v(x^2 + y^2)) = 0$
    $(x^2 + y^2) \left(1 + \frac{D}{A}u - \frac{E}{A}v\right) = 0$

    Since $x^2+y^2 \neq 0$ for points on the circle (unless it's the origin itself), we consider the second factor:
    $1 + \frac{D}{A}u - \frac{E}{A}v = 0$
    $\frac{D}{A}u - \frac{E}{A}v + 1 = 0$
    This is the equation of a line in the $w$-plane. Since the original equation was a circle passing through the origin in the $z$-plane, its image in the $w$-plane is a line that does NOT pass through the origin (because $z=0$ is mapped to infinity in $w$).

*   **Circles not passing through the origin ($F \neq 0$):**
    The equation is $A(x^2 + y^2) + Dx + Ey + F = 0$.
    Substitute $x^2 + y^2 = \frac{1}{u^2 + v^2}$, $x = \frac{u}{u^2 + v^2}$, $y = \frac{-v}{u^2 + v^2}$:
    $A \left(\frac{1}{u^2 + v^2}\right) + D \left(\frac{u}{u^2 + v^2}\right) + E \left(\frac{-v}{u^2 + v^2}\right) + F = 0$
    Multiply by $u^2 + v^2$:
    $A + Du - Ev + F(u^2 + v^2) = 0$
    $u^2 + v^2 + \frac{D}{F}u - \frac{E}{F}v + \frac{A}{F} = 0$

    This is the equation of a circle in the $w$-plane, provided $F \neq 0$. If $A=0$, it becomes a line.
    *   **Conclusion:** Circles not passing through the origin in the $z$-plane are mapped to circles not passing through the origin in the $w$-plane.

**Summary of Mappings:**

| $z$-plane object          | $w$-plane object                |
| :------------------------- | :------------------------------ |
| Line through origin        | Ray from origin                 |
| Line not through origin    | Circle through origin           |
| Circle through origin      | Line not through origin         |
| Circle not through origin  | Circle not through origin       |
| Unit circle $|z|=1$        | Unit circle $|w|=1$             |
| Right half-plane $\text{Re}(z) > 0$ | Right half-plane $\text{Re}(w) > 0$ |
| Upper half-plane $\text{Im}(z) > 0$ | Lower half-plane $\text{Im}(w) < 0$ |

**Reference:** Zill & Shanahan, Chapter 6.2 (Geometric Properties of Möbius Transformations).

### **4. Conformal Mapping Property**

The transformation $w = \frac{1}{z}$ is **conformal** everywhere except at $z=0$.

**Definition:** A transformation $w = f(z)$ is conformal at a point $z_0$ if it preserves angles between smooth curves passing through $z_0$, both in magnitude and orientation.

**Condition for Conformality:** A function $f(z)$ is conformal at $z_0$ if $f(z)$ is analytic at $z_0$ and $f'(z_0) \neq 0$.

**Analysis for $w = \frac{1}{z}$:**
$f(z) = \frac{1}{z} = z^{-1}$
$f'(z) = -z^{-2} = -\frac{1}{z^2}$

*   **Analyticity:** $f(z)$ is analytic everywhere except at $z=0$.
*   **Derivative:** $f'(z) = -\frac{1}{z^2}$. This is zero only if $z^2 \to \infty$ or $z=0$. However, at $z=0$, the function itself is not analytic, so it's not conformal there. For all $z \neq 0$, $f'(z) \neq 0$.

Therefore, $w = \frac{1}{z}$ is conformal for all $z \neq 0$.

**Relevance to Course Outcomes:** This directly relates to **CO2: Understand the analyticity of complex functions and apply it in conformal mapping.**

**Reference:** Kreyszig, Chapter 7.2 (Analytic Functions) and 7.3 (Conformal Mapping).

### **5. Applications in Electrical Science and Physical Science**

The $w = \frac{1}{z}$ transformation has several practical applications:

*   **Circuit Analysis:** In analyzing AC circuits, particularly in the context of impedance transformations or mapping filter characteristics in the frequency domain.
*   **Electrostatics:** Mapping electric fields and potential lines.
*   **Fluid Dynamics:** Mapping flow patterns, especially in irrotational flows.
*   **Image Processing:** Used in some image transformations and filters.
*   **Complex Analysis Tools:** Understanding the behavior of more complex Möbius transformations that are built upon this basic inverse function.

**Reference:** While specific applications might be in specialized textbooks for these fields, the foundational understanding of how this transformation warps regions is key. For example, in Fourier Transforms (CO1), understanding how frequency components are mapped can be conceptually related.

### **6. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**
Map the circle $|z-1|=1$ under the transformation $w = \frac{1}{z}$. Describe the resulting curve in the $w$-plane.

**Solution:**
The circle $|z-1|=1$ has center $z_0=1$ and radius $r=1$. This circle passes through the origin ($z=0$).
Let $z$ be on the circle. Then $z = 1 + e^{i\phi}$ for some $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$.
$w = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{i\phi}}$
$w = \frac{1}{1 + \cos\phi + i\sin\phi}$
$w = \frac{(1 + \cos\phi) - i\sin\phi}{(1 + \cos\phi)^2 + \sin^2\phi}$
$w = \frac{1 + \cos\phi - i\sin\phi}{1 + 2\cos\phi + \cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi}$
$w = \frac{1 + \cos\phi - i\sin\phi}{2 + 2\cos\phi} = \frac{1 + \cos\phi - i\sin\phi}{2(1 + \cos\phi)}$
$w = \frac{1}{2} - i \frac{\sin\phi}{2(1 + \cos\phi)}$

Using half-angle identities: $1 + \cos\phi = 2\cos^2(\frac{\phi}{2})$ and $\sin\phi = 2\sin(\frac{\phi}{2})\cos(\frac{\phi}{2})$.
$w = \frac{1}{2} - i \frac{2\sin(\frac{\phi}{2})\cos(\frac{\phi}{2})}{2(2\cos^2(\frac{\phi}{2}))} = \frac{1}{2} - i \frac{\sin(\frac{\phi}{2})}{2\cos(\frac{\phi}{2})} = \frac{1}{2} - i \frac{1}{2} \tan(\frac{\phi}{2})$

Let $u = \frac{1}{2}$ and $v = -\frac{1}{2} \tan(\frac{\phi}{2})$.
As $\phi$ varies from $0$ to $2\pi$, $\frac{\phi}{2}$ varies from $0$ to $\pi$.
$\tan(\frac{\phi}{2})$ ranges from $-\infty$ to $\infty$.
So, $v$ ranges from $\infty$ to $-\infty$.
The equation $u = \frac{1}{2}$ with $v$ varying over all real numbers represents a vertical line.
The line is $u = \frac{1}{2}$.

Alternatively, using the general formula $A(x^2 + y^2) + Dx + Ey + F = 0$.
The circle $|z-1|=1$ is $(x-1)^2 + y^2 = 1 \implies x^2 - 2x + 1 + y^2 = 1 \implies x^2 + y^2 - 2x = 0$.
Here, $A=1$, $D=-2$, $E=0$, $F=0$.
Since $F=0$, it's a circle through the origin. The mapping of a circle through the origin is a line not through the origin.
The equation in $w$-plane is $\frac{D}{F}u - \frac{E}{F}v + 1 = 0$ is not applicable when $F=0$.
Use the derived equation for lines not through the origin: $Au - Bv + C(u^2 + v^2) = 0$.
For $x^2 + y^2 - 2x = 0$, we have $A=1, B=0, C=0, D=-2, E=0$. The equation is $x^2+y^2 - 2x = 0$.
In $w$-plane: $\frac{1}{u^2+v^2} - 2 \frac{u}{u^2+v^2} = 0$.
Multiply by $u^2+v^2$: $1 - 2u = 0 \implies u = \frac{1}{2}$.
This is a vertical line in the $w$-plane.

**Answer:** The circle $|z-1|=1$ is mapped to the line $\text{Re}(w) = \frac{1}{2}$.

**Question 2:**
Map the region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ under the transformation $w = \frac{1}{z}$. Describe the resulting region in the $w$-plane.

**Solution:**
The region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ is the open half-plane to the right of the vertical line $x=1$.
The line $x=1$ does not pass through the origin. So, it will be mapped to a circle passing through the origin in the $w$-plane.
Consider the boundary line $x=1$.
Substitute $x = \frac{u}{u^2 + v^2}$:
$\frac{u}{u^2 + v^2} = 1$
$u = u^2 + v^2$
$u^2 - u + v^2 = 0$
Complete the square for $u$:
$(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 - \frac{1}{4} + v^2 = 0$
$(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$
This is a circle with center $(\frac{1}{2}, 0)$ and radius $\frac{1}{2}$. It passes through the origin $(0,0)$.

Now we need to determine which part of the circle the region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ maps to.
Let's pick a test point in the region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$, for example, $z = 2$.
$w = \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} + i0$. So, $(u,v) = (\frac{1}{2}, 0)$.
This point lies on the circle $(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$.
The center of the circle is at $(\frac{1}{2}, 0)$.
The origin $(0,0)$ in the $w$-plane is mapped from $z=\infty$.
The region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ includes points arbitrarily far from the origin, meaning $z \to \infty$.
As $z \to \infty$, $w = \frac{1}{z} \to 0$. So the origin $(0,0)$ in the $w$-plane is part of the mapped region.

The circle $(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$ divides the $w$-plane into two regions: the interior of the circle and the exterior.
The point $w = \frac{1}{2}$ (mapped from $z=2$) lies on the circle.
Let's pick a point in the $z$-plane, say $z = \infty$. This maps to $w=0$, which is inside the circle.
The original region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ does NOT contain the origin in the $z$-plane. It maps to a region that CONTAINS the origin in the $w$-plane.

The region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ lies to the right of $x=1$.
The image of $x=1$ is the circle $(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$.
The point $z=2$ maps to $w = 1/2$. This point is on the circle boundary.
Consider a point $z=1+i$. Its image is $w = \frac{1}{1+i} = \frac{1-i}{2} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{i}{2}$.
The coordinates $(u,v) = (\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2})$.
Let's check where this point lies relative to the circle $(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$.
$(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2})^2 + (-\frac{1}{2})^2 = 0 + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$.
This is exactly $(\frac{1}{2})^2$. So the point is on the circle. This should not be the case as $z=1+i$ is not on the line $x=1$. My selection of $z=2$ being on the circle boundary was correct.

Let's reconsider the test point.
The region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ is to the right of $x=1$.
The boundary $x=1$ maps to the circle $(u-\frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$.
Pick a point in the region $\text{Re}(z)>1$, e.g., $z=2$. $w = 1/2$. This point is on the circle.
Pick a point in the region $\text{Re}(z)>1$ which is further right, e.g., $z=3$. $w=1/3$.
$(1/3 - 1/2)^2 + 0^2 = (-1/6)^2 = 1/36$. This is less than $(1/2)^2 = 1/4$.
So $w=1/3$ is inside the circle.

The region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ contains points that go to infinity. As $z \to \infty$, $w \to 0$.
The origin $w=0$ lies INSIDE the circle $(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$.
Since $z=\infty$ maps to $w=0$, and $\text{Re}(z)>1$ contains points arbitrarily large, the image region must contain $w=0$.
Therefore, the region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ maps to the interior of the circle $(u - \frac{1}{2})^2 + v^2 = (\frac{1}{2})^2$.

**Answer:** The region $\text{Re}(z) > 1$ is mapped to the region $|w - \frac{1}{2}| < \frac{1}{2}$, which is the open disk centered at $\frac{1}{2}$ with radius $\frac{1}{2}$.

### **7. Key Points to Remember**

*   $w = \frac{1}{z}$ is a conformal mapping for all $z \neq 0$.
*   It maps lines and circles to lines and circles.
*   Lines through the origin map to rays from the origin.
*   Lines not through the origin map to circles through the origin.
*   Circles through the origin map to lines not through the origin.
*   Circles not through the origin map to circles not through the origin.
*   The origin ($z=0$) is mapped to the point at infinity, and the point at infinity is mapped to the origin ($w=0$).
*   The transformation is its own inverse: if $w = 1/z$, then $z = 1/w$. This means it's an involution.
