## DESIGN OF SOLAR PV SYSTEMS

### Module 4: Solar PV Systems – Stand-Alone and Grid-Connected

#### Topic: Solar PV Systems – Stand-Alone and Grid-Connected - Design Steps for a Stand-Alone System – Storage Batteries and Ultra-Capacitors

---

### Introduction

This module delves into the practical design aspects of solar photovoltaic (PV) systems, focusing on two primary configurations: **stand-alone** and **grid-connected**. We will explore the fundamental differences, design methodologies for stand-alone systems, and the crucial role of energy storage technologies like batteries and ultra-capacitors.

---

### 1. Solar PV Systems: Stand-Alone vs. Grid-Connected

**(Aligns with CO1, CO3)**

**1.1 Stand-Alone PV Systems:**

*   **Definition:** A stand-alone PV system operates independently of any utility grid. It is designed to power loads in remote locations where grid access is unavailable or uneconomical.
*   **Key Components:**
    *   **PV Array:** Converts sunlight into DC electricity.
    *   **Charge Controller:** Regulates the charging of batteries to prevent overcharging and deep discharge.
    *   **Battery Bank:** Stores excess energy generated by the PV array for use when sunlight is insufficient or unavailable.
    *   **Inverter (DC to AC):** Converts the DC power from the PV array and batteries into AC power to supply standard appliances.
    *   **Load:** The electrical devices or appliances to be powered.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Autonomy:** The system must be able to meet the load demand at all times, even during periods of low solar insolation. This requires careful sizing of the PV array and storage system.
    *   **Reliability:** Crucial for powering essential loads in off-grid locations.
    *   **Higher Capital Cost (per kW):** Generally more expensive than grid-connected systems due to the necessity of storage and independent operation.
*   **Applications:** Rural electrification, remote sensing stations, telecommunication towers, street lighting, water pumping in off-grid areas.

**1.2 Grid-Connected PV Systems:**

*   **Definition:** A grid-connected PV system operates in conjunction with the utility grid. It feeds excess power generated by the PV array into the grid and draws power from the grid when the PV generation is insufficient to meet the load demand.
*   **Key Components:**
    *   **PV Array:** Converts sunlight into DC electricity.
    *   **Inverter (DC to AC):** Converts DC power from the PV array into AC power that is synchronized with the grid frequency and voltage.
    *   **Grid Connection Equipment:** Includes metering, protective devices, and transformers to interface with the utility grid.
    *   **Load:** The electrical devices or appliances to be powered.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **No Battery Storage (typically):** Relies on the grid for backup, significantly reducing system cost and complexity.
    *   **Grid as Virtual Storage:** Excess energy is sent to the grid, and power is drawn from the grid when needed.
    *   **Lower Capital Cost (per kW):** More cost-effective due to the absence of large battery banks.
    *   **Efficiency:** Power conversion losses occur only during the conversion of DC to AC.
*   **Applications:** Rooftop solar installations for homes and businesses, utility-scale solar farms.

**Key Distinction:** The primary difference lies in the presence of energy storage and the reliance on an external power source (grid) for backup.

### 2. Design Steps for a Stand-Alone PV System

**(Aligns with CO2, CO4)**

Designing a stand-alone PV system requires a systematic approach to ensure it reliably meets the energy needs of the load. The process involves several crucial steps:

**2.1 Step 1: Load Assessment and Energy Audit**

*   **Objective:** To determine the daily energy consumption (in Watt-hours or Kilowatt-hours) and the peak power demand (in Watts or Kilowatts) of the loads.
*   **Procedure:**
    *   **List all appliances:** Identify every electrical device that will be powered by the system.
    *   **Determine wattage of each appliance:** This is usually found on the appliance's nameplate.
    *   **Estimate daily operating hours for each appliance:** This is the most critical and often challenging part. It requires careful observation and estimation of typical usage patterns.
    *   **Calculate daily energy consumption per appliance:** Wattage (W) × Hours/day (h) = Watt-hours/day (Wh/day).
    *   **Sum up the daily energy consumption of all appliances:** This gives the total daily energy requirement of the system.
    *   **Identify the peak load:** The sum of the wattages of all appliances that might operate simultaneously at any given time.
*   **Example:**
    *   Appliance 1: Light bulb (60W), 5 hours/day -> 60W * 5h = 300 Wh/day
    *   Appliance 2: Fan (75W), 10 hours/day -> 75W * 10h = 750 Wh/day
    *   Appliance 3: Refrigerator (150W average), 24 hours/day -> 150W * 24h = 3600 Wh/day
    *   **Total Daily Energy Consumption:** 300 + 750 + 3600 = 4650 Wh/day (or 4.65 kWh/day)
    *   **Peak Load:** If the fan and light are on simultaneously with the refrigerator, the peak load might be 150W (refrigerator) + 75W (fan) + 60W (light) = 285W. However, refrigerators cycle on and off, so the *starting surge* of the compressor should also be considered for inverter sizing.

**2.2 Step 2: Location and Solar Resource Assessment**

*   **Objective:** To determine the amount of solar energy available at the proposed installation site.
*   **Key Parameter:** **Peak Sun Hours (PSH)** or **Insolation**.
    *   **Definition:** The equivalent number of hours per day when solar irradiance averages 1000 W/m². It is a measure of the total solar energy received on a horizontal surface over a day.
    *   **Data Sources:**
        *   **NASA Surface meteorology and Solar Energy data:** Provides global horizontal irradiance (GHI) data.
        *   **PVGIS (Photovoltaic Geographical Information System):** A widely used online tool for solar resource assessment.
        *   **Local meteorological data:** If available and reliable.
        *   **Solar Pathfinders or Sun Charts:** For detailed site-specific analysis.
*   **Procedure:**
    *   Identify the PSH for the *least sunny month* of the year. This ensures the system can meet the load even during periods of low solar availability.
*   **Example:** A location might have an average daily insolation of 5 kWh/m²/day. If this is the total energy received on a horizontal surface, and we assume optimal tilt and orientation, the PSH would be 5 hours. However, PSH values are often directly provided by data sets or tools. A PSH of 4.5 is a common value for many temperate regions.

**2.3 Step 3: PV Array Sizing**

*   **Objective:** To determine the required capacity (in Watts-peak, Wp) of the PV array to meet the daily energy demand, considering system losses.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \text{PV Array Size (Wp)} = \frac{\text{Total Daily Energy Consumption (Wh/day)}}{\text{Peak Sun Hours (PSH) × System Derating Factor}} $$
*   **System Derating Factor:** Accounts for various losses in the system:
    *   **Module Temperature Losses:** PV modules perform less efficiently at higher temperatures. (e.g., 5-10%)
    *   **Soiling Losses:** Dust, dirt, and snow on the modules. (e.g., 3-5%)
    *   **Wiring Losses:** Resistance in wires and connections. (e.g., 1-3%)
    *   **Inverter Efficiency:** Conversion losses from DC to AC. (e.g., 90-98% depending on inverter type and load)
    *   **Charge Controller Efficiency:** (e.g., 95-99%)
    *   **Mismatch Losses:** Slight variations in performance between individual modules. (e.g., 1-2%)
    *   **Shading Losses:** If any part of the array is shaded. (Should be avoided as much as possible).
    *   **Overall Derating Factor:** Typically between 0.70 and 0.85 (meaning 70-85% of the rated power is actually available).
*   **Example:**
    *   Total Daily Energy Consumption: 4650 Wh/day
    *   Peak Sun Hours (least sunny month): 4.0 PSH
    *   System Derating Factor: 0.75
    $$ \text{PV Array Size (Wp)} = \frac{4650 \text{ Wh/day}}{4.0 \text{ PSH} \times 0.75} = \frac{4650}{3} = 1550 \text{ Wp} $$
    *   You would then select standard PV modules (e.g., 300Wp modules) and determine the number of modules required (1550 Wp / 300 Wp/module ≈ 5.17 modules). You would typically round up to the nearest whole module, so 6 modules. The actual array size would be 6 * 300Wp = 1800 Wp.

**2.4 Step 4: Battery Bank Sizing**

*   **Objective:** To determine the required storage capacity (in Ampere-hours, Ah) of the battery bank to meet the load during periods of low solar insolation and to ensure system autonomy.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Days of Autonomy:** The number of consecutive days the system can supply the load without any solar input. This is crucial for reliability. Typically 2-5 days, depending on the application.
    *   **Depth of Discharge (DoD):** The percentage of the battery's capacity that is discharged. To prolong battery life, it's recommended to limit DoD (e.g., 50% for lead-acid batteries, higher for Li-ion).
    *   **Battery Voltage:** The system voltage (12V, 24V, 48V) determines the number of batteries in series.
    *   **Battery Capacity (Ah):** The amount of charge a battery can deliver over time.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \text{Battery Capacity (Ah)} = \frac{\text{Total Daily Energy Consumption (Wh/day)} \times \text{Days of Autonomy}}{\text{Battery DoD Limit} \times \text{System Voltage (V)}} $$
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Determine the desired days of autonomy.
    2.  Select the appropriate DoD limit for the battery technology.
    3.  Determine the system voltage.
    4.  Calculate the required battery capacity in Wh first, then convert to Ah.
*   **Example:**
    *   Total Daily Energy Consumption: 4650 Wh/day
    *   Days of Autonomy: 3 days
    *   Battery DoD Limit: 0.50 (50% for lead-acid)
    *   System Voltage: 24V
    $$ \text{Battery Capacity (Wh)} = \frac{4650 \text{ Wh/day} \times 3 \text{ days}}{0.50} = \frac{13950}{0.50} = 27900 \text{ Wh} $$
    $$ \text{Battery Capacity (Ah)} = \frac{27900 \text{ Wh}}{24 \text{ V}} = 1162.5 \text{ Ah} $$
    *   You would then select standard battery units (e.g., 12V, 200Ah batteries) and configure them in series and parallel to achieve the required voltage and capacity. For a 24V system, you would need two 12V batteries in series. To get 1162.5 Ah at 24V, you would need 1162.5 Ah / 200 Ah per battery ≈ 5.8 batteries. You would round up to 6 batteries, wired as three parallel strings of two series-connected batteries each. This gives a total capacity of (6 batteries / 2 batteries per string) * 200Ah * 24V = 3 * 200Ah * 24V = 14400 Wh, which is more than the calculated 13950 Wh, providing a good margin.

**2.5 Step 5: Charge Controller Sizing**

*   **Objective:** To select a charge controller that can handle the maximum current from the PV array and the system voltage.
*   **Key Parameters:**
    *   **System Voltage:** Must match the battery bank voltage.
    *   **PV Array Short-Circuit Current (Isc):** The maximum current the array can produce under short-circuit conditions. This is found from the PV module datasheets and needs to be scaled for the number of modules in parallel.
    *   **Charging Current:** The current the controller will provide to the batteries.
*   **Types of Charge Controllers:**
    *   **PWM (Pulse Width Modulation):** Simpler, less efficient, can be less expensive. The PV array voltage is pulled down to the battery voltage, leading to some power loss, especially in cold weather when array voltage is higher.
    *   **MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking):** More sophisticated, more efficient. It tracks the maximum power point of the PV array and can convert higher array voltage to lower battery voltage, thereby capturing more energy. Recommended for most stand-alone systems.
*   **Sizing Formula:**
    $$ \text{Charge Controller Current Rating (A)} = \frac{\text{PV Array Isc} \times \text{Safety Factor}}{\text{System Voltage}} $$
    *   A safety factor of 1.25 is commonly used to account for temperature variations and higher irradiance.
*   **Example:**
    *   PV Array Isc per module: 10A
    *   Number of modules in series: 2 (for a 24V system, typically) -> Isc remains 10A
    *   Number of strings in parallel: 3
    *   Total PV Array Isc = 10A * 3 strings = 30A
    *   System Voltage: 24V
    $$ \text{Charge Controller Current Rating (A)} = \frac{30 \text{ A} \times 1.25}{1} = 37.5 \text{ A} $$
    *   You would choose an MPPT charge controller with a rating of at least 37.5A, typically a 40A or 50A controller for margin.

**2.6 Step 6: Inverter Sizing**

*   **Objective:** To select an inverter that can handle the peak AC load demand and the surge current required by some appliances (like motors).
*   **Key Parameters:**
    *   **Continuous Power Rating (W):** Must be greater than or equal to the total continuous AC load.
    *   **Peak Power Rating (W) / Surge Rating:** Must be sufficient to handle the starting surge of motors (e.g., refrigerators, pumps). This surge can be 3-7 times the continuous power.
    *   **Input Voltage (DC):** Must match the battery bank voltage.
    *   **Output Voltage and Frequency (AC):** Must match the grid standards or appliance requirements (e.g., 230V, 50Hz).
    *   **Inverter Efficiency:** Important for overall system performance.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Determine the continuous AC load.
    2.  Identify appliances with motors and their starting surge requirements.
    3.  Size the inverter to handle both continuous load and peak surge.
*   **Example:**
    *   Continuous AC Load: 285W (from the previous load assessment)
    *   Appliance with motor: Refrigerator (150W, surge factor of 5)
    *   Surge requirement for refrigerator = 150W * 5 = 750W
    *   Total continuous load = 285W
    *   Peak load considering surge = 285W (other loads) + 750W (refrigerator surge) = 1035W (This is a simplified assumption; it's better to sum peak loads of all appliances that might run simultaneously and add the largest surge).
    *   It's prudent to choose an inverter with a continuous rating significantly higher than the continuous load and a surge rating that can handle the largest motor. A 1000W continuous / 2000W surge inverter might be a good choice.

**2.7 Step 7: System Wiring and Protection**

*   **Objective:** To ensure safe and efficient interconnection of components with appropriate safety devices.
*   **Key Considerations:**
    *   **Wire Sizing:** Conductors must be sized to minimize voltage drop and prevent overheating, based on current and length. Standards like NEC (National Electrical Code) or IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) provide guidelines.
    *   **Fuses and Circuit Breakers:** For overcurrent protection of PV array, batteries, and loads.
    *   **Disconnect Switches:** To isolate components for maintenance or emergencies.
    *   **Grounding:** Essential for safety to prevent electric shock.
    *   **Lightning Protection:** Important in lightning-prone areas.

**2.8 Step 8: System Layout and Mounting**

*   **Objective:** To position PV modules for optimal sunlight exposure and ensure secure mounting.
*   **Key Considerations:**
    *   **Tilt Angle:** Usually set to the latitude of the location for year-round performance, or adjusted seasonally for optimal performance in winter or summer.
    *   **Orientation:** Facing true South in the Northern Hemisphere, true North in the Southern Hemisphere.
    *   **Shading:** Avoid shading from trees, buildings, or other obstructions throughout the day and year.
    *   **Mounting Structure:** Must withstand wind loads and environmental conditions.

**Important Note on Life Cycle Cost Analysis (CO4):** While not explicitly a design step in terms of component selection, understanding the cost implications of different component choices (e.g., battery type, inverter efficiency, array oversizing for longevity) is crucial for a cost-effective and sustainable system. This involves comparing initial capital costs with maintenance costs, replacement costs, and energy savings over the system's lifespan.

### 3. Storage Batteries and Ultra-Capacitors

**(Aligns with CO1, CO2)**

Energy storage is a cornerstone of stand-alone PV systems, enabling power availability when the sun isn't shining.

**3.1 Storage Batteries**

*   **Function:** Store DC electrical energy generated by the PV array and deliver it to the loads when needed.
*   **Key Parameters for Selection:**
    *   **Capacity (Ah/Wh):** As calculated in Step 4.
    *   **Voltage (V):** System voltage.
    *   **Depth of Discharge (DoD):** Affects lifespan.
    *   **Cycle Life:** The number of charge/discharge cycles a battery can undergo before its capacity degrades significantly.
    *   **Efficiency (Round-trip):** The energy lost during a charge-discharge cycle.
    *   **Cost:** Initial and lifetime cost.
    *   **Temperature Tolerance:** Performance and lifespan are affected by temperature.
    *   **Maintenance Requirements:** Some batteries require regular topping up of electrolyte.
*   **Common Battery Technologies for PV Systems:**
    *   **Lead-Acid Batteries:**
        *   **Types:** Flooded (vented), Sealed (AGM, Gel).
        *   **Advantages:** Mature technology, lower initial cost, readily available.
        *   **Disadvantages:** Lower energy density, shorter cycle life (especially with deep discharge), require maintenance (flooded), sensitive to temperature, lower DoD limit (typically 50% for good lifespan).
        *   **Reference:** Chetan Singh Solanki (PHI) discusses lead-acid battery characteristics extensively.
    *   **Lithium-Ion Batteries:**
        *   **Types:** LiFePO4 (Lithium Iron Phosphate), NMC (Nickel Manganese Cobalt), etc.
        *   **Advantages:** Higher energy density, longer cycle life, higher DoD limit (80-90%), lighter weight, faster charging, no memory effect, lower maintenance.
        *   **Disadvantages:** Higher initial cost, require sophisticated Battery Management Systems (BMS) for safety and optimal performance, can be more sensitive to extreme temperatures.
        *   **Reference:** G.N. Tiwari's book might touch upon advanced storage technologies.
    *   **Nickel-Cadmium (NiCd) and Nickel-Metal Hydride (NiMH) Batteries:** Less common in large PV systems due to cost and performance trade-offs compared to lead-acid or Li-ion.

**3.2 Ultra-Capacitors (Supercapacitors)**

*   **Function:** Store electrical energy electrostatically, unlike batteries which store it electrochemically.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   **Extremely High Power Density:** Can deliver and absorb large amounts of power very quickly.
    *   **Very Long Cycle Life:** Can undergo millions of charge-discharge cycles.
    *   **Fast Charging/Discharging:** Seconds or milliseconds.
    *   **Wide Operating Temperature Range:** Generally better than batteries.
    *   **Lower Energy Density:** Store significantly less energy than batteries for the same volume or weight.
    *   **Voltage Sag:** Voltage drops linearly with discharge, which can be challenging for some applications without voltage regulation.
*   **Applications in PV Systems:**
    *   **Hybrid Energy Storage:** Often used in conjunction with batteries to handle high power transients (e.g., rapid changes in solar irradiance, starting of motors) while the battery handles the bulk energy storage. This can improve battery lifespan.
    *   **Short-term Energy Buffering:** Smoothing out power fluctuations due to intermittency.
    *   **Peak Shaving:** Providing bursts of power to meet peak demand.
*   **Comparison with Batteries:**
    | Feature          | Batteries                                    | Ultra-Capacitors (Supercapacitors) |
    | :--------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- |
    | Energy Density   | High                                         | Low                                |
    | Power Density    | Low to Medium                                | Very High                          |
    | Cycle Life       | Hundreds to thousands                        | Millions                           |
    | Charge/Discharge | Slow (minutes to hours)                      | Very Fast (seconds to milliseconds) |
    | Voltage Drop     | Relatively flat discharge curve              | Linear voltage drop with discharge |
    | Efficiency       | Moderate (70-90%)                            | High (90-98%)                      |
    | Cost (per kWh)   | Moderate to High                             | Very High                          |
    | Cost (per kW)    | High                                         | Moderate                           |
    | Applications     | Bulk energy storage, long-duration power     | Power buffering, rapid charge/discharge |

**Important Note:** Ultra-capacitors are not typically a direct replacement for batteries in stand-alone PV systems due to their low energy density. Their role is more complementary, enhancing system performance and longevity.

### Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** A remote community requires a stand-alone PV system to power a school. The daily energy demand is 5 kWh. The site has an average of 3.5 Peak Sun Hours (PSH) during the worst month. Assuming a system derating factor of 0.70, calculate the required PV array capacity in Watts-peak (Wp).

**Answer:**
$$ \text{PV Array Size (Wp)} = \frac{5000 \text{ Wh/day}}{3.5 \text{ PSH} \times 0.70} = \frac{5000}{2.45} \approx 2041 \text{ Wp} $$
The required PV array capacity is approximately 2041 Wp.

**Question 2:** For the system in Question 1, the battery bank needs to provide 3 days of autonomy and operate with a maximum Depth of Discharge (DoD) of 50% (0.5). The system voltage is 48V. Calculate the total battery capacity required in Ampere-hours (Ah).

**Answer:**
$$ \text{Battery Capacity (Wh)} = \frac{5000 \text{ Wh/day} \times 3 \text{ days}}{0.50} = \frac{15000}{0.50} = 30000 \text{ Wh} $$
$$ \text{Battery Capacity (Ah)} = \frac{30000 \text{ Wh}}{48 \text{ V}} = 625 \text{ Ah} $$
The total battery capacity required is 625 Ah at 48V.

**Question 3:** Briefly explain the difference between a stand-alone PV system and a grid-connected PV system.

**Answer:**
A stand-alone PV system operates independently of the utility grid and requires energy storage (batteries) to provide power when solar generation is insufficient. A grid-connected PV system is connected to the utility grid, feeding excess power to the grid and drawing power from it when needed, typically without on-site battery storage.

**Question 4:** What is the primary advantage of using an MPPT charge controller over a PWM charge controller in a stand-alone PV system?

**Answer:**
The primary advantage of an MPPT charge controller is its higher efficiency. MPPT controllers actively track the maximum power point of the PV array, allowing them to convert higher array voltages to lower battery voltages more effectively. This results in capturing more energy from the PV array, especially under varying temperature and irradiance conditions, compared to PWM controllers which essentially pull the array voltage down to the battery voltage.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Load assessment is paramount:** Accurate estimation of daily energy consumption and peak demand is the foundation of any PV system design.
*   **Worst-case scenario:** Always design for the least sunny month to ensure reliable operation throughout the year.
*   **Derating factor:** Don't overlook system losses; they significantly impact the required PV array size.
*   **Battery longevity:** Respect the recommended Depth of Discharge (DoD) for your chosen battery technology to maximize its lifespan.
*   **MPPT vs. PWM:** MPPT controllers are generally preferred for stand-alone systems due to their superior efficiency.
*   **Inverter surge capacity:** Crucial for starting appliances with motors.
*   **Safety first:** Proper wiring, fusing, and grounding are essential for system safety.
*   **Ultra-capacitors complement, not replace:** They excel at power delivery, not energy storage.
