### 1. Introduction to Waste Heat Recovery (WHR)

**Key Concept:** Waste heat is defined as heat that is rejected from a process or system to the environment, rather than being used for a useful purpose.

**Definition:** Waste Heat Recovery (WHR) is the process of capturing heat that would otherwise be lost and repurposing it for another use within the same facility or a nearby one. This significantly reduces the primary energy input required and improves overall energy efficiency.

**Significance in Boiler Operations:** Boilers are inherently energy-intensive. They generate heat by combusting fuels, and a significant portion of this heat can be lost through flue gases, blowdown water, radiation, and convection. WHR systems aim to minimize these losses.

### 2. Sources of Waste Heat in Boilers

**Key Concept:** Identifying and quantifying waste heat sources is the first step in designing an effective WHR system.

**Common Sources:**

*   **Flue Gases:** This is the most significant source of waste heat in most boilers. Hot gases exiting the boiler (typically at temperatures of 150-300°C or higher) contain substantial thermal energy.
    *   **Factors influencing flue gas temperature:** Combustion efficiency, excess air, boiler load, and boiler design.
*   **Blowdown Water:** Water is continuously or intermittently removed from the boiler to prevent the buildup of dissolved solids. This blowdown water is hot (close to saturation temperature) and carries significant thermal energy.
    *   **Types of Blowdown:**
        *   **Continuous Blowdown:** Small, continuous stream to maintain a specific concentration of dissolved solids.
        *   **Intermittent (or Batch) Blowdown:** Larger volumes released periodically when dissolved solid concentrations reach a critical level.
*   **Radiant and Convective Losses:** Heat loss from the boiler shell and piping surfaces to the surrounding atmosphere. While typically smaller than flue gas losses, they can still be significant in poorly insulated systems.
*   **Ash Handling:** In solid fuel-fired boilers, hot ash removed from the furnace can also be a source of recoverable heat.

**Examples:**

*   A boiler operating with flue gas exiting at 250°C has a considerable amount of thermal energy that could be used for preheating combustion air or feedwater.
*   Hot blowdown water at 170°C discharged to a drain represents wasted thermal energy that could be used to preheat makeup water.

**Highlight:** **Flue gas is the primary target for waste heat recovery in most boiler systems.**

### 3. Waste Heat Recovery Systems (WHRS) for Boilers

**Key Concept:** WHRS technologies are designed to capture and transfer heat from one stream to another for a beneficial use.

**Types of WHRS Applicable to Boilers:**

*   **Economizers:**
    *   **Working Principle:** Economizers are heat exchangers that preheat the boiler feedwater using the heat from the flue gases before it enters the boiler drum.
    *   **Energy Saving Opportunity:** Reduces the amount of fuel required to heat the feedwater to its boiling point.
    *   **Typical Temperature Drop:** Flue gas temperature can be reduced by 30-70°C.
    *   **Example:** Preheating feedwater from 80°C to 120°C using flue gas heat. This saves approximately 1% of fuel for every 10°C rise in feedwater temperature (assuming constant heat output).
    *   **Textbook Reference:** *Energy Management and Conservation Handbook* by D. Yogi Goswami and Frank Kreith (CRC Press, 2007) extensively covers economizers as a key thermal recovery device.
*   **Air Preheaters (APH):**
    *   **Working Principle:** Air preheaters recover heat from the flue gases to preheat the combustion air before it enters the furnace.
    *   **Energy Saving Opportunity:** Reduces the amount of fuel needed to heat the combustion air. Also, preheated air leads to improved combustion efficiency, potentially reducing excess air requirements and further enhancing savings.
    *   **Typical Temperature Drop:** Flue gas temperature can be reduced by 20-50°C.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Recuperative:** Direct transfer of heat through a separating wall (e.g., tubular, plate).
        *   **Regenerative:** Heat is absorbed by a mass (e.g., rotating matrix, stationary matrix) and then transferred to the other fluid.
    *   **Example:** Preheating combustion air from ambient temperature (e.g., 25°C) to 150°C using flue gas heat.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** *Industrial energy conservation* by Charles M. Gottschalk (John Wiley & Sons, 1996) discusses various types of air preheaters and their application.
*   **Waste Heat Boilers (WHB):**
    *   **Working Principle:** Dedicated boilers designed to generate steam or hot water by utilizing the heat from high-temperature flue gases or other waste streams.
    *   **Energy Saving Opportunity:** Produces additional steam or hot water that can be used for process heating, power generation, or space heating, thereby reducing reliance on primary fuel.
    *   **Example:** A waste heat boiler attached to a gas turbine exhaust can generate steam for a steam turbine, increasing overall power output. In industrial boilers, it can be used to generate process steam.
*   **Blowdown Heat Recovery Systems:**
    *   **Working Principle:** Utilize heat exchangers to transfer the thermal energy from hot blowdown water to preheat the incoming makeup water.
    *   **Energy Saving Opportunity:** Reduces the fuel required to heat the makeup water, especially significant for boilers with high blowdown rates.
    *   **Types:** Flash tanks (where some hot blowdown water flashes into steam, which can then be used for preheating) and heat exchangers.
    *   **Example:** Using a heat exchanger to recover heat from continuous blowdown to preheat makeup water from 20°C to 50°C.
*   **Combined Systems:** Often, multiple WHR technologies are integrated for maximum efficiency. For example, flue gas might first pass through an air preheater and then an economizer.

### 4. Energy Saving Opportunities and Quantification

**Key Concept:** Quantifying the energy savings is crucial for justifying WHR investments.

**General Principles for Quantifying Savings:**

*   **Heat Balance:** Understanding the energy flow into and out of the boiler is fundamental.
*   **Specific Fuel Consumption:** The amount of fuel required to produce a unit of steam or a unit of heat.
*   **Temperature Difference (ΔT):** The driving force for heat transfer and a key factor in determining recoverable heat.
*   **Specific Heat Capacity:** The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a unit mass of a substance by one degree.

**Quantification of Savings (Example: Economizer):**

Let:
*   $m_{fw}$ = mass flow rate of feedwater (kg/hr)
*   $c_{p,fw}$ = specific heat capacity of feedwater (kJ/kg°C)
*   $T_{in,fw}$ = inlet temperature of feedwater (°C)
*   $T_{out,fw}$ = outlet temperature of feedwater (°C)
*   $Q_{rec}$ = heat recovered by the economizer (kJ/hr)
*   $E_{fuel}$ = energy input from fuel (kJ/hr)
*   $\eta_{boiler}$ = boiler efficiency

**Heat Recovered by Economizer:**
$Q_{rec} = m_{fw} \times c_{p,fw} \times (T_{out,fw} - T_{in,fw})$

**Fuel Saved:**
The heat recovered by the economizer directly reduces the amount of fuel needed.
Fuel Saved (in terms of heat energy) = $Q_{rec}$

**Percentage Fuel Saving:**
Percentage Fuel Saving = $(Q_{rec} / E_{fuel}) \times 100\%$
Since $E_{fuel} = (Q_{output} / \eta_{boiler})$, where $Q_{output}$ is the useful heat delivered as steam.
A simpler approximation often used is:
Percentage Fuel Saving $\approx (Q_{rec} / Q_{sensible\_heat\_required}) \times 100\%$
where $Q_{sensible\_heat\_required}$ is the sensible heat required to raise the feedwater from its inlet to its boiling point in the boiler drum.

**Rule of Thumb:** For every 10°C rise in feedwater temperature due to an economizer, fuel consumption is reduced by approximately 1%. This is a simplified but useful guideline.

**Quantification of Savings (Example: Air Preheater):**

Let:
*   $m_{air}$ = mass flow rate of combustion air (kg/hr)
*   $c_{p,air}$ = specific heat capacity of air (kJ/kg°C)
*   $T_{in,air}$ = inlet temperature of combustion air (°C)
*   $T_{out,air}$ = outlet temperature of combustion air (°C)
*   $Q_{rec,APH}$ = heat recovered by APH (kJ/hr)

**Heat Recovered by Air Preheater:**
$Q_{rec,APH} = m_{air} \times c_{p,air} \times (T_{out,air} - T_{in,air})$

**Fuel Saved:** Similar to the economizer, this heat directly reduces the fuel requirement.

**Consideration for Combustion Efficiency:** Preheating combustion air improves combustion efficiency, which can lead to further fuel savings beyond the direct heat recovery. This is harder to quantify without detailed combustion analysis but is a significant benefit.

**Quantification of Savings (Example: Blowdown Heat Recovery):**

Let:
*   $m_{bd}$ = mass flow rate of blowdown (kg/hr)
*   $c_{p,water}$ = specific heat capacity of water (kJ/kg°C)
*   $T_{bd}$ = temperature of blowdown water (°C)
*   $T_{makeup}$ = temperature of makeup water (°C)
*   $Q_{rec,BD}$ = heat recovered from blowdown (kJ/hr)

**Heat Recovered from Blowdown:**
$Q_{rec,BD} = m_{bd} \times c_{p,water} \times (T_{bd} - T_{makeup})$

**Percentage Blowdown:** This is crucial. If blowdown is 5% of boiler feedwater flow, it means 5% of the total steam produced is lost as blowdown.
Percentage Fuel Saving from Blowdown Recovery = $(Q_{rec,BD} / E_{fuel\_for\_makeup}) \times 100\%$
Where $E_{fuel\_for\_makeup}$ is the fuel energy used to heat the makeup water to the same extent as the recovered heat.

### 5. Economic Feasibility of WHR Systems

**Key Concept:** The economic viability of a WHR system is determined by comparing its costs with the benefits (energy savings).

**Key Economic Parameters:**

*   **Capital Cost (CAPEX):** Initial investment for purchasing and installing the WHR system (e.g., economizer, APH, heat exchangers).
*   **Operating & Maintenance Cost (OPEX):** Ongoing costs for running and maintaining the system (e.g., cleaning, repairs, pump energy).
*   **Energy Savings:** Calculated monetary value of the fuel saved per year.
*   **Payback Period:** The time it takes for the cumulative energy savings to recover the initial capital investment.
    *   $Payback\;Period = CAPEX / Annual\;Energy\;Savings$
*   **Return on Investment (ROI):** Measures the profitability of the investment.
    *   $ROI = (Annual\;Energy\;Savings - Annual\;OPEX) / CAPEX \times 100\%$
*   **Net Present Value (NPV):** Considers the time value of money, discounting future savings to their present value.
*   **Internal Rate of Return (IRR):** The discount rate at which the NPV of an investment becomes zero.

**Factors Influencing Feasibility:**

*   **Temperature of Waste Heat Source:** Higher temperatures generally lead to more efficient heat recovery and higher savings.
*   **Flow Rate of Waste Heat Source:** Higher flow rates mean more available thermal energy.
*   **Operating Hours of the Boiler:** The longer the boiler operates, the higher the annual savings.
*   **Fuel Cost:** Higher fuel prices make energy savings more valuable.
*   **Cost of WHR System:** Competitive pricing and efficient design are crucial.
*   **Boiler Efficiency:** Already efficient boilers might have less room for improvement.
*   **Availability of a Use for Recovered Heat:** The recovered heat must be used effectively to generate savings.

**Textbook Reference:** *Energy Management Hand Book* by Wayne C. Turner (The Fairmount Press, Inc., 1997) provides detailed chapters on economic analysis of energy conservation projects.

### 6. Integrated Energy Management and Boiler WHR

**Key Concept:** WHR is not an isolated solution but part of a holistic energy management strategy.

**Interconnections with Other Energy Management Areas:**

*   **Boiler Efficiency Improvement:** WHR systems complement efforts to improve boiler combustion efficiency, insulation, and blowdown control.
*   **Demand Side Management (DSM):** By reducing the overall energy demand of the facility through efficient boiler operation, WHR contributes to DSM efforts.
*   **Cogeneration/Combined Heat and Power (CHP):** Waste heat boilers can be a component of CHP systems, where both electricity and useful heat are generated.
*   **Process Integration:** Recovered heat can be directly integrated into other plant processes requiring thermal energy.
*   **Energy Auditing:** WHR opportunities are identified and quantified during energy audits.

**Question 1 (CO1, CO4 - K2):**
Explain the significance of waste heat recovery in the context of energy management for electrical utilities with boilers. Identify at least two distinct sources of waste heat in a typical boiler system.

**Answer:**
Significance: Waste heat recovery is crucial in energy management for boilers as it allows for the capture and reuse of thermal energy that would otherwise be lost to the environment. This directly reduces the amount of primary fuel required to generate steam, leading to lower operating costs, reduced greenhouse gas emissions, and improved overall energy efficiency of the utility.

Sources of Waste Heat:
1.  **Flue Gases:** Hot exhaust gases leaving the boiler.
2.  **Blowdown Water:** Hot water continuously or intermittently removed to control dissolved solids.

---

**Question 2 (CO2, CO4 - K2):**
Describe the working principle of an economizer and how it contributes to energy savings in a boiler.

**Answer:**
An economizer is a type of heat exchanger installed in the path of hot flue gases exiting the boiler. Its working principle is to preheat the incoming feedwater using the thermal energy present in these flue gases. The hot flue gases pass over tubes containing relatively cooler feedwater. Heat is transferred from the flue gases to the feedwater, raising the feedwater temperature before it enters the boiler drum.

This preheating reduces the amount of fuel the boiler needs to burn to bring the feedwater to its boiling point, thus saving fuel and lowering operating costs. For every 10°C increase in feedwater temperature achieved through an economizer, approximately 1% of fuel can be saved (under ideal conditions).

**Question 3 (CO5 - K3):**
A boiler consumes 1000 kg of fuel per hour, and the calorific value of the fuel is 40,000 kJ/kg. The boiler produces steam at an efficiency of 85%. An economizer is installed that preheats the feedwater by 30°C. The feedwater flow rate is 20,000 kg/hr, and its specific heat capacity is 4.18 kJ/kg°C. Calculate the annual fuel saving in GJ per year, assuming the boiler operates for 8000 hours per year.

**Solution:**

1.  **Calculate the heat recovered by the economizer per hour:**
    $Q_{rec} = m_{fw} \times c_{p,fw} \times \Delta T_{fw}$
    $Q_{rec} = 20,000 \, \text{kg/hr} \times 4.18 \, \text{kJ/kg°C} \times 30 \, \text{°C}$
    $Q_{rec} = 2,508,000 \, \text{kJ/hr}$

2.  **Calculate the total heat input from fuel per hour:**
    $E_{fuel\_input} = \text{Fuel consumption per hour} \times \text{Calorific value}$
    $E_{fuel\_input} = 1000 \, \text{kg/hr} \times 40,000 \, \text{kJ/kg}$
    $E_{fuel\_input} = 40,000,000 \, \text{kJ/hr}$

3.  **Calculate the percentage of fuel saved per hour:**
    $\% \text{Fuel Saving} = (Q_{rec} / E_{fuel\_input}) \times 100\%$
    $\% \text{Fuel Saving} = (2,508,000 \, \text{kJ/hr} / 40,000,000 \, \text{kJ/hr}) \times 100\%$
    $\% \text{Fuel Saving} = 6.27\%$

4.  **Calculate the annual fuel saving in kJ:**
    Annual Fuel Saving (kJ) = $Q_{rec} \times \text{Operating hours per year}$
    Annual Fuel Saving (kJ) = $2,508,000 \, \text{kJ/hr} \times 8000 \, \text{hr/year}$
    Annual Fuel Saving (kJ) = $20,064,000,000 \, \text{kJ/year}$

5.  **Convert kJ to GJ:**
    1 GJ = $10^9$ kJ
    Annual Fuel Saving (GJ) = $20,064,000,000 \, \text{kJ/year} / 10^9 \, \text{kJ/GJ}$
    Annual Fuel Saving (GJ) = **20.064 GJ/year**

**Question 4 (CO5 - K3):**
A company is considering installing a blowdown heat recovery system for its boiler. The continuous blowdown rate is 5% of the boiler's feedwater flow of 30,000 kg/hr. The blowdown water is at 180°C, and the makeup water is at 25°C. The cost of fuel is $10/GJ (for heating the makeup water). If the capital cost of the system is $50,000 and it has negligible operating costs, calculate the payback period for the investment. (Assume specific heat of water is 4.18 kJ/kg°C).

**Solution:**

1.  **Calculate the mass flow rate of blowdown:**
    Blowdown flow rate = 5% of 30,000 kg/hr = 0.05 * 30,000 kg/hr = 1500 kg/hr

2.  **Calculate the heat recovered from blowdown per hour:**
    $Q_{rec,BD} = m_{bd} \times c_{p,water} \times (T_{bd} - T_{makeup})$
    $Q_{rec,BD} = 1500 \, \text{kg/hr} \times 4.18 \, \text{kJ/kg°C} \times (180 \, \text{°C} - 25 \, \text{°C})$
    $Q_{rec,BD} = 1500 \, \text{kg/hr} \times 4.18 \, \text{kJ/kg°C} \times 155 \, \text{°C}$
    $Q_{rec,BD} = 971,550 \, \text{kJ/hr}$

3.  **Calculate the annual heat recovered in GJ:**
    Annual heat recovered (kJ) = $971,550 \, \text{kJ/hr} \times 8000 \, \text{hr/year}$ (assuming 8000 operating hours)
    Annual heat recovered (kJ) = $7,772,400,000 \, \text{kJ/year}$
    Annual heat recovered (GJ) = $7,772,400,000 \, \text{kJ/year} / 10^9 \, \text{kJ/GJ}$
    Annual heat recovered (GJ) = $7.7724 \, \text{GJ/year}$

4.  **Calculate the annual monetary savings:**
    Annual Savings = Annual heat recovered (GJ) $\times$ Cost of fuel per GJ
    Annual Savings = $7.7724 \, \text{GJ/year} \times \$10/\text{GJ}$
    Annual Savings = $\$77,724/\text{year}$

5.  **Calculate the payback period:**
    Payback Period = Capital Cost / Annual Savings
    Payback Period = $\$50,000 / \$77,724/\text{year}$
    Payback Period $\approx$ **0.64 years** (or approximately 7.7 months)
