# Power System Operation and Control - Module 2: Generation with Limited Supply

## Topic: Hydro-electric Plant Models and Scheduling Energy Problems

This module focuses on understanding how hydro-electric power plants are modeled and the complexities involved in scheduling their energy output, especially when their primary resource – water – is limited. This is a crucial aspect of ensuring reliable and economic power system operation.

### 1. Hydro-electric Plant Models

Hydro-electric plants convert the potential energy of water stored at a higher elevation into electrical energy. Their operation is fundamentally tied to the available water resources, making them a unique generation source with inherent limitations.

#### 1.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Reservoir:** A man-made or natural body of water impounded by a dam, used for storing water for various purposes, including power generation.
*   **Head:** The vertical distance between the water surface in the reservoir and the turbine runner. Higher head generally means more efficient energy conversion.
*   **Flow Rate (Discharge):** The volume of water passing through the turbine per unit time. This is the primary control variable for power output.
*   **Water Volume:** The total amount of water stored in the reservoir. This represents the "fuel" for the hydro-plant.
*   **Plant Efficiency:** The ratio of electrical energy output to the potential energy of the water passing through the turbines. This efficiency can vary with flow rate and head.
*   **Hydraulic Turbine:** The device that converts the kinetic and potential energy of water into mechanical energy. Common types include Francis, Pelton, and Kaplan turbines.
*   **Generator:** Converts the mechanical energy from the turbine into electrical energy.

#### 1.2. Mathematical Models of Hydro-electric Plants

The primary objective of modeling a hydro-plant is to relate its power output to the water available and the operational constraints.

*   **Power Output Equation:**
    The power output ($P$) of a hydro-electric plant can be approximated by:
    $P = \eta \times \rho \times g \times Q \times H$

    Where:
    *   $P$ is the power output (Watts)
    *   $\eta$ is the overall efficiency of the plant (turbine and generator)
    *   $\rho$ is the density of water (approximately 1000 kg/m³)
    *   $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity (approximately 9.81 m/s²)
    *   $Q$ is the flow rate (discharge) through the turbine (m³/s)
    *   $H$ is the effective head (meters)

    *Important Note:* In practice, the efficiency $\eta$ is not constant and can be a function of flow rate $Q$ and head $H$. Similarly, the head $H$ itself can change as the reservoir level drops.

*   **Reservoir Level and Volume Relationship:**
    The volume of water ($V$) in the reservoir is a function of its level ($h$). This relationship is often non-linear and depends on the shape of the reservoir.
    $V = f(h)$
    Conversely, the head $H$ is related to the reservoir level:
    $H = h - h_t$
    where $h_t$ is the tailrace water level.

*   **Water Continuity Equation:**
    The change in reservoir volume over a time interval ($\Delta t$) is determined by the inflow ($I$), outflow ($Q_{out}$), and evaporation/seepage losses ($L$).
    $V(t+\Delta t) - V(t) = (I(t) - Q_{out}(t) - L(t)) \times \Delta t$
    For scheduling purposes, $Q_{out}(t)$ is the sum of water used for power generation ($Q_{gen}(t)$) and water released for other purposes (e.g., irrigation, flood control, $Q_{other}(t)$).
    $Q_{out}(t) = Q_{gen}(t) + Q_{other}(t)$

    The term $Q_{gen}(t)$ is directly related to the power generated by the hydro-plant. If we consider a specific power output $P_{gen}(t)$, the required flow rate $Q_{gen}(t)$ can be determined from the power output equation, assuming known head and efficiency.

#### 1.3. Simplified Hydro-Electric Plant Model for Scheduling

For scheduling problems, simplified models are often used to capture the essential behavior without excessive complexity.

*   **Constant Efficiency Model:** Assume a constant efficiency $\eta$. The power output is then directly proportional to the flow rate and head.
*   **Linearized Head-Flow Relationship:** The relationship between head and reservoir level, and reservoir level and volume, can be approximated linearly over a certain operating range.
*   **Marginal Cost of Water:** The cost of using water is often represented by the opportunity cost of not using it for another purpose or the value of stored energy for future use. This is a critical input for economic dispatch.

### 2. Scheduling Energy Problems

Scheduling energy problems involve determining the optimal operation of power generation units over a specific time horizon to meet demand while minimizing costs, respecting system constraints, and ensuring reliability. For hydro-electric plants, the "limited supply" aspect – the available water – introduces significant complexity.

#### 2.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Economic Dispatch:** The process of allocating generation among available power plants to meet the total system load at the minimum operating cost, subject to the generating units' constraints.
*   **Unit Commitment:** The process of deciding which generating units to start up or shut down over a given period and how to dispatch them to meet the forecasted load, considering start-up and shut-down costs, as well as operating costs.
*   **Hydro-Thermal Scheduling:** A combined problem that involves scheduling both thermal (e.g., coal, gas, nuclear) and hydro-electric power plants to minimize the overall system operating cost. This is where the limited supply of water becomes a central challenge.
*   **Time Horizon:** The period over which the scheduling is performed (e.g., daily, weekly, monthly).
*   **Load Forecast:** The prediction of the total power demand for the system over the scheduling horizon.
*   **Water Value (Marginal Value of Water):** The incremental change in the total system cost resulting from an incremental increase in the available water for power generation. This is a key concept in hydro-thermal scheduling.

#### 2.2. Hydro-Thermal Scheduling Problem Formulation

The objective of hydro-thermal scheduling is to minimize the total operating cost of the system over the scheduling horizon. The total cost is typically the sum of fuel costs for thermal units and the opportunity cost of using water for hydro units.

**Objective Function:**
Minimize $\sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \sum_{i \in Thermal} C_{thermal,i}(P_{thermal,i}(t)) + \sum_{j \in Hydro} C_{hydro,j}(P_{hydro,j}(t), V_j(t)) \right)$

Where:
*   $T$ is the total number of time intervals in the horizon.
*   $C_{thermal,i}(P_{thermal,i}(t))$ is the fuel cost of thermal unit $i$ at time $t$.
*   $C_{hydro,j}(P_{hydro,j}(t), V_j(t))$ is the cost associated with using water for hydro unit $j$ at time $t$, which often reflects the marginal value of water and the change in reservoir storage $V_j(t)$.

**Constraints:**

1.  **Power Balance:** The total generation must equal the total demand plus losses.
    $\sum_{i \in Thermal} P_{thermal,i}(t) + \sum_{j \in Hydro} P_{hydro,j}(t) = P_{demand}(t) + P_{losses}(t)$

2.  **Hydro Plant Generation Constraints:**
    *   **Power Output Limits:**
        $P_{hydro,min,j}(t) \le P_{hydro,j}(t) \le P_{hydro,max,j}(t)$
    *   **Flow Rate Limits:** The power output is directly related to the flow rate, which is also constrained by the turbine capacity.
        $Q_{hydro,min,j}(t) \le Q_{hydro,j}(t) \le Q_{hydro,max,j}(t)$
        where $P_{hydro,j}(t)$ is determined by $Q_{hydro,j}(t)$ and $H_j(t)$.
    *   **Reservoir Water Balance:**
        $V_j(t+1) = V_j(t) + I_j(t) - Q_{gen,j}(t) - Q_{other,j}(t) - L_j(t)$
    *   **Reservoir Storage Limits:**
        $V_{j,min} \le V_j(t) \le V_{j,max}$
    *   **Initial and Final Reservoir Levels:** Often, specific reservoir levels are required at the beginning and end of the scheduling horizon.
        $V_j(1) = V_{j,initial}$
        $V_j(T+1) = V_{j,final}$

3.  **Thermal Plant Constraints:**
    *   **Generation Limits:**
        $P_{thermal,min,i} \le P_{thermal,i}(t) \le P_{thermal,max,i}$
    *   **Ramp Rate Limits:** The rate at which a thermal unit can change its output.
        $P_{thermal,i}(t) - P_{thermal,i}(t-1) \le RampRate_{up,i}$
        $P_{thermal,i}(t-1) - P_{thermal,i}(t) \le RampRate_{down,i}$
    *   **Minimum Up/Down Time:** Constraints on how long a unit must remain online or offline once started or stopped.

4.  **System-Wide Constraints:**
    *   **Transmission Constraints:** Limits on power flow over transmission lines. (More complex, often handled in security-constrained economic dispatch).
    *   **Reserve Requirements:** A certain amount of reserve capacity must be available to respond to unexpected changes in load or generation.

#### 2.3. Incorporating Water Value

The "value" of water is crucial. It's not just about the immediate cost of generating power but also the long-term impact of depleting the reservoir.

*   **Marginal Water Cost:** If water is released for generation now, it cannot be used later when it might be more valuable (e.g., during a dry season or for peak demand). This is captured by the Lagrange multiplier associated with the reservoir water balance equation.
*   **Dynamic Programming and Gradient Methods:** These optimization techniques are commonly used to solve hydro-thermal scheduling problems, explicitly incorporating the water value.

### 3. Types of Scheduling Problems

Scheduling problems can be categorized based on their objective, scope, and the types of units considered.

#### 3.1. Classification of Scheduling Problems

1.  **Economic Dispatch (ED):**
    *   **Objective:** Minimize immediate operating cost to meet the current load.
    *   **Time Horizon:** Real-time or short-term (e.g., 15-minute intervals).
    *   **Units Involved:** Primarily thermal units, but hydro can be included if water availability is not the primary constraint being optimized.
    *   **Key Aspect:** Assumes all units are online and available.

2.  **Unit Commitment (UC):**
    *   **Objective:** Minimize operating and start-up/shut-down costs over a longer horizon (e.g., 24 hours, a week).
    *   **Time Horizon:** Medium-term (e.g., hourly intervals for 24 hours).
    *   **Units Involved:** Both thermal and, potentially, hydro units.
    *   **Key Aspect:** Determines which units to start/stop to meet forecasted load and capacity requirements, considering commitment costs and operating constraints.

3.  **Hydro-Thermal Scheduling (HTS):**
    *   **Objective:** Minimize total system operating cost (fuel for thermal + water cost for hydro) over a longer period (e.g., weekly, monthly, seasonally).
    *   **Time Horizon:** Longer than UC, often considering the availability of water over weeks or months.
    *   **Units Involved:** Both thermal and hydro units are centrally coordinated.
    *   **Key Aspect:** Explicitly manages the reservoir levels of hydro plants to optimize the use of water over the entire horizon, considering the marginal value of water. This is a crucial problem where "limited supply" is central.

4.  **Security-Constrained Economic Dispatch (SCED) / Security-Constrained Unit Commitment (SCUC):**
    *   **Objective:** Minimize operating cost while ensuring system security (no overloaded transmission lines or voltage violations) under various contingencies (e.g., loss of a transmission line or generator).
    *   **Time Horizon:** Can be real-time (SCED) or short/medium-term (SCUC).
    *   **Units Involved:** All available units.
    *   **Key Aspect:** Integrates transmission network constraints into the dispatch and commitment decisions.

5.  **Stochastic Scheduling / Probabilistic Scheduling:**
    *   **Objective:** Minimize expected operating cost considering uncertainties in load forecasts, generator availability, and water inflow.
    *   **Time Horizon:** Varies.
    *   **Units Involved:** All units.
    *   **Key Aspect:** Uses probabilistic methods to hedge against uncertainties.

#### 3.2. Focus on "Limited Supply" in Hydro Scheduling

The core challenge in scheduling hydro-electric plants with limited supply lies in balancing the immediate need for power (which requires using water) against the future value of stored water.

*   **Longer Horizon:** Hydro-thermal scheduling typically addresses this by using longer time horizons (weekly, monthly) to allow for strategic water management.
*   **Water Value as a Cost Component:** The value of water is incorporated into the objective function. If water is plentiful, its value might be low, allowing for high hydro generation. If water is scarce, its value increases, leading to more conservative use and reliance on thermal generation.
*   **Interdependence:** Hydro plants are often dispatched to complement thermal generation, providing peaking capacity or operating during periods when thermal fuel costs are high.

#### 3.4. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
A hydro-electric plant has a turbine efficiency of 85%, and the density of water is 1000 kg/m³. Assume g = 9.81 m/s². If the head is 50 meters and the plant needs to generate 100 MW, what is the required flow rate of water through the turbine?

**Answer 1:**
The power output equation is $P = \eta \times \rho \times g \times Q \times H$.
We are given:
$P = 100 \, MW = 100 \times 10^6 \, W$
$\eta = 0.85$
$\rho = 1000 \, kg/m^3$
$g = 9.81 \, m/s^2$
$H = 50 \, m$

Rearranging the formula to solve for $Q$:
$Q = \frac{P}{\eta \times \rho \times g \times H}$
$Q = \frac{100 \times 10^6 \, W}{0.85 \times 1000 \, kg/m^3 \times 9.81 \, m/s^2 \times 50 \, m}$
$Q = \frac{100 \times 10^6}{416925} \, m^3/s$
$Q \approx 239.85 \, m^3/s$

**Question 2:**
Explain the concept of "water value" in the context of hydro-thermal scheduling. Why is it important for optimizing system operation with limited water supply?

**Answer 2:**
The "water value" in hydro-thermal scheduling refers to the marginal benefit of having an additional unit of water available for power generation. It represents the opportunity cost of using water now versus saving it for later. This value is typically determined by the difference in system operating cost if one more unit of water is available.

It is crucial because:
1.  **Optimization:** It allows for the optimal allocation of a limited resource (water) over time. If water is scarce, its value will be high, encouraging its conservation for periods of higher demand or higher thermal generation costs.
2.  **Balancing Short-term vs. Long-term:** It helps balance the immediate need for electricity (which requires water release) with the long-term benefits of maintaining reservoir storage for future use.
3.  **Economic Dispatch:** Incorporating the water value into the cost of hydro generation ensures that hydro plants are dispatched economically in conjunction with thermal plants, minimizing overall system operating expenses.

**Question 3:**
Differentiate between Economic Dispatch (ED) and Hydro-Thermal Scheduling (HTS). What is the primary difference in their scope and objective?

**Answer 3:**
*   **Economic Dispatch (ED):**
    *   **Scope:** Focuses on the immediate dispatch of available generating units (primarily thermal) to meet the current system load at minimum operating cost. It assumes units are already committed to be online.
    *   **Objective:** Minimize immediate fuel costs. It does not typically consider start-up/shut-down costs or the strategic management of limited resources like water over a longer horizon.

*   **Hydro-Thermal Scheduling (HTS):**
    *   **Scope:** A more comprehensive problem that coordinates the operation of both hydro and thermal units over a longer time horizon (e.g., weekly, monthly). It considers the commitment of units, the sequencing of operations, and the management of water stored in reservoirs.
    *   **Objective:** Minimize the total system operating cost, which includes both thermal fuel costs and the opportunity cost (value) of using water. It explicitly manages the limited water supply to ensure optimal system performance over the entire period.

### 4. Important Points to Remember

*   Hydro-electric plants are characterized by their dependence on water availability, making their scheduling distinct from purely thermal systems.
*   The power output of a hydro plant is a function of head, flow rate, and efficiency, all of which can vary.
*   The reservoir water balance equation is fundamental to modeling hydro plant operation over time.
*   Hydro-thermal scheduling aims to minimize overall system cost by optimally coordinating hydro and thermal generation.
*   The "water value" is a critical concept in hydro-thermal scheduling, representing the opportunity cost of using water and influencing dispatch decisions.
*   Scheduling problems can be classified into various types (ED, UC, HTS, SCED/SCUC) based on their objectives, time horizons, and the constraints considered.
*   The management of limited water supply is a primary concern for hydro-thermal scheduling, requiring careful consideration of water storage levels and future water availability.
