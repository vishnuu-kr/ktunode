# HVDC AND FACTS: Module 1 - Introduction to HVDC System: Comparison of AC and DC Transmission

## Topic: HVDC Controls

### 1. Functions of HVDC Controls

HVDC transmission systems require sophisticated control systems to ensure stable, reliable, and efficient operation. The primary functions of HVDC controls include:

*   **Power Transfer Control:** Precisely regulating the amount of power flowing through the DC link. This is crucial for meeting load demands and coordinating with the AC grid.
*   **Converter Control:** Managing the firing angles of the thyristors (or other switching devices) in the converters to control the DC voltage and current.
*   **AC System Stability Enhancement:** HVDC links can be used to damp oscillations in the AC system and improve transient stability by rapidly adjusting power transfer.
*   **Protection:** Implementing protection schemes to detect and isolate faults in the DC link or connected AC systems.
*   **Reactive Power Control:** Managing the reactive power consumed or generated by the converters to maintain desired voltage levels in the connected AC systems.
*   **Minimizing Harmonics:** Controlling the converters to reduce the generation of harmonic currents that can affect the AC system.
*   **Smooth Power Transfer:** Ensuring that power can be smoothly ramped up or down during normal operation and during system disturbances.

**Reference:**
*   *HVDC and FACTS Controllers* by Vijay K Sood (Springer, 2004) - Chapter 3, "Control of HVDC Systems."
*   *High Voltage DC Transmission* by K.R.Padiyar (Wiley, 1993) - Chapter 5, "Control of HVDC Converters."

### 2. Equivalent Circuit for a Two-Terminal DC Link

A two-terminal DC link can be represented by a simplified equivalent circuit for control analysis. This circuit typically includes:

*   **Rectifier Station (Converter 1):**
    *   Represents the AC voltage source connected to the rectifier.
    *   The rectifier itself is modeled as a DC voltage source whose magnitude is controlled by the firing angle ($\alpha$).
    *   Includes an effective DC resistance ($R_{dc}$) representing the total losses in the DC circuit (converter losses, line resistance).
*   **Inverter Station (Converter 2):**
    *   Represents the AC voltage source connected to the inverter.
    *   The inverter is also modeled as a DC voltage source controlled by its firing angle ($\beta$) or extinction angle ($\gamma$).
    *   Includes its own effective DC resistance ($R_{dc}$).
*   **DC Line:**
    *   Modeled as a resistance ($R_{dc}$) representing the total resistance of the DC line, smoothing reactor (if present), and converter internal resistance.

**Basic Equation:**

For a two-terminal DC link, the relationship between voltage and current at both ends can be expressed as:

$V_{d1} = V_{d01} \cos(\alpha_1) - I_d R_{dc1}$
$V_{d2} = V_{d02} \cos(\alpha_2) - I_d R_{dc2}$ (for rectifier operation at both ends)

Or for a rectifier and inverter system:

$V_{d1} = V_{d01} \cos(\alpha_1) - I_d R_{dc1}$ (Rectifier)
$V_{d2} = V_{d02} \cos(\alpha_2) + I_d R_{dc2}$ (Inverter, with $\alpha_2$ typically replaced by $\gamma$)

Where:
*   $V_{d1}$, $V_{d2}$: DC voltage at rectifier and inverter terminals respectively.
*   $V_{d01}$, $V_{d02}$: Ideal no-load DC voltage of the rectifier and inverter respectively.
*   $\alpha_1$: Firing angle of the rectifier.
*   $\alpha_2$: Firing angle of the inverter (often controlled via extinction angle $\gamma$).
*   $I_d$: DC current.
*   $R_{dc1}$, $R_{dc2}$: Equivalent DC resistance of rectifier and inverter circuits, respectively.

**Simplified DC Circuit Equation:**

$V_{d1} - V_{d2} = I_d (R_{dc1} + R_{dc2} + R_{line})$

Assuming $R_{dc1} \approx R_{dc2} \approx R_{dc\_station}$ and $R_{line}$ is the DC line resistance, then:

$V_{d1} - V_{d2} = I_d (2R_{dc\_station} + R_{line})$

For simplified control analysis, the DC circuit can be represented as a single DC voltage source and a total DC resistance.

**Reference:**
*   *HVDC and FACTS Controllers* by Vijay K Sood (Springer, 2004) - Chapter 2, "Basic HVDC Transmission."
*   *High Voltage DC Transmission* by K.R.Padiyar (Wiley, 1993) - Chapter 3, "Basic DC Link Equations."

### 3. Control Basics for a Two-Terminal DC Link

The fundamental principle of controlling a two-terminal HVDC link is to regulate the DC current ($I_d$) and/or DC voltage ($V_d$) by adjusting the firing angles of the converters.

*   **Constant Current (CC) Control:**
    *   The rectifier is typically controlled to maintain a constant DC current. This is the most common control mode for stable operation.
    *   The inverter operates in a voltage-dependent current order (VDCI) mode or constant extinction angle (CEA) mode to ensure proper commutation and stable power reversal if needed.
*   **Constant Voltage (CV) Control:**
    *   Less common, but can be used in specific scenarios. The rectifier is controlled to maintain a constant DC voltage.
*   **Constant Extinction Angle (CEA) Control (Inverter):**
    *   The inverter's firing angle ($\alpha_2$) is adjusted to maintain a constant extinction angle ($\gamma$). This is crucial for reliable commutation of thyristors in the inverter and prevents commutation failures.
*   **Constant Firing Angle (CFA) Control (Rectifier):**
    *   Used as a backup or for specific control objectives. The rectifier's firing angle ($\alpha_1$) is kept constant.

**Control Signal Flow:**

A typical control system involves:
1.  **Reference Signal:** A desired DC current or voltage command.
2.  **Feedback Signal:** The measured DC current or voltage.
3.  **Controller (e.g., PI controller):** Compares the reference and feedback signals and generates an output signal.
4.  **Firing Angle Regulator:** Translates the controller output into a firing angle command for the converter.

**Hierarchy of Controls:** In practical HVDC systems, multiple control levels are implemented to ensure robust operation under various conditions, as discussed in section 6.

### 4. Current Margin Control Method

The Current Margin (CM) control is a fundamental technique used in HVDC systems to ensure that the inverter operates reliably and that there is sufficient margin for the commutation process.

**Concept:**

The inverter's firing angle is controlled to maintain a specific "current margin" above the minimum current required for successful commutation. This margin provides a buffer against AC voltage fluctuations and other disturbances.

*   The inverter's firing angle $\alpha_2$ is adjusted to maintain a specific DC current, $I_{d\_inv\_control}$, which is related to the rectifier's current order, $I_{d\_rect\_order}$.
*   The difference between the rectifier's current order and the inverter's actual current is controlled to be above a minimum value.
*   $I_{d\_rect\_order} > I_{d\_actual\_inv} + \text{Current Margin}$

**Implementation:**

1.  **Inverter Control:** The inverter is typically operated under Constant Extinction Angle (CEA) control. The CEA controller adjusts $\alpha_2$ to maintain a constant $\gamma$.
2.  **Current Margin Calculation:** A current margin value ($I_{margin}$) is added to the inverter's desired current operating point.
3.  **Rectifier Current Order:** The rectifier's current order is set to be $I_{d\_rect\_order} = I_{d\_desired} + I_{margin}$.
4.  **DC Current Control:** The rectifier's current controller then regulates the DC current ($I_d$) to match $I_{d\_rect\_order}$.

**Benefits:**
*   Ensures reliable commutation at the inverter, preventing commutation failure.
*   Allows the rectifier to operate at a slightly higher current, thus maintaining the margin.
*   Provides a robust control strategy that is less susceptible to disturbances.

**Reference:**
*   *HVDC and FACTS Controllers* by Vijay K Sood (Springer, 2004) - Section 3.2.3, "Current Control."
*   *High Voltage DC Transmission* by K.R.Padiyar (Wiley, 1993) - Section 5.3, "Control of Inverter."

### 5. Current Control at the Rectifier

The rectifier station is typically responsible for regulating the DC current in the link. The most common control strategy is Constant Current (CC) control.

**Mechanism:**

1.  **Current Measurement:** The actual DC current ($I_d$) flowing through the DC link is measured.
2.  **Reference Current:** A reference DC current ($I_{d\_ref}$) is provided by the system operator or an automatic control system.
3.  **Error Calculation:** The difference between the reference current and the measured current ($e = I_{d\_ref} - I_d$) is calculated.
4.  **PI Controller:** A Proportional-Integral (PI) controller processes this error signal. The PI controller's output adjusts the rectifier's firing angle ($\alpha_1$).
5.  **Firing Angle Adjustment:**
    *   If $I_d < I_{d\_ref}$, the PI controller increases the output, which leads to a decrease in $\alpha_1$. A smaller $\alpha_1$ results in a higher DC voltage and thus a higher DC current.
    *   If $I_d > I_{d\_ref}$, the PI controller decreases the output, leading to an increase in $\alpha_1$. A larger $\alpha_1$ results in a lower DC voltage and thus a lower DC current.

**Equation:**

The rectifier DC voltage can be approximated as:
$V_{d1} \approx V_{d01} \cos(\alpha_1) - I_d R_{dc1}$

The PI controller's output (often denoted as $\Delta \alpha$) modifies the base firing angle:
$\alpha_{1\_actual} = \alpha_{1\_base} + \Delta \alpha$

**Limitations and Considerations:**
*   **Minimum Firing Angle:** The firing angle cannot be reduced below a certain minimum value ($\alpha_{min}$) due to commutation requirements and converter limitations.
*   **Maximum Firing Angle:** In inverter operation, the firing angle cannot be increased beyond a maximum value ($\alpha_{max}$) to avoid overlap at the inverter.
*   **AC Voltage Variations:** The rectifier CC controller must be able to compensate for variations in the connected AC voltage.

**Reference:**
*   *HVDC and FACTS Controllers* by Vijay K Sood (Springer, 2004) - Section 3.2.3, "Current Control."
*   *High Voltage DC Transmission* by K.R.Padiyar (Wiley, 1993) - Section 5.1, "Current Control of Rectifier."

### 6. Inverter Extinction Angle Control

The Inverter Extinction Angle (CEA) control is a critical control mode for the inverter station in an HVDC system. It ensures that the thyristors in the inverter have sufficient time to turn off (commute) after the current has reversed.

**Concept:**

*   **Extinction Angle ($\gamma$):** The angle between the valve voltage reaching zero and the valve turning off. A minimum extinction angle is required for successful commutation.
*   **CEA Control:** The inverter's firing angle ($\alpha_2$) is adjusted to maintain a constant extinction angle ($\gamma$).
*   **Relationship:** For a simple phase-controlled converter, the extinction angle $\gamma$ is related to the firing angle $\alpha$ and the overlap angle $\mu$ by $\gamma = 180^\circ - \alpha - \mu$.

**Mechanism:**

1.  **Extinction Angle Measurement:** The inverter's extinction angle ($\gamma$) is estimated or measured from the valve voltage waveforms.
2.  **Reference Extinction Angle:** A desired extinction angle ($\gamma_{ref}$) is set (typically between 15° and 25°).
3.  **Error Calculation:** The difference between the reference and measured extinction angle ($e = \gamma_{ref} - \gamma$) is calculated.
4.  **PI Controller:** A PI controller processes this error signal. The PI controller's output adjusts the inverter's firing angle ($\alpha_2$).
5.  **Firing Angle Adjustment:**
    *   If $\gamma < \gamma_{ref}$, the PI controller output increases, leading to an increase in $\alpha_2$. An increased $\alpha_2$ reduces the extinction angle, bringing it closer to $\gamma_{ref}$.
    *   If $\gamma > \gamma_{ref}$, the PI controller output decreases, leading to a decrease in $\alpha_2$. A decreased $\alpha_2$ increases the extinction angle.

**Why CEA Control is Important:**
*   **Commutation Failure Prevention:** If $\gamma$ becomes too small, the thyristor may not turn off properly, leading to a commutation failure and potential fault in the DC link.
*   **Stable Operation:** Maintaining a consistent $\gamma$ ensures smooth and stable operation of the inverter, even with fluctuations in the AC voltage.
*   **Power Reversal:** In systems capable of power reversal, CEA control on the inverter helps manage the commutation process when the roles of rectifier and inverter are swapped.

**Reference:**
*   *HVDC and FACTS Controllers* by Vijay K Sood (Springer, 2004) - Section 3.2.4, "Inverter Control."
*   *High Voltage DC Transmission* by K.R.Padiyar (Wiley, 1993) - Section 5.3, "Control of Inverter."

### 7. Hierarchy of Controls

HVDC systems employ a hierarchical control structure to manage different operational aspects and ensure robust performance under various conditions. This hierarchy allows for rapid response to disturbances while maintaining overall system stability and efficiency.

**Typical Hierarchy Levels:**

1.  **Level 1: Converter Control (Fast Controls):**
    *   **Purpose:** Provides rapid control of DC current and ensures stable commutation.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Current Control (Rectifier):** Constant Current (CC) control, often with a PI loop.
        *   **Extinction Angle Control (Inverter):** Constant Extinction Angle (CEA) control, often with a PI loop.
        *   **Power Control:** Directly controls power flow by adjusting the current reference.
    *   **Response Time:** Milliseconds to tens of milliseconds.

2.  **Level 2: Regulator Controls (Intermediate Controls):**
    *   **Purpose:** Regulates AC system voltage, frequency, and provides damping.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **AC Voltage Control (at converter stations):** Controls reactive power output of converters or associated static var compensators (SVCs) to maintain AC voltage.
        *   **AC Frequency Control:** Can be used to shed or add load during frequency excursions.
        *   **Power Oscillation Damping (POD):** Modifies the DC current order based on AC system power oscillations to provide damping.
        *   **Ramp Control:** Smoothly adjusts the power level during normal operation.
    *   **Response Time:** Tens of milliseconds to hundreds of milliseconds.

3.  **Level 3: System Control (Slow Controls/Master Controls):**
    *   **Purpose:** Overall coordination and optimization of the HVDC link and interconnected AC systems.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Remote DC Power Control:** Setting the DC power flow from a remote AC system control center.
        *   **Economic Dispatch:** Optimizing power flow for cost-effectiveness.
        *   **System Restoration:** Coordinating the restart of HVDC links after a major disturbance.
        *   **Load Shedding/Frequency Control Support:** High-level commands to assist AC system frequency control.
    *   **Response Time:** Seconds to minutes.

**Interactions between Levels:**

*   Higher levels provide setpoints or commands to lower levels. For instance, the System Control might set the desired DC power, which then becomes the reference for the Level 1 current controller.
*   Lower levels provide feedback to higher levels.
*   Protection systems override all control levels in case of emergencies.

**Example Scenario:**

During a disturbance on the AC system, the Level 2 Power Oscillation Damping (POD) controller detects oscillations. It sends a temporary signal to the Level 1 current controller to slightly reduce the DC current, thereby injecting damping into the AC system. Once the oscillations subside, the POD controller returns to its nominal state, and the Level 1 controller restores the DC current to its commanded value.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Thyristor Control:** HVDC controls are primarily based on controlling the firing angle ($\alpha$) of thyristors in the converter bridges.
*   **Rectifier vs. Inverter:** Rectifiers convert AC to DC and operate with $\alpha < 90^\circ$. Inverters convert DC to AC and operate with $\alpha > 90^\circ$ (or are controlled via extinction angle $\gamma$).
*   **Current Control Dominance:** Constant Current (CC) control is the most common strategy for the rectifier to ensure stable power transfer.
*   **Commutation Assurance:** Extinction Angle (CEA) control is crucial for the inverter to prevent commutation failures.
*   **Current Margin:** The current margin ensures reliable inverter operation by maintaining a buffer in current.
*   **Hierarchy for Robustness:** The multi-level control structure provides both fast responses for system stability and slower, coordinated adjustments for overall system optimization.
*   **Reactive Power:** HVDC converters consume reactive power, which needs to be compensated for. Control systems manage this reactive power exchange.

### Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:**
What are the primary functions of HVDC controls in a transmission system?

**Answer 1:**
The primary functions include power transfer control, converter control (firing angles), AC system stability enhancement, protection, reactive power control, harmonic minimization, and smooth power transfer.

**Question 2:**
Explain the concept of Current Margin (CM) control in HVDC systems. Why is it important?

**Answer 2:**
Current Margin control ensures that the inverter operates reliably by maintaining a specific margin above the minimum current required for successful commutation. It's important for preventing commutation failures, especially during AC voltage fluctuations. The rectifier's current order is set higher than the inverter's desired operating current by this margin.

**Question 3:**
Describe the role of the Constant Extinction Angle (CEA) control in an HVDC inverter.

**Answer 3:**
CEA control maintains a constant extinction angle ($\gamma$) at the inverter by adjusting its firing angle ($\alpha_2$). This is critical to ensure that the thyristors have sufficient time to turn off after the current has reversed, thus preventing commutation failures and ensuring stable inverter operation.

**Question 4:**
Illustrate the basic control hierarchy in an HVDC system. Give examples for each level.

**Answer 4:**
The hierarchy typically includes:
*   **Level 1 (Converter Control):** Fast controls like Constant Current (CC) at the rectifier and Constant Extinction Angle (CEA) at the inverter.
*   **Level 2 (Regulator Controls):** Intermediate controls like AC Voltage Control, Power Oscillation Damping (POD), and Ramp Control.
*   **Level 3 (System Control):** Slow, overarching controls like Remote DC Power Control and Economic Dispatch.

**Question 5:**
If the AC voltage at the inverter station drops suddenly, what control action would likely be taken by the inverter control system to maintain stability, and why?

**Answer 5:**
If the AC voltage drops, the extinction angle ($\gamma$) would tend to increase (become larger) because the overlap angle ($\mu$) would also increase for a given firing angle $\alpha$. The inverter's CEA controller would detect this increase in $\gamma$ (or deviation from $\gamma_{ref}$). To bring $\gamma$ back down, the CEA controller would increase the inverter's firing angle ($\alpha_2$). This increase in $\alpha_2$ reduces the DC voltage and current, potentially to a level where the inverter can still operate, but it might also trigger other control actions or protective measures if the voltage drop is severe.
