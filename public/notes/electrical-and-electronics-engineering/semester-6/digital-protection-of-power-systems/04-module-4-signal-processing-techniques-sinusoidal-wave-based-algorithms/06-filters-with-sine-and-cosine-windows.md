# Digital Protection of Power Systems - Module 4: Signal Processing Techniques: Sinusoidal Wave Based Algorithms

## Topic: Filters with Sine and Cosine Windows

This module delves into the fundamental signal processing techniques employed in digital relays, focusing on sinusoidal wave-based algorithms. This topic specifically addresses the application of sine and cosine window filters for extracting essential information from the sampled power system waveforms.

---

### 1. Introduction to Digital Signal Processing in Power System Protection

Digital relays process sampled voltage and current signals from the power system to detect faults and initiate appropriate protective actions. This requires sophisticated signal processing techniques to accurately extract fundamental frequency components and harmonic information.

*   **Why Signal Processing?**
    *   **Noise Reduction:** Power system signals can be corrupted by noise.
    *   **Feature Extraction:** Identifying specific characteristics of fault currents (e.g., magnitude, phase angle, harmonics).
    *   **Algorithm Implementation:** Digital algorithms rely on precise numerical representations of the system’s electrical quantities.
*   **Digital Relaying Process:**
    1.  **Analog-to-Digital Conversion (ADC):** Continuous analog signals are converted into discrete digital samples.
    2.  **Signal Processing:** Digital algorithms analyze these samples.
    3.  **Decision Making:** Based on the processed signals, the relay decides if a fault has occurred.
    4.  **Output (Tripping):** If a fault is detected, the relay sends a trip signal to the circuit breaker.

**(Referenced from: Johns & Salman, Chapter 3; Phadke & Thorpe, Chapter 2; Ram & Viswakarma, Chapter 1)**

---

### 2. The Need for Frequency Selective Filtering

Power system signals are generally sinusoidal at the fundamental frequency (e.g., 50 Hz or 60 Hz). However, during faults, these signals can be distorted by:

*   **Harmonics:** Multiples of the fundamental frequency generated by non-linear loads and switching events.
*   **DC Offset:** A decaying transient component that appears immediately after a fault, often due to the inductance of the power system.
*   **Transients:** High-frequency oscillations caused by switching or lightning.

To accurately determine the fundamental frequency component (which is crucial for protection functions like overcurrent, differential, and distance), these unwanted components must be filtered out.

**(Referenced from: Rebizant, Chapter 2)**

---

### 3. Sinusoidal Wave Based Algorithms: The Core Idea

These algorithms exploit the sinusoidal nature of the fundamental frequency component. The goal is to efficiently extract the amplitude and phase of the fundamental frequency sinusoid from a block of sampled data.

*   **Key Principle:** If we have a block of samples representing a complete or near-complete cycle of the fundamental sinusoid, we can use mathematical operations to isolate its fundamental component.

---

### 4. Filters with Sine and Cosine Windows

These filters are designed to extract the fundamental frequency component by correlating the sampled signal with sine and cosine waves at that frequency. This correlation effectively "selects" the fundamental frequency component while attenuating other frequencies.

#### 4.1 The Concept of Windowing

Windowing in signal processing involves multiplying a signal by a specific function (the window function) to isolate a segment of the signal or to taper the signal at its edges to reduce spectral leakage. In this context, the "sine and cosine windows" are essentially applied to the sampled data to extract specific sinusoidal components.

#### 4.2 The Sine and Cosine Window Function

Imagine a block of $N$ samples, $x[n]$, where $n = 0, 1, ..., N-1$. We want to extract the fundamental frequency component, which can be represented as:

$x(t) = A \cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$

where $\omega_0$ is the fundamental angular frequency.

At discrete time points $t = nT_s$ (where $T_s$ is the sampling period), the signal is $x[n] = A \cos(\omega_0 nT_s + \phi)$.

We can decompose this into sine and cosine components:

$x[n] = A_c \cos(\omega_0 nT_s) - A_s \sin(\omega_0 nT_s)$

where:
*   $A_c = A \cos(\phi)$ (the cosine component of the fundamental phasor)
*   $A_s = A \sin(\phi)$ (the sine component of the fundamental phasor)

The goal of the sine and cosine window filters is to compute $A_c$ and $A_s$ from the sampled data.

#### 4.3 The Algorithm

The core idea is to compute the following integrals (or their discrete equivalents):

*   **For the cosine component ($A_c$):**
    $A_c = \frac{2}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cos(\omega_0 nT_s)$
*   **For the sine component ($A_s$):**
    $A_s = -\frac{2}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \sin(\omega_0 nT_s)$

Where:
*   $N$ is the number of samples in the window.
*   $\omega_0$ is the fundamental angular frequency.
*   $T_s$ is the sampling period.
*   $\omega_0 T_s = 2\pi f_0 T_s = \frac{2\pi}{f_s} f_0 = \frac{2\pi}{N_{cycle}}$, where $f_s$ is the sampling frequency and $N_{cycle}$ is the number of samples per cycle of the fundamental frequency.

**Important Note:** The factor $2/N$ is a normalization factor. If $N$ is the number of samples in exactly one cycle ($N_{cycle}$), then the amplitude of the fundamental component is directly obtained.

#### 4.4 Why Sine and Cosine?

By multiplying the sampled data block with a cosine wave and summing, we effectively integrate (or sum) the product.
*   When $x[n]$ is in phase with the cosine, the product is large and positive.
*   When $x[n]$ is out of phase, the product is large and negative.
*   When $x[n]$ is a sine wave, the integral (or sum) of its product with a cosine wave over a full cycle is zero.
*   Similarly, when multiplying $x[n]$ with a sine wave and summing, we effectively extract the sine component.

This process is analogous to projecting the sampled signal onto the cosine and sine axes in the phasor domain.

#### 4.5 Practical Implementation Considerations

*   **Window Length (N):** The choice of $N$ is critical.
    *   **Full Cycle Window:** If $N$ is exactly the number of samples in one fundamental cycle ($N_{cycle}$), the algorithm perfectly extracts the fundamental component and eliminates all harmonics and DC offset. This is ideal but requires precise knowledge of the fundamental frequency.
    *   **Non-Integer Cycle Window:** If the window length is not an exact number of cycles, spectral leakage can occur, meaning some energy from harmonics might leak into the fundamental component.
*   **Sampling Frequency ($f_s$):** Higher sampling frequencies allow for shorter windows for a given number of cycles, potentially leading to faster relay response. However, they increase the computational load.
*   **Fundamental Frequency Variation:** Real power systems can experience frequency deviations. The algorithms need to be robust to these variations. Techniques like adaptive sampling or frequency estimation are employed.

**(Referenced from: Johns & Salman, Chapter 5; Phadke & Thorpe, Chapter 3)**

---

### 5. Advantages of Sine and Cosine Window Filters

*   **Effective Harmonic Rejection:** They are very effective at rejecting harmonics if the window length is a multiple of the fundamental period.
*   **DC Offset Removal:** The summation process inherently removes any DC offset present in the sampled data.
*   **Simplicity of Implementation:** The mathematical operations involved are relatively straightforward to implement in digital hardware.
*   **Direct Amplitude and Phase Calculation:** The results of the summations directly provide the cosine and sine components of the fundamental phasor, from which amplitude and phase can be easily calculated.

---

### 6. Disadvantages and Limitations

*   **Sensitivity to Window Length:** Performance degrades if the window length is not synchronized with the fundamental frequency.
*   **Transient Response:** During the initial stages of a fault, the signal might not be purely sinusoidal, and the presence of strong transients or DC offset can affect the accuracy of the fundamental component extraction until the window has captured a sufficient portion of the signal. This can lead to a delay in relay operation.
*   **Computational Overhead for High Sampling Rates:** While simple, calculating sums over many samples can be computationally intensive for very high sampling rates or complex algorithms.

**(Referenced from: Rebizant, Chapter 3)**

### 8. Examples and Applications

#### Example 1: Calculating Fundamental Amplitude and Phase

Assume a block of $N=8$ samples of a pure sine wave $x[n] = 10 \sin(\frac{2\pi}{8}n)$ with fundamental frequency such that $N_{cycle}=8$.

Let's sample at $t=0, T_s, 2T_s, \ldots, 7T_s$, where $T_s = \frac{1}{8f_0}$.
The samples are:
$x[0] = 10 \sin(0) = 0$
$x[1] = 10 \sin(\pi/4) = 10 \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 7.07$
$x[2] = 10 \sin(\pi/2) = 10$
$x[3] = 10 \sin(3\pi/4) = 10 \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 7.07$
$x[4] = 10 \sin(\pi) = 0$
$x[5] = 10 \sin(5\pi/4) = -10 \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx -7.07$
$x[6] = 10 \sin(3\pi/2) = -10$
$x[7] = 10 \sin(7\pi/4) = -10 \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx -7.07$

**Calculation of $A_c$:**
$A_c = \frac{2}{8} \sum_{n=0}^{7} x[n] \cos(\frac{2\pi}{8}n)$

$n=0: x[0]\cos(0) = 0 \times 1 = 0$
$n=1: x[1]\cos(\pi/4) \approx 7.07 \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 7.07 \times 0.707 \approx 5$
$n=2: x[2]\cos(\pi/2) = 10 \times 0 = 0$
$n=3: x[3]\cos(3\pi/4) \approx 7.07 \times (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \approx 7.07 \times (-0.707) \approx -5$
$n=4: x[4]\cos(\pi) = 0 \times (-1) = 0$
$n=5: x[5]\cos(5\pi/4) \approx -7.07 \times (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \approx -7.07 \times (-0.707) \approx 5$
$n=6: x[6]\cos(3\pi/2) = -10 \times 0 = 0$
$n=7: x[7]\cos(7\pi/4) \approx -7.07 \times (\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \approx -7.07 \times (0.707) \approx -5$

$\sum \approx 0 + 5 + 0 - 5 + 0 + 5 + 0 - 5 = 0$
$A_c = \frac{2}{8} \times 0 = 0$. This is correct because the signal is a pure sine wave, so its cosine component is zero.

**Calculation of $A_s$:**
$A_s = -\frac{2}{8} \sum_{n=0}^{7} x[n] \sin(\frac{2\pi}{8}n)$

$n=0: x[0]\sin(0) = 0 \times 0 = 0$
$n=1: x[1]\sin(\pi/4) \approx 7.07 \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 7.07 \times 0.707 \approx 5$
$n=2: x[2]\sin(\pi/2) = 10 \times 1 = 10$
$n=3: x[3]\sin(3\pi/4) \approx 7.07 \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 7.07 \times 0.707 \approx 5$
$n=4: x[4]\sin(\pi) = 0 \times 0 = 0$
$n=5: x[5]\sin(5\pi/4) \approx -7.07 \times (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \approx -7.07 \times (-0.707) \approx 5$
$n=6: x[6]\sin(3\pi/2) = -10 \times (-1) = 10$
$n=7: x[7]\sin(7\pi/4) \approx -7.07 \times (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \approx -7.07 \times (-0.707) \approx 5$

$\sum \approx 0 + 5 + 10 + 5 + 0 + 5 + 10 + 5 = 40$
$A_s = -\frac{2}{8} \times 40 = -10$.

The actual signal is $10 \sin(\omega_0 t)$. In the form $A_c \cos(\omega_0 t) - A_s \sin(\omega_0 t)$, this is $0 \cos(\omega_0 t) - (-10) \sin(\omega_0 t) = 10 \sin(\omega_0 t)$. The calculated $A_s$ is $-10$, which is correct for this representation.

The fundamental phasor can be represented as $I = I_c + jI_s$, where $I_c = A_c$ and $I_s = -A_s$.
In this case, $I = 0 + j(10) = 10j$.
Amplitude = $\sqrt{A_c^2 + (-A_s)^2} = \sqrt{0^2 + 10^2} = 10$.
Phase angle = $\arctan(\frac{-A_s}{A_c}) = \arctan(\frac{10}{0})$, which is 90 degrees.

#### Example 2: DC Offset and Harmonic Rejection

Consider a signal with a DC offset and a harmonic:
$x(t) = 10 \sin(\omega_0 t) + 2 + 3 \sin(3\omega_0 t)$
If we use a window of exactly one fundamental cycle ($N=N_{cycle}$), the sine and cosine window algorithm will:

*   **Reject the DC offset (2):** When multiplied by $\sin(\omega_0 nT_s)$ or $\cos(\omega_0 nT_s)$ and summed over a full cycle, the integral of a constant is zero.
*   **Reject the third harmonic (3 sin(3ω₀t)):** When multiplied by $\sin(\omega_0 nT_s)$ or $\cos(\omega_0 nT_s)$ and summed over a full cycle, the integral of $\sin(3\omega_0 t)$ will also be zero. This is because the third harmonic completes 3 cycles within the same time the fundamental completes 1 cycle, and the positive and negative areas cancel out.

**(Referenced from: Johns & Salman, Chapter 5; Phadke & Thorpe, Chapter 3)**

### 9. Practice Questions

1.  **Question:** Explain why filtering is essential in digital protection relays. (CO4, K2)
    **Answer:** Filtering is essential to remove unwanted components like DC offset, harmonics, and noise from the sampled voltage and current signals. This ensures that the relay accurately extracts the fundamental frequency component, which is crucial for correct fault detection and classification (e.g., magnitude, phase angle for distance relays).

2.  **Question:** Describe the fundamental principle behind the sine and cosine window filters. (CO4, K2)
    **Answer:** The sine and cosine window filters work by correlating the sampled signal with sine and cosine waves at the fundamental frequency over a defined block of samples. This process effectively isolates the fundamental frequency component by summing the products. The cosine window extracts the cosine component of the fundamental phasor, and the sine window extracts the sine component.

3.  **Question:** What is the primary advantage of using a window length that is exactly one fundamental cycle for sine and cosine window filters? (CO4, K2)
    **Answer:** The primary advantage is the perfect rejection of DC offset and all harmonics. This is because the integrals (or sums) of sine and cosine waves multiplied by a constant (DC offset) or by sine/cosine waves at frequencies that are integer multiples of the fundamental, over an integer number of fundamental cycles, are all zero.

4.  **Question:** A distance relay relies on the measurement of fundamental frequency voltage and current. How do sine and cosine window filters contribute to the accurate operation of a distance relay? (CO1, K3)
    **Answer:** Distance relays calculate impedance by dividing fundamental frequency voltage by fundamental frequency current. Sine and cosine window filters accurately extract these fundamental frequency components from potentially distorted sampled data, ensuring the impedance calculation is precise and leading to correct fault location and tripping decisions.

5.  **Question:** If a relay uses a window of $N=10$ samples, but the fundamental frequency dictates that $N_{cycle}=12$ samples per cycle, what potential issue might arise? (CO4, K2)
    **Answer:** Using a window that is not an integer multiple of the fundamental period can lead to spectral leakage. This means that energy from harmonics or other frequency components might "leak" into the estimated fundamental frequency component, reducing the accuracy of the measurement.

### 10. Important Points to Remember

*   **Purpose:** Extract fundamental frequency component.
*   **Mechanism:** Correlation with sine and cosine functions.
*   **Outputs:** Cosine component ($A_c$) and Sine component ($A_s$) of the fundamental phasor.
*   **Key Window Property:** $N_{cycle}$ (samples per fundamental cycle) is crucial.
*   **Ideal Scenario:** $N = N_{cycle}$ provides perfect harmonic and DC rejection.
*   **Practicality:** Frequency variations and transient conditions affect ideal windowing.
*   **Foundation for:** Overcurrent, differential, and distance protection algorithms.
