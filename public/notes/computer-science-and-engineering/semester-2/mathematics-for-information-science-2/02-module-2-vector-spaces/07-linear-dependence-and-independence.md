## Mathematics for Information Science – 2: Module 2: Vector Spaces

### Topic: Linear Dependence and Independence

Welcome, everyone, to our exploration of vector spaces! Today, we're diving into a fundamental concept that underpins so much of what we do in information science: **linear dependence and independence**. Think of this as understanding the core "building blocks" of vectors – are they all essential, or can some be expressed in terms of others? This concept is crucial for understanding how we represent data, solve systems of equations, and even how machine learning models learn.

We've already established what a vector space is – a collection of vectors where we can add them and multiply them by scalars, and these operations behave nicely. Now, we want to look at *relationships* between vectors within that space.

**Connecting to Course Outcomes:**

Before we get too deep, let's see how this topic ties into our overall course objectives.

*   **CO2: Understand the concepts of vector spaces and subspaces and to apply their properties.** Linear dependence and independence are direct applications of the properties of vector spaces. They help us understand the "size" and structure of subspaces, which are often generated by sets of vectors. For instance, if we have a set of data points, we want to know if all those data points give us new information, or if some are just combinations of others. This directly relates to understanding the dimension of a subspace, a key concept within CO2.
*   **CO1: Solve system of linear equations, to evaluate eigen values and eigen vectors of matrices and to diagonalize matrices.** Linear independence is the very backbone of solving systems of linear equations. When we talk about the rank of a matrix, for example, we are essentially counting the number of linearly independent rows or columns. Eigenvectors are also inherently linked to linear independence. If you have a set of linearly independent eigenvectors, you can diagonalize a matrix, which is a powerful tool for simplifying complex problems.
*   **CO4: Understand the concept of linear transformation and to apply its properties, to find the rank and nullity of a linear transformation and to find the matrices of linear transformations.** When we consider a linear transformation, its output space is spanned by the images of the basis vectors of its input space. The linear independence of these images tells us about the rank of the transformation – how much it "spreads" or "collapses" vectors.

So, you can see that this topic isn't just an isolated idea; it's deeply interwoven with many other essential concepts in our course.

### What Does it Mean for Vectors to be "Dependent" or "Independent"?

Let's start with an analogy. Imagine you're building a house. You have different materials: wood, nails, bricks, cement, paint, windows, doors.

Now, consider a set of vectors. If a set of vectors is **linearly dependent**, it means at least one of those vectors can be expressed as a combination of the others. In our house analogy, this would be like having a bag of pre-fabricated wall sections. You *could* build a wall by assembling individual bricks and mortar, but if you have a pre-built section, that section *is* a combination of bricks, mortar, and labor. The pre-built section doesn't add a fundamentally new capability to your construction; it's just a more convenient way of doing what you could already do with the basic components.

Conversely, if a set of vectors is **linearly independent**, each vector brings something unique to the table. None of them can be formed by combining the others. In our analogy, imagine your basic materials: wood, nails, bricks, cement. Each of these is essential. You can't build a strong wooden frame using only bricks and cement, or a solid foundation using only wood and nails. They are all fundamental and contribute independently to the final structure.

### Formal Definition: Linear Dependence

So, let's put this into mathematical terms.

Consider a set of vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ in a vector space $V$.

This set of vectors is said to be **linearly dependent** if there exist scalars $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k$, not all zero, such that:

$c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$ (the zero vector)

The key here is "not all zero." If we can find even *one* combination of these vectors, where we multiply each by a scalar (and at least one scalar is not zero), and the result is the zero vector, then the set is linearly dependent.

**Why is this important?** If we can achieve the zero vector with at least one non-zero coefficient, say $c_i \neq 0$ for vector $v_i$, we can rearrange the equation:

$c_i v_i = -c_1 v_1 - \dots - c_{i-1} v_{i-1} - c_{i+1} v_{i+1} - \dots - c_k v_k$

If we divide by $c_i$ (which we can do since $c_i \neq 0$), we get:

$v_i = -\frac{c_1}{c_i} v_1 - \dots - \frac{c_{i-1}}{c_i} v_{i-1} - \frac{c_{i+1}}{c_i} v_{i+1} - \dots - \frac{c_k}{c_i} v_k$

This shows that $v_i$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the *other* vectors in the set. This is precisely our analogy: one component can be built from the others.

**Example 1: A Simple Case**

Let's consider vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
$v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$

Are these vectors linearly dependent? Let's try to find scalars $c_1, c_2, c_3$, not all zero, such that:

$c_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + c_2 \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + c_3 \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$

This gives us a system of linear equations:
1*c₁ + 2*c₂ + 3*c₃ = 0
2*c₁ + 4*c₂ + 6*c₃ = 0

Notice something interesting here: the second equation is just twice the first equation! This tells us that if the first equation is satisfied, the second one automatically will be. This is a strong hint of dependence.

We have more variables ($c_1, c_2, c_3$) than independent equations. This usually means there are infinitely many solutions, including non-trivial ones (where not all $c_i$ are zero).

Let's pick some values. How about we try to express $v_2$ in terms of $v_1$?
We can see that $v_2 = 2 v_1$.
So, $2 v_1 - v_2 = \mathbf{0}$.
This means $2 v_1 + (-1) v_2 + 0 v_3 = \mathbf{0}$.
Here, $c_1 = 2$, $c_2 = -1$, and $c_3 = 0$. Since $c_1$ and $c_2$ are not zero, this set of vectors is linearly dependent.

Also, $v_3 = 3 v_1$, so $3 v_1 + 0 v_2 - v_3 = \mathbf{0}$. Here $c_1=3, c_2=0, c_3=-1$.

**Key Insight:** If one vector in a set is a scalar multiple of another vector in the same set, the set is linearly dependent. All these vectors lie on the same line (in $\mathbb{R}^2$, a line through the origin).

### Formal Definition: Linear Independence

Now for the opposite: **linear independence**.

A set of vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ in a vector space $V$ is said to be **linearly independent** if the *only* solution to the equation:

$c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$

is the **trivial solution**, where all the scalars are zero: $c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$.

In our house analogy, this means each fundamental material you have is indispensable. You can't make wood out of nails, or bricks out of cement. They are all unique and necessary components.

**Example 2: Independence in $\mathbb{R}^2$**

Let's consider the standard basis vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $e_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$

Are these linearly independent? Let's set up the equation:
$c_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + c_2 \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$

This expands to:
$\begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$

Which gives:
$\begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$

This immediately tells us that $c_1 = 0$ and $c_2 = 0$. Since the only solution is the trivial one (all scalars are zero), the set $\{e_1, e_2\}$ is linearly independent.

This makes intuitive sense. You can't get a vector pointing purely horizontally ($e_1$) by combining a horizontal vector with a vertical vector ($e_2$). Similarly, you can't get a purely vertical vector ($e_2$) from a horizontal one ($e_1$). They are independent directions.

**Example 3: Three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ are always dependent**

Consider three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
$v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$

We want to solve:
$c_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + c_2 \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + c_3 \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$

This leads to the system:
$c_1 + 2c_3 = 0$
$c_2 + 3c_3 = 0$

We have two equations and three unknowns ($c_1, c_2, c_3$). What does this usually mean? It means there are infinitely many solutions, and we can definitely find solutions where not all $c_i$ are zero.

For instance, let's pick $c_3 = 1$.
Then $c_1 + 2(1) = 0 \implies c_1 = -2$.
And $c_2 + 3(1) = 0 \implies c_2 = -3$.

So, we have $-2 v_1 - 3 v_2 + 1 v_3 = \mathbf{0}$.
Let's check: $-2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} - 3 \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 1 \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2+0+2 \\ 0-3+3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

It works! Since we found non-zero coefficients ($c_1 = -2, c_2 = -3, c_3 = 1$), the set $\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ is linearly dependent. In fact, $v_3 = 2v_1 + 3v_2$, so $v_3$ is a linear combination of $v_1$ and $v_2$.

**Theorem:** Any set of $m$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional vector space $V$ is linearly dependent if $m > n$.
This is a very important shortcut! In Example 3, we had 3 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (which is 2-dimensional), and $3 > 2$, so they *had* to be linearly dependent.

### Checking for Linear Dependence/Independence Using Matrices

This is where our knowledge of matrices, systems of equations, and determinants (which we'll touch upon) becomes incredibly useful, especially for exam-oriented questions.

Let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ be vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. We want to check if they are linearly dependent. We form the equation $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$.

If we arrange these vectors as columns of a matrix $A$, say $A = [v_1 | v_2 | \dots | v_k]$, then the equation becomes $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$, where $\mathbf{c} = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_k \end{pmatrix}$ is the vector of coefficients.

Now, the problem of linear dependence/independence boils down to determining if the homogeneous system $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution ($\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$) or non-trivial solutions.

**How do we check this using matrices?**

1.  **Form the matrix $A$ with the vectors as columns.**
2.  **Reduce the matrix $A$ to its Row Echelon Form (REF) or Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF).** This is often done using Gaussian elimination.
3.  **Analyze the RREF (or REF):**

    *   **If the RREF of $A$ has a pivot (leading 1) in every column, then the system $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution ($\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$).** This means the vectors are **linearly independent**.
    *   **If the RREF of $A$ has one or more columns *without* a pivot, then the system $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ has non-trivial solutions.** This means the vectors are **linearly dependent**. The number of free variables (columns without pivots) tells you how many ways you can form non-trivial solutions.

**Example 4: Using RREF**

Let's check the linear independence of:
$v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$

These are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Form the matrix $A$ with these vectors as columns:
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$

Now, let's reduce $A$ to RREF.
$R_2 \leftarrow R_2 + R_1$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$

$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 2R_1$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$

$R_2 \leftarrow \frac{1}{3}R_2$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$

$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + 3R_2$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$

$R_1 \leftarrow R_1 - 2R_2$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$

This is the RREF of $A$. Now, look at the columns:
*   Column 1 has a pivot (1).
*   Column 2 has a pivot (1).
*   Column 3 does *not* have a pivot.

Since there is a column without a pivot (the third column), the system $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ has non-trivial solutions. Therefore, the vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$ are **linearly dependent**.

The RREF tells us:
$c_1 - 2c_3 = 0$
$c_2 + c_3 = 0$

Here, $c_3$ is the free variable. If we let $c_3 = 1$, then $c_1 = 2$ and $c_2 = -1$.
So, $2 v_1 - 1 v_2 + 1 v_3 = \mathbf{0}$.
Let's check: $2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2-2+0 \\ -2-1+3 \\ 4-1-3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
It holds true!

**Important Connection:** The number of pivots in the RREF of $A$ is the **rank** of the matrix $A$. The rank is also equal to the maximum number of linearly independent columns (or rows) of $A$. In our example, the rank of $A$ is 2, meaning there are at most 2 linearly independent vectors among $v_1, v_2, v_3$.

### The Role of Determinants (for square matrices)

When you have a square matrix (i.e., you are dealing with $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$), there's a shortcut using determinants. This is covered well in Kreyszig and Larson.

For a square matrix $A$, the vectors (columns or rows) are **linearly independent if and only if the determinant of $A$ is non-zero ($\det(A) \neq 0$)**.

Conversely, the vectors are **linearly dependent if and only if the determinant of $A$ is zero ($\det(A) = 0$)**.

**Why does this work?** A non-zero determinant for a square matrix means the matrix is invertible. If $A$ is invertible, then the system $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ has a unique solution, which must be $\mathbf{c} = A^{-1}\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$. This is the trivial solution, implying linear independence. If $\det(A) = 0$, the matrix is not invertible, meaning $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ must have non-trivial solutions, implying linear dependence.

**Example 5: Using Determinants**

Let's re-examine Example 4 with vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$. Wait, these were 3 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, so the matrix $A$ was square. Let's calculate its determinant.
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$

$\det(A) = 1 \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 1 & -3 \end{vmatrix} - 2 \begin{vmatrix} -1 & 3 \\ 2 & -3 \end{vmatrix} + 0 \begin{vmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix}$

$\det(A) = 1((1)(-3) - (3)(1)) - 2((-1)(-3) - (3)(2)) + 0$
$\det(A) = 1(-3 - 3) - 2(3 - 6)$
$\det(A) = 1(-6) - 2(-3)$
$\det(A) = -6 + 6$
$\det(A) = 0$

Since the determinant is 0, the vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$ are linearly dependent. This matches our RREF result.

**Caution:** This determinant shortcut only works when you have $n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space (i.e., the matrix $A$ is square). If you have $m$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ where $m \neq n$, you *must* use the RREF method.

### Linear Dependence/Independence of Function Sets

This concept isn't just for numerical vectors; it applies to functions too! Consider functions $f_1(x), f_2(x), \dots, f_k(x)$. They are **linearly dependent** if there exist scalars $c_1, \dots, c_k$, not all zero, such that:

$c_1 f_1(x) + c_2 f_2(x) + \dots + c_k f_k(x) = 0$ for all $x$ in their domain.

They are **linearly independent** if the only solution is $c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$.

**Example 6: Functions**

Consider the functions $f_1(x) = e^x$, $f_2(x) = e^{2x}$, $f_3(x) = e^{3x}$. Are they linearly independent?
We look for $c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{2x} + c_3 e^{3x} = 0$ for all $x$.

If we set $x=0$: $c_1 e^0 + c_2 e^0 + c_3 e^0 = 0 \implies c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 0$.
If we set $x=1$: $c_1 e^1 + c_2 e^2 + c_3 e^3 = 0$.
If we set $x=2$: $c_1 e^2 + c_2 e^4 + c_3 e^6 = 0$.

This forms a system of linear equations for $c_1, c_2, c_3$. For functions, it's often easier to see if a combination can sum to zero using specific values of $x$.

However, a more robust method for functions is using the **Wronskian**. The Wronskian of a set of functions $f_1, \dots, f_k$ is defined as the determinant of the matrix whose rows are the functions and their derivatives up to order $k-1$:

$W(f_1, \dots, f_k)(x) = \begin{vmatrix} f_1(x) & f_2(x) & \dots & f_k(x) \\ f_1'(x) & f_2'(x) & \dots & f_k'(x) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ f_1^{(k-1)}(x) & f_2^{(k-1)}(x) & \dots & f_k^{(k-1)}(x) \end{vmatrix}$

**Theorem:** If the Wronskian $W(f_1, \dots, f_k)(x)$ is non-zero for at least one value of $x$ in the domain, then the set of functions $\{f_1, \dots, f_k\}$ is linearly independent.

**Important Note:** If the Wronskian is zero for *all* $x$, it does not necessarily imply linear dependence for all sets of functions. However, for solutions of linear homogeneous differential equations, a zero Wronskian everywhere implies linear dependence. For general functions, a non-zero Wronskian is a *guarantee* of independence.

Let's check the Wronskian for $f_1(x) = e^x$, $f_2(x) = e^{2x}$.
$f_1'(x) = e^x$, $f_2'(x) = 2e^{2x}$.

$W(e^x, e^{2x})(x) = \begin{vmatrix} e^x & e^{2x} \\ e^x & 2e^{2x} \end{vmatrix} = e^x (2e^{2x}) - e^{2x} (e^x) = 2e^{3x} - e^{3x} = e^{3x}$

Since $e^{3x}$ is never zero for any real $x$, the functions $e^x$ and $e^{2x}$ are **linearly independent**.

Now, for $f_1(x) = \sin^2 x$, $f_2(x) = \cos^2 x$.
$f_1'(x) = 2 \sin x \cos x$, $f_2'(x) = -2 \cos x \sin x$.

$W(\sin^2 x, \cos^2 x)(x) = \begin{vmatrix} \sin^2 x & \cos^2 x \\ 2 \sin x \cos x & -2 \cos x \sin x \end{vmatrix}$
$W = (\sin^2 x)(-2 \cos x \sin x) - (\cos^2 x)(2 \sin x \cos x)$
$W = -2 \sin^3 x \cos x - 2 \cos^3 x \sin x$
$W = -2 \sin x \cos x (\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x)$
Since $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$,
$W = -2 \sin x \cos x = -\sin(2x)$

The Wronskian is $-\sin(2x)$. This is zero when $2x = n\pi$, i.e., $x = n\pi/2$.
For example, at $x = \pi/2$, $W = -\sin(\pi) = 0$.
This means that while the Wronskian is zero at some points, it's not zero everywhere.
However, we can see that $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$. This isn't quite what we need for linear dependence of functions where the combination *must* be zero for all $x$.

Let's try another example: $f_1(x) = \sin x$, $f_2(x) = \cos x$, $f_3(x) = \sin x + \cos x$.
Here, $f_3(x) = 1 \cdot f_1(x) + 1 \cdot f_2(x)$.
So, $1 \cdot f_1(x) + 1 \cdot f_2(x) - 1 \cdot f_3(x) = 0$ for all $x$.
This is a linear dependence relation with non-zero coefficients ($c_1=1, c_2=1, c_3=-1$). So, these functions are linearly dependent.

### Why is Linear Independence Important?

*   **Basis of a Vector Space:** A set of linearly independent vectors that span a vector space forms a **basis** for that space. A basis is like the minimal set of "ingredients" you need to create any vector in the space. For example, $\{e_1, e_2\}$ is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$. The number of vectors in a basis is the dimension of the space. Understanding independence is key to finding and verifying bases. (Directly relates to CO2).
*   **Uniqueness of Representation:** If a set of vectors forms a basis, then any vector in the space can be written as a *unique* linear combination of those basis vectors. This is critical in data representation. If you have a set of features for your data, and some features are linearly dependent, you have redundant information. Removing dependent features can simplify models without losing essential information.
*   **Solving Systems of Linear Equations:** As mentioned earlier, the number of linearly independent rows/columns of a matrix determines its rank, which is fundamental to understanding the solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. (Directly relates to CO1).
*   **Efficiency in Data Science:** In machine learning, techniques like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) rely heavily on finding linearly independent components (principal components) that capture the most variance in the data. If your original features are highly correlated (often a sign of linear dependence), PCA can transform them into a new set of uncorrelated (and thus linearly independent) features.

### Summary: Key Takeaways

*   A set of vectors is **linearly dependent** if one vector can be written as a linear combination of the others, or equivalently, if there's a non-trivial solution to $c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$.
*   A set of vectors is **linearly independent** if the only way to get the zero vector is by setting all coefficients to zero in the linear combination: $c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$ implies $c_1=\dots=c_k=0$.
*   **To check for linear independence of vectors:**
    *   Form a matrix $A$ with the vectors as columns.
    *   Reduce $A$ to RREF.
    *   If every column has a pivot, the vectors are linearly independent. Otherwise, they are dependent.
*   **For a square matrix $A$:** Vectors (columns or rows) are linearly independent iff $\det(A) \neq 0$.
*   **For functions:** Check the Wronskian. If $W \neq 0$ for at least one $x$, the functions are linearly independent.
*   **Remember:** If you have more vectors than the dimension of the space ($m > n$ for vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$), the set is always linearly dependent.

This concept of linear dependence and independence is truly foundational. Mastering it will provide you with the tools to analyze relationships within data, understand the structure of vector spaces, and solve complex problems across information science.

### Sample Questions and Answers

**Q1 (Conceptual):** Explain in your own words the difference between linearly independent and linearly dependent sets of vectors. Use an analogy to help illustrate your point.

**Answer:**
Linearly independent vectors are like distinct ingredients in a recipe, each contributing a unique flavor that cannot be replicated by combining other ingredients. For example, in baking, flour, sugar, and eggs are essential and independent; you can't make "flour" from sugar and eggs. Mathematically, a set of vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is linearly independent if the only way to get the zero vector by summing them with scalar coefficients is if all coefficients are zero ($c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$ implies $c_1=\dots=c_k=0$).

Linearly dependent vectors are like pre-mixed ingredients or shortcuts. For instance, if you have cake mix, it already combines flour, sugar, and leavening agents. This cake mix is "dependent" on the individual ingredients; it's just a combination of them. Mathematically, a set of vectors is linearly dependent if at least one vector can be expressed as a combination of the others, or equivalently, if there's a way to sum them with coefficients where at least one coefficient is non-zero to get the zero vector ($c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$ with at least one $c_i \neq 0$). This means some vectors are redundant in terms of the "information" or "direction" they represent within the space.

**Q2 (Application - Vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$):** Determine if the following set of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is linearly independent or dependent:
$v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $v_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$

**Solution:**
To check for linear independence, we form a matrix $A$ with these vectors as columns and reduce it to RREF.
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$

Perform row operations:
$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$

$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_2$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$

This is the Row Echelon Form. We can see that the third column does not have a pivot (a leading 1). This means there are non-trivial solutions to $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$.

Alternatively, we can go to RREF by $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 - R_2$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
$R_1 \leftarrow R_1 + R_2$:
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$

The RREF has pivots in columns 1 and 2, but not in column 3. Therefore, the set of vectors is **linearly dependent**.

*Reasoning connection to CO2:* The rank of this matrix is 2. This tells us that the subspace spanned by these three vectors has a dimension of 2. This means the three vectors do not form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$; they are not enough to span the entire space, and one of them is redundant.

**Q3 (Application - Determinant):** Are the vectors $v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$ linearly independent in $\mathbb{R}^2$?

**Solution:**
Since we have 2 vectors in a 2-dimensional space, we can use the determinant. Form the square matrix $A$ with these vectors as columns:
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$

Calculate the determinant:
$\det(A) = (2 \times 6) - (4 \times 3) = 12 - 12 = 0$.

Since the determinant is zero, the vectors are **linearly dependent**. We could also have noticed that $v_2 = 2v_1$, which directly indicates dependence.

**Q4 (Conceptual/Application - Functions):** Consider the functions $f_1(x) = \cos x$ and $f_2(x) = \sin x$. Prove that they are linearly independent.

**Solution:**
We need to check if $c_1 \cos x + c_2 \sin x = 0$ for all $x$ implies $c_1 = c_2 = 0$.

Method 1: Using specific values of $x$.
If $x=0$: $c_1 \cos(0) + c_2 \sin(0) = 0 \implies c_1(1) + c_2(0) = 0 \implies c_1 = 0$.
Now we know $c_1=0$. The equation becomes $0 \cdot \cos x + c_2 \sin x = 0$, which simplifies to $c_2 \sin x = 0$.
For this to hold for all $x$, we must have $c_2=0$.
Since the only solution is $c_1=0, c_2=0$, the functions are linearly independent.

Method 2: Using the Wronskian.
$f_1(x) = \cos x \implies f_1'(x) = -\sin x$
$f_2(x) = \sin x \implies f_2'(x) = \cos x$

The Wronskian is:
$W(f_1, f_2)(x) = \begin{vmatrix} \cos x & \sin x \\ -\sin x & \cos x \end{vmatrix}$
$W(x) = (\cos x)(\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)$
$W(x) = \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x$
$W(x) = 1$

Since the Wronskian $W(x) = 1$ is non-zero for all $x$, the functions $f_1(x) = \cos x$ and $f_2(x) = \sin x$ are **linearly independent**.

*Reasoning connection to CO4:* The set $\{\cos x, \sin x\}$ forms a basis for the vector space of solutions to the differential equation $y'' + y = 0$. Understanding linear independence is crucial for identifying bases of solution spaces for linear differential equations, a core concept in analyzing systems.
