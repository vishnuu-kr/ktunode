# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - MODULE 3: MONETARY SYSTEM - CONCEPTS

## 1. Functions of Money and its Evolution

### 1.1 Functions of Money

Money serves three primary functions in an economy:

*   **Medium of Exchange:** Money allows for the easy exchange of goods and services, eliminating the need for barter.  This reduces transaction costs and improves efficiency.
    *   *Example:*  Instead of trading your engineering services directly for food (barter), you receive money and then use that money to purchase food.
*   **Unit of Account:** Money provides a common standard for measuring the relative value of goods and services. This simplifies accounting and comparison of prices.
    *   *Example:* Pricing a house in dollars ($) rather than in units of wheat or number of labor hours.
*   **Store of Value:** Money allows individuals to hold wealth over time.  While not perfect due to inflation, money generally maintains its purchasing power.
    *   *Example:* Saving money today to purchase a car next year.

### 1.2 Evolution of Money

Money has evolved through various forms over time:

*   **Commodity Money:** Items with intrinsic value that are used as money (e.g., gold, silver, salt, cattle).
    *   *Example:* Gold coins used as currency, the value derived from the gold itself.
    *   *Challenge:* Difficulty in portability, divisibility, and standardization.
*   **Representative Money:** Paper money that is backed by a commodity (e.g., gold standard).  Holders could redeem the paper money for the underlying commodity.
    *   *Example:* Banknotes that could be redeemed for gold at a specific rate.
    *   *Challenge:* Reliance on trust in the issuer to maintain sufficient reserves.
*   **Fiat Money:** Money that is declared legal tender by the government and is not backed by any physical commodity. Its value derives from government decree and public confidence.
    *   *Example:* US Dollars, Euros, and Japanese Yen.
    *   *Challenge:* Susceptible to inflation if the government prints too much money.

**Important Point to Remember:** Trust and confidence are crucial for the acceptance and stability of any monetary system, especially fiat money.

## 2. Types of Monetary Systems

### 2.1 Commodity-Based System

*   Currency is directly linked to a commodity, typically gold or silver.
*   The value of the currency is determined by the value of the commodity.
*   **Example:** Gold Standard.

### 2.2 Representative System

*   Currency is backed by a commodity but not directly convertible.
*   The government guarantees to redeem the currency for the commodity at a fixed rate.
*   **Example:** Silver Certificate Era

### 2.3 Fiat Money System

*   Currency is not backed by any physical commodity and is declared legal tender by the government.
*   The value of the currency is determined by supply and demand, influenced by government policy and public confidence.
*   **Example:** Modern monetary systems adopted by most countries today.

### 2.4 Hybrid Systems

*   Systems that combine elements of different monetary systems.  For example, a system might target a specific exchange rate against another currency.
*   **Example:** Currency boards, which issue domestic currency that is fully backed by a foreign currency.

## 3. Money Supply Measures

Money supply refers to the total amount of money in circulation in an economy. Different measures capture varying degrees of liquidity.

*   **M0 (Monetary Base):** Currency in circulation and commercial banks' reserves held at the central bank.  This is the narrowest measure.
*   **M1 (Narrow Money):** Currency in circulation plus checkable (demand) deposits.  Represents the most liquid forms of money readily available for transactions.
*   **M2 (Broad Money):** M1 plus savings deposits, small-denomination time deposits, and money market mutual funds.  Less liquid than M1.
*   **M3 (Broadest Money):** M2 plus large-denomination time deposits, repurchase agreements, and other less liquid assets.

**Example:** If the Central Bank prints more currency, it increases M0, which then leads to an increase in M1, M2, and M3 through the money multiplier effect (explained later).

## 4. Role of Central Banks

Central banks are the institutions responsible for managing a country's monetary system. Key functions include:

*   **Issuing Currency:** Printing and regulating the circulation of the nation's currency.
*   **Controlling the Money Supply:** Using monetary policy tools to influence the amount of money and credit in the economy.
    *   **Open Market Operations:** Buying (increasing money supply) or selling (decreasing money supply) government securities.
    *   **Reserve Requirements:** Setting the percentage of deposits that commercial banks must hold in reserve. Lowering reserve requirements increases the money supply.
    *   **Discount Rate (or Policy Rate):** The interest rate at which commercial banks can borrow money directly from the central bank.  Lowering the rate increases the money supply.
*   **Acting as a Lender of Last Resort:** Providing loans to commercial banks facing liquidity problems.
*   **Supervising and Regulating Banks:** Ensuring the stability and soundness of the banking system.
*   **Managing Exchange Rates:** Some central banks intervene in foreign exchange markets to influence the value of their currency.

**Example:** The Federal Reserve (Fed) in the US, the European Central Bank (ECB) in Europe, and the Bank of England (BoE) in the UK.

## 5. Inflation and Deflation

*   **Inflation:** A sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time.  This reduces the purchasing power of money.
    *   **Causes:** Excessive money supply growth, demand-pull inflation (excess demand), cost-push inflation (rising production costs).
    *   **Consequences:** Erosion of purchasing power, redistribution of wealth (favoring borrowers), uncertainty and reduced investment, menu costs (costs of changing prices).
*   **Deflation:** A sustained decrease in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time.
    *   **Causes:** Decrease in money supply, decrease in aggregate demand, increased productivity.
    *   **Consequences:** Delayed consumption (expectation of lower prices), increased real debt burden, reduced corporate profits, economic stagnation.

**Example:** Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe in the late 2000s, where prices doubled every few hours. Japan experienced prolonged deflation in the 1990s and 2000s.

## 6. Interest Rates

Interest rates represent the cost of borrowing money or the return on lending money.

*   **Nominal Interest Rate:** The stated interest rate, without accounting for inflation.
*   **Real Interest Rate:** The nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation.  It represents the true cost of borrowing or the true return on lending.
    *   *Formula:* Real Interest Rate ≈ Nominal Interest Rate - Inflation Rate
*   **Factors Influencing Interest Rates:**
    *   **Central Bank Policy:** Central banks influence interest rates through monetary policy tools.
    *   **Inflation Expectations:** Higher inflation expectations typically lead to higher nominal interest rates.
    *   **Demand for Credit:** Higher demand for credit pushes interest rates upward.
    *   **Supply of Credit:** Higher supply of credit pushes interest rates downward.
    *   **Risk:** Riskier borrowers typically pay higher interest rates.

**Example:** If the nominal interest rate on a loan is 5% and the inflation rate is 2%, the real interest rate is approximately 3%.

## 7. Money, Interest Rates, and Economic Activity

The relationship between money, interest rates, and economic activity is complex and multifaceted.

*   **Expansionary Monetary Policy:** Increasing the money supply and lowering interest rates aims to stimulate economic activity by encouraging borrowing and investment.
    *   Lower interest rates make it cheaper for businesses to borrow money for investment projects.
    *   Lower interest rates also make it cheaper for consumers to borrow money for purchases, like homes and cars.
*   **Contractionary Monetary Policy:** Decreasing the money supply and raising interest rates aims to curb inflation by reducing borrowing and spending.
    *   Higher interest rates make it more expensive for businesses to borrow money for investment projects.
    *   Higher interest rates also make it more expensive for consumers to borrow money for purchases, like homes and cars.

**The Money Multiplier:** An increase in the monetary base (M0) leads to a larger increase in the broader money supply (M1, M2, M3) due to the money multiplier effect. Banks lend out a portion of their deposits, which are then re-deposited in other banks, creating a ripple effect.

*   *Simplified Formula:* Money Multiplier = 1 / Reserve Requirement
    *   *Example:* If the reserve requirement is 10%, the money multiplier is 10. An initial deposit of $100 can potentially expand the money supply by $1000.

**Important Point to Remember:**  Monetary policy operates with a time lag. The effects of a monetary policy change may not be fully felt for several months or even years.

## Practice Questions/Exercises

1.  **What are the three main functions of money? Give an example of each.**
    *   **Answer:** Medium of exchange (buying groceries with dollars), unit of account (pricing a car in dollars), store of value (saving money for retirement).

2.  **Explain the difference between commodity money and fiat money.**
    *   **Answer:** Commodity money has intrinsic value (e.g., gold), while fiat money is declared legal tender by the government and has no intrinsic value.

3.  **What are the main tools that a central bank uses to control the money supply? Briefly describe each.**
    *   **Answer:** Open market operations (buying/selling government securities), reserve requirements (percentage of deposits banks must hold), and the discount rate (interest rate at which banks can borrow from the central bank).

4.  **If the nominal interest rate is 7% and the inflation rate is 3%, what is the approximate real interest rate?**
    *   **Answer:** Real interest rate ≈ 7% - 3% = 4%

5.  **How does increasing the money supply generally affect interest rates and economic activity?**
    *   **Answer:** Increasing the money supply typically lowers interest rates, which encourages borrowing and investment, leading to increased economic activity.

6.  **Define M1 and M2. What are the key differences?**
    *   **Answer:** M1 includes currency in circulation and checkable deposits (demand deposits), representing the most liquid forms of money. M2 includes M1 plus savings deposits, small-denomination time deposits, and money market mutual funds, which are less liquid than M1.

7. **Explain what would happen to the money multiplier if the reserve requirement was decreased from 20% to 5%.**
    * **Answer:** If the reserve requirement decreases from 20% to 5%, the money multiplier will increase.  Money Multiplier = 1 / Reserve Requirement.  At 20% the multiplier is 1/0.2 = 5.  At 5% the multiplier is 1/0.05 = 20.  This means that a given injection of reserves into the banking system will lead to a much larger expansion of the money supply when the reserve requirement is lower.
