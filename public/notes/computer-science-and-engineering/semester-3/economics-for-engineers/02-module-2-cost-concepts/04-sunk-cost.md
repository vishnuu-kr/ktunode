## ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts - Sunk Cost

**Learning Outcomes:**

*   Define sunk cost and explain its characteristics.
*   Differentiate sunk costs from other types of costs.
*   Explain why sunk costs should be ignored in decision-making.
*   Apply the concept of sunk cost to engineering and business scenarios.
*   Identify the pitfalls of considering sunk costs in decision-making.

---

**1. Definition and Characteristics of Sunk Cost**

*   **Definition:** A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. It represents an expenditure that has already happened, and no future decision can change it.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   **Irrecoverable:** Once the cost is incurred, it is gone. There is no way to get the money back.
    *   **Past Expenditure:** It represents an investment or expense that took place in the past.
    *   **Irrelevant to Future Decisions:** Because it's unrecoverable, a sunk cost should not influence future economic decisions.

**2. Differentiating Sunk Costs from Other Types of Costs**

| Cost Type        | Definition                                                                                   | Recoverability                                                                    | Relevance to Future Decisions                                                                |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sunk Cost**    | Cost already incurred and cannot be recovered.                                               | Irrecoverable                                                                       | Irrelevant                                                                                  |
| **Opportunity Cost** | The potential benefit that is given up when choosing one alternative over another.      | N/A - Represents a foregone benefit.                                              | Very relevant; crucial for comparing different alternatives.                                |
| **Marginal Cost**  | The additional cost incurred by producing one more unit of a good or service.               | Potentially recoverable, depending on production decisions and market conditions. | Highly relevant; used for determining optimal production levels.                              |
| **Variable Cost**  | Costs that change with the level of production.                                              | Recoverable if production stops (e.g., raw materials).                               | Relevant; part of the overall cost structure used for pricing and production decisions. |
| **Fixed Cost**     | Costs that do not change with the level of production (within a certain range).             | Recoverability varies; some can be reduced (e.g., rent renegotiation), others not. | Relevant for short-term decisions, especially in considering shutting down operations.      |

**3. Why Sunk Costs Should Be Ignored in Decision-Making**

*   **Rational Decision-Making:**  Economic decisions should be based on future costs and benefits. Sunk costs are irrelevant because they don't affect future outcomes.
*   **Avoiding the Sunk Cost Fallacy:**  The "sunk cost fallacy" is the tendency to continue investing in a project or decision simply because you've already invested time, money, or effort into it, even if there's evidence that it's no longer the best course of action.
*   **Focus on Marginal Analysis:**  Concentrate on the additional (marginal) costs and benefits of each available option.  Choose the option that maximizes net benefit (benefit - cost) from this point forward.

**4. Applying the Concept of Sunk Cost to Engineering and Business Scenarios**

*   **Scenario 1: Project Abandonment**
    *   A construction company has spent $1 million on preliminary engineering for a new bridge.  After this expenditure, a geological survey reveals unstable soil conditions, making the bridge significantly more expensive to build.
    *   **Sunk Cost:** The $1 million spent on preliminary engineering.
    *   **Decision:** The company should evaluate whether the *future* costs of building the bridge (taking into account the new geological information) are justified by the *future* benefits.  The $1 million is already spent and should not factor into the decision.
*   **Scenario 2: Equipment Upgrade**
    *   A manufacturing plant purchased a machine for $500,000 five years ago. Its current market value is $100,000. A new, more efficient machine is available for $800,000.
    *   **Sunk Cost:** The difference between the original purchase price ($500,000) and the current market value ($100,000), which equals $400,000.  Even if the current machine is not sold, the fact that the money is gone makes it a sunk cost.
    *   **Decision:** The company should compare the *future* operating costs and increased revenue from the new machine against the *future* costs of continuing to use the old machine, *and* the cost of the new machine ($800,000). The sunk cost from the old machine should not influence this decision. It might be that even though the sunk cost is high, it is still beneficial to purchase the new machine, or vice-versa.
*   **Scenario 3: Software Development**
    *   A company has invested $200,000 in developing a software product.  After a year of development, it becomes clear that the market is shrinking, and the potential revenue from the software will likely be less than originally projected.
    *   **Sunk Cost:** The $200,000 already invested in development.
    *   **Decision:** The company needs to assess the future costs required to complete the software (e.g., further development, marketing) and compare that against the projected *future* revenue.  If the remaining costs exceed the expected revenue, it may be better to abandon the project, even though $200,000 has already been spent.

**5. Pitfalls of Considering Sunk Costs in Decision-Making**

*   **Emotional Attachment:**  People often have an emotional attachment to projects or investments they've put time and effort into, making it difficult to make rational decisions.
*   **"Throwing Good Money After Bad":**  Continuing to invest in a failing project in an attempt to recoup sunk costs can lead to even greater losses.
*   **Hindering Innovation:**  Fear of writing off sunk costs can prevent businesses from adopting new technologies or pursuing more promising opportunities.
*   **Justifying Past Decisions:** People often use future actions to justify past decisions, even if those past decisions were mistakes.  Acknowledging a sunk cost means acknowledging a potential error in judgment, which can be psychologically difficult.

**6. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**

A consulting firm spent $50,000 on market research to determine the viability of a new product. The research indicated that the product has a low probability of success. Should the firm continue developing the product, considering the $50,000 investment?

*   **(a) Yes, because the firm has already invested $50,000.**
*   **(b) No, the firm should only consider future costs and benefits.**
*   **(c) Yes, if the firm can recoup the $50,000 investment.**
*   **(d) It depends on whether the competitors are also developing similar products.**

**Answer: (b)**  The $50,000 is a sunk cost and should not influence the decision. The firm should focus on future costs of development and future projected revenue.

**Question 2:**

An engineering firm has spent $100,000 designing a new type of engine. Due to changes in government regulations, the engine no longer meets environmental standards. What should the firm do?

*   **(a) Continue developing the engine to recoup the $100,000 investment.**
*   **(b) Abandon the project and write off the $100,000 as a sunk cost.**
*   **(c) Redesign the engine to meet the new regulations, regardless of the cost.**
*   **(d) Lobby the government to change the regulations.**

**Answer: (b)** Abandon the project. The $100,000 is a sunk cost. Developing or lobbying incurs further cost with no guarantee of success and the first option will almost certainly fail due to it not meeting environmental standards.

**Question 3:**

Explain why the sunk cost fallacy can lead to poor investment decisions. Provide an example.

**Answer:**

The sunk cost fallacy leads to poor investment decisions because it prioritizes past investments over future profitability. Decision-makers irrationally continue investing in projects or ventures that are likely to fail, simply because they've already invested significant resources.

**Example:** A company invests heavily in a marketing campaign. After initial results show little to no impact, instead of cutting their losses, they continue to pour money into the campaign, hoping to eventually see a return, even though the data indicates the campaign is ineffective. This is the sunk cost fallacy in action. They are letting the past investment, which is unrecoverable, influence their future decision making. A rational decision would be to cut the losses, and re-allocate resources to other, more promising campaigns.

**Exercise:**

A construction company starts building a residential complex. After completing 40% of the project, the company discovers that the foundation is unstable and would require an additional $500,000 to fix. The projected revenue from the completed complex is $1 million. The company has already spent $600,000 on the construction and materials.  Should the company continue with the project? Explain your reasoning.

**Answer:**

*   **Sunk Cost:** $600,000 (already spent)
*   **Future Cost:** $500,000 (to fix the foundation)
*   **Future Revenue:** $1,000,000
*   **Profit if the project is completed:** $1,000,000 - $500,000 = $500,000

Decision: The company should continue with the project. The fact that $600,000 has already been spent is a sunk cost and irrelevant. The relevant question is whether future revenue will be greater than future costs. In this case, future revenue ($1 million) exceeds future cost ($500,000) and the company will realise a $500,000 profit if it continues the project.

---

**7. Important Points to Remember**

*   Sunk costs are irrelevant to future decisions.
*   Focus on future costs and benefits when making economic choices.
*   Avoid the sunk cost fallacy.
*   Be willing to abandon projects that are no longer viable, even if you've invested significant resources.
*   Rational decision-making leads to better economic outcomes.
