# COMPUTATIONAL NUMBER THEORY - Module 4: Algebraic Number Theory

## Topic: Factorization in Number Fields

This module delves into the fascinating world of algebraic number theory, focusing on how familiar concepts like prime factorization extend to more general number systems known as number fields.

### 1. Number Fields and Rings of Integers

#### 1.1 What is a Number Field?

*   **Definition:** A **number field** is a finite extension of the field of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$. This means it's a field $K$ such that $[\mathbb{Q} \subseteq K] < \infty$, where $[ ]$ denotes the degree of the field extension.
*   **Examples:**
    *   $\mathbb{Q}$ itself is a number field of degree 1.
    *   Quadratic fields: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$, where $d$ is a square-free integer. For example, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ or $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$.
    *   Cyclotomic fields: $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$, where $\zeta_n$ is a primitive $n$-th root of unity. For example, $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3) = \mathbb{Q}(\frac{-1+\sqrt{-3}}{2})$.
*   **Degree of a Number Field:** The degree of a number field $K$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, denoted by $[K:\mathbb{Q}]$, is the dimension of $K$ as a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$.

#### 1.2 Ring of Integers

*   **Definition:** For a number field $K$, the **ring of integers** of $K$, denoted by $\mathcal{O}_K$, is the set of all elements in $K$ that are roots of monic polynomials with integer coefficients.
    $$ \mathcal{O}_K = \{ \alpha \in K \mid \exists p(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x], p(x) \text{ monic, } p(\alpha) = 0 \} $$
*   **Properties:**
    *   $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a ring.
    *   $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K$.
    *   $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a **Dedekind domain**. This is a crucial property that guarantees unique factorization of ideals.
*   **Examples:**
    *   For $K = \mathbb{Q}$, $\mathcal{O}_\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Z}$.
    *   For $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ (where $d$ is square-free):
        *   If $d \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4}$, then $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}] = \{a + b\sqrt{d} \mid a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
        *   If $d \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, then $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2}] = \{a + b\frac{1+\sqrt{d}}{2} \mid a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
        *   **Example:** For $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$. For $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})} = \mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}]$.

#### 1.3 Integral Basis

*   **Definition:** An **integral basis** for $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a set of elements $\{\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots, \omega_n\}$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$ such that every element $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$ can be uniquely written as a linear combination with integer coefficients:
    $$ \alpha = a_1 \omega_1 + a_2 \omega_2 + \dots + a_n \omega_n, \quad a_i \in \mathbb{Z} $$
    where $n = [K:\mathbb{Q}]$ is the degree of the number field.
*   **Significance:** The existence of an integral basis allows us to represent all elements of the ring of integers as vectors over $\mathbb{Z}$, which is fundamental for algorithmic approaches.
*   **Example:**
    *   For $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, an integral basis is $\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$.
    *   For $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, an integral basis is $\{1, \frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}\}$.

### 2. Ideals in the Ring of Integers

#### 2.1 Definition and Properties of Ideals

*   **Definition:** An **ideal** $I$ of a ring $R$ is a non-empty subset of $R$ such that:
    1.  For any $a, b \in I$, $a - b \in I$ (closure under subtraction).
    2.  For any $a \in I$ and $r \in R$, $ra \in I$ and $ar \in I$ (closure under multiplication by ring elements).
*   **Principal Ideals:** An ideal generated by a single element $a \in R$, denoted by $(a)$, is called a **principal ideal**.
    $$ (a) = \{ra \mid r \in R\} $$
*   **Properties of Ideals in $\mathcal{O}_K$:**
    *   **Finitely Generated:** Every ideal in $\mathcal{O}_K$ is finitely generated.
    *   **Principal Ideal Domain (PID):** If every ideal in $\mathcal{O}_K$ is principal, then $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a PID.
        *   **Example:** $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$ is also a PID.
    *   **Not Always PID:** However, not all rings of integers are PIDs.
        *   **Example:** $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. The ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is not principal. This is a key motivation for studying ideal factorization.

#### 2.2 Prime Ideals

*   **Definition:** A non-zero ideal $P$ of a ring $R$ is a **prime ideal** if:
    1.  $P \neq R$.
    2.  For any $a, b \in R$, if $ab \in P$, then $a \in P$ or $b \in P$.
*   **Prime Ideals in $\mathcal{O}_K$:**
    *   Prime ideals in $\mathcal{O}_K$ play the role of prime numbers in $\mathbb{Z}$.
    *   They are non-zero and maximal ideals in the context of Dedekind domains.
    *   **Example:** In $\mathbb{Z}$, the prime ideals are $(p)$ for each prime number $p$.

### 3. Unique Factorization of Ideals

#### 3.1 The Fundamental Theorem of Dedekind Domains

*   **Statement:** The ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of any number field $K$ is a Dedekind domain. A fundamental property of Dedekind domains is that every non-zero ideal can be uniquely factored into a product of prime ideals, up to the order of the factors.
    $$ I = P_1^{e_1} P_2^{e_2} \dots P_k^{e_k} $$
    where $P_i$ are distinct prime ideals and $e_i \geq 1$ are integers.
*   **Analogy to Integers:** This is analogous to the unique prime factorization of integers: $n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \dots p_k^{e_k}$.
*   **Example:** In $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})}$, we have the ideal $(6)$.
    *   In $\mathbb{Z}$, $6 = 2 \times 3$.
    *   In $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})}$, the prime factorization of $(6)$ is $(2)(3) = (2)(3)$.
    *   However, the prime ideals $(2)$ and $(3)$ themselves might be factorable.
    *   It turns out that in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})}$, $2\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{O}(\sqrt{-5})} = (2) = P_2^2$ and $3\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})} = (3) = P_3 P_3'$ where $P_2, P_3, P_3'$ are prime ideals.
    *   So, $(6) = P_2^2 P_3 P_3'$.

#### 3.2 Relating Factorization of Elements and Ideals

*   **Principal Ideals as Factors:** If an ideal $I$ is principal, say $I = (\alpha)$ for some $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$, then the factorization of the principal ideal $(\alpha)$ into prime ideals corresponds to the "factorization" of the element $\alpha$.
*   **Problem:** In a ring of integers that is *not* a PID, the factorization of an element $\alpha$ into irreducible elements (analogous to prime numbers) is *not* unique. However, the factorization of the principal ideal $(\alpha)$ into prime ideals *is* unique.
*   **Example:** In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the element $6$ can be factored in two ways into irreducibles:
    $$ 6 = 2 \times 3 $$
    $$ 6 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}) $$
    The elements $2, 3, (1 + \sqrt{-5}), (1 - \sqrt{-5})$ are irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, but they are not prime.
*   **Ideal Factorization:** Let's look at the ideal factorizations:
    *   $(6) = (2)(3)$.
    *   $(1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$.
    *   We found that $(2) = P_2^2$ and $(3) = P_3 P_3'$.
    *   It turns out that $(1 + \sqrt{-5}) = P_2 P_3'$ and $(1 - \sqrt{-5}) = P_2 P_3$ for some prime ideals $P_2, P_3, P_3'$.
    *   Thus, $(6) = P_2^2 P_3 P_3'$, which is a unique factorization.

#### 3.3 Prime Factorization of Principal Ideals

*   For any $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$, the principal ideal $(\alpha)$ can be uniquely factored into prime ideals:
    $$ (\alpha) = P_1^{e_1} P_2^{e_2} \dots P_k^{e_k} $$
*   **Finding the Prime Ideals:** The prime ideals in $\mathcal{O}_K$ arise from the factorization of rational primes $p$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$. Specifically, if $p$ is a rational prime, then the ideal $(p)$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$ factors into prime ideals:
    $$ (p) = P_1^{e_1} P_2^{e_2} \dots P_m^{e_m} $$
    The prime ideals $P_i$ appearing in this factorization are precisely the prime ideals of $\mathcal{O}_K$ lying above $p$.

### 4. Algorithms for Factorization

#### 4.1 Factoring Rational Primes in $\mathcal{O}_K$

*   **The Problem:** Given a rational prime $p$ and the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$, we want to find the prime ideal factorization of the principal ideal $(p)$.
*   **Connection to Polynomial Factorization:** There's a deep connection between the factorization of $(p)$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$ and the factorization of the minimal polynomial of a generator of $K$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ modulo $p$.
*   **Algorithm (using a generator $\theta$ and its minimal polynomial $f(x)$):**
    1.  Let $K = \mathbb{Q}(\theta)$ and let $f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be the minimal polynomial of $\theta$.
    2.  Consider the polynomial $f(x) \pmod{p}$.
    3.  Factor $f(x) \pmod{p}$ into irreducible polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_p$:
        $$ f(x) \equiv g_1(x)^{e_1} g_2(x)^{e_2} \dots g_m(x)^{e_m} \pmod{p} $$
        where $g_i(x)$ are distinct irreducible polynomials in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$.
    4.  Each factor $g_i(x)$ corresponds to a prime ideal $P_i$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$ lying above $p$. The ideal $P_i$ can be represented as:
        $$ P_i = \{a \in \mathcal{O}_K \mid a \equiv h( \theta ) \pmod{P_i} \text{ for some } h(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x] \text{ where } h(x) \equiv g_i(x) \pmod{p} \} $$
        More concretely, $P_i$ is the kernel of the ring homomorphism:
        $$ \phi_i: \mathcal{O}_K \to \mathbb{F}_p[\zeta] / (g_i(\zeta)) \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(g_i(x)) $$
        where $\zeta$ is a root of $g_i(x)$ in an extension field.
    5.  The exponent $e_i$ in the polynomial factorization corresponds to the exponent of the prime ideal $P_i$ in the factorization of $(p)$:
        $$ (p) = P_1^{e_1} P_2^{e_2} \dots P_m^{e_m} $$
*   **Example:** Factor $(2)$ in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})}$.
    *   $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. Minimal polynomial of $\sqrt{2}$ is $f(x) = x^2 - 2$.
    *   Consider $f(x) \pmod{2}$: $x^2 - 2 \equiv x^2 \pmod{2}$.
    *   $x^2$ is irreducible modulo 2.
    *   So, $(2) = P_1^2$, where $P_1$ is the prime ideal above 2.
    *   $P_1$ is the kernel of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})} \to \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2)$.
    *   $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$.
    *   Elements are of the form $a + b\sqrt{2}$.
    *   $a + b\sqrt{2} \pmod{P_1}$ corresponds to $a \pmod{2}$.
    *   So, $P_1 = \{a+b\sqrt{2} \mid a \in 2\mathbb{Z}\} = (2, \sqrt{2})$.
    *   Thus, $(2) = (2, \sqrt{2})^2$.

*   **Example:** Factor $(5)$ in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})}$.
    *   $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$. Minimal polynomial of $\sqrt{-5}$ is $f(x) = x^2 + 5$.
    *   Consider $f(x) \pmod{5}$: $x^2 + 5 \equiv x^2 \pmod{5}$.
    *   $x^2$ is irreducible modulo 5.
    *   So, $(5) = P_1^2$, where $P_1$ is the prime ideal above 5.
    *   $P_1 = \{a+b\sqrt{-5} \mid a \in 5\mathbb{Z}\} = (5, \sqrt{-5})$.
    *   Thus, $(5) = (5, \sqrt{-5})^2$.

*   **Example:** Factor $(2)$ in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})}$.
    *   $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$. Minimal polynomial is $f(x) = x^2 + 5$.
    *   Consider $f(x) \pmod{2}$: $x^2 + 5 \equiv x^2 + 1 \pmod{2}$.
    *   $x^2 + 1 = (x+1)^2 \pmod{2}$.
    *   So, $(2) = P_1^2$, where $P_1$ is the prime ideal above 2.
    *   $P_1$ corresponds to the factor $x+1 \pmod{2}$.
    *   $P_1 = \{a+b\sqrt{-5} \mid a+b \text{ is even}\}$.
    *   This ideal is generated by $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$. $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   Thus, $(2) = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})^2$.

#### 4.2 Factoring Principal Ideals $(\alpha)$

*   **Algorithm:**
    1.  To factor $(\alpha)$, first factor the rational integer $|\text{N}(\alpha)|$, where $\text{N}(\alpha)$ is the norm of $\alpha$. Let the prime factorization of $|\text{N}(\alpha)|$ be $p_1^{a_1} \dots p_k^{a_k}$.
    2.  For each prime $p_i$, find the prime ideal factorization of $(p_i)$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$. Let $(p_i) = P_{i1}^{e_{i1}} \dots P_{im}^{e_{im}}$.
    3.  Now, $(\alpha)$ must be a product of these prime ideals. For each prime ideal $P$ found in step 2, we need to determine its exponent in the factorization of $(\alpha)$.
    4.  If $P$ appears in the factorization of $(p_i)$, then $P$ will appear in the factorization of $(\alpha)$ with exponent $v_P(\alpha)$, where $v_P(\alpha)$ is the largest integer such that $P^{v_P(\alpha)}$ divides $(\alpha)$. This can be determined by checking the divisibility of $\alpha$ by powers of $P$.
    5.  Alternatively, if we know the factorization of each $(p_i)$ and $(\alpha) = \prod P_j^{f_j}$, then $\text{N}((\alpha)) = \prod \text{N}(P_j)^{f_j} = |\text{N}(\alpha)|$. We also know that $\text{N}((p_i)) = p_i^{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}$.
    6.  A practical approach is to find a basis for each prime ideal $P$ dividing $(p_i)$ and then use algorithms for ideal arithmetic to find the exponent.

*   **Computational Tools:** Modern computer algebra systems (like PARI/GP, Magma) have built-in functions for these computations, which often employ sophisticated algorithms beyond basic polynomial factorization. These algorithms might involve using Minkowski bounds and LLL-reduction for finding bases of ideals and for factoring elements.

### Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
*   Since $5 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, the ring of integers is $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})} = \mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}]$.
*   An integral basis is $\{1, \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\}$.

**Answer 2:**
*   (a) Since $-7 \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, the ring of integers is $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})} = \mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{-7}}{2}]$. An integral basis is $\{1, \frac{1+\sqrt{-7}}{2}\}$. Let $\omega = \frac{1+\sqrt{-7}}{2}$. Then $\omega^2 - \omega + 2 = 0$.
*   (b) Factor $(7)$ in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})}$.
    *   Minimal polynomial of $\omega$ is $x^2 - x + 2$.
    *   Consider $f(x) \pmod{7}$: $x^2 - x + 2 \pmod{7}$.
    *   We look for roots of $x^2 - x + 2 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$.
    *   Let's test values:
        *   $0^2 - 0 + 2 \equiv 2 \pmod{7}$
        *   $1^2 - 1 + 2 \equiv 2 \pmod{7}$
        *   $2^2 - 2 + 2 = 4 - 2 + 2 = 4 \pmod{7}$
        *   $3^2 - 3 + 2 = 9 - 3 + 2 = 8 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$
        *   $4^2 - 4 + 2 = 16 - 4 + 2 = 14 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$. So $x=4$ is a root.
        *   $5^2 - 5 + 2 = 25 - 5 + 2 = 22 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$
        *   $6^2 - 6 + 2 = 36 - 6 + 2 = 32 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}$
    *   So, $x^2 - x + 2 \equiv (x-4)(x-?) \pmod{7}$. The sum of roots is 1. If one root is 4, the other is $1-4 = -3 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}$.
    *   Thus, $x^2 - x + 2 \equiv (x-4)^2 \pmod{7}$.
    *   This means $(7)$ factors as $P^2$ for some prime ideal $P$.
    *   The ideal $P$ corresponds to the factor $x-4 \pmod{7}$.
    *   $P = \{ a + b\omega \mid a + b(4) \equiv 0 \pmod{7} \}$. This ideal is generated by $7$ and $4-\omega$.
    *   So, $(7) = (7, 4-\omega)^2$.

*   (c) Factor $(3)$ in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})}$.
    *   Minimal polynomial is $f(x) = x^2 - x + 2$.
    *   Consider $f(x) \pmod{3}$: $x^2 - x + 2 \pmod{3}$.
    *   Test roots:
        *   $0^2 - 0 + 2 \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$
        *   $1^2 - 1 + 2 = 2 \pmod{3}$
        *   $2^2 - 2 + 2 = 4 - 2 + 2 = 4 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$
    *   The polynomial $x^2 - x + 2$ is irreducible modulo 3.
    *   Therefore, $(3)$ is a prime ideal in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-7})}$.

**Answer 3:**
*   (a) To show irreducibility, one can use the norm. For an element $\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $\text{N}(\alpha) = a^2 + 5b^2$ if $\alpha = a + b\sqrt{-5}$.
    *   If $2 = \alpha\beta$, then $\text{N}(2) = 4 = \text{N}(\alpha)\text{N}(\beta)$. Possible norms for elements in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ are $a^2+5b^2$.
        *   $\text{N}(\alpha) = 1 \implies \alpha = \pm 1$.
        *   $\text{N}(\alpha) = 2$ has no integer solutions for $a^2+5b^2=2$.
        *   $\text{N}(\alpha) = 4$ has integer solutions $a=\pm 2, b=0$. So $\alpha = \pm 2$.
    *   Thus, if $2 = \alpha\beta$, either $\text{N}(\alpha)=1$ (so $\alpha$ is a unit) or $\text{N}(\alpha)=4$ (so $\alpha$ is an associate of 2). Therefore, 2 is irreducible.
    *   Similarly, for 3: $\text{N}(3)=9$. Possible norms are $1, 9$. $a^2+5b^2=3$ has no integer solutions. So 3 is irreducible.
    *   For $1+\sqrt{-5}$: $\text{N}(1+\sqrt{-5}) = 1^2 + 5(1)^2 = 6$. If $1+\sqrt{-5} = \alpha\beta$, then $6 = \text{N}(\alpha)\text{N}(\beta)$. Possible norms are 1, 2, 3, 6.
        *   $\text{N}(\alpha)=1 \implies \alpha$ is a unit.
        *   $\text{N}(\alpha)=2$ has no integer solutions for $a^2+5b^2=2$.
        *   $\text{N}(\alpha)=3$ has no integer solutions for $a^2+5b^2=3$.
    *   Thus, $1+\sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible. Since norms are the same, $1-\sqrt{-5}$ is also irreducible.

*   (b) To show $(2)$ is not principal, assume $(2) = (\alpha)$ for some $\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. Then $2 = u\alpha$ for some unit $u$. This implies $\alpha$ is an associate of $2$. The ideal generated by an associate of $2$ is $(2)$. So if $(2)$ were principal, it would be generated by an associate of $2$. However, the existence of the factorization $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ where the factors are irreducible but not associates suggests that the ring is not a PID, implying $(2)$ might not be principal. A more direct argument: If $(2) = (\alpha)$, then $\alpha \mid 2$. Since 2 is irreducible, $\alpha$ must be a unit or an associate of 2. If $\alpha$ is a unit, $(2)=(1)=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, which is false. If $\alpha$ is an associate of 2, then $(2) = (2)$, which doesn't preclude it being principal. The key issue is that the irreducibles $2, 3, 1+\sqrt{-5}, 1-\sqrt{-5}$ lead to different ideal factorizations. The fact that $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is a proper ideal dividing $(2)$ and is not $(2)$ itself implies $(2)$ is not principal.

*   (c) We showed in the lecture/notes that $(2) = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})^2$. Let $P_2 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   Check: $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})^2 = (4, 2(1+\sqrt{-5}), (1+\sqrt{-5})^2) = (4, 2+2\sqrt{-5}, 1 + 2\sqrt{-5} - 5) = (4, 2+2\sqrt{-5}, -4+2\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   Elements are of the form $4a + (2+2\sqrt{-5})b + (-4+2\sqrt{-5})c = 4(a-c) + 2\sqrt{-5}(b+c)$.
    *   Consider $2 \in (2)$. Can we write $2$ as $4A + 2\sqrt{-5}B$? Yes, if $A=0, B=0$ and $2 = 2\sqrt{-5}B$, impossible. This basis needs to be more carefully chosen.
    *   The basis for $P_2$ is $\{2, 1+\sqrt{-5}\}$.
    *   $P_2^2 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})(2, 1+\sqrt{-5}) = (4, 2(1+\sqrt{-5}), (1+\sqrt{-5})^2) = (4, 2+2\sqrt{-5}, 1+2\sqrt{-5}-5) = (4, 2+2\sqrt{-5}, -4+2\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   An element in $P_2^2$ is $4a + (2+2\sqrt{-5})b + (-4+2\sqrt{-5})c$.
    *   Consider $4 = 4(1) + (2+2\sqrt{-5})(0) + (-4+2\sqrt{-5})(0)$. So $4 \in P_2^2$.
    *   Consider $2+2\sqrt{-5} \in P_2^2$.
    *   Consider $-4+2\sqrt{-5} \in P_2^2$.
    *   We want to show $P_2^2 = (2)$.
    *   In $P_2^2$, we have $4$ and $2+2\sqrt{-5}$. Subtracting the second from the first: $4 - (2+2\sqrt{-5}) = 2 - 2\sqrt{-5}$.
    *   In $P_2^2$, we have $4$ and $-4+2\sqrt{-5}$. Adding them: $4 + (-4+2\sqrt{-5}) = 2\sqrt{-5}$.
    *   In $P_2^2$, we have $2+2\sqrt{-5}$ and $-4+2\sqrt{-5}$. Subtracting them: $(2+2\sqrt{-5}) - (-4+2\sqrt{-5}) = 6$.
    *   From $4$ and $2\sqrt{-5}$, we can get $4 - 2(2\sqrt{-5}) = 4-4\sqrt{-5}$.
    *   From $4$ and $2+2\sqrt{-5}$, we can get $4 - 2(2+2\sqrt{-5}) = 4 - 4 - 4\sqrt{-5} = -4\sqrt{-5}$.
    *   Since $2\sqrt{-5} \in P_2^2$ and $6 \in P_2^2$, then $6 - 2\sqrt{-5} \times k$ for some k.
    *   Let's use the norm. $\text{N}(P_2) = |\text{N}(2)| / \text{N}(1+\sqrt{-5}) = 4/6$, this is not correct.
    *   The norm of $P_2=(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is $\text{N}(P_2) = \text{N}((2))/\text{N}((2, 1+\sqrt{-5}))$, not useful.
    *   The norm of a prime ideal $P$ lying above $p$ is $p^f$, where $f$ is the inertia degree. For $P_2$ above 2, $f=1$ as $x+1$ has degree 1 mod 2. So $\text{N}(P_2) = 2^1 = 2$.
    *   Then $\text{N}(P_2^2) = \text{N}(P_2)^2 = 2^2 = 4$.
    *   Since $(2)$ has norm $4$, and $P_2^2$ divides $(2)$ with the same norm, $P_2^2 = (2)$.
    *   So, $(2) = P_2^2$.

*   (d) Factor $(3)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.
    *   The minimal polynomial is $f(x) = x^2+5$.
    *   Consider $f(x) \pmod{3}$: $x^2+5 \equiv x^2+2 \pmod{3}$.
    *   Test roots: $0^2+2=2$, $1^2+2=3 \equiv 0$, $2^2+2 = 4+2=6 \equiv 0$.
    *   So $x^2+2 \equiv (x-1)(x-2) \equiv (x+2)(x+1) \pmod{3}$.
    *   The factors are $x+1$ and $x+2$.
    *   This means $(3)$ splits into two distinct prime ideals, $P_3$ and $P_3'$.
    *   $P_3$ corresponds to $x+1 \pmod{3}$. $P_3 = (3, 1+\sqrt{-5})$. $\text{N}(P_3)=3$.
    *   $P_3'$ corresponds to $x+2 \pmod{3}$. $P_3' = (3, 2+\sqrt{-5})$. $\text{N}(P_3')=3$.
    *   So $(3) = P_3 P_3'$.

*   (e)
    *   We have $(6) = (2)(3) = P_2^2 P_3 P_3'$.
    *   We have $6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   We need to find the prime ideal factorization of $(1+\sqrt{-5})$ and $(1-\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   Consider $1+\sqrt{-5}$. Its norm is $6$.
    *   We know $(2) = P_2^2$ and $(3) = P_3 P_3'$.
    *   The prime ideals dividing $(1+\sqrt{-5})$ must be among $P_2, P_3, P_3'$.
    *   $\text{N}(1+\sqrt{-5})=6$. $\text{N}(P_2)=2$, $\text{N}(P_3)=3$, $\text{N}(P_3')=3$.
    *   The prime factors of $(1+\sqrt{-5})$ must have norms that multiply to 6.
    *   So $(1+\sqrt{-5})$ must be divisible by $P_2$ and one of $P_3, P_3'$. Let's say $P_3$.
    *   Thus, $(1+\sqrt{-5})$ is likely $P_2 P_3$.
    *   Let's check if $P_2 P_3$ divides $(1+\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   $P_2 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$. $P_3 = (3, 1+\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   $P_2 P_3 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})(3, 1+\sqrt{-5}) = (6, 2(1+\sqrt{-5}), 3(1+\sqrt{-5}), (1+\sqrt{-5})^2)$.
    *   $P_2 P_3 = (6, 2+2\sqrt{-5}, 3+3\sqrt{-5}, -4+2\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   Does $1+\sqrt{-5}$ belong to $P_2 P_3$?
    *   We need to express $1+\sqrt{-5}$ as a linear combination of the generators.
    *   Consider the element $1+\sqrt{-5} \in P_2$. Also $1+\sqrt{-5} \in P_3$.
    *   We need to find $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $1+\sqrt{-5} = a(6) + b(2+2\sqrt{-5}) + c(3+3\sqrt{-5}) + d(-4+2\sqrt{-5})$.
    *   Equating coefficients of $\sqrt{-5}$: $1 = 2b + 3c + 2d$.
    *   Equating constant terms: $1 = 6a + 2b + 3c - 4d$.
    *   From $1 = 2b + 3c + 2d$, if we take $b=0, c=0$, then $1=2d$, impossible. If we take $b=0, d=0$, then $1=3c$, impossible. If we take $c=0, d=0$, then $1=2b$, impossible.
    *   Let's use the property that if $P$ divides $(\alpha)$, then $\alpha \in P$.
    *   $1+\sqrt{-5} \in P_2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5} \in P_3$.
    *   So $1+\sqrt{-5}$ is a common multiple of $P_2$ and $P_3$. This means $(1+\sqrt{-5})$ is divisible by $P_2 P_3$.
    *   So $(1+\sqrt{-5}) = P_2 P_3$ or $(1+\sqrt{-5}) = P_2 P_3 K$ for some ideal $K$. Since norms match, $(1+\sqrt{-5}) = P_2 P_3$.
    *   Then $(1-\sqrt{-5})$ must be the other combination of prime ideal factors, which is $P_2 P_3'$.
    *   $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}) = (P_2 P_3)(P_2 P_3') = P_2^2 P_3 P_3'$. This matches the factorization of $(6)$.

### 6. Important Points to Remember:

*   **Number Field:** A finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$.
*   **Ring of Integers $\mathcal{O}_K$:** The "algebraic integers" within a number field $K$. It's a Dedekind domain.
*   **Dedekind Domain Property:** Every non-zero ideal has a unique factorization into prime ideals.
*   **Prime Ideals:** The fundamental building blocks for ideal factorization, analogous to prime numbers.
*   **Non-Uniqueness of Element Factorization:** In rings of integers that are not PIDs, elements may have multiple factorizations into irreducibles.
*   **Ideal Factorization Uniqueness:** The factorization of a *principal ideal* $(\alpha)$ into prime ideals is always unique, regardless of whether $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a PID.
*   **Polynomial Factorization Modulo $p$:** The factorization of a rational prime $p$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$ is directly related to the factorization of the minimal polynomial of a generator of $K$ modulo $p$.
*   **Computational Tools:** Utilize computational number theory software for practical factorization algorithms in number fields.
