### 1. Introduction to the Nervous System and Neurons

The nervous system is the body's primary control and communication network. It is responsible for sensing stimuli, processing information, and coordinating responses. The fundamental functional unit of the nervous system is the neuron.

**1.1 Neurons: The Building Blocks of the Nervous System**

*   **Definition:** A neuron, or nerve cell, is a specialized cell that transmits nerve impulses.
*   **Structure:**
    *   **Cell Body (Soma):** Contains the nucleus and organelles.
    *   **Dendrites:** Branch-like extensions that receive signals from other neurons.
    *   **Axon:** A long projection that transmits signals away from the cell body to other neurons, muscles, or glands.
    *   **Axon Terminals:** The ends of the axon that form synapses with other cells.
    *   **Myelin Sheath (in some neurons):** An insulating layer that speeds up nerve impulse transmission.

*   **How Neurons Communicate:**
    *   Neurons communicate via **electrochemical signals**.
    *   **Synapse:** The junction between two neurons where information is transmitted.
    *   **Neurotransmitters:** Chemical messengers released at the synapse.

**1.2 Resting Membrane Potential**

*   **Definition:** The electrical potential difference across the plasma membrane of a neuron or muscle cell when it is not stimulated.
*   **Mechanism:** Primarily due to the unequal distribution of ions (especially sodium, Na⁺, and potassium, K⁺) across the cell membrane, maintained by the sodium-potassium pump.
*   **Value:** Typically around -70 mV in neurons (the inside of the cell is negative relative to the outside).
*   **Reference:** Khandpur, R. S. (Third edition) discusses resting membrane potential as a fundamental concept in bioelectricity.

**1.3 Action Potential (Nerve Impulse)**

*   **Definition:** A rapid, transient change in the membrane potential of a neuron that propagates along the axon. It is the fundamental signal for communication in the nervous system.
*   **Phases:**
    1.  **Depolarization:** A rapid influx of Na⁺ ions into the cell, making the inside positive. Reaches a threshold potential (e.g., -55 mV).
    2.  **Repolarization:** Influx of Na⁺ stops, and K⁺ ions rush out of the cell, making the inside negative again.
    3.  **Hyperpolarization:** Membrane potential briefly becomes more negative than the resting potential due to slow closing of K⁺ channels.
    4.  **Return to Resting Potential:** Ion pumps restore the original ion distribution.
*   **All-or-None Principle:** An action potential is either generated fully or not at all. The strength of a stimulus does not affect the amplitude of an action potential, but rather the frequency of action potentials.
*   **Reference:** Cromwell, Weibell, & Pfeiffer (2nd Edition) provides detailed explanations of action potential generation and propagation.

**1.4 Action Potential of the Brain**

*   The brain, composed of billions of neurons, generates complex electrical activity through the summation of thousands of action potentials.
*   While individual neurons fire action potentials, the collective synchronized activity of large populations of neurons gives rise to measurable electrical patterns.

### 2. Brain Waves and Electroencephalography (EEG)

Brain waves represent the synchronized electrical activity of large neuronal populations in the brain. Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive technique used to record these electrical potentials.

**2.1 Brain Waves**

*   **Definition:** Rhythmic oscillations of electrical potentials recorded from the scalp, reflecting the underlying synchronized neuronal activity in the brain.
*   **Characteristics:** They are characterized by their frequency (Hz) and amplitude (µV).
*   **Types of Brain Waves (based on frequency):**
    *   **Delta (δ):** 0.5-4 Hz. Deep sleep, coma.
    *   **Theta (θ):** 4-8 Hz. Drowsiness, light sleep, meditation.
    *   **Alpha (α):** 8-13 Hz. Relaxed wakefulness, eyes closed. Most prominent in occipital regions.
    *   **Beta (β):** 13-30 Hz. Active thinking, alert, focused attention. Most prominent in frontal regions.
    *   **Gamma (γ):** >30 Hz. Higher cognitive functions, learning, memory.

*   **Factors Affecting Brain Waves:** State of consciousness, cognitive tasks, emotional state, age, medications, and neurological conditions.

**2.2 Electroencephalography (EEG)**

*   **Definition:** A neurophysiological monitoring method that records the electrical activity of the brain from the scalp.
*   **Princ:** Detects the minute voltage fluctuations generated by the postsynaptic potentials of cortical neurons. Action potentials are too brief and localized to be detected by scalp EEG.
*   **Applications:**
    *   Diagnosis of epilepsy and seizure disorders.
    *   Evaluation of sleep disorders.
    *   Monitoring brain activity during surgery.
    *   Detecting brain damage from head injuries, strokes, or tumors.
    *   Research into cognitive processes.
*   **Reference:** Webster (3rd edition) covers principles of bioelectric signal measurement, including EEG. Carr (4th edition) also provides insights into EEG equipment.

**2.3 Placement of Electrodes for EEG**

*   **System:** The **International 10-20 System** is the standard for electrode placement on the scalp. It ensures reproducible placement and consistent data acquisition across individuals.
*   **Rationale:** The 10-20 system is based on the relative distances between specific anatomical landmarks on the skull (nasion and inion). The numbers 10 and 20 refer to the percentage distance of electrode placement.
*   **Key Landmarks:**
    *   **Nasion:** The midline point between the eyes.
    *   **Inion:** The prominent bony projection at the back of the head.
    *   **Cz (Central Vertex):** Located at the vertex of the skull.
    *   **Fp (Frontopolar):** Forehead region.
    *   **F (Frontal):** Frontal lobe.
    *   **T (Temporal):** Temporal lobe.
    *   **P (Parietal):** Parietal lobe.
    *   **O (Occipital):** Occipital lobe.
    *   **Letters are combined with numbers (odd for left hemisphere, even for right) or 'z' for midline.** For example, Fp1 (left frontal pole), Fp2 (right frontal pole), Cz (central midline), F3 (left frontal), F4 (right frontal), T3 (left temporal), T4 (right temporal), P3 (left parietal), P4 (right parietal), O1 (left occipital), O2 (right occipital).
*   **Number of Electrodes:** Typically 19 to 23 electrodes are used, along with reference and ground electrodes.
*   **Reference and Ground Electrodes:**
    *   **Reference Electrode:** Provides a baseline potential against which the activity at other electrodes is measured. Often placed on the earlobe (A1 or A2) or mastoid.
    *   **Ground Electrode:** Used to minimize common-mode noise.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Precise and consistent electrode placement is crucial for reliable EEG recordings.

**2.4 EEG Recording**

*   **Components of an EEG Machine:**
    *   **Electrodes:** Conductive sensors placed on the scalp.
    *   **Amplifier:** Increases the very small amplitude of brain signals (microvolts, µV) to a measurable level. EEG amplifiers are differential amplifiers, meaning they amplify the difference in voltage between two electrodes.
    *   **Filters:** Remove unwanted noise (e.g., muscle artifacts, power line interference).
        *   **High-pass filter:** Allows frequencies above a certain cutoff to pass.
        *   **Low-pass filter:** Allows frequencies below a certain cutoff to pass.
        *   **Notch filter:** Removes specific frequency noise (e.g., 50/60 Hz from power lines).
    *   **Display/Recording Device:** Converts the amplified and filtered signal into a visual representation (e.g., on a screen or paper).
*   **Types of Recording Configurations:**
    *   **Monopolar Recording:** A single active electrode is placed on the scalp, and the signal is referenced to a distant electrode (e.g., on the earlobe).
    *   **Bipolar Recording:** The difference in potential between two active scalp electrodes is recorded. This helps in localizing the source of the signal.
*   **Artifacts:** Unwanted electrical signals that interfere with the EEG recording. Common sources include:
    *   **Electrode Artifacts:** Poor contact, impedance issues.
    *   **Physiological Artifacts:** Muscle activity (EMG), eye movements (EOG), heart activity (ECG), breathing.
    *   **External Artifacts:** Electrical interference from nearby equipment.
*   **Reference:** Khandpur (Third edition) and Cromwell, Weibell, & Pfeiffer (2nd Edition) detail the instrumentation and signal processing involved in bioelectric recordings like EEG.

### 3. Evoked Potentials (EPs)

Evoked potentials are changes in the electrical activity of the brain that occur in response to a specific sensory stimulus. They are a way to study the functional integrity of sensory pathways.

*   **Definition:** Small voltage changes occurring in specific areas of the brain that can be detected following a sensory stimulus.
*   **Princ:** The brain's spontaneous EEG activity is much larger in amplitude than the evoked potential. To isolate the EP, a stimulus is repeatedly presented, and the EEG signal is averaged over many trials. This process, called **Signal Averaging**, enhances the EP (which is time-locked to the stimulus) and reduces the random background EEG noise.
*   **Types of Evoked Potentials:**
    *   **Visual Evoked Potential (VEP):** In response to visual stimuli (e.g., flashing lights or patterned visual stimuli). Used to assess the visual pathway from the retina to the visual cortex.
    *   **Brainstem Auditory Evoked Potential (BAEP/ABR):** In response to auditory stimuli (clicks or tone bursts). Assesses the auditory pathway from the cochlea through the brainstem.
    *   **Somatosensory Evoked Potential (SSEP):** In response to electrical stimulation of peripheral nerves (e.g., on the wrist or ankle). Assesses the sensory pathways in the spinal cord and brain.
*   **Components of an Evoked Potential:** EPs are typically described by their components, which are positive or negative deflections occurring at characteristic latencies (time after stimulus presentation). These components are often labeled with letters (P for positive, N for negative) and their approximate latency in milliseconds (e.g., P100 for a positive wave at 100ms).
*   **Applications:**
    *   Diagnosing sensory pathway disorders (e.g., multiple sclerosis, optic neuritis, hearing loss).
    *   Assessing neurological function in unresponsive patients (e.g., coma).
    *   Monitoring neurological function during surgery.
*   **Reference:** Webster (3rd edition) and Aston provide information on signal averaging techniques and the measurement of evoked potentials.

### Key Points to Remember:

*   **Neurons:** Communicate via electrochemical signals, with action potentials being the primary signal for rapid transmission.
*   **EEG:** Measures synchronized postsynaptic potentials of large neuronal populations, not individual action potentials.
*   **International 10-20 System:** Standard for electrode placement in EEG for consistent and reproducible recordings.
*   **Signal Averaging:** Essential technique for isolating weak evoked potentials from background EEG noise.
*   **Artifacts:** A major challenge in bio-signal recording, requiring careful identification and mitigation.
*   **Distinction from ECG:** While both are bioelectric signals, EEG relates to brain activity, and ECG relates to heart activity.

### Practice Questions & Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary functional unit of the nervous system responsible for transmitting electrical signals?
**Answer:** Neuron.

**Question 2:** Which frequency band of brain waves is typically associated with deep sleep?
**Answer:** Delta (δ) waves.

**Question 3:** What is the standard system used for placing electrodes on the scalp for EEG recordings?
**Answer:** The International 10-20 System.

**Question 4:** Describe the main principle behind isolating evoked potentials from background EEG activity.
**Answer:** Signal averaging, where the EEG signal is averaged over multiple trials following repeated sensory stimuli. This enhances the time-locked evoked potential while attenuating random background noise.

**Question 5:** List three common types of artifacts encountered in EEG recordings and their potential sources.
**Answer:**
1.  **Muscle artifacts (EMG):** Due to muscle activity (e.g., blinking, chewing, facial movements).
2.  **Eye artifacts (EOG):** Due to the movement of the eyeball, which has a resting potential.
3.  **Powerline interference:** 50/60 Hz hum from electrical equipment.
