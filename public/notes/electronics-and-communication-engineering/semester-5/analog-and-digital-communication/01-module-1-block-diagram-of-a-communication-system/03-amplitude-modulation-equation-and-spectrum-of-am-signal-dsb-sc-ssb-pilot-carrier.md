### **1. Introduction to Communication Systems (CO1 - K2)**

A communication system is a collection of individual communications subsystems and facilities organized in a way that accomplishes a number of communication tasks. The primary goal is to transfer information from a source to a destination.

**Key Components of a Basic Communication System (as seen in the block diagram):**

*   **Information Source:** Generates the message signal (e.g., voice, music, data).
*   **Transmitter:**
    *   **Input Transducer:** Converts the message signal into an electrical signal.
    *   **Transmitter Processor:** Prepares the signal for transmission (e.g., modulation, amplification).
    *   **Antenna:** Radiates the electrical signal as electromagnetic waves.
*   **Channel:** The medium through which the signal travels (e.g., free space, co-axial cable, optical fiber). It can introduce noise and distortion.
*   **Receiver:**
    *   **Antenna:** Captures the electromagnetic waves.
    *   **Receiver Processor:** Recovers the original message signal (e.g., demodulation, filtering, amplification).
    *   **Output Transducer:** Converts the electrical signal back into a form understandable by humans or a machine.
*   **Destination:** The intended recipient of the message.

**Haykin & Moher (5th Ed.) emphasizes that modulation is a key process within the transmitter and receiver processors, enabling efficient transmission over the channel.**

### **2. Amplitude Modulation (AM)**

Amplitude Modulation (AM) is a modulation technique where the amplitude of a high-frequency carrier wave is varied in proportion to the instantaneous amplitude of the message signal.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Carrier Signal:** A high-frequency sinusoidal signal used to carry the message signal. Its frequency is much higher than the message signal's bandwidth.
*   **Message Signal (Modulating Signal):** The signal containing the information to be transmitted.
*   **Modulated Signal:** The resulting signal after modulation.

**Example (Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 3):** Imagine a radio station broadcasting at 98.7 MHz. This 98.7 MHz is the carrier frequency. The audio signal (voice or music) from the studio is the message signal. AM works by changing the strength (amplitude) of the 98.7 MHz carrier wave according to the loudness of the audio signal.

---

### **2.1. Equation of an AM Signal**

Let the message signal be denoted by $m(t)$ and the carrier signal be $c(t)$.

*   **Message Signal:** $m(t)$
*   **Carrier Signal:** $c(t) = A_c \cos(\omega_c t)$
    *   $A_c$: Amplitude of the carrier
    *   $\omega_c$: Angular frequency of the carrier ($2\pi f_c$)

In Amplitude Modulation, the amplitude of the carrier wave is varied by the message signal. The standard form of an AM signal is:

$s_{AM}(t) = A_c [1 + k_a m(t)] \cos(\omega_c t)$

where:
*   $k_a$: Amplitude sensitivity of the modulator. It determines how much the carrier amplitude changes for a given change in the message signal.
*   The term $[1 + k_a m(t)]$ represents the envelope of the AM signal.

**Important Consideration:** For proper demodulation, the envelope of the AM signal must follow the message signal. This requires that $|k_a m(t)| \le 1$ for all $t$. This condition is known as the **no-overmodulation condition**.

**Overmodulation:** If $|k_a m(t)| > 1$, the envelope no longer faithfully follows the message signal, leading to distortion.

**Under- or Exact Modulation:** If $|k_a m(t)| \le 1$, demodulation is possible without distortion.

**Modulation Index (m):**
The modulation index, $m$, is a measure of the extent of modulation. It is defined as:

$m = \max[k_a m(t)]$

The AM signal can also be written as:

$s_{AM}(t) = A_c \cos(\omega_c t) + A_c k_a m(t) \cos(\omega_c t)$

This shows that the AM signal consists of:
1.  The original carrier component.
2.  Two sideband components generated by modulating the carrier with the message signal.

### **2.2. Spectrum of an AM Signal**

Let's consider a simple message signal $m(t) = A_m \cos(\omega_m t)$.

Then, the AM signal is:
$s_{AM}(t) = A_c [1 + k_a A_m \cos(\omega_m t)] \cos(\omega_c t)$
$s_{AM}(t) = A_c \cos(\omega_c t) + A_c k_a A_m \cos(\omega_m t) \cos(\omega_c t)$

Using the trigonometric identity: $\cos(A)\cos(B) = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]$

$s_{AM}(t) = A_c \cos(\omega_c t) + \frac{A_c k_a A_m}{2} [\cos((\omega_c - \omega_m)t) + \cos((\omega_c + \omega_m)t)]$

**Analysis of the spectrum:**

1.  **Carrier Component:** $A_c \cos(\omega_c t)$
    *   This is a sinusoidal component at the carrier frequency $\omega_c$.
    *   In the frequency domain (spectrum), it appears as a single impulse (or a sharp peak) at $f_c$ (or $\omega_c$).

2.  **Lower Sideband (LSB) Component:** $\frac{A_c k_a A_m}{2} \cos((\omega_c - \omega_m)t)$
    *   This is a sinusoidal component at the frequency $\omega_c - \omega_m$.
    *   In the frequency domain, it appears as impulses at $f_c - f_m$ (or $\omega_c - \omega_m$).

3.  **Upper Sideband (USB) Component:** $\frac{A_c k_a A_m}{2} \cos((\omega_c + \omega_m)t)$
    *   This is a sinusoidal component at the frequency $\omega_c + \omega_m$.
    *   In the frequency domain, it appears as impulses at $f_c + f_m$ (or $\omega_c + \omega_m$).

**Bandwidth of AM:**
The bandwidth of the AM signal is the difference between the highest and lowest frequencies present in the modulated signal.
Highest frequency = $f_c + f_m$
Lowest frequency = $f_c - f_m$
Bandwidth (BW) = $(f_c + f_m) - (f_c - f_m) = 2f_m$.

**If the message signal $m(t)$ has a bandwidth $W$ (i.e., its spectrum extends from $-W$ to $W$), then:**
*   The carrier component is at $f_c$.
*   The LSB ranges from $f_c - W$ to $f_c$.
*   The USB ranges from $f_c$ to $f_c + W$.

The total bandwidth occupied by the AM signal is **$2W$**.

**Spectrum Representation (Frequency Domain):**

At $f_c$: Amplitude proportional to $A_c$
At $f_c - f_m$: Amplitude proportional to $\frac{A_c k_a A_m}{2}$
At $f_c + f_m$: Amplitude proportional to $\frac{A_c k_a A_m}{2}$

**(Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 4) provides a detailed analysis of the spectral components and power distribution in AM signals, highlighting the significant portion of power in the carrier, which is often wasted.**

### **3. Double Sideband Suppressed Carrier (DSB-SC) Modulation**

DSB-SC is a form of AM where the carrier component is suppressed, and only the two sidebands containing the message information are transmitted.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Suppressed Carrier:** The carrier signal is not transmitted.
*   **Double Sideband:** Both the upper and lower sidebands are transmitted.

**Equation of a DSB-SC Signal:**

The DSB-SC signal is obtained by multiplying the message signal with the carrier signal.

$s_{DSB-SC}(t) = m(t) \cos(\omega_c t)$

where:
*   $m(t)$: Message signal
*   $\cos(\omega_c t)$: Carrier signal

**Spectrum of a DSB-SC Signal:**

Let $m(t) = A_m \cos(\omega_m t)$.
$s_{DSB-SC}(t) = A_m \cos(\omega_m t) \cos(\omega_c t)$
$s_{DSB-SC}(t) = \frac{A_m}{2} [\cos((\omega_c - \omega_m)t) + \cos((\omega_c + \omega_m)t)]$

**Analysis of the spectrum:**

*   The spectrum consists only of the Lower Sideband (LSB) and Upper Sideband (USB) components.
*   There is no carrier component at $f_c$.
*   At $f_c - f_m$: Amplitude proportional to $\frac{A_m}{2}$
*   At $f_c + f_m$: Amplitude proportional to $\frac{A_m}{2}$

**Bandwidth of DSB-SC:**
Similar to AM, if the message signal has a bandwidth $W$, the bandwidth of the DSB-SC signal is **$2W$**.

**Advantages of DSB-SC:**
*   **Power Efficiency:** All transmitted power is in the sidebands, which carry the message. This is more power-efficient than standard AM, where a significant portion of power is in the carrier.

**Disadvantages of DSB-SC:**
*   **Complex Demodulation:** Requires a coherent (synchronous) demodulator, which needs a locally generated carrier that is perfectly in phase and frequency with the original carrier at the transmitter. This is more complex than the envelope detector used for standard AM.

**(Taub & Schilling, 4th Ed., Chapter 5) provides a thorough comparison of AM and DSB-SC, emphasizing the power efficiency benefits of DSB-SC.**

### **4. Single Sideband (SSB) Modulation**

In Single Sideband (SSB) modulation, only one of the two sidebands (either LSB or USB) is transmitted, along with a suppressed carrier.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Suppressed Carrier:** The carrier signal is not transmitted.
*   **Single Sideband:** Only one sideband (LSB or USB) is transmitted.

**Why SSB?**
Transmitting only one sideband significantly reduces the bandwidth required for transmission, leading to more efficient use of the frequency spectrum.

**Equations of SSB Signals:**

If $m(t)$ is the message signal with bandwidth $W$, and its Fourier transform is $M(f)$.
*   **Upper Sideband (USB) SSB:**
    $s_{USB}(t) = \frac{1}{2} [m(t) \cos(\omega_c t) - \hat{m}(t) \sin(\omega_c t)]$
    where $\hat{m}(t)$ is the Hilbert Transform of $m(t)$. The Hilbert Transform shifts the phase of all frequency components of $m(t)$ by $-90^\circ$.
    In terms of $M(f)$:
    $S_{USB}(f) = \frac{1}{2} [M(f-f_c) + M(f+f_c)]$, where $M(f)$ is zero for $|f| > W$.
    The spectrum consists of frequencies from $f_c$ to $f_c + W$.

*   **Lower Sideband (LSB) SSB:**
    $s_{LSB}(t) = \frac{1}{2} [m(t) \cos(\omega_c t) + \hat{m}(t) \sin(\omega_c t)]$
    In terms of $M(f)$:
    $S_{LSB}(f) = \frac{1}{2} [M(f-f_c) - M(f+f_c)]$, where $M(f)$ is zero for $|f| > W$.
    The spectrum consists of frequencies from $f_c - W$ to $f_c$.

**Spectrum of SSB Signals:**

*   **USB:** Consists of a single band of frequencies from $f_c$ to $f_c + W$.
*   **LSB:** Consists of a single band of frequencies from $f_c - W$ to $f_c$.

**Bandwidth of SSB:**
If the message signal has a bandwidth $W$, the bandwidth of an SSB signal is **$W$**. This is half the bandwidth of AM or DSB-SC.

**Advantages of SSB:**
*   **Bandwidth Efficiency:** Requires half the bandwidth compared to AM or DSB-SC, allowing more channels to be transmitted in a given frequency range.
*   **Power Efficiency:** All transmitted power is concentrated in the single sideband carrying the information.

**Disadvantages of SSB:**
*   **Complex Generation:** Generating SSB signals requires complex filtering techniques to suppress the unwanted sideband and carrier, or sophisticated phasing methods.
*   **Coherent Demodulation:** Like DSB-SC, SSB signals require coherent demodulation, meaning the receiver must generate a carrier that is synchronized in frequency and phase with the original carrier at the transmitter. Even small frequency errors can lead to significant distortion.

**(Proakis & Salehi, 6th Ed., Chapter 2) delves into the mathematical derivations of SSB signals and the complexities of their generation and demodulation.**

### **4.1. SSB with Pilot Carrier**

While pure SSB is spectrally efficient, the need for coherent demodulation presents a challenge. To overcome this, a **pilot carrier** can be added to the SSB signal.

**Key Concept:**
A small amount of the original carrier signal is reinserted into the transmitted SSB signal.

**Equation:**
$s_{SSB-PC}(t) = A_p \cos(\omega_c t) + s_{SSB}(t)$
where $A_p$ is the amplitude of the pilot carrier, and $s_{SSB}(t)$ is either the USB or LSB SSB signal.

**Advantages of Pilot Carrier:**

*   **Simplified Demodulation:** The presence of the pilot carrier allows for a simpler non-coherent or semi-coherent demodulator. The receiver can lock onto the pilot carrier to synchronize its local oscillator, making demodulation easier than with pure SSB or DSB-SC.
*   **Improved Receiver Performance:** Helps in AGC (Automatic Gain Control) at the receiver.

**Disadvantages of Pilot Carrier:**

*   **Reduced Power Efficiency:** A portion of the transmitted power is allocated to the pilot carrier, which does not carry any information. This reduces the overall power efficiency compared to pure SSB.
*   **Increased Bandwidth (Slightly):** While still much better than AM/DSB-SC, the pilot carrier adds a narrowband component.

**(Kennedy, 6th Ed., Chapter 7) explains the practical implementation of pilot carriers in SSB systems to ease demodulation complexity.**

### **5. Vestigial Sideband (VSB) Modulation**

Vestigial Sideband (VSB) modulation is a compromise between DSB-SC and SSB. It transmits one full sideband and a "vestige" (a small portion) of the other sideband.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Partial Sideband Suppression:** One sideband is largely suppressed, but a small portion of it is retained.
*   **Bandwidth Reduction:** Offers better bandwidth efficiency than AM or DSB-SC but less than SSB.

**How it's Achieved:**
A modified filter (VSB filter) is used at the transmitter to shape the spectrum such that one sideband is passed almost entirely, while the other sideband is attenuated, but not completely eliminated. The transition band of the VSB filter is centered around the carrier frequency.

**Equation of a VSB Signal:**
A VSB signal can be thought of as a combination of DSB-SC and a filtered version of the message signal.
$s_{VSB}(t) = m(t) \cos(\omega_c t) + m_{v}(t) \cos(\omega_c t)$
where $m_v(t)$ is a filtered version of $m(t)$ that provides the vestigial sideband.

Alternatively, consider the Fourier Transform $M(f)$:
The VSB spectrum $S_{VSB}(f)$ is similar to the DSB-SC spectrum $M(f-f_c) + M(f+f_c)$, but with a modified filter applied. For example, a USB-VSB would pass $M(f-f_c)$ fully and $M(f+f_c)$ partially.

**Spectrum of a VSB Signal:**

*   It contains one full sideband (e.g., USB) and a vestige of the other sideband (e.g., LSB).
*   The bandwidth occupied is approximately $1.25W$ to $1.5W$, depending on the roll-off factor of the VSB filter. A common bandwidth used is $1.25W$, where $W$ is the message bandwidth.

**Example Application: Television Broadcasting**
VSB modulation is famously used in analog television broadcasting (e.g., NTSC, PAL). The video signal has a wide bandwidth. Transmitting both sidebands would require a large bandwidth for each channel. Transmitting only one sideband would make tuning difficult due to phase distortion. VSB provides a good balance by transmitting one sideband fully and a vestige of the other, allowing for a bandwidth of about 1.25 times the baseband bandwidth. The vestige helps in the demodulation process at the TV receiver.

**Advantages of VSB:**

*   **Bandwidth Efficiency:** More bandwidth-efficient than AM and DSB-SC.
*   **Simplified Demodulation (relative to SSB):** While still requiring some degree of coherent detection, the vestigial sideband helps in smoother demodulation compared to pure SSB.

**Disadvantages of VSB:**

*   **Non-linear Phase Distortion:** The shaping filter can introduce non-linear phase distortion, which can affect the fidelity of the transmitted signal, especially in applications like television.
*   **Complexity:** Generation and demodulation are more complex than standard AM.

**(Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 3) and (Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 4) provide detailed explanations and spectral diagrams of VSB modulation, including its application in broadcasting.**

### **6. Comparison of Modulation Techniques**

| Feature        | Standard AM                               | DSB-SC                                   | SSB                                    | SSB with Pilot Carrier             | VSB                                    |
| :------------- | :---------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- |
| **Carrier**    | Transmitted                               | Suppressed                               | Suppressed                             | Partially transmitted (pilot)      | Transmitted (or partially)             |
| **Sidebands**  | Upper and Lower                           | Upper and Lower                          | Single sideband (USB or LSB)           | Single sideband (USB or LSB)       | One full, one vestigial                |
| **Bandwidth**  | $2W$                                      | $2W$                                     | $W$                                    | $W + \text{small}$                 | $\approx 1.25W$                        |
| **Power**      | Low efficiency (carrier power wasted)     | High efficiency (all power in sidebands) | Very high efficiency (all power in SSB) | Moderate efficiency (pilot power lost) | Moderate to High efficiency            |
| **Demodulator**| Simple (Envelope Detector)                | Complex (Coherent Detector)              | Complex (Coherent Detector)            | Simpler than pure SSB              | Complex (Coherent Detector with filter) |
| **Complexity** | Low                                       | Medium                                   | High                                   | Medium                             | Medium                                 |
| **Applications** | AM Radio Broadcasting, Aircraft Comms.  | Some specialized comms, carrier suppressed systems | HF Radio, Single-channel comms         | Some point-to-point comms        | Analog Television Broadcasting         |

### **8. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:**
A message signal $m(t)$ with a bandwidth of $5 \text{ kHz}$ is used to modulate a carrier wave with a frequency of $100 \text{ kHz}$.
(a) What is the bandwidth of the resulting standard AM signal?
(b) What is the bandwidth of the resulting DSB-SC signal?
(c) What is the bandwidth of the resulting SSB signal?
(d) What are the frequency components present in the spectrum of the standard AM signal if $m(t) = \cos(2\pi \times 5 \times 10^3 t)$ and the carrier is $100 \cos(2\pi \times 100 \times 10^3 t)$?

**Answer 1:**
(a) Bandwidth of AM = $2W = 2 \times 5 \text{ kHz} = 10 \text{ kHz}$.
(b) Bandwidth of DSB-SC = $2W = 2 \times 5 \text{ kHz} = 10 \text{ kHz}$.
(c) Bandwidth of SSB = $W = 5 \text{ kHz}$.
(d) For $m(t) = \cos(2\pi \times 5 \text{ kHz} t)$ and carrier $100 \cos(2\pi \times 100 \text{ kHz} t)$, the AM signal is $s_{AM}(t) = A_c [1 + k_a \cos(2\pi \times 5 \text{ kHz} t)] \cos(2\pi \times 100 \text{ kHz} t)$.
The frequency components are:
    *   Carrier: $100 \text{ kHz}$
    *   Lower Sideband: $100 \text{ kHz} - 5 \text{ kHz} = 95 \text{ kHz}$
    *   Upper Sideband: $100 \text{ kHz} + 5 \text{ kHz} = 105 \text{ kHz}$

**Question 2:**
What is the primary advantage of DSB-SC over standard AM?
(a) Wider bandwidth
(b) Simpler demodulation
(c) Better power efficiency
(d) Higher carrier frequency

**Answer 2:**
(c) Better power efficiency. DSB-SC transmits all power in the sidebands, unlike standard AM which wastes power on the carrier.

**Question 3:**
Which modulation technique is commonly used in analog television broadcasting and why?

**Answer 3:**
Vestigial Sideband (VSB) modulation is used in analog television broadcasting. It strikes a balance between bandwidth efficiency and demodulation complexity. Transmitting only one sideband (SSB) would require very strict filters and perfect synchronization, while transmitting both (DSB) would require twice the bandwidth. VSB transmits one sideband fully and a vestige of the other, optimizing the use of spectrum while keeping demodulation feasible for consumer television sets.

**Question 4:**
Explain the necessity of a coherent detector for DSB-SC and SSB signals.

**Answer 4:**
Coherent detection is necessary for DSB-SC and SSB signals because the carrier is suppressed at the transmitter. To recover the original message signal $m(t)$, the receiver needs to multiply the received modulated signal with a locally generated carrier that is precisely synchronized in both frequency and phase with the original carrier used at the transmitter. If the local carrier is not synchronized, the demodulated output will be distorted or completely lost. The equation for coherent demodulation of $m(t)\cos(\omega_c t)$ is $m(t)\cos(\omega_c t) \times \cos(\omega_c t) = m(t)\cos^2(\omega_c t) = m(t)\frac{1+\cos(2\omega_c t)}{2} = \frac{1}{2}m(t) + \frac{1}{2}m(t)\cos(2\omega_c t)$. Low-pass filtering removes the double frequency term, recovering $m(t)/2$.

### **9. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Modulation:** The process of varying one or more characteristics (amplitude, frequency, or phase) of a carrier wave in accordance with a message signal.
*   **AM Spectrum:** Carrier + LSB + USB. Bandwidth = $2W$. Power in carrier can be significant.
*   **DSB-SC Spectrum:** LSB + USB only. Bandwidth = $2W$. Power efficient, but needs coherent demodulation.
*   **SSB Spectrum:** Either LSB or USB only. Bandwidth = $W$. Most bandwidth efficient, but requires precise coherent demodulation.
*   **VSB:** A compromise, transmitting one full sideband and a vestige of the other. Bandwidth $\approx 1.25W$. Used in TV broadcasting.
*   **Coherent Demodulation:** Essential for DSB-SC and SSB systems. Requires a synchronized local oscillator at the receiver.
*   **Bandwidth Efficiency:** SSB > VSB > AM = DSB-SC.
*   **Power Efficiency:** SSB > DSB-SC > VSB > AM (generally, considering information-carrying power).
