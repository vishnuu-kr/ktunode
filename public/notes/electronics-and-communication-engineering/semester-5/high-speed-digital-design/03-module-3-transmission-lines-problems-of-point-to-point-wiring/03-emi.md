# HIGH SPEED DIGITAL DESIGN: Module 3 - Transmission Lines: Problems of Point-to-Point Wiring - EMI

## Introduction

In high-speed digital design, signals travel at very high frequencies and fast rise/fall times. When these signals propagate through interconnections, they can behave like electromagnetic waves, leading to various problems. This module focuses on the challenges posed by point-to-point wiring, particularly **Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)**, and how transmission line principles help us understand and mitigate these issues.

**Key Concept:** At high speeds, simple wires are no longer just conductors but become transmission lines with characteristic impedance and propagation delays.

## 1. Understanding Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)

EMI is the unwanted disruption of an electrical circuit by an electromagnetic field. In high-speed digital systems, this can manifest as corrupted data, increased bit error rates, and even system malfunction.

### 1.1 Sources of EMI in High-Speed Digital Systems

*   **Switching Digital Circuits:** Fast-changing current in digital signals creates rapidly changing electric and magnetic fields.
*   **High-Frequency Oscillators:** Clock signals and other high-frequency internal signals are potent sources of EMI.
*   **Power Supply Noise:** Switching power supplies and voltage regulators can generate noise that couples into signal lines.
*   **External Electromagnetic Sources:** Nearby devices, radio transmitters, and even lightning can induce EMI.

### 1.2 Mechanisms of EMI Coupling

EMI typically couples into circuits through three main mechanisms:

*   **Capacitive Coupling (Electric Field Coupling):** This occurs when two conductors are separated by a dielectric. A changing voltage on one conductor creates a changing electric field that induces a voltage on the other.
    *   **Mechanism:** Think of two parallel plates of a capacitor. The voltage difference creates an electric field. If the voltage changes rapidly, the electric field changes rapidly.
    *   **Relevance to Point-to-Point Wiring:** Adjacent signal traces, especially those carrying fast signals, can capacitively couple noise to each other. The parallel sections of wires are the primary culprits.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 7 (Shielding and Filtering) discusses how to mitigate capacitive coupling. Ott, Chapter 2 (Coupling Paths) provides detailed explanations.

*   **Inductive Coupling (Magnetic Field Coupling):** This occurs when a changing current in one conductor creates a changing magnetic field, which in turn induces a voltage in a nearby conductor.
    *   **Mechanism:** A loop formed by a signal trace and its return path creates a magnetic field when current flows through it. A changing current leads to a changing magnetic flux.
    *   **Relevance to Point-to-Point Wiring:** The return path of a signal is crucial. If the return path is not well-defined or is far away, the loop area increases, making the conductor more susceptible to inductive coupling and a stronger radiator.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 7. Ott, Chapter 2.

*   **Conducted Coupling:** This occurs when noise is transmitted directly through conductive paths, such as power supply lines or ground connections.
    *   **Mechanism:** Noise generated by one component on a shared power or ground rail can propagate to other components.
    *   **Relevance to Point-to-Point Wiring:** While not directly about signal traces, the quality of the power and ground distribution to the devices driving and receiving these point-to-point connections is critical. Poor power integrity leads to noise on the signal lines themselves.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 5 (Power Distribution Systems). Ott, Chapter 2.

### 1.3 EMI as a Transmission Line Problem

When we consider point-to-point wiring in the context of transmission lines, EMI becomes particularly problematic because:

*   **Reflections:** Mismatched impedances along the transmission line (due to stubs, vias, connectors, or end points) cause signal reflections. These reflections can create ringing and overshoot/undershoot, which are essentially high-frequency components that can radiate and be picked up by other circuits.
*   **Radiation:** Any discontinuity or imperfection in the transmission line (including sharp bends, vias, and unterminated ends) can act as an antenna, radiating electromagnetic energy. Conversely, these same imperfections can act as antennas that *receive* external electromagnetic energy.
    *   **Effective Antenna Length:** The amount of radiation or susceptibility is related to the length of the wire relative to the wavelength of the signal. At high frequencies, even short wires can be electrically long.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 7 discusses how signal integrity issues related to transmission lines lead to radiation. Hall, Hall, & McCall, Chapter 2 (Transmission Line Theory) provides the foundational understanding of reflections and radiation from transmission lines.

## 2. Impact of Point-to-Point Wiring on EMI

Simple point-to-point wiring, when not designed with transmission line principles in mind, becomes a significant source and victim of EMI.

### 2.1 Uncontrolled Return Paths

*   **Problem:** In a simple point-to-point connection, the signal trace is only half of the circuit. The *return path* is equally important for completing the circuit and defining the electromagnetic field. If the return path is not controlled (e.g., not a solid ground plane directly underneath the signal trace), the return current will find the path of least impedance, which can be a large loop.
*   **EMI Consequence:**
    *   **Increased Loop Area:** Larger loops act as more efficient antennas, both for radiating noise outwards and for picking up external noise.
    *   **Ground Bounce/Shift:** During fast switching, return currents can create voltage drops across impedance in the ground path, leading to a temporary shift in the ground reference, which can affect signal integrity and cause EMI.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 1 (The Realities of Signal Propagation) and Chapter 7. Dally & Poulton, Chapter 4 (Transmission Lines) emphasizes the importance of return paths.

### 2.2 Lack of Termination

*   **Problem:** When a transmission line is not terminated with its characteristic impedance ($Z_0$) at the receiving end, the signal encounters an impedance mismatch.
*   **EMI Consequence:**
    *   **Reflections:** The unmatched portion of the signal "bounces" back towards the source. These reflections create ringing, overshoot, and undershoot, which are high-frequency components that can radiate.
    *   **Increased Signal Distortion:** The ringing and overshoot can increase the electromagnetic energy emitted by the trace.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 1. Hall, Hall, & McCall, Chapter 2.

### 2.3 Uncontrolled Stubs and Vias

*   **Problem:** Stubs (short, unused branches off a main transmission line) and vias (electrical connections between different layers of a PCB) act as impedance discontinuities.
*   **EMI Consequence:**
    *   **Reflections:** Stubs and vias create reflections that contribute to signal degradation and EMI generation, similar to a lack of termination.
    *   **Radiation:** The physical structure of a via can act as a small antenna. Improperly designed vias can have parasitic inductance and capacitance that lead to significant reflections.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 7. Hall, Hall, & McCall, Chapter 5 (Via Design).

### 2.4 Parallel Coupling of Traces

*   **Problem:** When signal traces run parallel to each other for significant lengths, especially without adequate spacing or shielding.
*   **EMI Consequence:**
    *   **Capacitive Coupling:** The electric fields from one trace can induce noise onto a nearby trace.
    *   **Inductive Coupling:** The magnetic fields from one trace can induce noise onto a nearby trace.
    *   **Reference:** Ott, Chapter 2. Johnson & Graham, Chapter 7.

## 3. Applying Transmission Line Principles to Mitigate EMI

Understanding EMI in the context of transmission lines allows us to implement design strategies to minimize its effects.

### 3.1 Controlled Impedance Routing

*   **Concept:** Ensuring that the characteristic impedance ($Z_0$) of the signal trace is consistent along its entire length. This involves controlling the width of the trace, the thickness of the dielectric material between the trace and its reference plane, and the dielectric constant of the material.
*   **EMI Mitigation:**
    *   **Reduces Reflections:** Consistent impedance minimizes reflections at discontinuities, thus reducing ringing and the generation of higher-frequency EMI components.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 1. Hall, Hall, & McCall, Chapter 2.

### 3.2 Proper Termination

*   **Concept:** Terminating the transmission line at the receiving end with a resistor equal to its characteristic impedance ($Z_0$).
*   **EMI Mitigation:**
    *   **Absorbs Signal:** Prevents reflections by providing a matched load for the signal to dissipate into.
    *   **Reduces Ringing:** Eliminates the high-frequency oscillations caused by reflections, thereby reducing the potential for EMI.
    *   **Types of Termination:** Series termination (near source), parallel termination (near load), Thevenin termination, AC termination.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 1. Dally & Poulton, Chapter 4.

### 3.3 Controlled Return Paths

*   **Concept:** Providing a dedicated, low-impedance return path for the signal current, ideally a continuous ground plane directly adjacent to the signal trace.
*   **EMI Mitigation:**
    *   **Minimizes Loop Area:** Keeps the signal loop area small, reducing its effectiveness as an antenna for radiating or receiving EMI.
    *   **Lowers Ground Inductance:** Reduces voltage drops and ground bounce, improving signal integrity and reducing noise generation.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 1. Dally & Poulton, Chapter 4.

### 3.4 Minimizing Parallel Trace Lengths

*   **Concept:** Keeping parallel runs of high-speed signal traces to a minimum. If unavoidable, use wider spacing or place them on different layers with intervening ground planes.
*   **EMI Mitigation:**
    *   **Reduces Capacitive and Inductive Coupling:** Shorter parallel sections and increased separation weaken the electric and magnetic fields that couple between traces.
    *   **Reference:** Ott, Chapter 2.

### 3.5 Via Design and Placement

*   **Concept:** Designing vias to minimize their parasitic inductance and capacitance, and placing them strategically.
*   **EMI Mitigation:**
    *   **Reduce Reflections:** Minimize stub lengths on vias by ensuring the connected layers have a signal plane nearby.
    *   **Ground Stitching:** Use multiple vias to connect ground planes, especially when passing through high-speed signal traces, to provide a low-impedance return path.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham, Chapter 7. Hall, Hall, & McCall, Chapter 5.

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **At high speeds, wires are transmission lines.** Treat them as such to avoid EMI problems.
*   **EMI is caused by rapid changes in voltage and current.** Fast rise/fall times and high frequencies are the primary culprits.
*   **The return path is critical.** A controlled, low-impedance return path minimizes loop area and reduces EMI.
*   **Impedance mismatches cause reflections.** Reflections create ringing, which radiates and is susceptible to interference.
*   **Proper termination absorbs reflections.** This is a key EMI mitigation technique.
*   **Any discontinuity (vias, bends, connectors) can radiate or pick up EMI.**
*   **Parallel traces can couple noise capacitively and inductively.** Minimize parallel lengths or increase separation.
*   **Ground bounce and power supply noise contribute to EMI.** Good power integrity is essential for EMI control.

## Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **Explanation:** A fast-rising edge means the voltage on the signal trace changes rapidly. This rapid change in voltage creates a rapidly changing electric field. If the trace is not properly terminated or has impedance mismatches, reflections will occur, creating oscillating currents and voltages. Both the changing electric fields and the currents associated with reflections can radiate electromagnetic energy, causing EMI. From a transmission line perspective, the steep edge contains high-frequency components. When these components encounter impedance discontinuities or form large loops, they can effectively couple to the surrounding environment as electromagnetic radiation.
2.  **Capacitive Coupling:** A changing voltage on one trace creates a changing electric field that permeates the dielectric. If another trace is nearby, this changing electric field induces a voltage on the second trace. The closer the traces and the larger the parallel length, the stronger the coupling. Transmission line theory explains this by considering the capacitance per unit length between the traces.
    **Inductive Coupling:** A changing current flowing on one trace creates a changing magnetic field. This changing magnetic field, if it passes through a loop formed by another trace and its return path, induces a voltage on that second trace. Again, closer proximity and longer parallel runs increase the coupling. Transmission line theory describes this through the mutual inductance between the conductors and their respective return paths. At high speeds, the rapid current changes and the potential for large current loops (due to poor return paths) amplify these effects.
3.  **EMI Implications:**
    *   **Reflections:** The 50 Ohm trace encountering a 10 Ohm load represents a significant impedance mismatch. A large portion of the signal energy will be reflected back towards the source. The reflection coefficient can be calculated as $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0) / (Z_L + Z_0) = (10 - 50) / (10 + 50) = -40 / 60 = -2/3$. This means a significant portion of the signal energy is reflected.
    *   **Ringing and Overshoot:** These reflections will cause the signal at the load to exhibit significant ringing and potential overshoot. The voltage may exceed the intended levels, and the signal will oscillate around the desired value.
    *   **Increased Radiation:** The ringing and oscillations are effectively high-frequency components that are present on the trace. These oscillations, combined with the physical structure of the trace and its imperfect return path, can act as inefficient antennas, radiating electromagnetic energy into the environment. The continuous activity of reflections on the line increases the likelihood of coupled noise and radiated EMI.
4.  **Significance of Return Path:** The return path is equally as important as the signal trace. The current flowing on the signal trace must return to its source. In high-speed design, the return current tends to flow in the closest low-impedance path, typically a ground plane directly adjacent to the signal trace.
    *   **EMI Generation:** A poorly controlled return path (e.g., no adjacent ground plane, fragmented ground plane, or large loops) creates a larger loop area for the signal and return current. Larger loops are more efficient antennas, both for radiating electromagnetic energy outwards and for picking up external electromagnetic noise.
    *   **EMI Susceptibility:** Similarly, a large loop makes the circuit more susceptible to picking up EMI from external sources. The changing magnetic fields from external emitters will more readily induce currents in larger loops.
    *   **Reference:** Johnson & Graham emphasize that the loop formed by the signal trace and its return path is the primary radiator.
5.  **Via Influence on EMI:**
    *   **Impedance Discontinuities:** Vias are transition points between layers. They introduce parasitic inductance and capacitance due to their physical structure and the connections to different ground/power planes. These parasitics create impedance mismatches, leading to reflections.
    *   **Reflections and Radiation:** Reflections caused by vias contribute to signal degradation (ringing, overshoot) which, in turn, increases the high-frequency content of the signal and its potential to radiate EMI.
    *   **Stub Effect:** If a via is not connected to a signal plane on all relevant layers, the unused portion of the via forms a "stub." This stub acts like an antenna and can resonate at certain frequencies, significantly increasing EMI. Minimizing stub lengths by connecting to nearby signal planes is crucial.
    *   **Ground Stitching:** Multiple vias used to connect ground planes across layers are called "ground stitching." This provides a low-impedance path for return currents, reducing the loop area and mitigating EMI. Poorly placed or insufficient ground stitching can worsen EMI.
    *   **Reference:** Hall, Hall, & McCall's discussion on via design highlights these issues. Johnson & Graham also discuss the impact of vias as discontinuities in Chapter 7.
