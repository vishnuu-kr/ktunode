## 1. Transmission Line Model: Distributed Parameters

A transmission line is a structure designed to guide electromagnetic waves from one point to another. Unlike lumped-element circuits where components like inductors and capacitors are concentrated at specific points, transmission lines have their electrical properties distributed along their length.

### 1.1. Physical Structure

A typical two-wire transmission line consists of two parallel conductors separated by a dielectric material.

*   **Conductor Resistance ($R$):** Due to the finite conductivity of the conductors, there is resistance along the length of the line. This leads to power loss due to Joule heating.
*   **Inductance ($L$):** The magnetic field generated by the current flowing in the conductors stores energy, resulting in distributed inductance. This inductance arises from the self-inductance of each conductor and the mutual inductance between them.
*   **Conductance ($G$):** Leakage current can flow between the conductors through the dielectric material. This is represented by a distributed conductance. In good dielectrics, $G$ is usually small.
*   **Capacitance ($C$):** The electric field between the two conductors stores energy, resulting in distributed capacitance. This capacitance arises from the geometry of the conductors and the dielectric properties of the medium between them.

**(Refer to Sadiku, Chapter 10; Hayt & Buck, Chapter 9; Reddy, Chapter 4)**

### 1.2. Differential Length Element

To analyze a transmission line, we consider a small segment of length $\Delta z$. This segment can be modeled as a series impedance and a shunt admittance.

*   **Series Impedance per unit length ($z$):**
    $z = R + j\omega L$
    where:
    *   $R$ is the resistance per unit length ($\Omega/m$)
    *   $L$ is the inductance per unit length ($H/m$)
    *   $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the signal ($rad/s$)

*   **Shunt Admittance per unit length ($y$):**
    $y = G + j\omega C$
    where:
    *   $G$ is the conductance per unit length ($S/m$)
    *   $C$ is the capacitance per unit length ($F/m$)

**(Refer to Sadiku, Figure 10.1; Hayt & Buck, Figure 9.1)**

### 1.3. Primary Constants

The parameters $R$, $L$, $G$, and $C$ are known as the **primary constants** of the transmission line. They are intrinsic properties of the line itself, determined by its physical construction and the materials used.

### 1.4. Secondary Constants

From the primary constants, we can derive other important parameters, such as the propagation constant and characteristic impedance. These are often referred to as **secondary constants**.

## 2. The Telegrapher's Equations

The telegrapher's equations are a pair of coupled first-order differential equations that describe the voltage $V(z)$ and current $I(z)$ along a transmission line as a function of position $z$ and time. They are derived by applying Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws to the differential length element $\Delta z$.

Consider a short section of the transmission line of length $\Delta z$ at position $z$.

*   **Voltage Drop across Series Impedance:** The voltage drop across the series impedance $z \Delta z = (R + j\omega L) \Delta z$ is $I(z) z \Delta z$.
*   **Current through Shunt Admittance:** The current flowing through the shunt admittance $y \Delta z = (G + j\omega C) \Delta z$ is $V(z+\Delta z) y \Delta z$.

Applying KVL to the loop in the $\Delta z$ section:
$V(z) - V(z+\Delta z) - I(z) z \Delta z = 0$
$V(z) - V(z+\Delta z) = I(z) z \Delta z$

Dividing by $\Delta z$ and taking the limit as $\Delta z \to 0$:
$-\lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{V(z+\Delta z) - V(z)}{\Delta z} = I(z) z$
$-\frac{dV(z)}{dz} = z I(z)$  (Equation 2.1)

Applying KCL at the node $V(z+\Delta z)$:
$I(z) - I(z+\Delta z) - V(z+\Delta z) y \Delta z = 0$
$I(z) - I(z+\Delta z) = V(z+\Delta z) y \Delta z$

Dividing by $\Delta z$ and taking the limit as $\Delta z \to 0$:
$-\lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{I(z+\Delta z) - I(z)}{\Delta z} = V(z+\Delta z) y$
$-\frac{dI(z)}{dz} = y V(z)$  (Equation 2.2)

These are the **telegrapher's equations** in phasor form. They are fundamental to analyzing transmission lines and are a direct consequence of Maxwell's equations applied to the distributed circuit.

**(Refer to Sadiku, Equation 10.3 & 10.4; Hayt & Buck, Equation 9.3 & 9.4; Reddy, Equation 4.1 & 4.2)**

### 2.1. Wave Propagation

We can obtain second-order differential equations for $V(z)$ and $I(z)$ by differentiating the telegrapher's equations.

Differentiate Equation 2.1 with respect to $z$:
$-\frac{d^2V(z)}{dz^2} = z \frac{dI(z)}{dz}$

Substitute $\frac{dI(z)}{dz}$ from Equation 2.2 ($-\frac{dI(z)}{dz} = y V(z)$):
$-\frac{d^2V(z)}{dz^2} = z (-y V(z))$
$\frac{d^2V(z)}{dz^2} = zy V(z)$  (Equation 2.3: Voltage wave equation)

Similarly, differentiate Equation 2.2 with respect to $z$:
$-\frac{d^2I(z)}{dz^2} = y \frac{dV(z)}{dz}$

Substitute $\frac{dV(z)}{dz}$ from Equation 2.1 ($-\frac{dV(z)}{dz} = z I(z)$):
$-\frac{d^2I(z)}{dz^2} = y (-z I(z))$
$\frac{d^2I(z)}{dz^2} = zy I(z)$  (Equation 2.4: Current wave equation)

### 2.2. Propagation Constant ($\gamma$)

The equations for $V(z)$ and $I(z)$ are standard second-order linear homogeneous differential equations. The solutions involve exponential functions. We define the **propagation constant** $\gamma$ as:

$\gamma = \sqrt{zy} = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)}$

The units of $\gamma$ are Nepers/meter (Np/m) for magnitude and radians/meter (rad/m) for phase.

The wave equations can be rewritten as:
$\frac{d^2V(z)}{dz^2} - \gamma^2 V(z) = 0$
$\frac{d^2I(z)}{dz^2} - \gamma^2 I(z) = 0$

The general solutions for voltage and current are:
$V(z) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + V_0^- e^{\gamma z}$
$I(z) = I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + I_0^- e^{\gamma z}$

where:
*   $V_0^+$ and $I_0^+$ are the amplitudes of the forward-traveling voltage and current waves.
*   $V_0^-$ and $I_0^-$ are the amplitudes of the backward-traveling voltage and current waves.

**(Refer to Sadiku, Equation 10.6 & 10.7; Hayt & Buck, Equation 9.10 & 9.11; Reddy, Equation 4.7)**

### 2.3. Lossless Transmission Line Approximation

In many practical scenarios, especially at high frequencies and with good conductors and dielectrics, we can make the following approximations:

*   $R \approx 0$ (low conductor resistance)
*   $G \approx 0$ (low dielectric leakage)

In this case, the primary constants become:
*   Series impedance per unit length: $z = j\omega L$
*   Shunt admittance per unit length: $y = j\omega C$

The propagation constant simplifies to:
$\gamma = \sqrt{(j\omega L)(j\omega C)} = \sqrt{-\omega^2 LC} = j\omega \sqrt{LC}$

This indicates that for a lossless line, the propagation constant is purely imaginary, meaning there is no attenuation ($e^{-\alpha z}$ where $\alpha=0$). The propagation is purely phase shift.

The solutions for voltage and current on a lossless line are:
$V(z) = V_0^+ e^{-j\beta z} + V_0^- e^{j\beta z}$
$I(z) = I_0^+ e^{-j\beta z} + I_0^- e^{j\beta z}$
where $\beta = \omega \sqrt{LC}$ is the phase constant.

**(Refer to Sadiku, Section 10.2; Hayt & Buck, Section 9.3; Reddy, Section 4.3)**

## 3. Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$)

Characteristic impedance is a fundamental property of a transmission line that describes the ratio of voltage to current for a wave traveling in one direction along the line. It is independent of the length of the line and depends only on its distributed primary constants ($R, L, G, C$) and the frequency.

### 3.1. Derivation

From the telegrapher's equations, we can relate the voltage and current for the forward-traveling waves:

From Equation 2.1: $-\frac{dV}{dz} = z I(z)$
From Equation 2.2: $-\frac{dI}{dz} = y V(z)$

Consider the forward-traveling waves: $V(z) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$ and $I(z) = I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$.
Differentiating $V(z)$ with respect to $z$: $\frac{dV}{dz} = -\gamma V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} = -\gamma V(z)$.
Substituting this into Equation 2.1:
$-(-\gamma V(z)) = z I(z)$
$\gamma V(z) = z I(z)$

The **characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)** is defined as the ratio of the voltage to the current for the forward-traveling wave:

$Z_0 = \frac{V(z)}{I(z)} = \frac{\gamma}{z}$

Alternatively, using Equation 2.2: $\frac{dI}{dz} = -\gamma I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} = -\gamma I(z)$.
Substituting this into Equation 2.2:
$-(-\gamma I(z)) = y V(z)$
$\gamma I(z) = y V(z)$

Rearranging for the voltage-to-current ratio:
$\frac{V(z)}{I(z)} = \frac{\gamma}{y}$

This seems to contradict the previous result. Let's check the derivation.
The correct relationship should be obtained by substituting $V(z)$ into the current equation or vice-versa.

From $-\frac{dI(z)}{dz} = y V(z)$, substitute $V(z) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$:
$-\frac{dI(z)}{dz} = y V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$

We expect $I(z) = I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$. Differentiating with respect to $z$:
$\frac{dI(z)}{dz} = -\gamma I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$

So, $-(-\gamma I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}) = y V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$
$\gamma I_0^+ = y V_0^+$
$\frac{V_0^+}{I_0^+} = \frac{\gamma}{y}$

Let's re-evaluate using the original telegrapher's equations and the general solutions.
For the forward wave: $V(z) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$ and $I(z) = I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$.
$\frac{dV}{dz} = -\gamma V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$
$\frac{dI}{dz} = -\gamma I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$

Substitute into $-\frac{dV}{dz} = z I(z)$:
$-(-\gamma V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}) = z I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$
$\gamma V_0^+ = z I_0^+$
$\frac{V_0^+}{I_0^+} = \frac{z}{\gamma}$

Substitute into $-\frac{dI}{dz} = y V(z)$:
$-(-\gamma I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}) = y V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z}$
$\gamma I_0^+ = y V_0^+$
$\frac{V_0^+}{I_0^+} = \frac{\gamma}{y}$

Therefore, we must have $\frac{z}{\gamma} = \frac{\gamma}{y}$.
This implies $\gamma^2 = zy$, which is consistent with our definition of $\gamma$.

The characteristic impedance $Z_0$ is defined as the ratio of the voltage to current for a single traveling wave (either forward or backward). For the forward wave:
$Z_0 = \frac{V_0^+}{I_0^+} = \frac{z}{\gamma}$

For the backward wave: $V(z) = V_0^- e^{\gamma z}$ and $I(z) = I_0^- e^{\gamma z}$.
$\frac{dV}{dz} = \gamma V_0^- e^{\gamma z}$
$\frac{dI}{dz} = \gamma I_0^- e^{\gamma z}$

Substitute into $-\frac{dV}{dz} = z I(z)$:
$-(\gamma V_0^- e^{\gamma z}) = z I_0^- e^{\gamma z}$
$-\gamma V_0^- = z I_0^-$
$\frac{V_0^-}{I_0^-} = -\frac{z}{\gamma} = -Z_0$

This means that for the backward wave, the ratio of voltage to current is $-Z_0$. This is important when considering reflections.

**Important Point:** The characteristic impedance is the impedance seen by a wave traveling in one direction on an infinitely long line.

**(Refer to Sadiku, Equation 10.10; Hayt & Buck, Equation 9.14; Reddy, Equation 4.11)**

### 3.2. Formula for Characteristic Impedance

Substitute $\gamma = \sqrt{zy}$:
$Z_0 = \frac{\sqrt{zy}}{z} = \sqrt{\frac{zy}{z^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{y}{z}}$

So, the characteristic impedance is given by:
$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{R + j\omega L}{G + j\omega C}}$

The units of $Z_0$ are Ohms ($\Omega$).

**(Refer to Sadiku, Equation 10.11; Hayt & Buck, Equation 9.15; Reddy, Equation 4.12)**

### 3.3. Characteristic Impedance of a Lossless Transmission Line

For a lossless transmission line ($R=0, G=0$):
$z = j\omega L$
$y = j\omega C$

$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{j\omega L}{j\omega C}} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$

For a lossless line, the characteristic impedance is real and purely resistive, and depends only on the ratio of inductance to capacitance per unit length.

**(Refer to Sadiku, Equation 10.13; Hayt & Buck, Equation 9.19; Reddy, Equation 4.13)**

### 3.4. Physical Significance of Characteristic Impedance

*   **Intrinsic Property:** $Z_0$ is an intrinsic property of the transmission line, determined by its geometry and the dielectric material between conductors. It is independent of the length of the line.
*   **Impedance of an Infinite Line:** It represents the impedance seen by a source connected to an infinitely long transmission line, where no reflections occur.
*   **Wave Impedance:** It is the ratio of the transverse electric field to the transverse magnetic field for a wave propagating along the line. For a two-wire line, it relates the voltage and current of the traveling wave.
*   **Matching:** If the load impedance connected to the end of a transmission line is equal to its characteristic impedance ($Z_L = Z_0$), there are no reflections, and the line is said to be "matched." This leads to maximum power transfer and efficient signal transmission.

**(Refer to Sadiku, Section 10.3; Hayt & Buck, Section 9.3)**

### 3.5. Examples of Characteristic Impedance Calculations

**Example 1: Two-Wire Line**
Consider a two-wire line with conductors of radius $a$ separated by a distance $d$.
Inductance per unit length: $L = \frac{\mu}{\pi} \cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)$
Capacitance per unit length: $C = \frac{\pi\epsilon}{\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)}$

$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{\mu}{\pi} \cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)}{\frac{\pi\epsilon}{\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)}}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}} \cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)$
$Z_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0 \mu_r}{\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r}} \cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right) = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_r}{\epsilon_r}} \eta_0 \cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)$
where $\eta_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\epsilon_0}} \approx 377 \Omega$ is the intrinsic impedance of free space.

For air dielectric ($\epsilon_r \approx 1, \mu_r \approx 1$):
$Z_0 \approx \frac{377}{\pi} \cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)$

If $d \gg a$, $\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right) \approx \ln\left(\frac{2d}{a}\right)$.
$Z_0 \approx \frac{377}{\pi} \ln\left(\frac{2d}{a}\right) \approx 120 \ln\left(\frac{2d}{a}\right)$

**Example 2: Coaxial Cable**
A coaxial cable with inner conductor radius $a$ and outer conductor radius $b$, filled with a dielectric.
Inductance per unit length: $L = \frac{\mu}{2\pi} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$
Capacitance per unit length: $C = \frac{2\pi\epsilon}{\ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)}$

$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{\mu}{2\pi} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)}{\frac{2\pi\epsilon}{\ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$
$Z_0 = \frac{\eta_0}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{\mu_r}{\epsilon_r}} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$

For air dielectric:
$Z_0 \approx \frac{377}{2\pi} \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) \approx 60 \ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$

**(Refer to Sadiku, Example 10.3; Hayt & Buck, Section 9.3; Reddy, Section 4.3.1)**

## 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **Distributed Parameters:** Transmission lines are characterized by distributed inductance ($L$) and capacitance ($C$), along with resistance ($R$) and conductance ($G$).
*   **Telegrapher's Equations:**
    *   $-\frac{dV}{dz} = z I(z) = (R + j\omega L) I(z)$
    *   $-\frac{dI}{dz} = y V(z) = (G + j\omega C) V(z)$
*   **Propagation Constant ($\gamma$):** $\gamma = \sqrt{zy} = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)}$. It determines how waves propagate and attenuate along the line.
*   **Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$):** $Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{z}{y}} = \sqrt{\frac{R + j\omega L}{G + j\omega C}}$. It's the ratio of voltage to current for a wave traveling in one direction on an infinitely long line.
*   **Lossless Line:** For $R=0$ and $G=0$, $\gamma = j\omega\sqrt{LC}$ (purely imaginary, no attenuation) and $Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$ (real and constant).
*   **Matching:** When the load impedance equals the characteristic impedance ($Z_L = Z_0$), reflections are minimized, ensuring maximum power transfer.

## 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A uniform transmission line has primary constants $R = 10 \Omega/km$, $L = 1 mH/km$, $G = 1 \mu S/km$, and $C = 0.01 \mu F/km$. Calculate the propagation constant and characteristic impedance at a frequency of 10 kHz.
**(Course Outcome: CO4, Knowledge Level: K3)**

**Solution 1:**
Given:
$R = 10 \Omega/km$
$L = 1 \times 10^{-3} H/km$
$G = 1 \times 10^{-6} S/km$
$C = 0.01 \times 10^{-6} F/km$
Frequency $f = 10$ kHz, so $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi (10 \times 10^3) = 20000\pi \approx 62832 \text{ rad/s}$.

Primary constants per meter:
$R = 10/1000 = 0.01 \Omega/m$
$L = 1 \times 10^{-3}/1000 = 1 \times 10^{-6} H/m$
$G = 1 \times 10^{-6}/1000 = 1 \times 10^{-9} S/m$
$C = 0.01 \times 10^{-6}/1000 = 1 \times 10^{-11} F/m$

Series impedance per meter:
$z = R + j\omega L = 0.01 + j(62832)(1 \times 10^{-6}) = 0.01 + j0.062832 \, \Omega/m$

Shunt admittance per meter:
$y = G + j\omega C = 1 \times 10^{-9} + j(62832)(1 \times 10^{-11}) = 1 \times 10^{-9} + j0.62832 \times 10^{-6} \, S/m$

Propagation constant:
$\gamma = \sqrt{zy} = \sqrt{(0.01 + j0.062832)(1 \times 10^{-9} + j0.62832 \times 10^{-6})}$
$\gamma = \sqrt{(0.01 + j0.062832)(1 \times 10^{-9})(1 + j628.32)}$
$\gamma \approx \sqrt{(0.062832 \angle 80.9^\circ)(1 \times 10^{-9})(628.32 \angle 89.0^\circ)}$
$\gamma \approx \sqrt{(0.062832 \times 10^{-9} \times 628.32) \angle (80.9^\circ + 89.0^\circ)}$
$\gamma \approx \sqrt{39.48 \times 10^{-9} \angle 169.9^\circ}$
$\gamma \approx \sqrt{39.48 \times 10^{-9}} \angle (169.9^\circ / 2)$
$\gamma \approx 6.28 \times 10^{-4.5} \angle 84.95^\circ$
$\gamma \approx 6.28 \times 10^{-4.5} (\cos(84.95^\circ) + j\sin(84.95^\circ))$
$\gamma \approx 6.28 \times 10^{-4.5} (0.088 + j0.996)$
$\gamma \approx (0.55 \times 10^{-4.5} + j6.26 \times 10^{-4.5})$
$\gamma \approx (5.5 \times 10^{-5} + j6.26 \times 10^{-4}) \text{ Np/m, rad/m}$
(Note: The imaginary part of $\gamma$ is the phase constant $\beta$)

Characteristic Impedance:
$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{z}{y}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.01 + j0.062832}{1 \times 10^{-9} + j0.62832 \times 10^{-6}}}$
$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{0.062832 \angle 80.9^\circ}{0.62832 \times 10^{-6} \angle 89.0^\circ}}$
$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{0.062832}{0.62832 \times 10^{-6}} \angle (80.9^\circ - 89.0^\circ)}$
$Z_0 = \sqrt{10^5 \angle -8.1^\circ}$
$Z_0 = \sqrt{10^5} \angle (-8.1^\circ / 2)$
$Z_0 = 316.2 \angle -4.05^\circ \, \Omega$
$Z_0 \approx 316.2 (\cos(-4.05^\circ) + j\sin(-4.05^\circ))$
$Z_0 \approx 316.2 (0.997 - j0.071) \, \Omega$
$Z_0 \approx 315.3 - j22.45 \, \Omega$

**Question 2:**
A lossless transmission line has an inductance of $0.25 \mu H/m$ and a capacitance of $100 pF/m$. Calculate its characteristic impedance and the phase velocity of the wave.
**(Course Outcome: CO4, Knowledge Level: K3)**

**Solution 2:**
Given:
$L = 0.25 \mu H/m = 0.25 \times 10^{-6} H/m$
$C = 100 pF/m = 100 \times 10^{-12} F/m$

Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$) for a lossless line:
$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.25 \times 10^{-6} H/m}{100 \times 10^{-12} F/m}}$
$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{0.25 \times 10^{-6}}{100 \times 10^{-12}}} \, \Omega = \sqrt{\frac{0.25}{100} \times 10^6} \, \Omega$
$Z_0 = \sqrt{0.0025 \times 10^6} \, \Omega = \sqrt{2500} \, \Omega = 50 \, \Omega$

Phase velocity ($v_p$) for a lossless line:
The propagation constant $\gamma = j\omega \sqrt{LC}$. The phase constant $\beta = \omega \sqrt{LC}$.
The phase velocity is given by $v_p = \omega/\beta$.
$v_p = \frac{\omega}{\omega \sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$
$v_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(0.25 \times 10^{-6} H/m)(100 \times 10^{-12} F/m)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{25 \times 10^{-18}}} \, m/s$
$v_p = \frac{1}{5 \times 10^{-9}} \, m/s = \frac{1}{5} \times 10^9 \, m/s = 0.2 \times 10^9 \, m/s = 2 \times 10^8 \, m/s$
