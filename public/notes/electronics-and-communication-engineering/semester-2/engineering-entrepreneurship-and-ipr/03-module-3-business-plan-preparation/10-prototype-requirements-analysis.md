# Module 3: Business Plan Preparation - Prototype Requirements Analysis

Welcome everyone! Today, we're diving into a crucial stage of building a successful engineering venture: **Prototype Requirements Analysis**. This isn't just about making something that *works*, it's about making sure it works for your target customer and aligns with your overall business strategy. Think of this as the bridge between your brilliant idea and a tangible product that people will actually want to buy.

In our journey through Engineering Entrepreneurship and IPR, we've already touched upon identifying business ideas (CO2) and understanding customer needs (CO3). Prototype requirements analysis is where these foundational steps solidify into concrete product specifications. It directly supports our goal of creating a comprehensive business plan (CO4) and developing effective prototyping and stakeholder engagement skills (CO5).

## What is a Prototype and Why Analyze its Requirements?

Before we get into the "how," let's clarify the "what."

**A prototype** is a preliminary model or early version of a product, service, or system. It’s not the final, polished version. Instead, it's a tool for learning, testing, and gathering feedback. Prototypes can range from simple sketches and wireframes to functional, albeit basic, working models.

Now, **Prototype Requirements Analysis** is the systematic process of defining *what* your prototype needs to do, *how* it should behave, and *what constraints* it must operate within. It’s about translating abstract customer needs and business goals into specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART) requirements for your prototype.

Why is this so important?

*   **Reduces Risk:** By testing key assumptions early with a prototype, you avoid investing heavily in a product nobody wants. It's far cheaper to change a feature on a prototype than on a fully manufactured product. This ties directly into mitigating business risks, a core concern for any entrepreneur.
*   **Validates Assumptions:** Does your core technology work as you envisioned? Does the user interface actually make sense to someone unfamiliar with your idea? A prototype answers these questions. As Osterwalder and Pigneur emphasize in *Business Model Generation*, understanding your customer is paramount, and a prototype is a fantastic way to get that crucial feedback.
*   **Facilitates Communication:** A prototype is a concrete artifact that stakeholders – your team, investors, potential customers – can interact with and understand. It speaks a universal language, making discussions more productive. This is vital for stakeholder engagement (CO5).
*   **Guides Development:** Clearly defined requirements act as a roadmap for your development team. It ensures everyone is working towards the same goals and prevents scope creep, where the project keeps expanding beyond its original intent.

Think of it this way: If you're building a house, a prototype is like a scaled-down model or a mock-up of a few rooms. You wouldn't start building the entire mansion without first confirming where the doors go, how big the windows should be, and if the layout feels right, would you? Prototype requirements analysis is that crucial planning phase for your product.

## Connecting Prototype Requirements to the Business Plan (CO4)

This analysis is not an isolated activity. It's an integral part of your business plan. The requirements you define for your prototype will directly influence several sections of your plan:

*   **Product/Service Description:** The core features and functionalities you prototype become the foundation of your product description. What problems does it solve? How does it work?
*   **Market Analysis:** Your prototype testing will reveal how your product fits into the market and resonates with your target audience. This feeds into your competitive analysis and market positioning.
*   **Development Plan/Roadmap:** The requirements dictate the milestones and resources needed for the prototype and subsequent development phases.
*   **Financial Projections:** Understanding what you need to build for the prototype helps in estimating development costs.

Remember, your business plan is a living document. The insights gained from prototype requirements analysis will help you refine and iterate on your entire plan, ensuring it remains realistic and aligned with market realities.

## Key Aspects of Prototype Requirements Analysis

So, what exactly do we need to analyze when defining prototype requirements? Let's break it down:

### 1. Functional Requirements: "What Does it Do?"

These are the most obvious requirements. They describe the specific actions or behaviors your prototype must perform. For an engineering product, this could be anything from a sensor accurately measuring temperature to a software interface allowing users to book appointments.

*   **User Actions:** What specific tasks should the user be able to accomplish with the prototype? (e.g., "The user must be able to upload a document," "The device must display the current battery level.")
*   **System Behaviors:** What actions must the system perform, perhaps without direct user input? (e.g., "The sensor must automatically record data every minute," "The application must send an alert if the temperature exceeds 50°C.")
*   **Data Handling:** What kind of data will be processed, stored, or displayed? (e.g., "The system must store user login credentials securely," "The report generated must include date, time, and measurement values.")

**Example:** Imagine you're developing a smart irrigation system for a small urban garden.
*   **Functional Requirement:** The prototype must be able to detect soil moisture levels and automatically activate the water pump when moisture drops below a set threshold (e.g., 20%). It should also allow the user to manually turn the pump on or off via a mobile app interface.

This is about defining the core *utility* of your product. What problem are you solving, and how does your product *functionally* address it?

### 2. Non-Functional Requirements: "How Well Does it Do It?"

These requirements specify the quality attributes and constraints of your prototype. They describe *how* the system should perform, rather than *what* it should do. These are often critical for user satisfaction and the overall viability of your product.

*   **Performance:** How fast or efficient should it be? (e.g., "The data processing should take less than 5 seconds," "The battery should last for at least 24 hours on a single charge.")
*   **Usability:** How easy is it for users to learn and operate? (e.g., "A new user should be able to complete the primary task within 2 minutes without training," "The interface should be intuitive and require minimal navigation.") This is where understanding customer needs (CO3) becomes highly practical.
*   **Reliability:** How consistently does it perform without failure? (e.g., "The system should have a 99% uptime during operating hours," "The device should operate without error for at least 1000 cycles.")
*   **Security:** What measures are in place to protect data and prevent unauthorized access? (e.g., "User passwords must be encrypted," "Access to sensitive data requires administrator privileges.")
*   **Maintainability:** How easy is it to modify, update, or fix? (e.g., "The code should be well-commented and modular.")
*   **Scalability:** Can it handle increased load or data in the future? (e.g., "The system should be able to support up to 100 concurrent users.")
*   **Constraints:** These are limitations imposed by the project, such as budget, time, or existing technologies. (e.g., "The prototype must be built using open-source software," "The total development cost for the prototype must not exceed $X.")

**Example (Continuing the smart irrigation system):**
*   **Non-Functional Requirements:**
    *   **Usability:** The mobile app interface for manual control should be simple enough for a novice gardener to use within 30 seconds.
    *   **Reliability:** The moisture sensor readings should be accurate within ±5% of actual levels.
    *   **Performance:** The system should respond to a moisture drop by activating the pump within 1 minute.
    *   **Battery Life:** The system's battery should last at least one week between charges.
    *   **Constraint:** The prototype must operate within a budget of $500 for hardware components.

These non-functional requirements often differentiate a "working" product from a *successful* product. Clayton Christensen, in *The Innovator's DNA*, talks about understanding the "job to be done" for customers. Usability and reliability are critical to fulfilling that job effectively.

### 3. User Interface (UI) and User Experience (UX) Requirements

While often overlapping with non-functional requirements, UI/UX deserves its own focus, especially for consumer-facing products. This is about how the user *feels* when interacting with your product.

*   **Visual Design:** What should it look like? (Colors, fonts, layout.)
*   **Interaction Design:** How does the user interact with the interface? (Buttons, menus, navigation flow.)
*   **User Flow:** The sequence of steps a user takes to complete a task.
*   **Feedback Mechanisms:** How does the system inform the user about its status? (e.g., loading indicators, success messages, error messages.)

**Example (Smart irrigation system app):**
*   **UI/UX Requirement:** The app should display current moisture levels and pump status prominently on the main screen. A clear "On/Off" toggle button for manual pump control should be easily accessible. Visual cues (e.g., a green bar filling up) should indicate rising moisture levels.

This is where your understanding of customer needs (CO3) truly shines. A clunky, confusing interface can kill even the most technologically advanced product.

### 4. Stakeholder Requirements

It's not just about the end-user. Who else has an interest in your prototype and what do they need from it?

*   **Investors:** They need to see a viable concept, potential for return on investment, and a clear path to market. The prototype needs to demonstrate this potential.
*   **Team Members:** They need clear specifications to build the prototype efficiently.
*   **Potential Partners/Suppliers:** They might need to understand how your product integrates with their offerings.

**Example (Smart irrigation system):**
*   **Stakeholder Requirement (Investor):** The prototype must demonstrate that the core moisture-sensing and automated watering mechanism functions reliably, showcasing the potential for cost savings and convenience for urban gardeners.

Gathering and analyzing these diverse stakeholder requirements ensures your prototype serves multiple purposes beyond just technical validation.

## Iterative Process: It's Not a One-Time Thing!

A critical takeaway here is that prototype requirements analysis is **iterative**. You don't just define them once and stick to them rigidly. As you learn from building and testing your prototype, you will inevitably discover that some initial requirements were incorrect, incomplete, or need modification.

This is perfectly normal and, frankly, expected. The goal of a prototype is to learn and adapt. This iterative nature aligns perfectly with developing skills for prototyping and stakeholder engagement (CO5) and refining your business plan (CO4).

**Think of it like this:** You plan your route to a new city (initial requirements). You start driving, but you might encounter unexpected road closures or discover a more scenic detour (feedback and new information). You then adjust your plan (iterate requirements) to reach your destination effectively.

## Connecting to Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

While this section focuses on *product* requirements, it's crucial to remember our IPR context (CO1). As you define what your prototype does and how it works, you're also implicitly defining the intellectual property you're creating.

*   **Novelty:** Does your prototype introduce new technical solutions? This is key for patentability.
*   **Inventive Step/Non-obviousness:** Is your solution not something that would be obvious to an expert in the field?
*   **Industrial Applicability:** Can your invention be made or used in industry?

As you define functional and non-functional requirements, ask yourself: "Is there something novel and inventive here that we should protect?" For instance, if your smart irrigation system uses a unique algorithm for optimizing water usage based on predictive weather data, that algorithm might be a candidate for patent protection.

Also, consider the **User Interface (UI)** and **User Experience (UX)** requirements. While patents protect functionality, **design patents** can protect the visual appearance and aesthetic design of your product. The look and feel you define for your app, for example, could be protectable as a design.

Understanding copyright and trademark is also relevant. The software code you write for the prototype is protected by copyright. The name and logo you might use even at this early stage could be candidates for trademark protection.

Prabuddha Ganguli’s work on IPR emphasizes that identifying and protecting IP is an ongoing process, starting from the earliest stages of innovation. Your prototype requirements analysis is an excellent opportunity to flag potential IP assets.

## Practical Steps for Prototype Requirements Analysis

So, how do you actually *do* this?

1.  **Gather Input:** Talk to potential customers, subject matter experts, mentors, and your team. Conduct user interviews, surveys, and brainstorming sessions. Reference *The Innovator's DNA* for effective questioning techniques.
2.  **Define User Stories:** Frame requirements from the user's perspective. A user story typically follows the format: "As a [type of user], I want to [perform some task] so that [I can achieve some goal]."
    *   **Example:** "As a gardener, I want to see the current soil moisture level on the app so that I know if watering is needed."
3.  **Create Use Cases:** Describe how a user will interact with the system to achieve a specific goal. This details the steps involved.
4.  **Document Requirements:** Use a requirements document or backlog to list all functional, non-functional, UI/UX, and stakeholder requirements. Tools like Jira, Trello, or even a well-structured spreadsheet can be useful.
5.  **Prioritize Requirements:** Not all requirements are created equal. Which are essential for a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) or the initial prototype? Which can be added later? Use methods like MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have) to prioritize.
6.  **Validate and Refine:** Review the documented requirements with stakeholders. Get feedback and make necessary adjustments. This is where the iterative nature comes into play.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

*   **Vague Requirements:** "The app should be user-friendly" is not a requirement. "Users should be able to complete task X in Y seconds with Z clicks" is much better.
*   **Gold Plating:** Adding features that are not essential for the prototype's core purpose, often driven by personal preference rather than customer need. This can inflate costs and delay testing. Stick to the core problem you're solving.
*   **Ignoring Non-Functional Requirements:** Focusing solely on "what it does" and neglecting "how well it does it" can lead to a product that technically works but is unusable or unreliable.
*   **Not Involving Stakeholders:** Failing to get input from all relevant parties can lead to overlooking critical needs or misunderstandings.
*   **Treating the Prototype as the Final Product:** Remember its purpose is learning and validation, not perfection.

## Summary: The Foundation for Success

Prototype requirements analysis is a critical bridge between concept and reality. It's about meticulously defining *what* your prototype needs to be and *how* it needs to perform to validate your business assumptions and guide your development. By systematically analyzing functional, non-functional, UI/UX, and stakeholder needs, you lay a solid foundation for your business plan (CO4), ensure your product resonates with users (CO3), and navigate the complex world of engineering entrepreneurship with reduced risk.

Remember this: **Clear, well-defined prototype requirements are not a bureaucratic hurdle; they are a strategic advantage.** They save you time, money, and significantly increase your chances of building a product that truly meets a market need. And as you define these, keep an eye on those potential IP assets!

## Sample Questions and Answers

**1. Conceptual Question:** Explain why prototyping is considered an iterative process and how this relates to the analysis of prototype requirements.

**Answer:** Prototyping is iterative because the initial assumptions made about customer needs, technical feasibility, and market viability are often incomplete or may prove incorrect during testing. The process of building and testing a prototype generates new insights and feedback. This feedback then informs revisions to the prototype and, crucially, to its requirements. For instance, a user might find a feature difficult to use, leading to a revised usability requirement. Or, a technical limitation might surface, necessitating a change in performance requirements. This continuous cycle of building, testing, and refining ensures that the prototype evolves towards a solution that truly meets user needs and business goals, making the requirement analysis a dynamic, not a static, activity.

**2. Exam-Oriented Question:** A startup is developing a mobile application for personalized fitness plans. List and briefly explain three critical non-functional requirements for their prototype, and explain how a failure in one of these could impact the business plan.

**Answer:**
Three critical non-functional requirements could be:

*   **Usability:** The prototype app should allow a new user to create a basic fitness profile and generate a simple workout plan within 3 minutes without prior training. *Impact of Failure:* If the app is too complex or confusing (poor usability), users will abandon it, leading to low adoption rates. This would negatively impact market analysis and revenue projections in the business plan, potentially making the venture seem unviable.
*   **Reliability:** The prototype must accurately calculate calorie burn based on user-inputted activity data with a variance of no more than 5%. *Impact of Failure:* Inaccurate calculations erode user trust and can lead to dissatisfaction if users feel their fitness tracking is unreliable. This could damage the brand reputation and lead to poor customer retention, directly affecting projected customer lifetime value and profitability.
*   **Performance:** The app should load the personalized workout plan within 2 seconds after the user requests it. *Impact of Failure:* Slow loading times are frustrating for users and can lead to them switching to faster alternatives. This affects user engagement metrics, potentially leading to a revised market penetration strategy and lower-than-expected user growth in the business plan.

**3. Application/Integration Question:** You are analyzing prototype requirements for a new type of smart lock. The core functionality is secure access. How might you incorporate Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) considerations into your analysis of prototype requirements? Provide a specific example.

**Answer:**
When analyzing prototype requirements for a smart lock, IPR considerations can be woven in by identifying potentially protectable innovations.

*   **Functional Requirement Example:** The prototype must use a unique multi-factor authentication system combining biometric fingerprint scan with a time-based one-time password (TOTP) generated by a proprietary algorithm to unlock the door.
*   **IPR Integration:**
    *   **Patentability:** The "proprietary algorithm" for the TOTP generation could be a novel and inventive step, thus a candidate for patent protection. The requirement analysis should document the specifics of this algorithm and its unique advantages over existing methods.
    *   **Design Patentability:** The physical design and user interface of the smart lock (e.g., the placement and shape of the fingerprint scanner, the visual cues for authentication status) could be subject to design patents. The UI/UX requirements should detail these aesthetic aspects.
    *   **Trade Secret:** The specific implementation details or a critical part of the algorithm not easily reverse-engineered could be protected as a trade secret.

By explicitly documenting novel functionalities and design elements as requirements, the team can proactively identify and plan for IP protection strategies early in the development lifecycle.
