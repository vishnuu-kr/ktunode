# ANTENNA THEORY AND DESIGN - Module 2: Microstrip Antennas: Radiation Mechanism

## Topic: Radiation Efficiency

---

### **1. Introduction to Radiation Efficiency**

Radiation efficiency is a crucial parameter that quantifies how effectively an antenna converts the input power into radiated power. In essence, it tells us how much of the power delivered to the antenna terminals is actually transmitted into space as electromagnetic waves, and how much is lost due to various dissipative mechanisms.

**Definition:**

Radiation efficiency ($\eta_r$) is defined as the ratio of the **radiated power ($P_{rad}$)** to the **total power accepted by the antenna at its input terminals ($P_{in}$)**.

Mathematically, it can be expressed as:

$\eta_r = \frac{P_{rad}}{P_{in}}$

Where:
*   $P_{rad}$ is the power radiated by the antenna.
*   $P_{in}$ is the total power delivered to the antenna terminals.

**Relationship with other efficiencies:**

Radiation efficiency is one of the components that contribute to the overall **total efficiency ($\eta_{total}$)** of an antenna. The total efficiency is also influenced by:

*   **Radiation Efficiency ($\eta_r$):** Accounts for power lost due to radiation.
*   **Conduction Losses ($\eta_c$):** Accounts for power lost due to resistive losses in the antenna conductors (e.g., copper losses).
*   **Dielectric Losses ($\eta_d$):** Accounts for power lost due to resistive losses in the dielectric substrate of the antenna.

The total efficiency is given by:

$\eta_{total} = \eta_r \times \eta_c \times \eta_d$

**Importance in Antenna Design:**

A high radiation efficiency is desirable for several reasons:

*   **Maximizes radiated power:** For a given input power, a higher radiation efficiency means more power is transmitted, leading to a stronger signal at the receiver.
*   **Improves antenna performance:** Contributes to a higher overall antenna gain and a better signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
*   **Reduces power consumption:** More efficient antennas require less input power to achieve a desired radiated signal strength.
*   **Crucial for low-power applications:** Especially important in battery-powered devices or applications where power is limited.

### **2. Factors Affecting Radiation Efficiency in Microstrip Antennas**

Microstrip antennas, due to their planar nature and use of dielectric substrates, are susceptible to various loss mechanisms that can significantly reduce their radiation efficiency. The primary factors are:

#### **2.1. Conduction Losses**

*   **Mechanism:** These losses arise from the finite conductivity of the metallic radiating elements (patch and ground plane) and the transmission lines used for excitation (e.g., microstrip line). When current flows through these conductors, ohmic losses occur due to their resistance.
*   **Factors Influencing Conduction Losses:**
    *   **Material Conductivity:** Higher conductivity materials (e.g., copper, silver) result in lower resistance and thus lower conduction losses.
    *   **Thickness of the Conductor:** Thicker conductors have lower resistance.
    *   **Skin Effect:** At higher frequencies, current tends to flow on the surface of the conductor, increasing the effective resistance. This is a significant factor in microwave frequencies where microstrip antennas are typically used.
    *   **Surface Roughness:** A rough surface can also increase the effective resistance due to increased path length for the current.
*   **Mitigation:** Using highly conductive metals, ensuring adequate conductor thickness, and employing smooth surface finishes.
*   **Reference:** Balanis (4th Ed., 2016) extensively discusses conductor losses in Chapter 7 (Transmission Lines and Waveguides) and Chapter 2 (Antenna Parameters). Pozar (4th Ed., 2012) also covers conductor losses in microwave transmission lines in Chapter 2.

#### **2.2. Dielectric Losses**

*   **Mechanism:** These losses occur within the dielectric substrate material used in microstrip antennas. The dielectric material is not a perfect insulator, and when subjected to an alternating electric field (generated by the antenna's fields), energy is dissipated as heat within the material. This is typically modeled as a loss tangent ($\tan \delta$) of the dielectric.
*   **Factors Influencing Dielectric Losses:**
    *   **Loss Tangent ($\tan \delta$) of the Substrate:** This is the primary parameter. Materials with lower loss tangents (e.g., Teflon, Rogers Duroid) are preferred for high-efficiency antennas.
    *   **Dielectric Constant ($\epsilon_r$):** While not a direct loss mechanism, a higher dielectric constant can confine fields more tightly to the substrate, increasing the interaction with the dielectric and potentially exacerbating dielectric losses.
    *   **Frequency:** Dielectric losses generally increase with frequency.
    *   **Thickness of the Substrate:** A thicker substrate will contain a larger volume of dielectric material, potentially leading to higher total dielectric losses.
*   **Mitigation:** Selecting low-loss dielectric substrates with low $\tan \delta$.
*   **Reference:** Balanis (4th Ed., 2016) discusses dielectric losses in Chapter 7 in the context of transmission lines. Garg (2001) dedicates significant attention to substrate material selection and its impact on losses in microstrip antennas in various chapters.

#### **2.3. Surface Wave Losses**

*   **Mechanism:** In microstrip antennas, especially those printed on thick substrates with high dielectric constants, electromagnetic energy can be coupled into surface waves. Surface waves are non-radiating modes that propagate along the dielectric-ground plane interface. These waves carry energy away from the radiating element without contributing to the far-field radiation, effectively reducing radiation efficiency.
*   **Factors Influencing Surface Wave Losses:**
    *   **Substrate Thickness ($h$):** Thicker substrates are more prone to supporting surface waves.
    *   **Dielectric Constant ($\epsilon_r$):** Higher dielectric constants increase the likelihood of surface wave excitation.
    *   **Antenna Geometry:** The size and shape of the radiating element can influence the coupling to surface waves.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Using thin substrates.
    *   Employing low dielectric constant substrates.
    *   Introducing slots or discontinuities in the ground plane around the radiating element to disrupt surface wave propagation.
*   **Reference:** Garg (2001) provides detailed analysis of surface waves in microstrip antennas. Balanis (4th Ed., 2016) also touches upon surface wave phenomena in Chapter 7 when discussing printed circuit antennas.

#### **2.4. Spurious Radiation and Coupling Losses**

*   **Mechanism:** In microstrip circuits, various unwanted radiation phenomena can occur, diverting power away from the intended radiation pattern. These include:
    *   **Radiation from transmission lines:** Microstrip lines, especially at bends or discontinuities, can radiate energy.
    *   **Coupling to parasitic modes:** Energy can be coupled to higher-order modes or surface waves.
    *   **Ground plane discontinuities:** Slots or holes in the ground plane can lead to spurious radiation.
*   **Factors Influencing Spurious Radiation:**
    *   **Circuit layout and discontinuities:** Bends, vias, and transitions in the feeding network.
    *   **Ground plane integrity:** Presence of slots, gaps, or unexpected openings.
    *   **Improper shielding or grounding.**
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Careful design of the feeding network to minimize discontinuities.
    *   Using rounded corners instead of sharp bends.
    *   Ensuring a continuous and properly designed ground plane.
    *   Using electromagnetic shielding where necessary.
*   **Reference:** Pozar (4th Ed., 2012) discusses radiation from discontinuities in transmission lines. Milligan (2nd Ed., 2005) provides practical design considerations to minimize spurious radiation in antenna circuits.

### **3. Calculating Radiation Efficiency**

Radiation efficiency can be calculated directly or indirectly using antenna parameters.

#### **3.1. Direct Calculation (from power)**

This method requires accurately measuring or calculating the radiated power and the input power.

$P_{in} = P_{rad} + P_{loss}$

Where $P_{loss}$ is the total power dissipated in the antenna.

$\eta_r = \frac{P_{rad}}{P_{in}} = \frac{P_{rad}}{P_{rad} + P_{loss}}$

*   **Challenge:** Accurately measuring $P_{rad}$ and $P_{loss}$ can be difficult. $P_{loss}$ itself is composed of conduction, dielectric, and surface wave losses, which are often hard to isolate.

#### **3.2. Using Antenna Parameters (Input Impedance and Radiation Resistance)**

A more common and practical approach is to relate radiation efficiency to the antenna's impedance. The input impedance of an antenna can be represented as:

$Z_{in} = R_{in} + jX_{in}$

Where $R_{in}$ is the total input resistance, and $X_{in}$ is the input reactance. The total input resistance can be further decomposed into:

$R_{in} = R_{rad} + R_{loss}$

*   **Radiation Resistance ($R_{rad}$):** This is the equivalent resistance that, when driven by the same current, would dissipate the same power as that radiated by the antenna.
*   **Loss Resistance ($R_{loss}$):** This represents all the resistive components causing power dissipation (conduction, dielectric, etc.).

The radiation efficiency can then be expressed as:

$\eta_r = \frac{P_{rad}}{P_{in}} = \frac{I_{in}^2 R_{rad}}{I_{in}^2 R_{in}} = \frac{R_{rad}}{R_{rad} + R_{loss}} = \frac{R_{rad}}{R_{in}}$

**How to determine $R_{rad}$ and $R_{loss}$:**

*   **Radiation Resistance ($R_{rad}$):** This is usually determined from the fundamental antenna theory for specific geometries (e.g., dipole, patch). For microstrip antennas, calculating $R_{rad}$ directly often involves solving for the fields radiated by the aperture of the patch.
*   **Loss Resistance ($R_{loss}$):** This is the most challenging to determine directly. It can be estimated by analyzing the components of loss:
    *   $R_{loss} = R_{c} + R_{d} + R_{sw}$
        *   $R_c$: Equivalent resistance due to conductor losses.
        *   $R_d$: Equivalent resistance due to dielectric losses.
        *   $R_{sw}$: Equivalent resistance due to surface wave losses.
    These equivalent resistances are calculated based on the geometry, material properties, and frequency.
*   **Total Input Resistance ($R_{in}$):** This is the real part of the measured or calculated input impedance of the antenna.

**Example (Conceptual):**

Consider a microstrip patch antenna.
1.  Measure or calculate the input impedance $Z_{in}$. Let's say $Z_{in} = 50 + j10 \, \Omega$. So, $R_{in} = 50 \, \Omega$.
2.  Calculate the radiation resistance $R_{rad}$ for the specific patch geometry and its surrounding environment. Let's assume $R_{rad} = 40 \, \Omega$.
3.  Calculate the loss resistance $R_{loss}$. From $R_{in} = R_{rad} + R_{loss}$, we get $R_{loss} = R_{in} - R_{rad} = 50 - 40 = 10 \, \Omega$.
4.  Calculate the radiation efficiency: $\eta_r = \frac{R_{rad}}{R_{in}} = \frac{40}{50} = 0.8$ or 80%.

**Important Note:** This simplified example assumes $R_{loss}$ solely represents losses that contribute to the *real part* of the input impedance. In a full analysis, dielectric and conduction losses contribute to the real part of the impedance. Surface wave losses are more complex and might not directly manifest as an increase in $R_{in}$ in the same way. More rigorous methods involve calculating power dissipated in each loss mechanism and summing them.

### **4. Techniques to Improve Radiation Efficiency in Microstrip Antennas**

Given the inherent loss mechanisms, several design techniques are employed to maximize radiation efficiency:

#### **4.1. Substrate Selection**

*   **Low-Loss Dielectrics:** Use substrates with low dielectric constants ($\epsilon_r \approx 2-4$) and low loss tangents ($\tan \delta < 0.001$). Examples include Rogers RT/duroid series (e.g., 5880, 5870), Teflon, or specialized low-loss materials. Avoid standard FR-4 for high-efficiency applications due to its higher loss tangent.
*   **Thin Substrates:** Thinner substrates reduce the volume of dielectric material and minimize surface wave excitation.
*   **Reference:** Garg (2001) emphasizes substrate selection as a critical factor for performance. Singh et al. (2nd Ed., 2021) also discuss the role of substrate properties in the context of metamaterial-based antennas, where efficient coupling to desired modes is paramount.

#### **4.2. Conductor Material and Thickness**

*   **High Conductivity Metals:** Use copper or silver for the radiating elements and ground plane.
*   **Adequate Thickness:** Ensure the conductor thickness is sufficient to mitigate skin effect losses, especially at higher frequencies. Typically, a thickness of 1 oz or 2 oz copper is standard.

#### **4.3. Reducing Surface Wave Excitation**

*   **Thin and Low-$\epsilon_r$ Substrates:** As mentioned above, these are primary methods.
*   **Ground Plane Slots/Discontinuities:** Introducing strategically placed slots or notches in the ground plane around the radiating element can effectively disrupt the propagation of surface waves, preventing energy loss. This technique is particularly effective for thicker substrates or those with higher dielectric constants.
*   **Reference:** Garg (2001) provides design guidelines for using ground plane slots to suppress surface waves.

#### **4.4. Antenna Geometry Optimization**

*   **Patch Shape:** While a rectangular patch is common, other shapes like circular, triangular, or more complex geometries might offer better radiation efficiency in specific applications by influencing current distribution and radiation patterns.
*   **Element Spacing in Arrays:** For arrays, careful spacing can minimize mutual coupling effects that might lead to parasitic losses or reduced radiation efficiency.
*   **Reference:** Balanis (4th Ed., 2016) discusses radiation from different patch shapes. Kraus (3rd Ed., 2002) provides a broad overview of various antenna geometries and their characteristics.

#### **4.5. Feed Network Design**

*   **Minimize Discontinuities:** Ensure the feed lines have minimal bends, vias, or impedance mismatches. If bends are necessary, use chamfered or quarter-wavelength section bends to reduce radiation.
*   **Proper Impedance Matching:** Ensure good matching at the feed point to maximize power transfer into the antenna and minimize reflections.
*   **Reference:** Pozar (4th Ed., 2012) details techniques for minimizing losses and spurious radiation in microwave circuits and feed networks. Milligan (2nd Ed., 2005) offers practical advice on feed design.

#### **4.6. Using Metamaterials and Advanced Structures**

*   **Metamaterial Substrates:** Metamaterials can be engineered to exhibit unusual electromagnetic properties, potentially allowing for improved radiation efficiency by controlling field confinement or enhancing radiation. However, metamaterials themselves can introduce losses, so careful design is crucial.
*   **Reference:** Singh et al. (2nd Ed., 2021) explores metamaterial applications for antennas, where efficiency is a key consideration in performance enhancement.

### **5. Practical Implications and Design Considerations**

*   **Trade-offs:** Improving radiation efficiency often involves trade-offs. For instance, using thinner substrates to reduce surface waves might make the antenna narrower in bandwidth. Choosing low-loss substrates can increase cost.
*   **Frequency Dependence:** Losses (especially dielectric and skin effect) generally increase with frequency. Therefore, radiation efficiency needs to be considered carefully for higher frequency applications (e.g., millimeter-wave).
*   **Application Requirements:** The acceptable level of radiation efficiency depends on the application. High-performance systems (e.g., satellite communications, radar) demand very high efficiency, while lower-performance consumer devices might tolerate lower efficiency to achieve lower cost or smaller size.
*   **Measurement:** Radiation efficiency is often determined indirectly through measurements of return loss ($S_{11}$) and total efficiency. If the gain of the antenna is measured accurately and compared to its directivity, the radiation efficiency can be derived.
    *   Total Efficiency ($\eta_{total}$) = Radiation Efficiency ($\eta_r$) $\times$ Efficiency of Matching ($\eta_m$)
    *   Gain ($G$) = $\eta_{total} \times$ Directivity ($D$)
    *   $\eta_r = \frac{G}{D \times \eta_m}$
    *   Typically, $\eta_m$ is assumed to be close to 1 if the antenna is well-matched (low $S_{11}$).

### **7. Key Points to Remember**

*   **Radiation efficiency is the ratio of radiated power to input power.**
*   **Major loss mechanisms in microstrip antennas are conduction, dielectric, and surface waves.**
*   **Low-loss substrates (low $\tan \delta$) and thin substrates are crucial for high efficiency.**
*   **Conductor material and thickness impact conduction losses.**
*   **Surface waves are reduced by using thin, low-$\epsilon_r$ substrates and can be mitigated by ground plane slots.**
*   **Radiation efficiency can be calculated as $R_{rad} / R_{in}$.**
*   **Minimizing discontinuities in the feed network is important.**
*   **High efficiency is critical for applications requiring maximum radiated power or power-constrained systems.**

### **8. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** Define radiation efficiency and express it in terms of antenna resistances.

**Answer:** Radiation efficiency ($\eta_r$) is the ratio of power radiated ($P_{rad}$) to the total power accepted by the antenna at its input terminals ($P_{in}$). Mathematically, $\eta_r = P_{rad} / P_{in}$. In terms of resistances, it is given by $\eta_r = R_{rad} / R_{in}$, where $R_{rad}$ is the radiation resistance and $R_{in}$ is the total input resistance ($R_{in} = R_{rad} + R_{loss}$).

**Question 2:** List the primary factors that cause power loss in a microstrip antenna and thus reduce its radiation efficiency.

**Answer:** The primary factors are:
1.  Conduction losses (due to finite conductivity of conductors).
2.  Dielectric losses (due to finite conductivity of the substrate material).
3.  Surface wave losses (energy propagating along the dielectric-ground plane interface).
4.  Spurious radiation losses (unwanted radiation from feed network or discontinuities).

**Question 3:** A microstrip patch antenna has an input impedance of $Z_{in} = 60 + j15 \, \Omega$. If the radiation resistance is calculated to be $R_{rad} = 45 \, \Omega$, what is the radiation efficiency of the antenna?

**Answer:**
Given:
$Z_{in} = 60 + j15 \, \Omega \implies R_{in} = 60 \, \Omega$
$R_{rad} = 45 \, \Omega$

Radiation efficiency $\eta_r = \frac{R_{rad}}{R_{in}} = \frac{45 \, \Omega}{60 \, \Omega} = 0.75$

So, the radiation efficiency is 75%.

**Question 4:** What are two common techniques to reduce surface wave losses in microstrip antennas?

**Answer:** Two common techniques are:
1.  Using thin substrates.
2.  Using substrates with a low dielectric constant ($\epsilon_r$).
3.  (Additional technique) Introducing slots or discontinuities in the ground plane around the radiating element.

**Question 5:** Why is it important to select low-loss dielectric materials for high-efficiency microstrip antennas?

**Answer:** Low-loss dielectric materials (with low loss tangent, $\tan \delta$) minimize the power dissipated as heat within the substrate when it is subjected to the antenna's electromagnetic fields. This directly reduces dielectric losses, which is a significant contributor to overall power loss and reduced radiation efficiency.
