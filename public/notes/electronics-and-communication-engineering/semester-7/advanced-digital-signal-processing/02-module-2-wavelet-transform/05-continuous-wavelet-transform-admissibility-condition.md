### 1. Introduction to Wavelet Transform

The Wavelet Transform (WT) is a powerful signal processing tool that decomposes a signal into different frequency components, each localized in time. Unlike the Fourier Transform, which provides frequency information but lacks time localization, the WT offers both time and frequency localization. This makes it ideal for analyzing non-stationary signals (signals whose frequency content changes over time), such as speech, audio, and biomedical signals.

**Key Concept: Mother Wavelet**
The core of the Wavelet Transform is the **mother wavelet**, denoted by $\psi(t)$. This is a finite-energy, oscillating function with a zero average value. All other wavelets used in the transform, known as **daughter wavelets**, are generated by scaling and translating the mother wavelet:

$\psi_{a,b}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|a|}} \psi\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)$

where:
*   $a$ is the **scale parameter**, controlling the dilation or compression of the wavelet. A large scale $a$ corresponds to a stretched wavelet, analyzing low-frequency components, while a small scale $a$ corresponds to a compressed wavelet, analyzing high-frequency components.
*   $b$ is the **translation parameter**, controlling the time shift of the wavelet. This allows for time-localized analysis of the signal.

*   **CO1 (K2):** Understanding the basic components of the wavelet transform (mother wavelet, scaling, translation) contributes to classifying and operating on signals.

### 2. Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT)

The Continuous Wavelet Transform of a signal $x(t)$ with respect to a mother wavelet $\psi(t)$ is defined as:

$CWT_x(\tau, s) = \langle x(t), \psi_{\tau,s}(t) \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) \psi_{\tau,s}^*(t) dt$

where:
*   $\psi_{\tau,s}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|}} \psi\left(\frac{t-\tau}{s}\right)$ is the daughter wavelet.
    *   $s$ is the scale parameter (corresponding to $a$ above).
    *   $\tau$ is the translation parameter (corresponding to $b$ above).
*   $*$ denotes the complex conjugate. If the mother wavelet is real, the conjugate is not needed.

The CWT computes the correlation between the signal $x(t)$ and scaled and translated versions of the mother wavelet. A high correlation at a specific scale $s$ and translation $\tau$ indicates that the signal $x(t)$ has a component similar to $\psi(\frac{t-\tau}{s})$ at that time and frequency.

**Textbook Reference:** Oppenheim & Willsky (Ch. 10: Fourier Series and Transforms - General concepts of transforms, though wavelets are a more advanced topic, the idea of analyzing signals via basis functions is fundamental). Haykin's "Signals and Systems" also provides foundational understanding of signal analysis techniques.

**Example:**
Consider a signal with a sudden jump. A Morlet wavelet (a complex sinusoid modulated by a Gaussian) with a small scale would capture the high-frequency discontinuity. A Haar wavelet (a simple square pulse) can also effectively detect edges.

### 3. The Admissibility Condition

For the Continuous Wavelet Transform to be well-defined and for the signal $x(t)$ to be reconstructible from its CWT coefficients, the mother wavelet $\psi(t)$ must satisfy a crucial condition known as the **admissibility condition**. This condition ensures that the wavelet has the necessary properties to effectively analyze and represent signals.

**Derivation and Frequency Domain Perspective:**

The CWT involves correlating the signal with scaled versions of the wavelet. This suggests that the wavelet must possess energy distributed across different frequencies to represent different components of the signal.

Let $\Psi(\omega)$ be the Fourier Transform of the mother wavelet $\psi(t)$:
$\Psi(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt$

The CWT can also be expressed in the frequency domain. The Fourier Transform of the daughter wavelet $\psi_{\tau,s}(t)$ is:
$\Psi_{\tau,s}(\omega) = \sqrt{|s|} e^{-j\omega\tau} \Psi(s\omega)$

The CWT in the frequency domain is given by:
$CWT_x(\tau, s) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(\omega) \Psi_{\tau,s}^*(\omega) d\omega$
$CWT_x(\tau, s) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(\omega) \left(\sqrt{|s|} e^{-j\omega\tau} \Psi(s\omega)\right)^* d\omega$
$CWT_x(\tau, s) = \frac{\sqrt{|s|}}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(\omega) e^{j\omega\tau} \Psi^*(s\omega) d\omega$

For the inverse CWT to exist, the energy of the wavelet must be concentrated around a central frequency, and it must not have zero energy at DC (zero frequency). If $\Psi(0) = 0$, it means the wavelet has zero average value in the time domain, which is essential for localized analysis.

The admissibility condition is typically stated in the frequency domain as:

$\boxed{C_\psi = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} d\omega < \infty}$

This condition implies two key properties for the mother wavelet $\psi(t)$:

1.  **Zero Average Value (DC Component):** In the frequency domain, this translates to $\Psi(0) = 0$. This means the wavelet does not respond to the DC component of the signal.
    *   $\Psi(0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(t) e^{-j(0)t} dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(t) dt$
    *   Therefore, the admissibility condition requires $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(t) dt = 0$. This ensures that the wavelet is oscillatory and localized.

2.  **Non-zero Energy Distribution Across Frequencies:** The term $\frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|}$ ensures that the wavelet has significant energy at frequencies other than DC. The $|\omega|$ in the denominator implies that the wavelet should not have too much energy concentrated at very low frequencies if it's meant to be a good analyzing wavelet.

**Time Domain Interpretation:**
The condition $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(t) dt = 0$ means that the positive and negative lobes of the wavelet cancel each other out in terms of area. This property allows the wavelet to act as a band-pass filter.

**Reference Books:** Lathi's "Principles of Signal Processing & Linear systems" and Ziemer's "Signals & Systems - Continuous and Discrete" often cover the mathematical underpinnings of signal transforms, providing context for such conditions. Ambardar's "Analog and Digital Signal Processing" and Apte's "Signals and systems - Principles and Applications" would also likely delve into transform properties.

### 4. Implications of the Admissibility Condition

The admissibility condition dictates the types of functions that can be used as mother wavelets.

*   **Wavelets with Zero Mean:** Any wavelet with a zero average value will satisfy $\Psi(0) = 0$. Examples include the Haar wavelet, Morlet wavelet, Mexican Hat wavelet, and Daubechies wavelets.
*   **Band-pass Property:** The condition $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} d\omega < \infty$ implies that the wavelet's energy should not be solely concentrated at DC. This means the wavelet is effectively a band-pass filter. A signal with a significant DC component cannot be analyzed using a wavelet that satisfies the admissibility condition.

**Reconstruction of the Signal:**
The admissibility condition is also crucial for the **reconstruction** of the original signal $x(t)$ from its CWT coefficients. If the condition is met, the inverse CWT formula exists:

$x(t) = \frac{1}{C_\psi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} CWT_x(\tau, s) \frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|}} \psi\left(\frac{t-\tau}{s}\right) \frac{ds d\tau}{s^2}$

This formula shows that the original signal can be rebuilt by summing up scaled and translated versions of the mother wavelet, weighted by the CWT coefficients.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The admissibility condition is a property of the **mother wavelet**, not the signal.
*   It ensures the wavelet has a zero mean and a finite energy distributed across frequencies (not exclusively at DC).
*   It guarantees the existence of the CWT and the possibility of reconstructing the original signal.
*   A wavelet that violates the admissibility condition cannot be used for the CWT.

### 5. Examples of Wavelets and Admissibility

Let's examine a couple of common mother wavelets:

**Example 1: Haar Wavelet**

The Haar wavelet is the simplest wavelet. In its basic form, it is defined as:
$\psi(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & 0 \le t < 1/2 \\ -1 & 1/2 \le t < 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$

*   **Time Domain:** It has a zero average value: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(t) dt = (1 \times 1/2) + (-1 \times 1/2) = 0$.
*   **Frequency Domain:** The Fourier Transform of the Haar wavelet is:
    $\Psi(\omega) = \int_0^1 \psi(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt = \int_0^{1/2} e^{-j\omega t} dt - \int_{1/2}^1 e^{-j\omega t} dt$
    $\Psi(\omega) = \left[\frac{e^{-j\omega t}}{-j\omega}\right]_0^{1/2} - \left[\frac{e^{-j\omega t}}{-j\omega}\right]_{1/2}^1$
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{e^{-j\omega/2} - 1}{-j\omega} - \frac{e^{-j\omega} - e^{-j\omega/2}}{-j\omega}$
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1}{-j\omega} (e^{-j\omega/2} - 1 - e^{-j\omega} + e^{-j\omega/2}) = \frac{1}{-j\omega} (2e^{-j\omega/2} - 1 - e^{-j\omega})$
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{e^{-j\omega/2}}{-j\omega} (2 - e^{-j\omega/2} - e^{-j\omega/2}) = \frac{e^{-j\omega/2}}{-j\omega} (2 - 2\cos(\omega/2))$ (using $e^{-j\phi} + e^{j\phi} = 2\cos\phi$)
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{e^{-j\omega/2}}{-j\omega} 2 \sin^2(\omega/4)$ (using $1 - \cos\theta = 2\sin^2(\theta/2)$)
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{e^{-j\omega/2}}{-j\omega} 2 \left(\frac{e^{j\omega/4} - e^{-j\omega/4}}{2j}\right)^2 = \frac{e^{-j\omega/2}}{-j\omega} 2 \frac{e^{j\omega/2} - 2 + e^{-j\omega/2}}{-4}$
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1}{2j\omega} (2 - (e^{j\omega/2} + e^{-j\omega/2})) = \frac{1}{2j\omega} (2 - 2\cos(\omega/2))$
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1 - \cos(\omega/2)}{j\omega}$

    Let's re-calculate $\Psi(\omega)$ more carefully.
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1}{-j\omega} [e^{-j\omega/2} - 1 - (e^{-j\omega} - e^{-j\omega/2})] = \frac{1}{-j\omega} [2e^{-j\omega/2} - 1 - e^{-j\omega}]$
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1}{-j\omega} [2(\cos(\omega/2) - j\sin(\omega/2)) - 1 - (\cos\omega - j\sin\omega)]$
    For $\omega \to 0$, using Taylor series $e^{ix} \approx 1 + ix - (ix)^2/2$:
    $\Psi(\omega) \approx \frac{1}{-j\omega} [2(1 - j\omega/2 - (j\omega/2)^2/2) - 1 - (1 - j\omega - (j\omega)^2/2)]$
    $\Psi(\omega) \approx \frac{1}{-j\omega} [2(1 - j\omega/2 + \omega^2/8) - 1 - (1 - j\omega + \omega^2/2)]$
    $\Psi(\omega) \approx \frac{1}{-j\omega} [2 - j\omega + \omega^2/4 - 1 - 1 + j\omega - \omega^2/2]$
    $\Psi(\omega) \approx \frac{1}{-j\omega} [\omega^2/4 - \omega^2/2] = \frac{1}{-j\omega} [-\omega^2/4] = \frac{j\omega}{4}$
    So, $\lim_{\omega \to 0} \Psi(\omega) = 0$.

    More formally, $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1 - e^{-j\omega/2}}{j\omega} - \frac{e^{-j\omega} - e^{-j\omega/2}}{j\omega} = \frac{1 - 2e^{-j\omega/2} + e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega}$.
    Using $1 - 2e^{-jx} + e^{-2jx} = (1-e^{-jx})^2$. Let $x=\omega/2$.
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{(1-e^{-j\omega/2})^2}{j\omega}$.
    For $\omega \to 0$, $(1-e^{-j\omega/2})^2 \approx (1 - (1 - j\omega/2 - (j\omega/2)^2/2))^2 \approx (j\omega/2 + \omega^2/8)^2 \approx (j\omega/2)^2 = -\omega^2/4$.
    $\Psi(\omega) \approx \frac{-\omega^2/4}{j\omega} = \frac{j\omega}{4}$.
    Thus, $\Psi(0)=0$.

*   **Admissibility Condition:**
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 = \left|\frac{1-e^{-j\omega/2}}{j\omega}\right|^2 \times |1-e^{-j\omega/2}|^2 = \frac{|1-(\cos(\omega/2)-j\sin(\omega/2))|^2}{|\omega|^2} \times |1-(\cos\omega-j\sin\omega)|^2$
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 = \frac{|(1-\cos(\omega/2))+j\sin(\omega/2)|^2}{\omega^2} \times |(1-\cos\omega)+j\sin\omega|^2$
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 = \frac{1-2\cos(\omega/2)+\cos^2(\omega/2)+\sin^2(\omega/2)}{\omega^2} \times (1-2\cos\omega+\cos^2\omega+\sin^2\omega)$
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 = \frac{2-2\cos(\omega/2)}{\omega^2} \times (2-2\cos\omega) = \frac{4\sin^2(\omega/4)}{\omega^2} \times (2\sin^2(\omega/2))$
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 = \frac{4\sin^2(\omega/4)}{\omega^2} \times 2 \frac{\omega^2}{4} = 2 \sin^2(\omega/4) \times \frac{\omega^2}{4}$

    Let's use the simplified form of $\Psi(\omega)$ often quoted: $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1-e^{-j\omega}}{2j\omega} - \frac{1-e^{-j\omega/2}}{2j\omega}$ is not quite right.
    A common Fourier Transform of Haar is $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1-e^{-j\omega/2}}{j\omega} - \frac{e^{-j\omega}-e^{-j\omega/2}}{j\omega} = \frac{1 - 2e^{-j\omega/2} + e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega}$.
    Using $1-e^{-j\phi} = e^{-j\phi/2}(e^{j\phi/2}-e^{-j\phi/2}) = e^{-j\phi/2}(2j\sin(\phi/2))$.
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{(1-e^{-j\omega/2})^2}{j\omega}$.
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 = \frac{|1-e^{-j\omega/2}|^4}{\omega^2} = \frac{|e^{-j\omega/4}(e^{j\omega/4}-e^{-j\omega/4})|^4}{\omega^2} = \frac{|e^{-j\omega/4}(2j\sin(\omega/4))|^4}{\omega^2}$
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 = \frac{|2j\sin(\omega/4)|^4}{\omega^2} = \frac{16|\sin(\omega/4)|^4}{\omega^2}$.

    Now, consider the integral:
    $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} d\omega = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{16\sin^4(\omega/4)}{\omega^2 |\omega|} d\omega$
    This integral is problematic due to $\frac{1}{\omega^3}$ behavior near $\omega=0$.
    However, the "standard" Haar wavelet is defined slightly differently by some authors to ensure admissibility, or its application is careful. The condition $\Psi(0)=0$ is met. The issue is with the $|\omega|$ in the denominator.

    A common form of the admissibility condition that works for Haar is sometimes stated with a different weighting or as a necessary condition for *some* wavelets. Let's re-evaluate the integral for the given Haar.
    The integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{|\omega|^p} d\omega$ diverges for $p \ge 1$.
    Here, the term $|\Psi(\omega)|^2$ is roughly proportional to $\omega^4$ near $\omega=0$. So $|\Psi(\omega)|^2/|\omega|$ is roughly proportional to $\omega^3$ near $\omega=0$. This integral should converge.
    Let's check $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1-e^{-j\omega/2}}{j\omega} - \frac{e^{-j\omega}-e^{-j\omega/2}}{j\omega}$.
    $\Psi(\omega) = \frac{1}{-j\omega}(2e^{-j\omega/2}-1-e^{-j\omega})$
    For small $\omega$: $e^{-jx} \approx 1 - jx - x^2/2 + jx^3/6$.
    $2(1-j\omega/2 - \omega^2/8 + j\omega^3/48) - 1 - (1-j\omega - \omega^2/2 + j\omega^3/6)$
    $= 2-j\omega - \omega^2/4 + j\omega^3/24 - 1 - 1 + j\omega + \omega^2/2 - j\omega^3/6$
    $= \omega^2/4 - j\omega^3/4$.
    $\Psi(\omega) \approx \frac{1}{-j\omega}(\omega^2/4) = \frac{j\omega}{4}$. This is $\Psi(0)=0$.
    $|\Psi(\omega)|^2 \approx |\frac{j\omega}{4}|^2 = \frac{\omega^2}{16}$.
    So, $\frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} \approx \frac{\omega^2/16}{|\omega|} = \frac{|\omega|}{16}$ for small $\omega$. This part of the integral converges.

    Let's look at large $\omega$. $\Psi(\omega)$ will oscillate and decay. The integral $\int \frac{1}{|\omega|} d\omega$ diverges as $|\omega| \to \infty$.
    This suggests that the Haar wavelet, in its most basic form, might not strictly satisfy the admissibility condition in the way some other wavelets do, or there's a nuance in the definition or its usage.
    **Key Point:** The admissibility condition is a sufficient and necessary condition for the *existence of a unique inverse transform*. Some wavelets might have transforms that are "almost" invertible or have specific applications where the full inverse is not needed.

    For typical analysis, Haar is often used, and its admissibility is discussed with caveats or by using scaled versions that might improve its properties. The crucial part is $\Psi(0)=0$.

**Example 2: Morlet Wavelet**

The Morlet wavelet is a complex sine wave modulated by a Gaussian window:
$\psi(t) = e^{j\omega_0 t} e^{-t^2 / (2\sigma^2)}$

*   **Time Domain:** The Gaussian window ensures the wavelet is localized in time. The complex exponential makes it oscillatory. The average value is zero due to the oscillatory nature.
*   **Frequency Domain:** The Fourier Transform of the Morlet wavelet is a Gaussian function centered at $\omega_0$:
    $\Psi(\omega) = \sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{-\sigma^2 (\omega - \omega_0)^2 / 2}$

*   **Admissibility Condition:**
    $\Psi(0) = \sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{-\sigma^2 (-\omega_0)^2 / 2} = \sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{-\sigma^2 \omega_0^2 / 2}$
    For $\Psi(0)=0$, we need $\omega_0 \ne 0$. The Gaussian never becomes exactly zero. However, if $\omega_0$ is sufficiently large compared to $\sigma$, $\Psi(0)$ becomes negligibly small.
    The admissibility condition $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} d\omega < \infty$ is satisfied for the Morlet wavelet, provided $\omega_0 \ne 0$. The Gaussian decay ensures the integral converges.

    **Implication:** The Morlet wavelet is a very popular choice for CWT because it meets the admissibility condition well and provides good time-frequency localization. The parameter $\sigma$ controls the width of the Gaussian, affecting the trade-off between time and frequency resolution.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
State the admissibility condition for a mother wavelet $\psi(t)$ in the frequency domain.

**Answer:**
The admissibility condition for a mother wavelet $\psi(t)$ is given by:
$C_\psi = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} d\omega < \infty$, where $\Psi(\omega)$ is the Fourier Transform of $\psi(t)$.

---

**Question 2:**
What are the two main implications of the admissibility condition for a mother wavelet?

**Answer:**
1.  **Zero Average Value:** The wavelet must have a zero mean in the time domain, which translates to $\Psi(0) = 0$ in the frequency domain. This means the wavelet is oscillatory and not sensitive to the DC component of a signal.
2.  **Non-zero Energy at Frequencies other than DC:** The condition ensures that the wavelet has energy distributed across a range of frequencies (effectively acting as a band-pass filter) and the integral of energy density divided by frequency is finite.

---

**Question 3:**
Consider a hypothetical wavelet $\phi(t)$ whose Fourier Transform is $\Phi(\omega) = \frac{1}{\omega^2 + 1}$. Can this wavelet be used for the Continuous Wavelet Transform? Justify your answer.

**Answer:**
To determine if $\phi(t)$ can be used for CWT, we need to check the admissibility condition.
First, let's find $\Phi(0)$: $\Phi(0) = \frac{1}{0^2 + 1} = 1$.
Since $\Phi(0) \ne 0$, the wavelet has a non-zero DC component. This means it does not have a zero average value in the time domain. Therefore, this wavelet cannot be used for the Continuous Wavelet Transform as it violates the fundamental requirement of $\Psi(0)=0$.

---

**Question 4:**
A signal $x(t)$ has a CWT computed with a mother wavelet $\psi(t)$ that satisfies the admissibility condition. If the CWT coefficients are $CWT_x(\tau, s)$, write down the formula for reconstructing the original signal $x(t)$.

**Answer:**
The formula for reconstructing the original signal $x(t)$ from its CWT coefficients $CWT_x(\tau, s)$ is:
$x(t) = \frac{1}{C_\psi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} CWT_x(\tau, s) \frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|}} \psi\left(\frac{t-\tau}{s}\right) \frac{ds d\tau}{s^2}$
where $C_\psi = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} d\omega$ is the admissibility constant.

### 7. Summary and Key Takeaways

*   The Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) analyzes signals using scaled and translated versions of a **mother wavelet**.
*   The **admissibility condition** is a critical property of the mother wavelet, ensuring the CWT is well-defined and the signal can be reconstructed.
*   In the frequency domain, this condition is stated as $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{|\omega|} d\omega < \infty$.
*   Key implications of the admissibility condition are that the mother wavelet must have a **zero mean** ($\Psi(0)=0$) and a finite energy distribution across frequencies.
*   The Haar wavelet and Morlet wavelet are common examples, with the Morlet wavelet being particularly well-suited due to its adherence to the admissibility condition.
*   Understanding the admissibility condition is fundamental to selecting appropriate mother wavelets for specific signal analysis tasks.
