# Engineering Economics: Module 1 - Basic Economic Concepts

This module provides a foundational understanding of core economic principles essential for engineering decision-making. We will explore how individual preferences, market forces, and price changes influence economic outcomes, enabling us to make more informed choices in engineering projects and management.

---

## 1. Utility and Consumer Behavior

**Key Concept:** Utility represents the satisfaction or benefit a consumer derives from consuming a good or service. It's a subjective measure and helps explain consumer choices.

**Definition:**
*   **Utility:** The satisfaction, happiness, or benefit that an individual receives from the consumption of a good or service.

**Types of Utility:**
*   **Total Utility (TU):** The total satisfaction gained from consuming all units of a good.
*   **Marginal Utility (MU):** The additional satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good.

**Relationship between TU and MU:**
*   As consumption increases, TU generally increases, but at a decreasing rate.
*   MU is the change in TU divided by the change in quantity consumed: $MU = \Delta TU / \Delta Q$.
*   MU typically declines as consumption increases.

---

### 1.1 Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility

**Key Concept:** This fundamental law states that as a consumer consumes more and more of a particular good, the additional satisfaction (marginal utility) from each successive unit tends to decrease, assuming other factors remain constant.

**Definition:**
*   **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility:** For any given period, as a person consumes more units of a particular good, the marginal utility derived from each additional unit will eventually decline.

**Explanation:**
Imagine you are very thirsty and drink a glass of water. The first glass provides immense satisfaction. The second glass might still be satisfying, but less so than the first. The third, fourth, and subsequent glasses will likely provide even less additional satisfaction, and eventually, you might even experience dissatisfaction.

**Example (Geetika, Ghosh & Chodhury):**
*   A student studying for an exam might find the first hour of studying highly productive and satisfying. The second hour might still be beneficial, but the student may experience fatigue or reduced concentration. The third and subsequent hours might yield diminishing returns in terms of learning and satisfaction.

**Implications for Engineering Economics:**
*   Understanding this law helps in pricing strategies, product design (offering variety to maintain consumer interest), and understanding demand for products.
*   It's crucial for understanding consumer behavior and predicting how changes in consumption patterns affect demand. (Relates to **CO1**)

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   This law assumes that consumption occurs over a single period.
*   It assumes that no other goods are consumed during the period.
*   The units of the good consumed must be of standard size and quality.

## 2. Demand and Supply

These are the two fundamental forces that determine prices and quantities in a market economy.

### 2.1 Law of Demand

**Key Concept:** The law of demand describes the inverse relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity demanded by consumers, ceteris paribus (all other factors remaining constant).

**Definition:**
*   **Law of Demand:** As the price of a good or service increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and as the price decreases, the quantity demanded increases, assuming all other factors remain constant.

**Explanation:**
When prices are high, consumers tend to buy less because the good is less affordable or they can find substitutes. When prices are low, consumers are more willing and able to purchase more.

**Demand Schedule:** A table showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded at each price.

**Demand Curve:** A graphical representation of the demand schedule, plotting price on the vertical axis and quantity demanded on the horizontal axis. The demand curve typically slopes downwards.

**Factors Affecting Demand (Determinants of Demand) - Shifts in Demand Curve:**
*   **Consumer Income:**
    *   **Normal Goods:** Demand increases as income rises (e.g., cars, vacations).
    *   **Inferior Goods:** Demand decreases as income rises (e.g., public transport, generic brands).
*   **Prices of Related Goods:**
    *   **Substitutes:** Goods that can be used in place of each other. If the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good increases (e.g., if coffee prices rise, demand for tea might increase).
    *   **Complements:** Goods that are used together. If the price of a complement rises, demand for the original good decreases (e.g., if gasoline prices rise, demand for SUVs might decrease).
*   **Consumer Tastes and Preferences:** Changes in fashion, trends, or advertising can shift demand.
*   **Consumer Expectations:** Beliefs about future prices or income can influence current demand.
*   **Number of Buyers:** An increase in the number of consumers in the market increases demand.

**Example (Paneerselvam):**
*   If the price of smartphones decreases, consumers will generally buy more smartphones. If the price of data plans (a complement) increases significantly, people might buy fewer smartphones.

**Implications for Engineering Economics:**
*   Understanding demand is crucial for forecasting sales, setting prices, and designing products that meet consumer needs.
*   Engineers need to consider market demand when planning production capacity and resource allocation. (Relates to **CO1**)

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   A movement *along* the demand curve is caused by a change in the price of the good itself.
*   A *shift* of the entire demand curve is caused by a change in one of the other determinants of demand.

### 2.2 Law of Supply

**Key Concept:** The law of supply describes the direct relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity supplied by producers, ceteris paribus.

**Definition:**
*   **Law of Supply:** As the price of a good or service increases, the quantity supplied increases, and as the price decreases, the quantity supplied decreases, assuming all other factors remain constant.

**Explanation:**
When prices are high, producers are incentivized to produce and sell more because they can earn higher profits. When prices are low, the profit margin is smaller, so producers may reduce output or even exit the market.

**Supply Schedule:** A table showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity supplied at each price.

**Supply Curve:** A graphical representation of the supply schedule, plotting price on the vertical axis and quantity supplied on the horizontal axis. The supply curve typically slopes upwards.

**Factors Affecting Supply (Determinants of Supply) - Shifts in Supply Curve:**
*   **Prices of Inputs (Factors of Production):** If the cost of raw materials, labor, or energy increases, supply will decrease at any given price.
*   **Technology:** Improvements in technology usually lead to increased productivity and lower production costs, thus increasing supply.
*   **Government Policies:**
    *   **Taxes:** Taxes on production generally decrease supply.
    *   **Subsidies:** Subsidies to producers generally increase supply.
    *   **Regulations:** Stricter regulations can increase production costs and decrease supply.
*   **Expectations of Producers:** If producers expect prices to rise in the future, they might reduce current supply to sell later at a higher price.
*   **Number of Sellers:** An increase in the number of producers in the market increases supply.
*   **Prices of Related Goods in Production:** If a producer can make multiple products, changes in the price of one product can affect the supply of another.

**Example (Thuesen & Fabrycky):**
*   If the market price for a specific type of steel beam increases, steel manufacturers will be motivated to increase the production of that beam, assuming they have the capacity and access to resources. If the cost of iron ore (an input) rises significantly, the supply of steel beams will likely decrease.

**Implications for Engineering Economics:**
*   Understanding supply helps in forecasting production costs, managing inventory, and understanding the competitive landscape.
*   Engineers involved in production must consider factors that influence the cost and availability of inputs. (Relates to **CO1**)

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   A movement *along* the supply curve is caused by a change in the price of the good itself.
*   A *shift* of the entire supply curve is caused by a change in one of the other determinants of supply.

## 3. Equilibrium

**Key Concept:** Equilibrium in a market occurs when the quantity demanded by consumers equals the quantity supplied by producers at a specific price. This is the point where the market clears, with no surplus or shortage.

**Definition:**
*   **Market Equilibrium:** The state where the quantity demanded of a good or service is equal to the quantity supplied at a given price.
*   **Equilibrium Price:** The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
*   **Equilibrium Quantity:** The quantity of a good or service bought and sold at the equilibrium price.

**Graphical Representation:** Equilibrium is found at the intersection of the demand curve and the supply curve.

**Forces Driving to Equilibrium:**
*   **Surplus (Price above Equilibrium):** If the price is above equilibrium, the quantity supplied will exceed the quantity demanded. Producers will have unsold inventory and will be incentivized to lower prices to sell their excess stock. This price reduction moves the market towards equilibrium.
*   **Shortage (Price below Equilibrium):** If the price is below equilibrium, the quantity demanded will exceed the quantity supplied. Consumers will compete for the limited goods, and producers will realize they can raise prices. This price increase moves the market towards equilibrium.

**Example (Park):**
Consider the market for electrical components used in electric vehicles.
*   If the price of these components is set too high, more manufacturers will want to produce them than consumers (automakers) are willing to buy, leading to a surplus. Manufacturers will then lower prices to clear inventory.
*   If the price is set too low, automakers will want to buy more than manufacturers are willing to produce, leading to a shortage. This will push prices up as demand outstrips supply.
*   The equilibrium price is where the number of components automakers want to buy matches the number manufacturers want to sell.

**Implications for Engineering Economics:**
*   Understanding equilibrium helps in making pricing decisions, forecasting market outcomes, and understanding the impact of external factors on product viability.
*   Engineers need to consider the market equilibrium price when evaluating the cost-effectiveness of new designs or production methods. (Relates to **CO1**)

## 4. Changes in Demand and Supply and Their Effects

Changes in the determinants of demand and supply (other than the price of the good itself) cause the entire demand or supply curve to shift, leading to a new equilibrium price and quantity.

### 4.1 Changes in Demand

**Effect of an Increase in Demand (Demand curve shifts to the right):**
*   **Cause:** Increase in consumer income (for normal goods), rise in price of a substitute, fall in price of a complement, favorable change in tastes, increase in number of buyers, expected future price rise.
*   **Result:**
    *   **Equilibrium Price Increases.**
    *   **Equilibrium Quantity Increases.**

**Example:** If a new study shows that a particular type of solar panel significantly increases home energy efficiency, demand for these panels will increase. This shift in demand will lead to a higher equilibrium price and a greater quantity of solar panels being sold.

### 4.2 Changes in Supply

**Effect of an Increase in Supply (Supply curve shifts to the right):**
*   **Cause:** Decrease in input costs, technological advancements, favorable government policies (subsidies), expected future price fall, increase in number of sellers.
*   **Result:**
    *   **Equilibrium Price Decreases.**
    *   **Equilibrium Quantity Increases.**

**Example:** If a new, more efficient manufacturing process for turbine blades is developed, the cost of producing blades will decrease. This will shift the supply curve to the right, leading to a lower equilibrium price and a higher quantity of turbine blades produced and sold.

### 4.3 Effect of a Decrease in Demand (Demand curve shifts to the left):

*   **Cause:** Decrease in consumer income (for normal goods), fall in price of a substitute, rise in price of a complement, unfavorable change in tastes, decrease in number of buyers, expected future price fall.
*   **Result:**
    *   **Equilibrium Price Decreases.**
    *   **Equilibrium Quantity Decreases.**

### 4.4 Effect of a Decrease in Supply (Supply curve shifts to the left):

*   **Cause:** Increase in input costs, technological regress, unfavorable government policies (taxes), expected future price rise, decrease in number of sellers.
*   **Result:**
    *   **Equilibrium Price Increases.**
    *   **Equilibrium Quantity Decreases.**

**Implications for Engineering Economics:**
*   Engineers must be aware of how market dynamics (changes in demand and supply) can affect the economic viability of their projects.
*   For example, a project to produce a new type of battery might be profitable at current market prices, but a sudden drop in demand due to changing consumer preferences or an increase in the supply of alternative technologies could make the project unprofitable. (Relates to **CO1**)

## 5. Elasticity

**Key Concept:** Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one economic variable to a change in another. In engineering economics, the most common types are price elasticity of demand and price elasticity of supply.

### 5.1 Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

**Key Concept:** PED measures how much the quantity demanded of a good responds to a change in its price.

**Definition:**
*   **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED):** The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

**Formula:**
$PED = (\% \text{ Change in Quantity Demanded}) / (\% \text{ Change in Price})$

$PED = [(\Delta Q_d / Q_d) / (\Delta P / P)]$

Alternatively, using the midpoint formula (for more accuracy):
$PED = [(\Delta Q_d) / ((Q_{d1} + Q_{d2}) / 2)] / [(\Delta P) / ((P_1 + P_2) / 2)]$

**Interpretation of PED Values:**
*   **Elastic Demand (PED > 1):** Quantity demanded changes significantly with a small change in price. Consumers are very responsive.
*   **Inelastic Demand (PED < 1):** Quantity demanded changes only slightly with a change in price. Consumers are not very responsive.
*   **Unit Elastic Demand (PED = 1):** Quantity demanded changes by the same percentage as the price.
*   **Perfectly Elastic Demand (PED = ∞):** Consumers will buy an infinite amount at a specific price but nothing above it. The demand curve is horizontal.
*   **Perfectly Inelastic Demand (PED = 0):** Quantity demanded does not change at all with a change in price. The demand curve is vertical.

**Factors Affecting PED:**
*   **Availability of Substitutes:** More substitutes lead to higher elasticity (e.g., many brands of coffee vs. water).
*   **Necessity vs. Luxury:** Necessities tend to be inelastic (e.g., medicine), while luxuries are elastic (e.g., yachts).
*   **Proportion of Income:** Goods that consume a larger portion of income tend to be more elastic (e.g., cars vs. chewing gum).
*   **Time Horizon:** Demand tends to be more elastic in the long run than in the short run, as consumers have more time to adjust.

**Example (Khan M.Y. - related to financial markets and consumer spending):**
*   **Elastic:** If the price of a specific model of imported car increases by 10%, and the quantity demanded falls by 20%, the demand is elastic ($PED = -20\% / 10\% = -2$). Consumers might switch to other car brands or delay their purchase.
*   **Inelastic:** If the price of essential medication increases by 10%, and the quantity demanded falls by only 2%, the demand is inelastic ($PED = -2\% / 10\% = -0.2$). People will still buy it due to its necessity.

### 5.2 Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

**Key Concept:** PES measures how much the quantity supplied of a good responds to a change in its price.

**Definition:**
*   **Price Elasticity of Supply (PES):** The percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price.

**Formula:**
$PES = (\% \text{ Change in Quantity Supplied}) / (\% \text{ Change in Price})$

$PES = [(\Delta Q_s / Q_s) / (\Delta P / P)]$

**Interpretation of PES Values:**
*   **Elastic Supply (PES > 1):** Quantity supplied changes significantly with a small change in price. Producers are responsive.
*   **Inelastic Supply (PES < 1):** Quantity supplied changes only slightly with a change in price. Producers are not very responsive.
*   **Unit Elastic Supply (PES = 1):** Quantity supplied changes by the same percentage as the price.
*   **Perfectly Elastic Supply (PES = ∞):** Producers will supply an infinite amount at a specific price but nothing below it. The supply curve is horizontal.
*   **Perfectly Inelastic Supply (PES = 0):** Quantity supplied does not change at all with a change in price. The supply curve is vertical (e.g., fixed supply of artwork).

**Factors Affecting PES:**
*   **Availability of Inputs:** If it's easy to get more raw materials or labor, supply is more elastic.
*   **Time Horizon:** Supply is generally more elastic in the long run than in the short run, as producers have more time to adjust production levels.
*   **Mobility of Factors of Production:** If resources can easily switch between producing different goods, supply is more elastic.
*   **Excess Capacity:** Firms with spare capacity can respond to price increases more easily.

**Example (Newman & Lavelle):**
*   Consider a factory producing custom-designed electronic circuit boards. If the price for these boards doubles, the factory might be able to ramp up production relatively quickly if they have idle machinery and skilled labor. This would indicate elastic supply.
*   However, if the factory is already operating at full capacity, and hiring and training new engineers takes a long time, an increase in price might not lead to a significant immediate increase in the quantity supplied, indicating inelastic supply in the short term.

### 5.3 Measurement of Elasticity and its Applications

**Applications of Price Elasticity of Demand:**
*   **Pricing Decisions by Firms:** If demand is elastic, firms must be cautious about raising prices, as it can lead to a significant drop in sales and potentially lower total revenue. If demand is inelastic, firms have more room to increase prices without a large loss in sales, potentially increasing total revenue.
    *   **Total Revenue (TR) = Price (P) x Quantity Demanded (Qd)**
    *   If demand is elastic, a price increase leads to a decrease in TR.
    *   If demand is inelastic, a price increase leads to an increase in TR.
*   **Government Policy (Taxation):** Governments often tax goods with inelastic demand (like cigarettes or gasoline) because the tax burden is more easily passed on to consumers, and the quantity demanded is less likely to decrease significantly, thus generating more tax revenue.
*   **Forecasting Sales:** Understanding elasticity helps predict how changes in price will affect sales volume.

**Applications of Price Elasticity of Supply:**
*   **Production Planning:** Businesses can better plan production capacity and inventory based on the responsiveness of supply to price changes.
*   **Government Policy (Subsidies):** Subsidies are more effective in increasing quantity supplied when supply is elastic.
*   **Market Stability:** Markets with elastic supply are generally more stable as producers can adjust output to meet demand fluctuations.

**Implications for Engineering Economics:**
*   Engineers involved in product development, manufacturing, and marketing need to understand elasticity to make sound economic decisions regarding pricing, production levels, and market entry.
*   For instance, an engineer designing a new product must estimate its price elasticity of demand to forecast potential revenue and profitability. (Relates to **CO1**, **CO2**)

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   Elasticity is a concept of responsiveness.
*   The sign of PED is typically negative (as price goes up, quantity demanded goes down), but economists often refer to the absolute value.
*   Elasticity can vary along a single demand or supply curve.

**Answer 7:**
*   $P_1 = 10$, $Q_{d1} = 100$
*   $P_2 = 8$, $Q_{d2} = 120$
*   $\Delta Q_d = 120 - 100 = 20$
*   $\Delta P = 8 - 10 = -2$
*   Midpoint of Price: $(10 + 8) / 2 = 9$
*   Midpoint of Quantity: $(100 + 120) / 2 = 110$
*   $PED = (\Delta Q_d / \text{Midpoint } Q_d) / (\Delta P / \text{Midpoint } P)$
*   $PED = (20 / 110) / (-2 / 9)$
*   $PED = (0.1818) / (-0.2222)$
*   $PED \approx -0.818$
    (Note: Often we look at the absolute value, which is 0.818, indicating inelastic demand.)

**Answer 8:**
b) Total revenue will increase. (Because demand is inelastic, a price increase leads to a proportionally smaller decrease in quantity demanded, thus increasing total revenue.)

**Answer 9:**
The supply curve typically slopes upward because, as the price of a good increases, producers are incentivized to supply more. This is due to the potential for higher profits and the law of increasing marginal costs (as production increases, costs per unit may rise, requiring a higher price to cover these costs).

Factors that can cause the supply curve to shift include:
*   Changes in the cost of inputs (raw materials, labor, energy).
*   Technological advancements.
*   Changes in government policies (taxes, subsidies, regulations).
*   Producer expectations about future prices.
*   Number of sellers in the market.
*   Prices of related goods in production.

**Answer 10:**
A technological breakthrough that reduces the cost of producing solar panels is a positive supply shock. This means the cost of production falls at every level of output.
*   **Effect on Supply:** The supply curve for solar panels will shift to the **right**.
*   **Effect on Equilibrium:**
    *   The **equilibrium price** of solar panels will **decrease**.
    *   The **equilibrium quantity** of solar panels sold will **increase**.
This is because at any given price, producers are now willing and able to supply more, and the lower price encourages more consumers to purchase solar panels.

## Key Points to Remember from Module 1

*   **Utility** measures satisfaction, and **Marginal Utility** is the satisfaction from the last unit consumed. The **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility** is central to understanding consumer behavior.
*   The **Law of Demand** describes an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, while the **Law of Supply** describes a direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.
*   The **equilibrium price and quantity** are determined by the intersection of demand and supply. Any deviation from equilibrium will naturally move the market back towards it.
*   **Shifts** in demand or supply curves, caused by factors other than the good's own price, change the equilibrium outcome.
*   **Elasticity** measures responsiveness. **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)** indicates how sensitive quantity demanded is to price changes, and **Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)** indicates how sensitive quantity supplied is to price changes.
*   Understanding elasticity is crucial for firms' pricing strategies and for governments formulating economic policies (like taxation).
*   These basic economic concepts are fundamental to making sound economic decisions in engineering and business contexts, directly supporting **CO1** by understanding laws of demand, supply, and elasticity.
