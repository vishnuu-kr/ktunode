# Engineering Economics: Module 2 - Cost Concepts and Short-Run Cost Curves

## Introduction

This module delves into the fundamental concepts of cost in engineering economics, crucial for informed decision-making in various engineering projects and business ventures. Understanding different types of costs and how they behave in the short run is essential for analyzing profitability, optimizing resource allocation, and achieving organizational goals.

**Alignment with Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO1 (K2):** This module lays the groundwork by introducing basic economic concepts related to costs, which are integral to understanding demand, supply, and production.
*   **CO2 (K3):** The core of this module focuses on cost concepts and their application in decision-making, particularly in understanding the functioning of firms in different market situations through short-run cost analysis.
*   **CO4 (K3):** Concepts like opportunity cost are directly applicable to cost-benefit analysis and capital budgeting decisions.

## 1. Fundamental Cost Concepts

### 1.1. Private Cost

**Definition:** Private cost refers to the cost incurred by an individual firm or producer in obtaining or producing a good or service. It represents the direct expenses borne by the decision-maker.

**Key Components:**

*   **Explicit Costs:** These are readily identifiable, out-of-pocket expenses that involve a monetary transaction.
    *   **Examples:** Wages paid to labor, cost of raw materials, rent for factory space, electricity bills, interest payments on loans, transportation expenses.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Generally discussed in the initial chapters of most managerial economics and engineering economics textbooks, including **Geetika, Piyali Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015)** and **Paneerselvam (2012)**.

*   **Implicit Costs:** These are the opportunity costs of using resources that the firm already owns or possesses. They represent the value of the next best alternative foregone. Implicit costs do not involve direct monetary payments but are crucial for accurate economic decision-making.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   The salary a business owner could have earned if they worked for another company.
        *   The return on capital invested in the business if it were invested elsewhere.
        *   The rental income a firm could have generated if it leased out its owned property.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Often discussed alongside explicit costs to highlight the difference between accounting profit and economic profit. **Geetika, Piyali Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015)** and **Thuesen, Fabrycky (1966)** would cover this distinction.

### 1.2. Social Cost

**Definition:** Social cost encompasses all the costs incurred by society as a result of a firm's production or consumption activities. It includes both private costs and any external costs imposed on third parties who are not directly involved in the transaction.

**Key Components:**

*   **Private Cost:** As defined above, these are the costs borne by the firm.
*   **External Cost (Externalities):** These are costs imposed on third parties that are not compensated by the producer or consumer. They can be negative (costs) or positive (benefits). In the context of social cost, we focus on negative externalities.
    *   **Examples of Negative Externalities:**
        *   **Pollution:** Air or water pollution from factories can lead to health problems and environmental degradation for the surrounding community.
        *   **Noise Pollution:** Factories operating with loud machinery can disturb residents.
        *   **Congestion:** Increased traffic caused by a firm's operations can lead to longer travel times and increased fuel consumption for others.
        *   **Resource Depletion:** Over-extraction of common resources (like water) by a firm can reduce availability for others.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** This concept is often introduced when discussing market failures and the role of government intervention. **Park (2001)** and **Khan M. Y. (2011)** (though focused on finance, might touch upon broader economic impacts) could discuss externalities.

**Formula:**
$$ \text{Social Cost} = \text{Private Cost} + \text{External Cost} $$

**Important Point to Remember:** Economic decisions should ideally consider social costs to ensure overall societal welfare, not just the private profitability of a firm.

### 1.3. Sunk Cost

**Definition:** A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. It is irrelevant to future decision-making because it will remain the same regardless of the choice made.

**Key Characteristics:**

*   **Irrecoverable:** Once spent, the money is gone.
*   **Irrelevant to Future Decisions:** Focusing on sunk costs can lead to the "sunk cost fallacy" or "escalation of commitment," where decisions are made to justify past expenditures rather than on future prospects.

**Examples:**

*   Money spent on a feasibility study for a project that is later abandoned.
*   Research and development expenses for a product that is never launched.
*   Specialized equipment purchased for a project that cannot be resold.
*   The initial investment in a marketing campaign for a product that is discontinued.

**Textbook Reference:** Found in chapters discussing decision-making and the irrelevance of past costs. **Blank P.E., Tarquin P. E. (7TH Edition)** and **Newman, Lavelle (2002)** would likely address this in the context of investment analysis.

**Important Point to Remember:** When making future decisions, ignore sunk costs. Focus only on future costs and benefits.

### 1.4. Opportunity Cost

**Definition:** Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative that must be foregone when a particular choice is made. It represents the benefits missed by choosing one option over another.

**Key Characteristics:**

*   **Foregone Benefits:** It's about what you give up.
*   **Crucial for Economic Decision-Making:** Implicit costs are a form of opportunity cost.

**Examples:**

*   If you have \$1000 and choose to invest it in stocks, the opportunity cost is the interest you could have earned if you had deposited it in a savings account or the potential profit from investing in bonds.
*   A company decides to use its own factory to produce a new product. The opportunity cost is the profit the company could have earned by renting out that factory to another business.
*   Attending a full-time engineering program means forgoing the salary you could have earned by working during that period.

**Formula:**
$$ \text{Opportunity Cost} = \text{Value of the best forgone alternative} $$

**Textbook Reference:** A fundamental concept in economics, covered extensively in **Geetika, Piyali Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015)**, **Paneerselvam (2012)**, and **Thuesen, Fabrycky (1966)**.
