## Topic: Power Amplifiers, Class C and D Power Amplifiers - Efficiency and Distortion

### **Introduction to Power Amplifiers**

Power amplifiers are circuits designed to amplify signals to a level sufficient to drive a load, such as a speaker, motor, or antenna. Unlike small-signal amplifiers, which focus on fidelity and gain, power amplifiers prioritize delivering power to the load. This often involves trade-offs between efficiency, distortion, and output power.

**Key Concept:** The primary function of a power amplifier is to deliver maximum power to a load with acceptable efficiency and distortion.

### **Class C Power Amplifiers**

**Description:**
Class C amplifiers are designed for **high efficiency** but at the expense of **significant distortion**. They are typically used in radio frequency (RF) applications where the output signal is often a narrow band and can be filtered to remove harmonics generated by the amplifier.

**Key Characteristics:**
*   **Conduction Angle:** The active device (transistor) conducts for **less than 180 degrees** of the input signal cycle. The conduction angle is deliberately kept small, often around 90-120 degrees.
*   **Bias:** The amplifier is biased **far below cutoff**. This means the input signal must have sufficient amplitude to drive the device into its active region.
*   **Output:** The output current flows only during a small portion of the input cycle, resulting in a pulsed waveform.
*   **Tuning:** Class C amplifiers usually incorporate a **tuned resonant circuit** (LC tank circuit) at the output. This circuit resonates at the desired fundamental frequency and filters out the harmonics produced by the pulsed output, effectively reconstructing a sinusoidal waveform.

**Efficiency:**
*   **High Theoretical Efficiency:** Class C amplifiers can achieve very high theoretical efficiencies, often **exceeding 75% and potentially reaching up to 90%**. This is because the active device is turned off for a significant portion of the input cycle, minimizing power dissipation within the device.
*   **Practical Efficiency:** Practical efficiencies are lower due to losses in the resonant circuit and imperfect switching.

**Distortion:**
*   **High Harmonic Distortion:** Due to the severely limited conduction angle and the pulsed output, Class C amplifiers introduce **significant harmonic distortion**. The output waveform is a distorted version of the input.
*   **Waveform Shape:** The output is a series of pulses. The tuned circuit is responsible for filtering these pulses into a near-sinusoidal output at the fundamental frequency.
*   **Suitability:** Not suitable for audio amplification or any application requiring high fidelity.

**Important Points to Remember about Class C:**
*   **High Efficiency, High Distortion.**
*   **Conduction angle < 180 degrees.**
*   **Biased below cutoff.**
*   **Requires a tuned output circuit for filtering.**
*   **Primarily used in RF transmitters.**

**Example Application:**
*   RF power amplifiers in radio transmitters for AM broadcasting or FM transmission.

**Referenced Content:**
*   **Sedra & Smith:** Discusses Class C operation in the context of harmonic distortion and efficiency, emphasizing the role of the tuned circuit.
*   **Bogart, Beasley, & Rico:** Explains the biasing and operation of Class C amplifiers, detailing why they are efficient but non-linear.

### **Class D Power Amplifiers**

**Description:**
Class D amplifiers are **switching amplifiers** that achieve extremely high efficiencies by operating the output transistors as electronic switches, either fully ON or fully OFF, rather than in their linear active region.

**Key Characteristics:**
*   **Switching Operation:** The transistors operate as switches. When ON, they have very low resistance, and when OFF, they have very high resistance. This minimizes power dissipation.
*   **Modulation Technique:** The input audio or analog signal is converted into a series of pulses whose width or density varies in proportion to the instantaneous amplitude of the input signal. Common modulation techniques include Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) and Pulse Density Modulation (PDM).
*   **Output Waveform:** The output is a high-frequency square wave or pulse train modulated by the input signal.
*   **Demodulation/Filtering:** A low-pass filter (usually an LC filter) is placed at the output to remove the high-frequency switching components and reconstruct the amplified analog signal.

**Efficiency:**
*   **Very High Theoretical and Practical Efficiency:** Class D amplifiers can achieve theoretical efficiencies of **up to 90-95% and even higher in practice**. This is because the transistors spend very little time in the transition region between ON and OFF, where most power dissipation occurs in linear amplifiers.
*   **Switching Losses:** The primary source of inefficiency is the switching loss, which occurs during the brief moments when the transistors are transitioning between ON and OFF states. This loss is dependent on the switching frequency and the characteristics of the transistors.

**Distortion:**
*   **Low Harmonic Distortion of the Audio Signal:** When designed properly with effective filtering, Class D amplifiers can achieve very low distortion of the desired audio signal.
*   **Switching Artifacts:** The primary source of "distortion" is not harmonic distortion of the audio but rather the presence of high-frequency switching noise. This noise needs to be effectively filtered out to prevent interference with the audio signal and electromagnetic interference (EMI).
*   **Modulation Scheme Dependency:** The quality and distortion of the output are highly dependent on the modulation scheme used (e.g., PWM, PDM) and the quality of the demodulating filter.

**Important Points to Remember about Class D:**
*   **Extremely High Efficiency.**
*   **Operates transistors as switches.**
*   **Uses Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) or similar techniques.**
*   **Requires a low-pass filter to reconstruct the output signal.**
*   **Low distortion of the audio signal if well-designed.**
*   **Potential for switching noise and EMI.**

**Example Application:**
*   High-fidelity audio amplifiers (home stereos, car audio).
*   Class D switching mode power supplies (SMPS).
*   Telecommunication systems.

### **Comparison of Class C and Class D Amplifiers**

| Feature             | Class C Amplifier                                    | Class D Amplifier                                        |
| :------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Primary Goal**    | High Efficiency                                      | Very High Efficiency                                     |
| **Conduction Angle**| Less than 180 degrees (small)                        | 360 degrees (always ON or OFF, switching)                |
| **Bias**            | Biased below cutoff                                  | No DC bias; driven by modulated switching signals        |
| **Output Waveform** | Pulsed (requires tuned circuit for sinusoidal output) | High-frequency square/pulse wave (requires low-pass filter)|
| **Efficiency**      | High (up to ~90%)                                    | Very High (up to ~95%+)                                  |
| **Distortion**      | High harmonic distortion of the signal               | Low signal distortion, potential for switching noise     |
| **Application**     | RF transmitters                                      | Audio amplifiers, SMPS                                   |
| **Complexity**      | Tuned circuit design                                 | Modulation and filter design, fast switching devices     |

### **Practice Questions/Exercises**

1.  **What is the primary characteristic that defines a Class C amplifier?**
    *   **Answer:** The active device conducts for less than 180 degrees of the input signal cycle, and it is biased far below cutoff.

2.  **Which type of power amplifier is known for its extremely high efficiency and operates its transistors as switches?**
    *   **Answer:** Class D amplifier.

3.  **Explain why a Class C amplifier requires a tuned resonant circuit at its output.**
    *   **Answer:** The output of a Class C amplifier is a series of pulses, which contain significant harmonics. The tuned circuit resonates at the fundamental frequency of the input signal and filters out these harmonics, reconstructing a nearly sinusoidal output waveform.

4.  **What is the main type of distortion associated with Class C amplifiers, and why is it generally not suitable for audio applications?**
    *   **Answer:** Class C amplifiers introduce high harmonic distortion because the device only conducts for a small portion of the input cycle, significantly altering the waveform shape. This makes them unsuitable for high-fidelity audio reproduction.

5.  **Describe the fundamental principle behind the high efficiency of a Class D amplifier.**
    *   **Answer:** Class D amplifiers achieve high efficiency by operating their output transistors as electronic switches. This means the transistors are either fully ON (low resistance, low voltage drop, minimal power dissipation) or fully OFF (high resistance, no current flow, minimal power dissipation), spending very little time in the linear region where power dissipation is significant.

6.  **What additional component is crucial for a Class D amplifier to produce a clean output signal?**
    *   **Answer:** A low-pass filter (typically an LC filter) is needed to remove the high-frequency switching components and reconstruct the amplified analog signal.

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **Efficiency vs. Fidelity:** Power amplifier classes represent a trade-off between how efficiently they convert DC power to AC power and how accurately they reproduce the input signal.
*   **Class C:** Best for RF applications where fidelity is less critical but efficiency is paramount, and a tuned circuit is acceptable.
*   **Class D:** Revolutionary for high-fidelity audio and power supplies due to its exceptional efficiency, but requires careful design to manage switching noise.
*   **Output Devices:** While the notes focus on the classification and characteristics, the actual implementation of these classes relies on the proper selection and configuration of output devices (BJTs, MOSFETs, IGBTs) capable of handling the required power and switching speeds.
