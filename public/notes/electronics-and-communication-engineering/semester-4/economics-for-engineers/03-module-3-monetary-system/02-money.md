# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS: Module 3: Monetary System - Topic: Money

## 1. Introduction to Money

### 1.1. What is Money?

*   **Definition:** Money is anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange for goods and services. It is a tool that facilitates transactions and overcomes the inefficiencies of barter.
*   **Historical Context:** Before money, societies relied on **barter**, the direct exchange of goods and services. Barter systems suffer from the **double coincidence of wants**, meaning both parties must have what the other desires for an exchange to occur. This is a significant inefficiency.
*   **Evolution of Money:** Money has evolved through various forms:
    *   **Commodity Money:** Objects with intrinsic value (e.g., gold, silver, salt, shells).
    *   **Fiat Money:** Money declared by a government to be legal tender, but without intrinsic value (e.g., paper currency, coins). Its value is based on trust and government decree.
    *   **Bank Money/Deposit Money:** Funds held in bank accounts that can be transferred via checks or electronic transfers.

**(Reference: Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury, Chapter 11: Money and Banking)**

### 1.2. Functions of Money

Money serves several crucial functions in an economy:

*   **Medium of Exchange:** The most fundamental function. Money is accepted by everyone as payment for goods and services. This eliminates the need for the double coincidence of wants.
    *   *Example:* You use money to buy groceries instead of trading your engineering skills for produce.
*   **Unit of Account (Measure of Value):** Money provides a common measure to value different goods and services. It allows us to compare the worth of diverse items and keeps track of economic activity.
    *   *Example:* A laptop costs $1000, and a car costs $20,000. We understand the relative value of these items.
*   **Store of Value:** Money can be saved and used later. It allows individuals to transfer purchasing power from the present to the future. However, inflation can erode the purchasing power of money over time.
    *   *Example:* You save $500 from your salary to buy a new tool next month.
*   **Standard of Deferred Payment:** Money can be used to settle debts and future payments. This is essential for credit transactions and long-term contracts.
    *   *Example:* A loan agreement specifies repayment in a certain amount of money over time.

**(Reference: Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury, Chapter 11: Money and Banking; Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam, Chapter 12: Money and Capital)**

## 2. Characteristics of Good Money

For a commodity to serve effectively as money, it should possess certain characteristics:

*   **Durability:** It should be able to withstand repeated use without significant deterioration.
*   **Portability:** It should be easy to carry and transport.
*   **Divisibility:** It should be easily divisible into smaller units without losing value.
*   **Uniformity (Homogeneity):** Each unit of money should be identical in value and quality.
*   **Scarcity:** Its supply should be limited to maintain its value. Too much money can lead to inflation.
*   **Acceptability:** It must be generally accepted by people as a medium of exchange.
*   **Recognizability:** It should be easily identifiable and distinguishable from counterfeit.

**(Reference: Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury, Chapter 11: Money and Banking)**

## 3. Supply of Money

### 3.1. Definition of Money Supply

*   **Money Supply:** The total stock of monetary assets available in an economy at a specific point in time. It includes currency in circulation and various types of deposits held by the public in banks.

### 3.2. Measures of Money Supply

Central banks typically define different measures of money supply to capture the liquidity in the economy. These measures vary across countries, but common definitions include:

*   **M0 (Monetary Base):** The most narrow measure, consisting of currency in circulation plus banks' reserves held at the central bank.
*   **M1:** Includes currency in circulation held by the public + demand deposits with the banking system + other checkable deposits. This is considered highly liquid.
*   **M2:** Includes M1 + savings deposits + small-time deposits (e.g., certificates of deposit) + money market mutual fund balances. This is less liquid than M1.
*   **M3:** Includes M2 + large-time deposits + institutional money market funds + other larger liquid assets. This is a broader measure.

**(Note:** The specific definitions and symbols (M0, M1, M2, M3) can vary slightly depending on the central bank, e.g., Reserve Bank of India (RBI) uses M0, M1, M2, M3, and M4. For engineering economics, understanding that there are different measures of money reflecting varying degrees of liquidity is key.)

**(Reference: Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury, Chapter 11: Money and Banking; Indian Financial System by Khan M. Y., Chapter 4: Money Market)**

## 4. Demand for Money

### 4.1. Theories of Money Demand

Economists have developed theories to explain why individuals and firms hold money:

*   **Classical/Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher's Equation of Exchange):**
    *   **Equation:** $M \times V = P \times T$
        *   $M$: Quantity of Money
        *   $V$: Velocity of Money (the average number of times a unit of money is spent)
        *   $P$: Price Level
        *   $T$: Volume of Transactions (or Real Output/Income, $Y$, in simpler versions)
    *   **Key Idea:** The price level is directly proportional to the quantity of money, assuming $V$ and $T$ are relatively stable. This implies that an increase in money supply leads to a proportional increase in prices (inflation).
*   **Keynesian Theory of Money Demand (Liquidity Preference Theory):**
    *   **Key Idea:** People hold money for three main motives:
        *   **Transactions Motive:** To carry out day-to-day transactions. This demand depends positively on income.
        *   **Precautionary Motive:** To meet unforeseen contingencies and emergencies. This demand also depends positively on income and potentially negatively on interest rates (as holding money instead of interest-earning assets has an opportunity cost).
        *   **Speculative Motive:** To profit from expected changes in asset prices, particularly bonds. If interest rates are expected to rise (bond prices to fall), people will hold more money. This demand depends negatively on the current interest rate.
    *   **Liquidity Trap:** A situation where interest rates are so low that people are indifferent between holding money and bonds, and monetary policy becomes ineffective in stimulating the economy.

**(Reference: Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury, Chapter 11: Money and Banking; Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam, Chapter 12: Money and Capital)**

## 5. Inflation and its Impact

### 5.1. What is Inflation?

*   **Definition:** Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time. When the general price level rises, each unit of currency buys fewer goods and services; consequently, inflation reflects a reduction in the purchasing power per unit of money.

### 5.2. Causes of Inflation

*   **Demand-Pull Inflation:** Occurs when there is an increase in aggregate demand without a corresponding increase in aggregate supply. "Too much money chasing too few goods."
    *   *Example:* Government spending increases, leading to more disposable income, which boosts consumer spending.
*   **Cost-Push Inflation:** Occurs due to an increase in the costs of production, such as wages, raw materials, or energy prices. These increased costs are then passed on to consumers in the form of higher prices.
    *   *Example:* A sudden surge in oil prices increases transportation and production costs across many industries.
*   **Built-in Inflation:** Often arises from adaptive expectations, where workers and firms expect current inflation rates to continue and build these expectations into future wage and price decisions.

### 5.3. Effects of Inflation

*   **Redistribution of Income and Wealth:**
    *   **Debtors benefit:** They repay loans with money that is worth less than when they borrowed it.
    *   **Creditors lose:** They receive payments that have less purchasing power.
    *   **Fixed-income earners suffer:** Their real income decreases.
*   **Impact on Savings and Investment:** High inflation can discourage saving as the real return on savings diminishes. It can also create uncertainty, making long-term investment planning difficult.
*   **Impact on Exports and Imports:**
    *   **Exports become more expensive:** Making them less competitive in international markets.
    *   **Imports become cheaper:** Leading to a potential increase in imports and a trade deficit.
*   **Menu Costs:** The costs incurred by businesses when they have to change their listed prices (e.g., reprinting menus, updating price tags).
*   **Shoe-leather Costs:** The costs associated with people trying to minimize their cash holdings by frequently going to the bank to withdraw money, as inflation erodes the value of cash.

**(Reference: Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury, Chapter 11: Money and Banking; Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam, Chapter 12: Money and Capital)**

## 6. Monetary Policy and its Tools

### 6.1. What is Monetary Policy?

*   **Definition:** Monetary policy refers to the actions undertaken by a central bank to manipulate the money supply and credit conditions to stimulate or restrain economic activity. Its primary goals are typically price stability, full employment, and moderate long-term interest rates.

### 6.2. Objectives of Monetary Policy

*   **Price Stability:** Controlling inflation and maintaining a stable price level.
*   **Full Employment:** Promoting a high level of employment.
*   **Economic Growth:** Encouraging sustainable economic expansion.
*   **Exchange Rate Stability:** Managing the nation's currency exchange rate.

### 6.3. Tools of Monetary Policy

Central banks use various tools to influence the money supply and interest rates:

*   **Open Market Operations (OMOs):** The buying and selling of government securities (bonds) by the central bank in the open market.
    *   **Buying Securities:** Injects money into the economy, increasing the money supply and lowering interest rates (expansionary policy).
    *   **Selling Securities:** Withdraws money from the economy, decreasing the money supply and raising interest rates (contractionary policy).
    *   *Example:* If the central bank wants to combat inflation, it will sell government bonds. Banks buy these bonds, reducing their cash reserves, which limits their ability to lend and thus reduces the money supply.
*   **Reserve Requirements:** The fraction of deposits that banks are required to hold in reserve, either as cash in their vaults or as deposits with the central bank.
    *   **Lowering Reserve Requirements:** Allows banks to lend out a larger portion of their deposits, increasing the money supply and stimulating the economy (expansionary).
    *   **Raising Reserve Requirements:** Forces banks to hold more reserves, reducing their lending capacity and contracting the money supply (contractionary).
*   **Discount Rate/Bank Rate:** The interest rate at which commercial banks can borrow money directly from the central bank.
    *   **Lowering the Discount Rate:** Makes it cheaper for banks to borrow, encouraging them to lend more and increasing the money supply (expansionary).
    *   **Raising the Discount Rate:** Makes it more expensive for banks to borrow, discouraging lending and reducing the money supply (contractionary).
*   **Moral Suasion:** The central bank may use advice, persuasion, or appeals to the self-interest of banks to influence their lending and investment activities.

**(Reference: Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury, Chapter 11: Money and Banking; Indian Financial System by Khan M. Y., Chapter 4: Money Market)**

## 7. Money in Engineering Economics

While the core concepts of money are economic, understanding them is vital for engineers involved in decision-making, especially in project evaluation and investment appraisal.

*   **Time Value of Money:** The fundamental principle in engineering economy states that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow due to its potential earning capacity. This is directly linked to the concept of interest rates, which are influenced by monetary policy. (See Module 2: Time Value of Money for details).
*   **Inflation and Cost Estimation:** Engineers must account for inflation when estimating future costs of projects. Inflation erodes the purchasing power of future revenues and increases the cost of materials and labor. This impacts the real value of cash flows.
    *   *Example:* When planning a factory construction project that will take five years, an engineer must adjust the estimated cost of materials and labor for expected inflation over those five years.
*   **Interest Rates and Project Viability:** Monetary policy directly influences interest rates. Higher interest rates increase the cost of borrowing for capital projects, making fewer projects economically viable. Conversely, lower interest rates can make more projects attractive.
    *   *Example:* A company considering a new product development needs to borrow capital. The prevailing interest rate, influenced by the central bank's monetary policy, will significantly affect the project's net present value (NPV) and internal rate of return (IRR).
*   **Purchasing Power Parity:** While more relevant in international finance, the concept of purchasing power parity relates to how exchange rates should adjust to equalize the purchasing power of different currencies. This is indirectly influenced by inflation rates in different countries.

**(Reference: Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky, Chapter 1: Introduction; Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam, Chapter 12: Money and Capital; Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park, Chapter 1: Introduction to Engineering Economics)**

## Practice Questions and Answers:

**Question 1:**
What are the four primary functions of money? Briefly explain each.

**Answer:**
1.  **Medium of Exchange:** Facilitates transactions, eliminating the need for barter.
2.  **Unit of Account:** Provides a common measure of value for goods and services.
3.  **Store of Value:** Allows purchasing power to be saved for future use.
4.  **Standard of Deferred Payment:** Enables the settlement of debts and future payments.

**Question 2:**
If a central bank wants to reduce inflation, which of the following actions would it likely take, and why?
a) Buy government bonds in the open market.
b) Lower the reserve requirement.
c) Sell government bonds in the open market.
d) Lower the discount rate.

**Answer:**
c) Sell government bonds in the open market.

**Explanation:** Selling government bonds withdraws money from the economy, reducing the money supply. This typically leads to higher interest rates, which discourages borrowing and spending, thereby cooling down aggregate demand and reducing inflationary pressures.

**Question 3:**
An engineer is planning a project with significant costs expected in five years. How would an increase in the expected inflation rate affect the real cost of those future expenses?

**Answer:**
An increase in the expected inflation rate would mean that the purchasing power of money decreases over time. Therefore, if the nominal cost of expenses remains the same, the *real cost* (adjusted for inflation) of those future expenses would actually decrease. However, typically, nominal costs are also expected to rise with inflation. If nominal costs rise faster than the discount rate, the real cost could be higher or lower depending on the relative rates. A higher inflation rate generally increases the uncertainty and can lead to higher nominal interest rates, which in turn can increase the present value of future costs. However, strictly speaking about the *purchasing power of future expenses*, higher inflation means fewer goods and services can be bought with the same nominal amount. If the engineer estimates higher nominal costs due to inflation, then the real cost might still be high. The key is that inflation erodes the value of money.

**Question 4:**
According to Keynes's Liquidity Preference Theory, what are the three motives for holding money?

**Answer:**
The three motives are:
1.  **Transactions Motive:** For everyday purchases.
2.  **Precautionary Motive:** For unexpected expenses.
3.  **Speculative Motive:** To profit from anticipated changes in asset prices (like bonds).

## Important Points to Remember:

*   Money is defined by its general acceptance as a medium of exchange, not by its intrinsic value.
*   The functions of money (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value, standard of deferred payment) are crucial for economic efficiency.
*   Inflation is a general increase in price levels, reducing purchasing power. It can be caused by demand-pull or cost-push factors.
*   Monetary policy tools (OMOs, reserve requirements, discount rate) are used by central banks to control the money supply and influence interest rates.
*   For engineers, understanding money, inflation, and interest rates is vital for accurate financial planning, project evaluation, and investment decisions, particularly concerning the time value of money and the impact of monetary policy on project viability.
*   The Quantity Theory of Money ($MV=PT$) provides a simplified relationship between money supply, velocity, prices, and transactions.
*   Keynesian theory adds the motives for holding money, highlighting the role of interest rates in the demand for money.
