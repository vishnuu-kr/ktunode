# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts

## Topic: Sunk Cost

### 1. Introduction to Sunk Costs

A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. It is irrelevant to future decisions because it will remain the same regardless of the choice made. In engineering economics, understanding sunk costs is crucial for making rational decisions, as they should not influence our choices about future actions.

**Key Concept:** Sunk costs are historical costs.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This topic directly contributes to **CO2** by helping engineers develop decision-making capabilities by differentiating between relevant and irrelevant costs. It also indirectly supports **CO4** by laying the groundwork for understanding cost-benefit analysis, where only future costs and benefits are considered.

**Knowledge Level Alignment (CO2):** K3 (Applying concepts relating to costs)

### 2. Definition of Sunk Cost

**Sunk Cost:** A cost that has already been paid and cannot be retrieved, regardless of future actions or decisions.

**Synonyms/Related Terms:** Historical cost, irrecoverable cost.

**Characteristics:**
*   Already incurred.
*   Unrecoverable.
*   Independent of future decisions.
*   Should be ignored in future decision-making.

### 3. Why Sunk Costs Are Irrelevant to Future Decisions

The core principle in economics and engineering decision-making is to focus on **incremental costs and benefits**. Sunk costs, by definition, do not change with any future course of action. Therefore, they do not offer any differential advantage or disadvantage between alternative choices.

Consider two options: Option A and Option B. If a certain cost has been incurred irrespective of whether you choose A or B, then this cost will not affect the *difference* in outcomes between A and B. Rational decision-making involves maximizing the net benefit (benefits minus costs), and sunk costs do not alter the net benefit of one option over another.

**Example:**

Imagine you bought a non-refundable ticket for a concert for $100. On the day of the concert, you are feeling unwell and have to choose between going to the concert or staying home to rest.

*   **Sunk Cost:** The $100 for the ticket. You have already spent it and cannot get it back whether you go to the concert or not.
*   **Future Costs/Benefits:**
    *   **Going to the concert:** You incur the cost of transportation ($20), and you might get enjoyment (benefit). However, you might also worsen your health.
    *   **Staying home:** You save the transportation cost ($20), and you get the benefit of resting and recovering.

The decision should be based on whether the benefit of going to the concert (enjoyment minus potential health deterioration) outweighs the cost of going (transportation), considering the alternative of staying home. The $100 for the ticket is irrelevant. If you stayed home, you'd have $120 ($100 ticket + $20 transportation saved). If you went, you'd have spent $120 ($100 ticket + $20 transportation) and would have the concert enjoyment but potentially poorer health. The $100 does not change the *difference* in your financial or well-being outcome between going and staying.

**Textbook Reference Integration (Thuesen & Fabrycky):** Thuesen and Fabrycky likely emphasize that focusing on sunk costs leads to the "sunk cost fallacy," where individuals continue to invest in a failing project simply because they have already invested heavily in it.

**Course Outcome Alignment (CO2):** This example demonstrates applying cost concepts to make a decision. Engineers need to recognize that past expenditures do not justify future expenditures if those future expenditures are not economically beneficial on their own.

### 4. Examples of Sunk Costs in Engineering

Sunk costs can arise in various engineering contexts:

*   **Research and Development (R&D) Costs:** Money spent on developing a new product or technology that is ultimately not commercialized.
*   **Feasibility Study Costs:** Expenses incurred for conducting studies to determine the viability of a project. If the project is abandoned, these costs are sunk.
*   **Acquisition of Specialized Equipment:** If a piece of equipment is purchased for a specific project that is later canceled, the cost of the equipment becomes a sunk cost if it has no alternative use or resale value.
*   **Training Costs:** Money spent training personnel for a specific task or project that is subsequently discontinued.
*   **Initial Marketing and Setup Costs:** Expenses incurred before a product or service is launched. If the launch is canceled or significantly altered, these initial costs are sunk.

**Example:**

A manufacturing company has spent \$500,000 on designing and tooling for a new car model. Due to a sudden shift in market demand towards electric vehicles, the company is considering abandoning this gasoline-powered car project and focusing on developing an electric vehicle instead.

*   **Sunk Cost:** The \$500,000 already spent on design and tooling. This money is gone regardless of whether they continue with the gasoline car or switch to the electric car.
*   **Decision Point:** The company needs to decide whether to continue with the gasoline car project or switch to the electric car project.
*   **Relevant Factors:** The potential profitability of the gasoline car (considering future production, marketing, and sales costs versus projected revenue) versus the potential profitability of the electric car (considering its development, production, marketing, and sales costs versus its projected revenue).
*   **Irrelevant Factor:** The \$500,000 spent. It should not influence the decision of which project is more likely to be profitable going forward. If the electric car project offers a better return on future investment, it should be pursued, even if it means abandoning the gasoline car and writing off the \$500,000.

**Textbook Reference Integration (Paneerselvam):** Paneerselvam would likely use such examples to illustrate how sunk costs can lead to poor investment decisions if they are not properly identified and excluded from the analysis.

**Course Outcome Alignment (CO4):** This scenario relates to capital budgeting decisions and cost-benefit analysis. An engineer needs to assess the future costs and benefits of each option, not get bogged down by past investments.

### 5. The Sunk Cost Fallacy (Concorde Fallacy)

The **sunk cost fallacy** (also known as the Concorde fallacy) is the psychological tendency to continue an endeavor as a result of previously invested resources (time, money, or effort), even when it is clear that continuing is not the best decision.

**Why it's a fallacy:** It leads to throwing "good money after bad." Instead of making a decision based on future prospects, individuals or organizations get emotionally attached to their past investments and continue to pour resources into a failing venture.

**The Concorde Example:** The supersonic passenger jet Concorde was a massive project with huge R&D costs. Despite ongoing technical problems and a lack of commercial viability, the British and French governments continued to fund it because they had already invested so much. This is a classic example of the sunk cost fallacy.

**Important Point to Remember:** Decisions should be based on **opportunity costs** and **future expected outcomes**, not on past expenditures.

**Reference Book Integration (Khan M. Y. - Indian Financial System):** While Khan's book focuses on the financial system, discussions on investment appraisal and project finance will implicitly touch upon the importance of evaluating projects based on their future cash flows, thereby excluding sunk costs.

**Course Outcome Alignment (CO2):** Recognizing and avoiding the sunk cost fallacy is fundamental to developing sound decision-making capabilities in the face of uncertainty and past commitments.

### 6. Decision-Making Framework: Ignoring Sunk Costs

When faced with a decision where sunk costs are present, follow these steps:

1.  **Identify all costs** associated with each alternative.
2.  **Classify costs** as sunk (already incurred, unrecoverable) or relevant (future costs that differ between alternatives).
3.  **Ignore sunk costs** in your analysis.
4.  **Compare the relevant costs and benefits** of each alternative.
5.  **Choose the alternative** that offers the greatest net benefit (or least net cost).

**Example:**

A company has invested \$1 million in developing a new software product. They are now halfway through the development.

*   **Scenario 1:** Continue with the project.
    *   Future development costs: \$2 million
    *   Expected revenue: \$3 million
    *   Net profit from this point: \$3 million - \$2 million = \$1 million

*   **Scenario 2:** Abandon the project and develop a different software.
    *   Investment in new software: \$1.5 million
    *   Expected revenue: \$2.5 million
    *   Net profit from this point: \$2.5 million - \$1.5 million = \$1 million

In this simplified example, both scenarios yield the same future profit of \$1 million. The initial \$1 million spent is a sunk cost and is irrelevant to the decision. The company should choose based on which project has a higher probability of success or other strategic advantages.

Now, let's change the revenue for Scenario 1:
*   **Scenario 1 (Revised):** Continue with the project.
    *   Future development costs: \$2 million
    *   Expected revenue: \$2.5 million
    *   Net profit from this point: \$2.5 million - \$2 million = \$0.5 million

*   **Scenario 2:** Abandon the project and develop a different software.
    *   Investment in new software: \$1.5 million
    *   Expected revenue: \$2.5 million
    *   Net profit from this point: \$2.5 million - \$1.5 million = \$1 million

In this revised case, abandoning the first project and starting the second one is more profitable by \$0.5 million (\$1 million vs. \$0.5 million). The initial \$1 million spent on the first project should not influence the decision to continue if the second project offers a better future return.

**Textbook Reference Integration (Park - Contemporary Engineering Economics):** Park's book would likely present similar comparative analyses, emphasizing the exclusion of sunk costs for optimal decision-making.

**Course Outcome Alignment (CO4):** This framework directly aids in solving business problems using cost-benefit analysis. Engineers must be able to dissect a problem to identify which costs matter for future choices.

### 7. Importance in Project Management and Investment Decisions

**Project Management:**
*   **Go/No-Go Decisions:** When a project faces significant challenges, a rational decision can be made only by evaluating future costs and benefits, not by dwelling on past investments.
*   **Resource Allocation:** Resources should be allocated to projects that promise the best future returns, irrespective of initial investments made in other potentially less promising ventures.

**Investment Decisions:**
*   **Capital Budgeting:** When evaluating potential investments, only future cash flows are considered. Past expenditures are irrelevant for determining the Net Present Value (NPV) or Internal Rate of Return (IRR) of an investment.
*   **Evaluating Existing Assets:** If an asset was acquired at a high cost but its current market value is low, or it no longer serves its intended purpose efficiently, its original cost is a sunk cost. Decisions about replacing or retiring it should be based on its future utility and the costs/benefits of alternatives.

**Reference Book Integration (Blank & Tarquin - Engineering Economy):** Blank and Tarquin would strongly advocate for decision-making based on future costs and benefits, directly addressing the irrelevance of sunk costs in project evaluation.

**Course Outcome Alignment (CO2 & CO4):** This reinforces how cost concepts are applied in decision-making and how techniques like break-even analysis and capital budgeting rely on distinguishing between past and future costs.

### 8. Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

**Pitfall:** Emotional attachment to past investments.
**Avoidance:** Focus on the objective, future financial and operational implications of each choice. If possible, have an independent party review the decision.

**Pitfall:** Misinterpreting sunk costs as relevant.
**Avoidance:** Develop a clear mental model or checklist to identify and segregate sunk costs before making any analysis.

**Pitfall:** Justifying continued investment in a failing project with "we've already spent so much."
**Avoidance:** Frame the decision as "Should I spend *more* money on this project, or should I start fresh/redirect resources?" rather than "Should I continue because I've spent money?"

### 9. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A civil engineering firm has spent \$50,000 on designing a new bridge. Due to unexpected geological surveys, the cost of construction has significantly increased, making the project less profitable than initially anticipated. The firm must decide whether to proceed with the bridge construction or to invest the remaining funds in a new highway project.

*   Identify the sunk cost in this scenario.
*   Explain why this cost should be ignored when deciding whether to proceed with the bridge or the highway project.

**Answer:**
*   **Sunk Cost:** The \$50,000 already spent on the bridge design is the sunk cost.
*   **Reason for Ignoring:** This \$50,000 is gone regardless of whether the firm builds the bridge or the highway. The decision to proceed with the bridge or choose the highway should be based on the *future* costs, *future* benefits, and *future* profitability of each project. Continuing with the bridge solely because of the \$50,000 already spent would be an example of the sunk cost fallacy. The firm should compare the net present value of future cash flows for the bridge versus the highway and select the one that offers a better return on future investment.

**Question 2:**
You bought a \$200 non-refundable annual gym membership. After three months, you realize you are not using the gym regularly due to a demanding work schedule. You have two options:
A) Continue going to the gym sporadically, incurring \$5 per visit for transportation and workout.
B) Cancel your gym membership (which is not possible for a refund) and use the time for home workouts, incurring no additional cost.

Which option should you choose, and why?

**Answer:**
*   **Sunk Cost:** The \$200 for the annual gym membership is a sunk cost because it cannot be refunded.
*   **Decision Analysis:**
    *   **Option A (Continue Gym):**
        *   Sunk Cost: \$200 (irrelevant)
        *   Future Cost: \$5 per visit (transportation/workout) + your time.
        *   Benefit: Potential health improvement and enjoyment.
    *   **Option B (Home Workouts):**
        *   Sunk Cost: \$200 (irrelevant)
        *   Future Cost: \$0 (additional costs) + your time.
        *   Benefit: Potential health improvement and time saved.
*   **Choice:** You should choose Option B (home workouts) if the future benefits (health, time) of home workouts outweigh the future benefits (health, enjoyment) minus future costs (\$5 per visit) of going to the gym. The decision should not be influenced by the fact that you already paid \$200. If you were to continue going to the gym, you would still have spent the \$200 plus the additional \$5 per visit. By not going, you save the \$5 per visit and potentially your time.

**Question 3:**
A company invested \$1 million in research and development for a new product. They are now in the production planning phase. The estimated total cost for production, marketing, and sales is \$3 million, with projected revenues of \$3.5 million. However, a competitor has just announced a similar product that is expected to significantly reduce market share and potential revenue. The company estimates that with the new competitor, their projected revenue for their product will now only be \$2.5 million.

*   What is the sunk cost?
*   Should the company proceed with production based on the initial projected revenue or the revised projection considering the competitor? Explain your reasoning.

**Answer:**
*   **Sunk Cost:** The \$1 million invested in research and development is the sunk cost.
*   **Decision:** The company should make its decision based on the **revised projection** of \$2.5 million in revenue.
    *   **Analysis based on revised projection:**
        *   Sunk Cost (R&D): \$1 million (irrelevant)
        *   Future Costs (Production, Marketing, Sales): \$3 million
        *   Revised Future Revenue: \$2.5 million
        *   **Net Future Outcome:** \$2.5 million - \$3 million = -\$0.5 million (a loss of \$0.5 million)
    *   **Reasoning:** Since the revised projection shows a future loss of \$0.5 million, proceeding with production would be financially detrimental. The \$1 million already spent should not prevent the company from cutting its losses and avoiding further expenditure that will result in a net loss. They should abandon the project and evaluate other opportunities.

### 10. Summary and Key Takeaways

*   **Definition:** A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered.
*   **Irrelevance:** Sunk costs are irrelevant to future decisions because they do not change regardless of the choice made.
*   **Focus on the Future:** Rational decision-making requires focusing on incremental future costs and benefits.
*   **Sunk Cost Fallacy:** Avoid the trap of continuing an endeavor simply because of past investments (throwing good money after bad).
*   **Examples:** R&D costs, feasibility studies, initial setup costs, training costs for discontinued projects.
*   **Application:** Crucial in project evaluation, investment decisions, and resource allocation.

By consistently identifying and ignoring sunk costs, engineers can make more objective, economically sound decisions that lead to better outcomes.
