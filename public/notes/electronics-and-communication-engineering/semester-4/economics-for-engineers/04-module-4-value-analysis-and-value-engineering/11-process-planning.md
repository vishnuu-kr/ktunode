# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - MODULE 4: VALUE ANALYSIS AND VALUE ENGINEERING

## Topic: Process Planning

### 1. Introduction to Process Planning

Process planning is a critical aspect of manufacturing and production that translates a design into a manufacturable product. It involves determining the sequence of operations, the machines to be used, the tooling required, and the methods to be employed to transform raw materials into finished goods efficiently and economically. In the context of Value Analysis (VA) and Value Engineering (VE), process planning plays a pivotal role in identifying opportunities for cost reduction and performance improvement without compromising or enhancing the product's essential functions.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Design to Manufacturing Link:** Process planning bridges the gap between the engineering design and the actual manufacturing floor.
*   **Efficiency and Cost-Effectiveness:** The primary goal is to achieve the desired product with minimal waste of time, material, and resources.
*   **Functional Orientation:** Process planning should consider the functions the product is intended to perform and ensure that the chosen processes support these functions.
*   **Value Addition:** Every step in the process should ideally add value to the product, aligning with the core principles of VA/VE.

**References:**

*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** While not directly detailing process planning, their discussions on production and operations management (Chapter 9 in some editions) implicitly touch upon the efficiency and cost aspects relevant to process planning.
*   **Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam (PHI, 2012):** Chapters on production planning and control, and manufacturing costs, are highly relevant. Paneerselvam emphasizes the economic justification of different manufacturing processes.
*   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park (Prentice Hall of India Ltd, 2001):** Park's work often integrates economic considerations into engineering design and manufacturing, making his discussions on production economics and cost estimation valuable for understanding the economic underpinnings of process planning.

### 2. Role of Process Planning in Value Analysis and Value Engineering

Process planning is not just about how to make a product; it's about *how to make it best*. Within VA/VE, process planning is a fertile ground for generating value-adding ideas.

**Key Connections:**

*   **Identifying Non-Value-Adding Steps:** A well-defined process plan allows for the scrutiny of each step to determine if it contributes to the product's function or customer's need. Steps that don't add value are prime targets for elimination or simplification.
*   **Selecting Economical Processes:** VA/VE encourages exploring alternative manufacturing processes that might be more cost-effective, faster, or require less specialized equipment, all while achieving the same or better functional outcomes.
*   **Optimizing Resource Utilization:** Efficient process planning ensures that machines, labor, and materials are used optimally, minimizing waste and reducing costs.
*   **Improving Quality and Reliability:** The choice of processes directly impacts product quality. VE often seeks processes that enhance durability, reduce defects, and improve overall performance.
*   **Simplifying Manufacturing:** Streamlining processes, reducing the number of operations, or using simpler techniques can significantly lower costs and improve throughput.

**Example:**

Consider the assembly of a simple electronic device.

*   **Traditional Process Plan:** May involve manual soldering for all connections, using multiple screws for casing assembly, and individual testing of each component.
*   **Value Engineered Process Plan:**
    *   **Soldering:** Explore the use of automated wave soldering or reflow soldering for higher volumes, reducing labor and inconsistency.
    *   **Casing Assembly:** Replace screws with snap-fits or ultrasonic welding, eliminating the need for tools and saving assembly time.
    *   **Testing:** Implement in-circuit testing or functional testing at a later stage to catch errors more efficiently.

This VE approach, driven by a re-evaluation of the process plan, leads to reduced assembly time, lower labor costs, and potentially higher quality.

### 3. Key Stages and Activities in Process Planning

Process planning typically involves a structured approach to defining the manufacturing pathway.

**Core Stages:**

1.  **Product Analysis:** Understanding the design, specifications, materials, and functional requirements of the product. This involves dissecting the Bill of Materials (BOM) and engineering drawings.
2.  **Operation Sequencing:** Determining the logical order of operations required to transform raw materials into the finished product.
3.  **Process Selection:** Choosing the most appropriate manufacturing processes (e.g., machining, casting, forging, welding, injection molding, 3D printing) for each operation.
4.  **Machine and Equipment Selection:** Identifying the specific machines, tools, and fixtures needed for each process.
5.  **Time Estimation:** Estimating the time required for each operation (setup time, processing time, auxiliary time). This is crucial for costing and scheduling.
6.  **Tooling and Fixture Design:** Specifying or designing the necessary tools, jigs, and fixtures to hold the workpiece and guide the cutting tools.
7.  **Quality Control Points:** Identifying where inspections and quality checks are necessary throughout the process.
8.  **Work Instructions and Documentation:** Creating detailed instructions for operators and documenting the entire process.

**VE/VA Integration in Stages:**

*   **Product Analysis:** Questioning the necessity of certain features or tolerances that might drive complex and costly processes.
*   **Operation Sequencing:** Can steps be combined? Can the sequence be rearranged for better flow?
*   **Process Selection:** Are there cheaper or more efficient alternative processes that achieve the same functional outcome? (e.g., switching from machining to stamping if tolerances allow).
*   **Machine Selection:** Can standard machines be used instead of specialized ones? Can automation be leveraged?
*   **Time Estimation:** Identifying bottlenecks and opportunities for reducing cycle times.
*   **Tooling:** Can simpler or standardized tooling be used?

**References:**

*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky (PHI, 1966):** Thuesen and Fabrycky's classic text provides foundational principles of engineering economy applied to manufacturing. Their chapters on production costs and capital investment for equipment are directly relevant to process selection and justification.
*   **Engineering Economics and analysis by Donald G. Newman, Jerome P. Lavelle (Engg. Press, Texas, 2002):** This book offers practical insights into the economic evaluation of engineering alternatives, including manufacturing processes. They likely cover methods for comparing the costs of different process plans.

### 4. Economic Considerations in Process Planning

The core of process planning from an economic perspective is to minimize total cost while meeting functional and quality requirements.

**Key Economic Factors:**

*   **Material Costs:** Choice of materials and methods of material utilization (e.g., minimizing scrap).
*   **Labor Costs:** Direct labor involved in operations and indirect labor for support.
*   **Machine and Equipment Costs:** Capital costs, depreciation, maintenance, and operating costs of machinery.
*   **Tooling Costs:** Design, manufacturing, and maintenance of tooling.
*   **Setup Costs:** Costs incurred to prepare a machine or line for a specific production run.
*   **Overhead Costs:** Indirect costs associated with production (utilities, supervision, etc.).
*   **Quality Costs:** Costs associated with inspection, rework, scrap, and warranty.
*   **Inventory Costs:** Costs related to holding raw materials, work-in-progress, and finished goods.
*   **Lead Time:** The time from order placement to product delivery, impacting customer satisfaction and inventory holding.

**VA/VE Impact:**

*   **Cost-Benefit Analysis:** Evaluating the economic viability of proposed process changes.
*   **Break-Even Analysis:** Determining the production volume at which a particular process or technology becomes profitable.
*   **Capital Budgeting Techniques (NPV, IRR, Payback Period):** Used to evaluate the economic feasibility of investing in new equipment or technologies for process improvement. (CO4)
*   **Make or Buy Decisions:** Evaluating whether to manufacture a component in-house or outsource it, which heavily depends on process capabilities and costs.

**Example (Break-Even Analysis for Process Selection):**

Suppose a company needs to produce 10,000 units of a part.

*   **Process A (Manual):** Higher variable cost per unit (labor intensive), lower fixed costs. Total Cost = $10 \times \text{units} + \$50,000$.
*   **Process B (Automated):** Lower variable cost per unit (machine driven), higher fixed costs (automation investment). Total Cost = $5 \times \text{units} + \$150,000$.

To find the break-even point, set Total Cost A = Total Cost B:
$10 \times \text{units} + \$50,000 = 5 \times \text{units} + \$150,000$
$5 \times \text{units} = \$100,000$
$\text{units} = 20,000$

This means if production is less than 20,000 units, Process A is cheaper. If it's more, Process B is cheaper. A VA/VE team would consider this when choosing a process for mass production.

This entire section directly supports CO4 by demonstrating how economic tools are applied within process planning for value improvement.

### 5. Value Engineering Opportunities in Process Planning

VE specifically aims to improve the value of a product or service by enhancing its functions while reducing its cost. Process planning is a prime area for VE initiatives.

**Key VE Strategies for Process Planning:**

1.  **Simplification:**
    *   Reducing the number of operations.
    *   Eliminating unnecessary steps or inspections.
    *   Using simpler joining methods (e.g., snap-fits instead of fasteners).
2.  **Standardization:**
    *   Using standard components and fasteners.
    *   Employing standard tooling and machinery.
    *   Adopting standard process parameters.
3.  **Combination of Operations:**
    *   Performing multiple operations on a single machine or setup (e.g., milling and drilling on a CNC machine).
    *   Integrating inspection within the manufacturing process.
4.  **Elimination of Processes:**
    *   Can a process be bypassed entirely by a design change?
    *   Can a material change eliminate the need for a specific processing step (e.g., using pre-finished materials)?
5.  **Use of Advanced Manufacturing Technologies:**
    *   Leveraging automation, robotics, or additive manufacturing (3D printing) to reduce labor, improve consistency, or enable complex geometries that simplify assembly.
6.  **Product Design Modifications (often linked):**
    *   While VE is often applied to existing products, the process planning phase is where the implications of design choices on manufacturability become clear. VE suggests feeding this information back to design. For example, a design that requires very tight tolerances might necessitate expensive grinding operations; VE might explore if slightly relaxed tolerances can be achieved with a cheaper milling process.

**Example (Simplification):**

A part requiring several drilling operations could be redesigned with features that allow for a single drilling operation or even eliminate the need for a hole altogether if the function can be served differently. This simplifies the process, reduces machine time, and lowers costs.

### 6. Practice Questions & Exercises

**Question 1:**
A company is deciding between two processes for manufacturing a component:
*   **Process X:** Fixed Costs = $100,000, Variable Cost per unit = $20
*   **Process Y:** Fixed Costs = $250,000, Variable Cost per unit = $12

If the expected production volume is 15,000 units, which process is more economical? Calculate the break-even volume between the two processes.

**Answer 1:**
*   **Cost of Process X:** $100,000 + (20 \times 15,000) = \$100,000 + \$300,000 = \$400,000$
*   **Cost of Process Y:** $250,000 + (12 \times 15,000) = \$250,000 + \$180,000 = \$430,000$
    Therefore, **Process X is more economical** for a production volume of 15,000 units.

*   **Break-Even Calculation:**
    Set Cost X = Cost Y
    $100,000 + 20V = 250,000 + 12V$
    $8V = 150,000$
    $V = 150,000 / 8 = 18,750$ units
    The break-even volume is 18,750 units.

**Question 2:**
During a Value Engineering study of an electronic device, a process planner identifies that the casing is assembled using 10 small screws. Each screw takes 30 seconds to insert and tighten. If the company produces 50,000 units per year, and labor cost is $15 per hour, calculate the annual savings if the casing could be redesigned for snap-fit assembly, eliminating the need for screws.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Time per unit for screw assembly:** 10 screws * 30 seconds/screw = 300 seconds = 5 minutes
*   **Total annual time for screw assembly:** 50,000 units * 5 minutes/unit = 250,000 minutes
*   **Total annual hours:** 250,000 minutes / 60 minutes/hour = 4166.67 hours
*   **Annual labor cost for screw assembly:** 4166.67 hours * $15/hour = $62,500.05
    If snap-fits eliminate this labor, the potential annual savings are approximately **$62,500**. (This assumes no additional cost for the snap-fit design itself, or that it's offset by other material/process savings).

**Question 3:**
A company is considering purchasing a new CNC machine for $200,000 that will reduce labor costs by $30,000 per year and material scrap by $10,000 per year. The machine has an expected life of 10 years with no salvage value. If the company requires a minimum rate of return of 12%, use the Net Present Worth (NPW) method to determine if this investment is financially viable. Assume an annual maintenance cost of $5,000.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Annual Net Savings:** (Labor Savings + Scrap Savings) - Maintenance Costs
    Annual Net Savings = ($30,000 + $10,000) - $5,000 = $35,000 per year.
*   **Calculate the Present Worth of the Savings:** Using the present worth factor for an annuity (P/A, i, n) at 12% for 10 years.
    From annuity tables, (P/A, 12%, 10) ≈ 5.6502
*   **Present Worth of Savings:** $35,000 * 5.6502 = \$197,757$
*   **Net Present Worth (NPW):** Present Worth of Savings - Initial Investment
    NPW = $197,757 - \$200,000 = -\$2,243$

Since the NPW is negative, the investment is **not financially viable** at a 12% required rate of return. This capital budgeting decision directly impacts process planning by influencing the choice of manufacturing technology.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Process planning is a crucial link between design and production, directly impacting product cost and quality.**
*   **Value Engineering actively seeks to improve processes by simplifying, standardizing, combining, or eliminating operations.**
*   **Economic analysis tools (break-even, cost-benefit, capital budgeting) are essential for justifying process changes and investments.**
*   **The goal is to select processes that are cost-effective, efficient, and meet the required functional and quality standards.**
*   **Consider the entire lifecycle cost of a process, not just the initial capital expenditure.**
*   **Always question existing processes: "Can it be done better, cheaper, or faster while maintaining or improving function?"**
*   **The choice of manufacturing process is heavily influenced by production volume.**

This comprehensive study of process planning within the context of Value Analysis and Value Engineering equips engineers with the knowledge to optimize manufacturing operations, reduce costs, and enhance product value.
