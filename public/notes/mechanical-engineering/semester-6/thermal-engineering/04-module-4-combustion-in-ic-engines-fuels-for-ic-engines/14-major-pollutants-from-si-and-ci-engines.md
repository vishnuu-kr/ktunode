# Thermal Engineering: Module 4 - Combustion in IC Engines: Fuels for IC Engines

## Topic: Major Pollutants from S.I. and C.I. Engines

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO4: Explain the combustion phenomenon and pollution in IC engines. (Knowledge Level: K2)** - This module directly addresses the types and sources of pollution in IC engines.

---

### 1. Introduction to Engine Pollutants

Internal Combustion (IC) engines, while crucial for transportation and power generation, are significant sources of air pollution. The combustion process, even with optimized fuels and engine designs, inevitably produces byproducts that impact the environment and human health. Understanding these pollutants, their formation mechanisms, and their effects is vital for developing cleaner engine technologies.

**Key Concept:** **Incomplete Combustion:** A major driver of pollutant formation in IC engines, occurring when there is insufficient oxygen or time for complete oxidation of fuel.

---

### 2. Major Pollutants from Spark Ignition (S.I.) Engines

S.I. engines, typically used in gasoline-powered vehicles, operate on a lean or stoichiometric air-fuel mixture. The combustion is initiated by a spark plug.

#### 2.1. Carbon Monoxide (CO)

*   **Formation:** CO is a product of incomplete combustion. It forms when there isn't enough oxygen to convert all the carbon in the fuel to carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$). This is more prevalent in rich air-fuel mixtures where oxygen is limited.

    *   **Reaction:** $\text{2C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{2CO}$
    *   **Reaction:** $\text{C} + \text{CO}_2 \rightarrow \text{2CO}$ (Boudouard reaction, can occur at high temperatures)

*   **Sources in S.I. Engines:**
    *   Rich air-fuel ratios.
    *   Cold starts where the mixture is deliberately enriched.
    *   Engine misfires.
    *   Low combustion temperatures and pressures.

*   **Effects:** CO is a toxic gas that reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood by binding to hemoglobin. It can cause dizziness, headaches, and in high concentrations, can be fatal.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Maintaining a lean air-fuel ratio.
    *   Use of catalytic converters.

*   **Reference:** Gupta (2018) highlights that CO emissions are a direct indicator of the richness of the mixture.

#### 2.2. Unburnt Hydrocarbons (HC) or Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs)

*   **Formation:** HC emissions occur due to incomplete combustion, similar to CO. They are essentially unburnt or partially burnt fuel molecules.

*   **Sources in S.I. Engines:**
    *   **Wall Quenching:** During combustion, a thin layer of fuel-air mixture near the cylinder walls is rapidly cooled, preventing complete combustion.
    *   **Crevice Flow:** Small amounts of mixture can get trapped in crevices (e.g., piston ring gaps, valve seats) and are not burned.
    *   **Incomplete Mixing:** Poor atomization and mixing of fuel and air.
    *   **Rich Mixture Operation:** Insufficient oxygen.
    *   **Low Cylinder Temperatures/Pressures:** Especially during cold starts.

*   **Effects:**
    *   Contribute to the formation of ground-level ozone (smog) in the presence of sunlight and nitrogen oxides.
    *   Some HCs are carcinogenic (e.g., benzene).
    *   Contribute to foul odors.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Leaner mixtures.
    *   Improved fuel injection and atomization.
    *   Catalytic converters.
    *   Optimized combustion chamber design.

*   **Reference:** Heywood (2011) extensively discusses the various mechanisms leading to HC emissions, including wall quenching and crevice effects.

#### 2.3. Nitrogen Oxides ($\text{NO}_x$)

*   **Formation:** $\text{NO}_x$ (primarily NO and $\text{NO}_2$) are formed at high temperatures when nitrogen and oxygen from the air react. The S.I. engine combustion temperatures can reach these critical levels.

    *   **Zeldovich Mechanism:** The primary mechanism for $\text{NO}_x$ formation.
        *   $\text{N}_2 + \text{O} \leftrightarrow \text{NO} + \text{N}$
        *   $\text{N} + \text{O}_2 \leftrightarrow \text{NO} + \text{O}$
        *   $\text{N} + \text{OH} \leftrightarrow \text{NO} + \text{H}$

*   **Sources in S.I. Engines:**
    *   High combustion temperatures (especially during high load and speed).
    *   Lean air-fuel ratios, which increase peak combustion temperatures.
    *   Advanced ignition timing.

*   **Effects:**
    *   Contribute to acid rain.
    *   Formation of smog.
    *   Irritation of respiratory systems.
    *   Can lead to the formation of carcinogenic nitrosamines.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR): Recirculating a portion of exhaust gas back into the intake reduces combustion temperatures.
    *   Lean Burn (LB) or Ultra-Lean Burn (ULB) strategies (though this can increase $\text{NO}_x$ if not managed).
    *   Three-way catalytic converters (efficiently reduce $\text{NO}_x$ to $\text{N}_2$).

*   **Reference:** Rajput (2010) emphasizes that $\text{NO}_x$ formation is highly temperature-dependent.

#### 2.4. Particulate Matter (PM) / Soot

*   **Formation:** Soot is formed through the incomplete combustion and pyrolytic decomposition of fuel at high temperatures, leading to the formation of complex carbonaceous particles.

    *   **Nucleation:** Formation of initial carbon nuclei.
    *   **Growth:** Aggregation and surface growth of these nuclei through acetylene (C2H2) addition and coagulation.

*   **Sources in S.I. Engines:**
    *   While C.I. engines are more notorious for soot, S.I. engines can produce soot, particularly under rich conditions, high loads, and with certain fuel types (e.g., direct injection gasoline engines).
    *   Incomplete combustion in crevices.

*   **Effects:**
    *   Respiratory and cardiovascular problems.
    *   Reduce visibility.
    *   Can act as carriers for other harmful pollutants.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Leaner air-fuel mixtures.
    *   Proper fuel atomization.
    *   Catalytic converters.
    *   Diesel Particulate Filters (DPFs) are more common for C.I. engines but the principle of filtration applies.

*   **Important Point to Remember:** Soot formation is a surface phenomenon and is highly dependent on temperature and fuel-air mixing.

#### 2.5. Aldehydes

*   **Formation:** Aldehydes (e.g., formaldehyde, acetaldehyde) are formed by the partial oxidation of unburnt hydrocarbons.

*   **Sources in S.I. Engines:**
    *   Incomplete combustion.
    *   High temperature oxidation of fuel fragments.

*   **Effects:**
    *   Eye and respiratory irritants.
    *   Contribute to smog.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Three-way catalytic converters are very effective in oxidizing aldehydes.

### 3. Major Pollutants from Compression Ignition (C.I.) Engines

C.I. engines, typically used in diesel engines, operate with a lean air-fuel mixture and auto-ignition of fuel due to high compression temperatures.

#### 3.1. Particulate Matter (PM) / Soot

*   **Formation:** This is a primary pollutant from diesel engines. Soot formation in diesel engines occurs in diffusion flames where fuel and air mix locally, leading to rich zones and high temperatures, promoting pyrolysis.

    *   **Process:**
        1.  **Fuel Vaporization and Pyrolysis:** Liquid fuel evaporates and breaks down into smaller hydrocarbon radicals.
        2.  **Incubation/Nucleation:** Formation of aromatic ring structures, leading to initial soot nuclei.
        3.  **Surface Growth:** Addition of hydrocarbon species to the nuclei, forming larger particles.
        4.  **Coagulation:** Collision and merging of soot particles.
        5.  **Oxidation:** Combustion of soot particles by oxygen.

*   **Sources in C.I. Engines:**
    *   Local rich zones in the diffusion flame.
    *   Inadequate mixing of fuel and air.
    *   High combustion pressures and temperatures.
    *   Lower cetane number fuels can lead to more soot.

*   **Effects:**
    *   Serious respiratory and cardiovascular health issues.
    *   Contribute to climate change.
    *   Reduced visibility.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   **Diesel Particulate Filters (DPFs):** Physical filters that trap soot. Require periodic regeneration (burning off trapped soot).
    *   **Optimized Injection Strategies:** Pilot injections, multiple injections to improve mixing.
    *   **Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR):** Reduces combustion temperatures, which can reduce soot formation but may increase PM due to incomplete combustion.
    *   **Leaner Operation:** Generally leads to lower PM, but can increase $\text{NO}_x$.
    *   **Fuel Additives.**
    *   **Water Injection/Emulsification.**

*   **Reference:** Ganesan (2002) provides a detailed explanation of soot formation mechanisms in diesel engines. Rathore (2010) also covers mitigation strategies like DPFs.

#### 3.2. Nitrogen Oxides ($\text{NO}_x$)

*   **Formation:** Similar to S.I. engines, $\text{NO}_x$ is formed by the reaction of nitrogen and oxygen at high temperatures, as described by the Zeldovich mechanism. Diesel engines, with their high compression ratios and lean operation, often achieve high combustion temperatures, leading to significant $\text{NO}_x$ formation.

*   **Sources in C.I. Engines:**
    *   High combustion temperatures (peak temperatures are generally higher in diesel engines than in S.I. engines under similar operating conditions due to lean operation).
    *   Lean air-fuel ratios, which promote higher peak temperatures.
    *   High compression ratios.

*   **Effects:** Same as in S.I. engines (acid rain, smog, respiratory issues).

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   **Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR):** Crucial for reducing $\text{NO}_x$ in diesel engines by lowering combustion temperatures.
    *   **Variable Geometry Turbochargers (VGT) & Intercoolers:** Control air intake to manage combustion conditions.
    *   **Common Rail Direct Injection (CRDI) Systems:** Precise fuel injection timing and multiple injections can help manage combustion and $\text{NO}_x$.
    *   **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR):** Uses a reducing agent (like urea-based DEF) to convert $\text{NO}_x$ to nitrogen and water in the exhaust.
    *   **Lean $\text{NO}_x$ Traps (LNT):** Store $\text{NO}_x$ during lean operation and release it during brief rich periods for reduction in a catalyst.

*   **Reference:** Sajith & Thomas (2017) discuss the dual challenge for diesel engines: reducing both PM and $\text{NO}_x$, often involving trade-offs.

#### 3.3. Carbon Monoxide (CO)

*   **Formation:** While diesel engines operate on lean mixtures, CO can still be formed, especially during transient operations or when the engine is not running efficiently.

*   **Sources in C.I. Engines:**
    *   Incomplete combustion during cold starts.
    *   Low engine load.
    *   Misfires.
    *   Malfunctioning fuel injection systems.

*   **Effects:** Same as in S.I. engines.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Proper engine maintenance.
    *   Efficient fuel injection.
    *   Oxidation catalysts in exhaust systems (less common in older diesel systems but used in modern ones).

#### 3.4. Unburnt Hydrocarbons (HC) / Diesel Particulate Matter (DPM)

*   **Formation:** Similar to S.I. engines, HCs are unburnt fuel. In diesel engines, these can be present as volatile components in the particulate matter or as gaseous emissions.

*   **Sources in C.I. Engines:**
    *   Incomplete atomization and mixing.
    *   Wall impingement (less of an issue than in S.I. with port injection, but can occur with direct injection).
    *   Low temperatures.

*   **Effects:** Contribute to smog and respiratory issues.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Improved fuel injection and atomization.
    *   Optimized combustion chamber design.
    *   DPFs can capture PM, which includes some HC components.

#### 3.5. Sulfur Oxides ($\text{SO}_x$)

*   **Formation:** Formed from sulfur present as an impurity in diesel fuel, reacting with oxygen during combustion.

    *   **Reaction:** $\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$

*   **Sources in C.I. Engines:**
    *   Sulfur content in diesel fuel.

*   **Effects:**
    *   Contribute to acid rain.
    *   Respiratory irritants.
    *   Can degrade engine components and exhaust after-treatment systems.

*   **Mitigation:**
    *   Use of ultra-low sulfur diesel (ULSD) fuel. This is the primary mitigation strategy.

*   **Important Point to Remember:** The reduction of sulfur content in fuels has been a major global effort to reduce $\text{SO}_x$ emissions from all combustion sources, including diesel engines.

### 4. Comparison of Pollutants from S.I. and C.I. Engines

| Pollutant                  | S.I. Engines                                     | C.I. Engines                                          | Key Differences                                                                 |
| :------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Carbon Monoxide (CO)**   | Higher, especially with rich mixtures.           | Generally lower due to lean operation.                | Primarily related to mixture richness.                                          |
| **Unburnt Hydrocarbons (HC)** | Higher, due to wall quenching, crevice flow.     | Generally lower in gaseous form, but present in PM.   | Mechanisms of formation differ; S.I. has more gaseous HC.                       |
| **Nitrogen Oxides ($\text{NO}_x$)** | Moderate to High, dependent on timing/temp.      | High, due to high combustion temperatures and lean mix. | C.I. engines typically produce more $\text{NO}_x$ under similar operating conditions. |
| **Particulate Matter (PM)** | Lower, mainly from rich zones or DI engines.     | High, primary pollutant due to diffusion flame.       | C.I. engines are the dominant source of PM in the context of automotive.        |
| **Aldehydes**              | Present, but largely controlled by 3-way cats.   | Can be present, often as part of PM.                 | S.I. control technologies are very effective.                                   |
| **Sulfur Oxides ($\text{SO}_x$)** | Dependent on gasoline sulfur content.            | Dependent on diesel fuel sulfur content.              | Historically, diesel fuel had higher sulfur, leading to higher $\text{SO}_x$.   |

**Reference:** Heywood (2011) provides extensive comparative data on emissions from various engine types.

### 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** Which pollutant is primarily formed due to insufficient oxygen during combustion?
    *   (a) $\text{NO}_x$
    *   (b) $\text{SO}_x$
    *   (c) CO and HC
    *   (d) Particulate Matter

    **Answer:** (c) CO and HC. Both are direct indicators of incomplete combustion where oxygen is limited. $\text{NO}_x$ forms at high temperatures, and $\text{SO}_x$ from sulfur impurities.

**Question 2:** In S.I. engines, what is the primary mechanism responsible for unburnt hydrocarbon emissions related to the cylinder walls?
    *   (a) Crevice flow
    *   (b) Wall quenching
    *   (c) Misfire
    *   (d) Rich mixture

    **Answer:** (b) Wall quenching. This refers to the thin layer of fuel-air mixture near the cold cylinder walls that doesn't burn completely. Crevice flow is another mechanism but related to gaps, not the wall surface itself.

**Question 3:** Which type of engine is generally associated with higher particulate matter (soot) emissions?
    *   (a) Spark Ignition (S.I.) Engine
    *   (b) Compression Ignition (C.I.) Engine
    *   (c) Both produce similar amounts
    *   (d) Neither produces significant PM

    **Answer:** (b) Compression Ignition (C.I.) Engine. The diffusion flame characteristics and local rich zones in diesel combustion lead to significant soot formation.

**Question 4:** What is the main method used to reduce $\text{NO}_x$ emissions in modern diesel engines?
    *   (a) Three-way catalytic converter
    *   (b) Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR)
    *   (c) Catalytic oxidation
    *   (d) Fuel additives only

    **Answer:** (b) Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR). While SCR and LNT are also crucial, EGR is a fundamental and widely adopted method to lower combustion temperatures and thus $\text{NO}_x$ formation in C.I. engines. Three-way catalysts are primarily for S.I. engines.

**Question 5:** Explain the role of catalytic converters in reducing emissions from S.I. engines.
    **Answer:** Catalytic converters use catalysts (like platinum, palladium, rhodium) to convert harmful pollutants into less harmful substances. A three-way catalytic converter simultaneously oxidizes CO to $\text{CO}_2$, oxidizes unburnt HC to $\text{CO}_2$ and water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), and reduces $\text{NO}_x$ to nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$) and oxygen ($\text{O}_2$). This requires precise control of the air-fuel ratio around stoichiometry.

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Rich mixtures favor CO and HC formation.**
*   **Lean mixtures and high temperatures favor $\text{NO}_x$ formation.**
*   **Diesel engines are prone to PM (soot) due to diffusion flames.**
*   **Wall quenching and crevice flow are significant sources of HC in S.I. engines.**
*   **EGR is a critical technology for $\text{NO}_x$ reduction in C.I. engines.**
*   **DPFs are essential for PM control in C.I. engines.**
*   **Fuel quality, especially sulfur content, significantly impacts $\text{SO}_x$ emissions.**
*   **Catalytic converters are highly effective for S.I. engine emissions control.**
