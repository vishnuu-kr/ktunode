# Thermal Engineering: Module 4 - Combustion in IC Engines: Fuels for IC Engines

## Topic: Ignition Limits

---

### **1. Introduction to Ignition Limits**

Ignition limits define the range of conditions under which a combustible mixture can propagate flame. In the context of Internal Combustion (IC) engines, these limits are crucial for understanding the combustion process and ensuring reliable engine operation. They dictate the boundaries of the fuel-air mixture that will ignite and burn effectively.

**Key Concept:** Flame propagation is essential for sustained combustion in an IC engine. If the mixture is too lean or too rich, flame propagation may not occur, or it may be too slow to be useful.

---

### **2. Definitions of Ignition Limits**

Ignition limits are typically expressed in terms of the concentration of fuel in the air (or oxidizer) and are also influenced by other factors.

*   **Lower Ignition Limit (LIL) / Lower Explosive Limit (LEL):** The minimum concentration of fuel in the mixture (typically expressed as a percentage by volume) that can support flame propagation at a given temperature and pressure.
    *   **Description:** Below the LIL, the mixture is too lean in fuel. There isn't enough fuel to sustain the heat generated by the initial ignition event to preheat and ignite subsequent layers of the mixture.
    *   **Example:** For gasoline in air, the LIL is approximately 1.4% to 1.8% by volume.
*   **Upper Ignition Limit (UIL) / Upper Explosive Limit (UEL):** The maximum concentration of fuel in the mixture that can support flame propagation at a given temperature and pressure.
    *   **Description:** Above the UIL, the mixture is too rich in fuel. There is an excess of fuel, leading to insufficient oxygen for complete combustion, and the excess fuel molecules act as a diluent, absorbing heat and preventing flame propagation.
    *   **Example:** For gasoline in air, the UIL is approximately 7.6% to 8.0% by volume.

**Important Point to Remember:** The term "explosive" is often used interchangeably with "combustible" when discussing these limits in the context of engine operation, as a rapid combustion process can lead to an explosion if confined.

---

### **3. Factors Affecting Ignition Limits**

Ignition limits are not fixed values and are significantly influenced by various environmental and operating conditions.

*   **Temperature:**
    *   **Effect:** As temperature increases, both LIL and UIL tend to decrease.
    *   **Reason:** Higher temperatures mean the mixture is closer to its autoignition temperature, requiring less energy for ignition and enabling flame propagation even with a leaner mixture. Also, increased kinetic energy of molecules aids reaction. (Rudra Moorthy, 2003)
*   **Pressure:**
    *   **Effect:** As pressure increases, LIL generally decreases, and UIL also tends to decrease, but the effect on UIL is less pronounced and can sometimes be negligible.
    *   **Reason:** Higher pressure increases the density of the mixture, leading to more frequent molecular collisions and faster reaction rates, thus facilitating flame propagation even at lower fuel concentrations. (R.K Rajput, 2010)
*   **Inert Diluents (e.g., Nitrogen, CO2, Water Vapor):**
    *   **Effect:** The addition of inert diluents increases both LIL and UIL.
    *   **Reason:** Inert gases absorb heat from the combustion reaction without participating in it. This means more fuel is needed to reach the self-sustaining temperature for flame propagation, thus widening the limits.
*   **Oxygen Concentration:**
    *   **Effect:** Decreasing oxygen concentration widens both LIL and UIL.
    *   **Reason:** Less oxygen means combustion is less efficient, and a higher concentration of fuel is required to achieve the necessary heat release for flame propagation.
*   **Geometry and Size of the Combustion Chamber:**
    *   **Effect:** Smaller volumes and larger surface-area-to-volume ratios tend to widen the ignition limits.
    *   **Reason:** In smaller chambers or with configurations leading to high heat loss to the walls, more fuel is needed to sustain combustion due to increased heat transfer.
*   **Turbulence:**
    *   **Effect:** Increased turbulence generally widens the ignition limits, particularly the LIL.
    *   **Reason:** Turbulence enhances mixing and heat transfer, promoting faster and more efficient combustion, which can help propagate flames in leaner mixtures. (J.B.Heywood, 2011)
*   **Presence of Catalysts or Impurities:**
    *   **Effect:** Certain substances can lower autoignition temperatures and broaden the flammability limits.

---

### **4. Significance of Ignition Limits in IC Engines**

Understanding ignition limits is critical for several aspects of IC engine design and operation.

*   **Fuel-Air Ratio Control:**
    *   **Application:** Engines operate with a range of fuel-air ratios. To ensure stable combustion and prevent misfires, the operating fuel-air ratio must be kept well within the ignition limits.
    *   **Example:** In gasoline engines, stoichiometric or slightly lean mixtures are preferred for efficiency and emissions. Operating too close to the LIL or UIL can lead to rough running or incomplete combustion.
*   **Engine Starting:**
    *   **Application:** During starting, the engine may be cold, and the fuel mixture might not be perfectly vaporized. The ignition limits help determine the conditions needed for reliable ignition.
    *   **Example:** Cold starting often requires a richer mixture than normal operation to overcome the cooling effect of the engine walls and ensure ignition.
*   **Misfire Detection:**
    *   **Application:** If the fuel-air mixture falls outside the ignition limits, the cylinder may not combust, leading to a misfire. Monitoring engine performance can help detect these conditions.
*   **Emissions Control:**
    *   **Application:** Operating at very lean (near LIL) or very rich (near UIL) conditions can lead to increased emissions of unburned hydrocarbons (HC) and carbon monoxide (CO) due to incomplete combustion.
*   **Knock and Detonation:**
    *   **Application:** While ignition limits primarily concern flame propagation, understanding the conditions that promote rapid combustion is related to preventing uncontrolled combustion phenomena like knock. (V. Ganesan, 2002)

---

### **5. Relationship with other Combustion Concepts**

*   **Autoignition Temperature:** The minimum temperature at which a substance in air will spontaneously ignite without an external ignition source. Ignition limits are defined for a mixture where an ignition source (like a spark plug) is present to initiate combustion.
*   **Flammability Limits vs. Explosive Limits:** In many contexts, these terms are used interchangeably. However, "flammability limits" might refer to the broader range of mixtures that can burn, while "explosive limits" specifically refer to mixtures that can undergo rapid, pressure-wave generating combustion when ignited in confinement.
*   **Ignition Delay:** The time lag between the injection of fuel and the start of combustion. Ignition limits influence whether combustion can even occur, irrespective of the delay. (V Sajith & Shijo Thomas, 2017)

**Example Scenario:**
Consider a gasoline engine operating at atmospheric pressure and ambient temperature. If the fuel-air mixture is only 1% gasoline by volume, it is below the LIL, and a spark plug might not be able to initiate sustained combustion, leading to a misfire. If the mixture is 9% gasoline, it is above the UIL, and again, combustion may fail. The engine operates safely and efficiently when the mixture is between approximately 2% and 7% gasoline.

### **8. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** Define the Lower Ignition Limit (LIL) and Upper Ignition Limit (UIL) for a combustible mixture in an IC engine.

**Answer 1:**
*   **LIL:** The minimum concentration of fuel in the mixture that can support flame propagation. Below this, the mixture is too lean.
*   **UIL:** The maximum concentration of fuel in the mixture that can support flame propagation. Above this, the mixture is too rich.

**Question 2:** How does an increase in ambient temperature affect the ignition limits of a fuel-air mixture?

**Answer 2:** An increase in ambient temperature generally causes both the LIL and UIL to decrease. This means a wider range of mixture compositions can be ignited and propagate flame at higher temperatures.

**Question 3:** Explain why adding inert gases like nitrogen to a fuel-air mixture typically widens the ignition limits.

**Answer 3:** Inert gases absorb heat generated by the combustion reaction without participating in it. This dilutes the mixture and requires a higher concentration of fuel to provide enough heat to preheat and ignite adjacent layers of the mixture, thus widening both LIL and UIL.

**Question 4:** If a gasoline engine's fuel-air mixture composition falls outside the ignition limits, what is a likely consequence?

**Answer 4:** A likely consequence is a **misfire**, where combustion fails to occur in the cylinder. This can lead to rough engine operation, reduced power output, and increased emissions.

**Question 5:** Which of the following factors would tend to *widen* the ignition limits?
    a) Increasing operating temperature
    b) Increasing operating pressure
    c) Adding inert diluents
    d) Increasing turbulence

**Answer 5:** c) Adding inert diluents.
*   Increasing temperature and pressure generally *narrow* the ignition limits (making it easier to ignite).
*   Increasing turbulence can *widen* the limits, particularly the LIL, but adding inert diluents has a more pronounced widening effect on both limits.

---

### **9. Important Points to Remember**

*   Ignition limits are critical for ensuring stable and complete combustion in IC engines.
*   LIL represents the leanest mixture, and UIL represents the richest mixture that can sustain flame propagation.
*   Temperature, pressure, diluents, oxygen concentration, and chamber geometry are key factors influencing these limits.
*   Operating outside these limits leads to misfires, incomplete combustion, and potential performance issues.
*   The exact values of ignition limits are specific to the fuel and the surrounding conditions.
