# Thermal Engineering: Module 2 - Steam Turbines: Classification

## 1. Introduction to Steam Turbines

Steam turbines are prime movers that convert the thermal energy of steam into mechanical energy, which is then used to drive generators, pumps, or other machinery. They are a cornerstone of modern power generation.

**Key Concept:** The operation of a steam turbine is based on the principle of **Newton's second and third laws of motion**. The high-velocity steam jet impinges on the turbine blades, exerting a force and causing rotation.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This section provides foundational knowledge essential for understanding all subsequent topics and contributes to CO1 (applying thermodynamic principles) and CO2 (analyzing performance).

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 15 (Steam Turbines) and R.K Rajput, Chapter 16 (Steam Turbines).

## 2. Classification of Steam Turbines

Steam turbines can be classified based on various criteria, allowing for a systematic understanding of their diverse applications and operating principles.

**Key Concept:** Classification helps in understanding the specific design features, operating characteristics, and suitability for different power generation scenarios.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This is the core of this topic and directly addresses the learning outcome of classifying steam turbines. It is crucial for CO2 (analyzing performance and identifying improvements).

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 15 (Steam Turbines), Section 15.2 (Classification of Steam Turbines) and R.K Rajput, Chapter 16 (Steam Turbines), Section 16.2 (Classification of Steam Turbines).

---

### 2.1. Classification Based on the Action of Steam on Blades

This is a fundamental classification based on how the steam's kinetic energy is utilized to produce mechanical work.

#### 2.1.1. Impulse Turbines

*   **Definition:** In impulse turbines, the steam expands and its pressure drops entirely in the nozzle. This expansion converts the entire enthalpy drop into kinetic energy, producing a high-velocity jet of steam. This jet then impinges on the blades, causing a change in momentum and thus an impulse force.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   Pressure drop occurs only in the stationary nozzles.
    *   The steam velocity relative to the blades is high.
    *   The blades are designed to deflect the steam jet, causing a change in momentum.
    *   The force on the blades is due to the change in momentum of the steam.
    *   The blade passages are typically large to accommodate the high-velocity steam.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **De Laval Turbine:** This is a simple, single-stage impulse turbine where steam expands through a nozzle and then strikes a single row of blades attached to a rotor. It is efficient at high speeds but suffers from the disadvantage of very high rotor speeds, requiring special materials and construction.
    *   **Rateau Turbine:** This is a multi-stage impulse turbine. It uses a series of pressure stages, with a set of stationary nozzles (acting as pressure nozzles) between each row of moving blades. Each stage involves an expansion of steam in a nozzle and subsequent impulse on a row of moving blades. This arrangement reduces the rotational speed and improves efficiency.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** Understanding impulse turbines is foundational for CO2, as it helps in analyzing the energy conversion process and its efficiency.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.1 (Impulse Turbines), De Laval Turbine (Section 15.3) and Rateau Turbine (Section 15.4). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.1 (Impulse Turbines), De Laval Turbine (Section 16.3) and Rateau Turbine (Section 16.4).

#### 2.1.2. Reaction Turbines

*   **Definition:** In reaction turbines, the steam expands both in the stationary nozzles (or guide vanes) and in the moving blades. The steam pressure drops progressively as it flows through the turbine stages. This pressure drop in the moving blades results in a change in momentum due to the acceleration of steam within the blade passages, creating a reaction force.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   Pressure drop occurs in both stationary and moving blades.
    *   The steam velocity relative to the blades is lower than in impulse turbines.
    *   The blades are designed as aerofoils, with converging passages that cause acceleration of steam.
    *   The force on the blades is due to both the impulse effect (change in momentum) and the reaction effect (acceleration of steam within the moving blades).
    *   The blade passages are narrower than in impulse turbines.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Parsons Turbine:** This is a classic example of a reaction turbine. In this design, the stationary blades and moving blades have identical symmetrical aerofoil shapes. The steam expands equally in both the stationary and moving blade passages, resulting in an equal pressure drop and velocity increase across each row of blades. This leads to a gradual reduction in steam pressure and velocity as it flows through the turbine.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** Understanding reaction turbines is crucial for CO2, enabling comparison with impulse turbines and analysis of different efficiency improvement strategies.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.2 (Reaction Turbines), Parsons Turbine (Section 15.5). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.2 (Reaction Turbines), Parsons Turbine (Section 16.5).

#### 2.1.3. Impulse-Reaction Turbines (Mixed Flow Turbines)

*   **Definition:** These turbines combine features of both impulse and reaction turbines. Some stages may operate on the impulse principle, while others operate on the reaction principle, or even within a single stage, there can be a partial pressure drop in the moving blades.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   Offers a compromise between the advantages of impulse and reaction designs.
    *   Can be designed for higher efficiencies over a wider range of operating conditions.
*   **Examples:** Many modern high-pressure turbine designs incorporate impulse-reaction characteristics.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This classification helps in understanding the evolution of turbine design and the pursuit of higher efficiencies, relevant to CO2.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.3 (Impulse-Reaction Turbines). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.3 (Impulse-Reaction Turbines).

### 2.2. Classification Based on the Direction of Steam Flow

This classification relates to the path the steam takes through the turbine casing and blades.

#### 2.2.1. Axial Flow Turbines

*   **Definition:** In axial flow turbines, the steam flows parallel to the axis of rotation. The steam passes through a series of rows of stationary and moving blades arranged around the rotor shaft.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   Steam flows in a direction parallel to the turbine shaft.
    *   Most common type of steam turbine used in power plants due to high efficiency and power output.
    *   Requires precise blade design and manufacturing.
*   **Examples:** The vast majority of large-scale steam turbines in power plants are axial flow turbines.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This is a fundamental structural classification relevant to understanding the overall design and operation, supporting CO2.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.4 (Axial Flow Turbines). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.4 (Axial Flow Turbines).

#### 2.2.2. Radial Flow Turbines (Centripetal Turbines)

*   **Definition:** In radial flow turbines, the steam flows in a direction perpendicular to the axis of rotation, typically from the periphery towards the center or vice versa.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   Steam flow is radial.
    *   Less common in large power generation applications compared to axial flow turbines.
    *   Can be used in specific applications where space is limited or for smaller turbines.
*   **Examples:** Some types of small turbochargers and specific industrial applications.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** While less common, understanding radial flow turbines provides a broader perspective on turbine design and is relevant for CO2 in comparing different configurations.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.5 (Radial Flow Turbines). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.5 (Radial Flow Turbines).

#### 2.2.3. Mixed Flow Turbines

*   **Definition:** In mixed flow turbines, the steam flow has both axial and radial components. The steam enters radially and exits axially, or vice versa.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   Combines axial and radial flow characteristics.
    *   Used in specific applications where the flow path is designed to be intermediate.
*   **Examples:** Some types of turbochargers and specific process turbines.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** Similar to impulse-reaction turbines, this classification aids in understanding design variations and their impact on performance (CO2).

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.6 (Mixed Flow Turbines). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.6 (Mixed Flow Turbines).

### 2.3. Classification Based on Steam Admission

This classification is based on how steam is supplied to the turbine blades.

#### 2.3.1. Simple Impulse Turbine (Un-bladed)

*   **Definition:** Steam is admitted to the entire circumference of the rotor. This is a very basic type, often not used in practice for power generation due to low efficiency.

#### 2.3.2. Multi-stage Impulse Turbines

*   **Definition:** Steam is admitted to multiple stages, with pressure drops occurring in separate nozzle banks (e.g., Rateau turbine).

#### 2.3.3. Single-stage Impulse Turbine

*   **Definition:** Steam is admitted to the entire circumference of the rotor, and the entire pressure drop occurs in a single set of nozzles. The rotor has a single row of blades. (Example: De Laval turbine).

#### 2.3.4. Multi-nozzle Impulse Turbine

*   **Definition:** Steam is admitted to only a portion of the circumference of the rotor through a limited number of nozzles. This leads to a partial admission, where steam only acts on a section of the blades at any given time.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** Understanding steam admission is important for analyzing efficiency losses and performance characteristics, directly contributing to CO2.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.7 (Classification based on steam admission). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.7 (Classification based on steam admission).

### 2.4. Classification Based on the Number of Stages

This classification is based on how the total enthalpy drop of the steam is utilized across multiple turbine stages.

#### 2.4.1. Single-Stage Turbine

*   **Definition:** The entire expansion of steam and conversion of thermal energy to mechanical energy occurs in a single stage, comprising a nozzle and a row of moving blades.
*   **Example:** De Laval turbine.
*   **Limitations:** High rotational speeds, high blade tip speeds, and limited efficiency due to high steam velocity at exit.

#### 2.4.2. Multi-Stage Turbine

*   **Definition:** The total enthalpy drop of the steam is divided into multiple stages. Each stage typically consists of a set of stationary nozzles (or guide vanes) followed by a row of moving blades. This allows for controlled expansion and more efficient energy transfer.
*   **Types of Multi-stage Turbines:**
    *   **Pressure Compounded Turbines (e.g., Rateau Turbine):** The total pressure drop is divided into a series of pressure stages. Each stage involves a nozzle and a blade row. The steam pressure drops in the nozzles, and the kinetic energy is utilized by the blades.
    *   **Velocity Compounded Turbines (e.g., Curtis Turbine):** The total pressure drop occurs in the first set of nozzles. The high-velocity steam then passes through alternating rows of fixed and moving blades. The steam velocity is gradually reduced by successive blade rows, with a portion of the kinetic energy being extracted in each moving blade row. This allows for a significant reduction in rotational speed.
    *   **Pressure-Velocity Compounded Turbines:** These turbines combine features of both pressure compounding and velocity compounding to achieve high efficiencies and moderate rotational speeds.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This is a critical classification for understanding how turbine efficiency is improved. It directly relates to CO2 by explaining methods to optimize energy extraction.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.8 (Classification based on number of stages), Curtis Turbine (Section 15.6). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.8 (Classification based on number of stages), Curtis Turbine (Section 16.6).

### 2.5. Classification Based on Pressure

This classification categorizes turbines based on the pressure at which steam enters and leaves the turbine.

#### 2.5.1. High-Pressure (HP) Turbines

*   **Definition:** These turbines receive steam directly from the boiler at high pressure and temperature. They handle the initial expansion of steam.

#### 2.5.2. Intermediate-Pressure (IP) Turbines

*   **Definition:** Steam from the HP turbine exhaust is fed into the IP turbine for further expansion.

#### 2.5.3. Low-Pressure (LP) Turbines

*   **Definition:** Steam from the IP turbine exhaust is fed into the LP turbine, where it expands to the lowest pressure (often vacuum) before entering the condenser.

#### 2.5.4. Condensing Turbines

*   **Definition:** The exhaust steam is discharged into a condenser, where it is condensed into water. This creates a vacuum at the turbine exhaust, significantly increasing the enthalpy drop across the turbine and thus its efficiency.
*   **Key Benefit:** Higher efficiency due to the larger pressure difference and expansion down to a vacuum.

#### 2.5.5. Non-Condensing Turbines (Back Pressure Turbines)

*   **Definition:** The exhaust steam is discharged at a pressure above atmospheric to be used for process heating or other applications.
*   **Applications:** Often used in co-generation plants where both power and heat are required. The exhaust steam acts as a useful byproduct.

#### 2.5.6. Pass-out Turbines (Bleeder Turbines)

*   **Definition:** Steam is extracted at one or more intermediate stages of the turbine for process heating or other uses, while the remaining steam continues to expand to the condenser.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This classification is essential for understanding the overall steam power plant layout and the role of different turbine sections. It directly relates to CO2 by explaining different operational modes and their impact on efficiency and application.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.9 (Classification based on pressure). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.9 (Classification based on pressure).

### 2.6. Classification Based on Application

This classification is based on the specific purpose for which the steam turbine is used.

#### 2.6.1. Power Generation Turbines

*   **Definition:** Turbines used in power plants to drive generators for electricity production. These are typically large-scale, high-efficiency units.

#### 2.6.2. Mechanical Drive Turbines

*   **Definition:** Turbines used to directly drive machinery such as pumps, compressors, fans, and mills.

#### 2.6.3. Marine Turbines

*   **Definition:** Turbines used for propulsion of ships.

#### 2.6.4. Turbochargers

*   **Definition:** Small turbines used in internal combustion engines to increase power output by utilizing exhaust gas energy to drive a compressor that forces more air into the engine cylinders.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** This classification helps understand the diverse applications of steam turbines and connects to CO2 (efficiency improvements) and even CO3 and CO4 indirectly by showing how prime movers are designed for different purposes.

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy, Section 15.2.10 (Classification based on application). R.K Rajput, Section 16.2.10 (Classification based on application).

## 3. Key Concepts and Definitions Recap

*   **Nozzle:** A passage designed to expand steam and convert its thermal energy into kinetic energy.
*   **Blade:** The component that interacts with the steam jet to extract energy and produce rotational torque.
*   **Stage:** A combination of a set of stationary nozzles (or guide vanes) and a row of moving blades.
*   **Enthalpy Drop:** The reduction in the specific enthalpy of steam as it expands through the turbine. This represents the thermal energy converted into mechanical work.
*   **Specific Speed:** A dimensionless parameter used to characterize the type of turbine based on its design speed, power output, and head (or pressure drop). It helps in selecting the most suitable turbine for a given application.
*   **Blade Velocity Ratio (u/V):** The ratio of the tangential velocity of the blade to the velocity of the steam jet. This ratio is crucial for determining the efficiency of energy transfer.
*   **Degree of Reaction:** In a reaction turbine, it is the ratio of the enthalpy drop in the moving blades to the total enthalpy drop in the stage.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** These definitions are crucial for a deep understanding of turbine operation and performance analysis, supporting CO1 and CO2.

## 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **Impulse turbines** convert thermal energy to kinetic energy in stationary nozzles, and the impulse force on blades drives the rotor.
*   **Reaction turbines** convert thermal energy to kinetic energy in both stationary and moving blades, and the reaction force on blades contributes to rotation.
*   **Axial flow turbines** are the most common for large-scale power generation due to their efficiency and power output.
*   **Multi-stage turbines** are essential for achieving high efficiencies by dividing the total enthalpy drop into smaller, more manageable stages.
*   **Pressure compounding (Rateau)** and **velocity compounding (Curtis)** are key methods for designing multi-stage turbines.
*   **Condensing turbines** offer higher efficiency than non-condensing turbines due to the vacuum at the exhaust.
*   **Blade velocity ratio (u/V)** is critical for maximizing energy transfer. For ideal impulse turbines, the optimal u/V is 0.5. For ideal reaction turbines, it can be closer to 1.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:** These summary points reinforce the key takeaways and directly support CO2 by highlighting principles for performance improvement.

## 6. Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **Impulse Turbines:** Steam expands entirely in stationary nozzles, creating a high-velocity jet. This jet impinges on the blades, exerting an impulse force due to the change in momentum.
    **Reaction Turbines:** Steam expands in both stationary and moving blades. The pressure drop in moving blades accelerates the steam, creating a reaction force that contributes to rotation. The fundamental difference lies in where the primary pressure drop and energy conversion occur.

2.  **De Laval Turbine:** Classified as a **single-stage impulse turbine**.
    **Parsons Turbine:** Classified as a **multi-stage reaction turbine**.

3.  **Pressure Compounding (Rateau Turbine):** The total pressure drop of the steam is divided into a series of stages. In each stage, steam expands in a nozzle, and the resulting kinetic energy is converted into work by the moving blades. This method aims to reduce the steam velocity at the exit of each stage and thus reduce rotational speed.
    **Velocity Compounding (Curtis Turbine):** The entire pressure drop occurs in the first set of nozzles. The high-velocity steam then passes through alternate rows of fixed and moving blades. The fixed blades redirect the steam, and the moving blades extract a portion of the kinetic energy. This process is repeated through multiple rows of blades in a single stage, allowing for significant reduction in rotational speed and efficient energy extraction at high initial steam velocities.

4.  **Condensing Turbines:** Exhaust steam is condensed into water by a condenser. This creates a vacuum at the turbine exhaust, significantly increasing the pressure difference across the turbine and thus its efficiency. They are used in power plants aiming for maximum electricity generation.
    **Non-Condensing Turbines (Back Pressure Turbines):** Exhaust steam is discharged at a pressure above atmospheric for process heating or other applications. They are typically found in **co-generation plants** or industrial facilities where both power and heat are simultaneously required.

5.  **Axial flow turbines** are preferred for large-scale power generation because:
    *   They allow for a smooth, continuous flow of steam parallel to the axis, enabling efficient energy transfer over a large number of stages.
    *   They can handle very large volumes of steam, which is characteristic of high-power output.
    *   The design of axial flow blades allows for higher aerodynamic efficiency compared to radial flow designs for large turbines.
    *   They offer a more compact design for a given power output compared to radial flow turbines.

6.  The **blade velocity ratio (u/V)** is the ratio of the tangential velocity of the blade ($u$) to the absolute velocity of the steam jet ($V$) entering the blades. It is a crucial parameter for determining the efficiency of energy transfer from the steam to the rotor.
    *   For optimal energy transfer in an **ideal impulse turbine**, the blade velocity should be half the steam jet velocity (i.e., $u/V = 0.5$). This ensures that the steam leaves the blades with minimum kinetic energy, maximizing the work done.
    *   In **reaction turbines**, the optimal blade velocity ratio is generally higher, potentially approaching 1, due to the reaction forces involved.
    *   Deviations from the optimal blade velocity ratio lead to reduced efficiency, as either the steam leaves with excessive kinetic energy (high velocity ratio) or the blades have to rotate too fast to extract all the energy (low velocity ratio).
