# Thermal Engineering Lab-2: Module 7 - Valve Timing Diagram of IC Engines

## Introduction to Valve Timing Diagrams

The valve timing diagram is a crucial graphical representation that illustrates the sequence and duration of opening and closing of the intake and exhaust valves in an internal combustion engine relative to the crankshaft position. It is fundamental to understanding how an IC engine breathes and expels exhaust gases, directly impacting its performance, efficiency, and emissions.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Four-Stroke Cycle:** The fundamental cycle of operation for most IC engines, consisting of Intake, Compression, Power (Combustion), and Exhaust strokes.
*   **Crankshaft Angle (CA):** The angular position of the crankshaft, usually measured in degrees (°). The engine cycle is often represented over 360° or 720° (for a complete four-stroke cycle).
*   **Top Dead Center (TDC):** The position of the piston when it is at its highest point in the cylinder.
*   **Bottom Dead Center (BDC):** The position of the piston when it is at its lowest point in the cylinder.
*   **Intake Valve:** Controls the flow of the air-fuel mixture (petrol engine) or air (diesel engine) into the cylinder.
*   **Exhaust Valve:** Controls the flow of burnt exhaust gases out of the cylinder.
*   **Overlap:** The period during which both the intake and exhaust valves are open simultaneously. This occurs near TDC between the exhaust and intake strokes.
*   **Port Timing:** Refers to the timing of the opening and closing of ports in two-stroke engines, similar to valve timing in four-stroke engines.

## Importance of Valve Timing

The timing of valve opening and closing significantly influences:

*   **Volumetric Efficiency:** The engine's ability to fill its cylinders with fresh charge.
*   **Engine Power and Torque:** Optimized timing leads to maximum power output.
*   **Fuel Consumption:** Proper valve timing can improve fuel efficiency.
*   **Exhaust Emissions:** Overlap and timing affect the scavenging of exhaust gases and the residual exhaust gases in the cylinder, influencing emission levels (CO2, NOx, unburnt hydrocarbons).
*   **Engine Starting:** Valve timing plays a role in the ease of starting the engine.

## Valve Timing Diagram for Four-Stroke Engines

**Basic Principle:** The intake and exhaust valves are mechanically operated by camshafts, which are driven by the crankshaft, typically through a timing gear or belt, at half the crankshaft speed (1:2 ratio).

### 1. Petrol Engine Valve Timing Diagram

**Key Features:**

*   **Intake Valve Opens (IVO):** Typically opens *before* TDC on the intake stroke to allow for inertia effects of the incoming charge.
*   **Intake Valve Closes (IVC):** Typically closes *after* TDC on the compression stroke. This "late closing" is to utilize the momentum of the incoming charge to pack more air-fuel mixture into the cylinder, thereby increasing volumetric efficiency.
*   **Exhaust Valve Opens (EVO):** Typically opens *before* BDC on the power stroke. This "early opening" allows the high-pressure exhaust gases to start escaping due to their own pressure, reducing the load on the piston during the exhaust stroke.
*   **Exhaust Valve Closes (EVC):** Typically closes *after* TDC on the intake stroke. This "late closing" contributes to valve overlap.
*   **Overlap:** The period when both intake and exhaust valves are open. This overlap is crucial for scavenging and can be adjusted to optimize performance at different engine speeds.

**Typical Crank Angle Ranges (Approximate):**

*   IVO: 5-15° Before TDC
*   IVC: 30-50° After TDC
*   EVO: 40-60° Before BDC
*   EVC: 5-15° After TDC

**Diagram Representation:** A typical diagram will show the crankshaft angle on the x-axis (0° to 720°) and the state of the valves (open/closed) on the y-axis. Lines will indicate the duration of opening and closing.

### 2. Diesel Engine Valve Timing Diagram

**Key Differences from Petrol Engines:**

*   **No Throttle Body/Carburetor:** Diesel engines ingest only air during the intake stroke. Fuel is injected directly into the cylinder at the end of the compression stroke.
*   **Higher Compression Ratios:** Leading to different thermal efficiencies and often more robust valve operation.
*   **Intake Valve Timing:** Similar to petrol engines, IVO and IVC are designed to maximize air intake.
*   **Exhaust Valve Timing:** Similar to petrol engines, EVO and EVC are set to allow efficient expulsion of exhaust gases.
*   **Overlap:** Generally, diesel engines have less overlap than petrol engines. Excessive overlap can lead to the loss of fresh air into the exhaust manifold, reducing combustion efficiency and increasing emissions.

**Typical Crank Angle Ranges (Approximate - can vary more than petrol engines):**

*   IVO: 5-15° Before TDC
*   IVC: 25-45° After TDC
*   EVO: 40-60° Before BDC
*   EVC: 5-15° After TDC

## Valve Timing Diagram for Two-Stroke Engines

Two-stroke engines use ports in the cylinder wall instead of mechanical valves, operated by the piston's movement.

**Key Features:**

*   **Ports:**
    *   **Intake Port:** Allows the fresh charge (air-fuel mixture in petrol, air in diesel) into the crankcase.
    *   **Transfer Port:** Allows the fresh charge from the crankcase to enter the cylinder above the piston.
    *   **Exhaust Port:** Allows the burnt exhaust gases to escape from the cylinder.
*   **Piston as Valve:** The piston itself uncovers and covers these ports as it moves up and down.
*   **Scavenging:** The process of removing burnt gases from the cylinder and filling it with fresh charge. This is a critical aspect of two-stroke operation.

**Timing Representation:** Similar to four-stroke engines, the timing of port opening and closing is represented relative to the crankshaft angle.

**Typical Port Timing (Approximate):**

*   **Exhaust Port Opening (EPO):** Occurs shortly before the piston reaches BDC.
*   **Exhaust Port Closing (EPC):** Occurs after the piston has moved significantly up from BDC.
*   **Transfer Port Opening (TPO):** Occurs around the same time or slightly after EPO.
*   **Transfer Port Closing (TPC):** Occurs after the piston has passed TDC on the compression stroke, sealing the cylinder for compression.
*   **Intake Port Opening (IPO):** Occurs when the piston is near BDC, allowing fresh charge into the crankcase.
*   **Intake Port Closing (IPC):** Occurs when the piston reaches a certain point on its upward stroke, sealing the crankcase.

**Example of Port Operation in a Two-Stroke Engine:**

1.  **Upward Stroke (Compression & Intake into Crankcase):**
    *   As the piston moves up, it covers the transfer and exhaust ports, initiating compression of the charge in the cylinder.
    *   Simultaneously, it uncovers the intake port, allowing fresh charge to enter the crankcase.
2.  **Downward Stroke (Power & Transfer to Cylinder):**
    *   Combustion pushes the piston down (power stroke).
    *   As the piston descends, it uncovers the exhaust port, allowing exhaust gases to escape.
    *   Shortly after, it uncovers the transfer port, and the compressed charge in the crankcase rushes into the cylinder, pushing out the remaining exhaust gases (scavenging).

## Factors Affecting Valve Timing

*   **Engine Speed:** At higher speeds, longer valve opening durations and greater overlap are generally beneficial to improve volumetric efficiency and scavenging. Variable valve timing (VVT) systems are designed to adjust timing based on engine speed.
*   **Engine Load:** Valve timing can be optimized for different load conditions.
*   **Combustion Characteristics:** The type of fuel and combustion strategy influence optimal timing.
*   **Emissions Regulations:** Strict emission standards may necessitate adjustments to valve timing, particularly overlap, to control pollutants.

## Variable Valve Timing (VVT)

Modern engines often employ VVT systems to dynamically adjust valve timing based on operating conditions. This provides a wider operating range of optimal performance, improved fuel economy, and reduced emissions. Technologies include:

*   **Cam Phasing:** Adjusts the phase (timing) of the camshaft relative to the crankshaft.
*   **Valve Lift Variation:** Changes the amount the valve opens.
*   **Valve Duration Variation:** Changes how long the valve remains open.

## Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

What is the significance of valve overlap in a four-stroke petrol engine?
    a) To reduce heat loss from the cylinder
    b) To improve scavenging and volumetric efficiency
    c) To prevent detonation
    d) To reduce friction

**Answer:** b) To improve scavenging and volumetric efficiency

**Question 2:**

A diesel engine typically has:
    a) Less valve overlap than a petrol engine
    b) More valve overlap than a petrol engine
    c) No valve overlap
    d) The same valve overlap as a petrol engine

**Answer:** a) Less valve overlap than a petrol engine

**Question 3:**

Explain why the intake valve is often closed *after* TDC on the compression stroke in a petrol engine. (Relates to CO2, CO1)

**Answer:** This is done to take advantage of the inertia of the incoming air-fuel mixture. As the piston moves up towards TDC, the momentum of the charge continues to push it into the cylinder, effectively filling the cylinder more completely and increasing volumetric efficiency.

**Question 4:**

How does adjusting valve timing (e.g., through VVT) affect engine emissions? (Relates to CO3)

**Answer:** Adjusting valve timing can significantly impact emissions. For example, increasing valve overlap can improve scavenging but might lead to some fresh charge escaping into the exhaust, increasing unburnt hydrocarbon emissions. Conversely, reducing overlap can minimize this but might reduce the efficiency of exhaust gas expulsion. VVT systems aim to find an optimal balance for different operating conditions to meet emission standards.

**Question 5 (Conceptual Exercise):**

Imagine an engine where the intake valve opens very late (say, 30° after TDC) and closes very early (say, 10° before TDC). Describe the potential effects on engine performance (power, efficiency) and why this timing might be undesirable. (Relates to CO2, K4)

**Answer:**
*   **Power:** Very late intake valve opening and early closing would severely restrict the amount of air-fuel mixture entering the cylinder. This would drastically reduce volumetric efficiency, leading to significantly lower power output.
*   **Efficiency:** While reducing the amount of mixture might seem to reduce fuel consumption, the overall efficiency would likely be poor because the engine would not be able to produce much power for the work required to turn it over. The breathing would be so poor that it might struggle to run.
*   **Undesirable:** This timing is undesirable because it prevents the engine from effectively breathing, severely limiting its ability to generate power. It's the opposite of what is optimized for most engines.

## Important Points to Remember

*   The valve timing diagram is a visual representation of the sequential opening and closing of intake and exhaust valves relative to crankshaft position.
*   Valve timing is critical for optimizing engine performance (power, torque, efficiency) and controlling emissions.
*   The camshaft operates the valves at half the speed of the crankshaft.
*   Key timing events include Intake Valve Opening (IVO), Intake Valve Closing (IVC), Exhaust Valve Opening (EVO), and Exhaust Valve Closing (EVC).
*   Valve overlap is the period when both intake and exhaust valves are open, important for scavenging.
*   Diesel engines generally have less valve overlap than petrol engines.
*   Two-stroke engines use ports controlled by the piston instead of mechanical valves.
*   Variable Valve Timing (VVT) systems dynamically adjust valve timing for improved performance and emissions across a wider operating range.
