# Module 2: Solar Collectors - Flat Plate Collector
## Topic: Classifications of Concentrators

---

## 1. Introduction to Solar Concentrators

Solar concentrators are devices that use reflective or refractive surfaces to focus incoming solar radiation onto a smaller area, thereby increasing the intensity of sunlight. This increased intensity is crucial for achieving higher temperatures required for various thermal applications and for improving the efficiency of some photovoltaic devices. While flat plate collectors typically operate at lower temperatures, concentrators are essential for applications demanding higher temperatures.

**Key Concept:** Concentration Ratio (CR)
The concentration ratio is defined as the ratio of the aperture area of the collector to the area of the absorber.
$$CR = \frac{\text{Aperture Area}}{\text{Absorber Area}}$$
A higher concentration ratio leads to a higher concentration of solar radiation on the absorber.

## 2. Need for Solar Concentration

Flat plate collectors are suitable for low to medium temperature applications (e.g., domestic water heating, space heating). However, many solar thermal applications, such as industrial process heat, solar thermal power generation, and high-efficiency photovoltaics, require significantly higher temperatures. To achieve these higher temperatures efficiently, solar radiation needs to be concentrated.

**Key Concept:** Temperature Requirement vs. Collector Type
*   **Low Temperatures (<100°C):** Flat Plate Collectors
*   **Medium Temperatures (100°C - 250°C):** Evacuated Tube Collectors, Low-Concentration Parabolic Troughs
*   **High Temperatures (>250°C):** High-Concentration Parabolic Troughs, Parabolic Dishes, Central Receivers

**Important Point to Remember:** The primary driver for using concentrators is the need to achieve higher operating temperatures than what flat plate collectors can provide efficiently.

## 3. Classification of Solar Concentrators

Solar concentrators can be broadly classified based on the geometry of their concentrating element and the resulting concentration ratio they can achieve.

### 3.1. Based on Geometry

This classification focuses on the shape of the optical element used to concentrate sunlight.

#### 3.1.1. Line Focus Concentrators

These concentrators focus sunlight onto a line.

*   **Parabolic Trough Collectors (PTCs):**
    *   **Description:** Consist of a parabolic reflector that focuses parallel sun rays onto a focal line. An absorber tube, typically coated with a selective surface and enclosed in a glass envelope (often evacuated), is placed along this focal line.
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Typically ranges from 15 to 100.
    *   **Applications:** Industrial process heat, solar thermal power generation (steam production), desalination.
    *   **Reference:** **Sukhatme & Sukhatme** provides detailed design and performance analysis of parabolic trough collectors.
    *   **Example:** CSP (Concentrated Solar Power) plants using PTCs to generate steam for turbines.

*   **Linear Fresnel Reflectors (LFRs):**
    *   **Description:** Use a series of long, narrow, flat or slightly curved mirror strips arranged in a way that they approximate a parabolic shape. These strips focus sunlight onto a fixed absorber tube placed above them.
    *   **Advantages:** Simpler manufacturing and installation compared to parabolic troughs, can be built with flatter, more manageable mirror segments.
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Typically lower than PTCs, around 3 to 10.
    *   **Applications:** Medium-temperature industrial process heat.
    *   **Reference:** **Boyle, Renewable Energy** discusses LFRs as an alternative to parabolic troughs.

#### 3.1.2. Point Focus Concentrators

These concentrators focus sunlight onto a point.

*   **Parabolic Dish Collectors (PDCs):**
    *   **Description:** Feature a parabolic dish-shaped reflector that focuses sunlight onto a focal point. The absorber (receiver) is placed at this focal point. These are usually mounted on a tracking system to follow the sun precisely.
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Can achieve very high concentration ratios, typically 200 to 2000.
    *   **Applications:** High-temperature industrial processes, solar thermal power generation (especially with Stirling engines), advanced PV applications.
    *   **Reference:** **Goswami, Kreith, & Kreider** thoroughly covers the optical design and thermodynamics of parabolic dish systems.
    *   **Example:** A parabolic dish coupled with a Stirling engine to produce electricity.

*   **Central Receiver Systems (CRSs) / Solar Towers:**
    *   **Description:** Consist of a large field of individually steerable mirrors (heliostats) that track the sun and reflect sunlight onto a central receiver located at the top of a tall tower.
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Very high, allowing for extremely high temperatures.
    *   **Applications:** Large-scale solar thermal power generation, high-temperature research.
    *   **Reference:** **Tiwari, Handbook of Solar Energy** explains the principles and components of central receiver systems.
    *   **Example:** The Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System in California, which uses thousands of heliostats.

*   **Compound Parabolic Concentrators (CPCs):**
    *   **Description:** Utilize two parabolic segments to reflect and concentrate incident radiation onto a target area. Unlike parabolic troughs, CPCs are non-imaging concentrators, meaning they don't form a sharp image of the sun. They can accept sunlight over a wider range of angles.
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Typically low to medium, around 1.5 to 10.
    *   **Applications:** Often used with evacuated tube collectors or PV cells to enhance performance without requiring precise sun tracking. Can achieve moderate concentration ratios with moderate acceptance angles.
    *   **Reference:** **Sukhatme & Sukhatme** provides the geometrical analysis and advantages of CPCs.

### 3.2. Based on Concentration Ratio

This classification categorizes concentrators by the degree of intensity increase.

*   **Low Concentration Concentrators (LCC):**
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Up to approximately 1.5 to 10.
    *   **Examples:** Compound Parabolic Concentrators (CPCs), some Linear Fresnel Reflectors.
    *   **Tracking:** May not require precise tracking or can tolerate some tracking error.
    *   **Application:** Heating applications where moderate temperature rise is sufficient, often integrated with evacuated tubes or flat plates.

*   **Medium Concentration Concentrators (MCC):**
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Approximately 10 to 100.
    *   **Examples:** Parabolic Trough Collectors (PTCs).
    *   **Tracking:** Require single-axis tracking for optimal performance.
    *   **Application:** Industrial process heat, solar thermal power generation.

*   **High Concentration Concentrators (HCC):**
    *   **Concentration Ratio:** Greater than 100, often in the hundreds or thousands.
    *   **Examples:** Parabolic Dish Collectors (PDCs), Central Receiver Systems (CRSs).
    *   **Tracking:** Require precise dual-axis tracking.
    *   **Application:** High-temperature industrial processes, high-efficiency solar thermal power generation, concentrated photovoltaics (CPV).
    *   **Reference:** **Messenger & Ventre, Photovoltaic Systems Engineering** discusses the application of HCCs in CPV systems.

### 3.3. Based on Imaging vs. Non-Imaging

*   **Imaging Concentrators:**
    *   **Description:** These concentrators form a real image of the sun. They require very precise tracking because even small deviations cause the image to move off the absorber.
    *   **Examples:** Parabolic Troughs, Parabolic Dishes, Central Receivers.
    *   **Advantage:** Can achieve very high concentration ratios.

*   **Non-Imaging Concentrators:**
    *   **Description:** These concentrators do not form a real image of the sun. They collect and redirect sunlight over a wider range of incident angles onto the absorber. They are more tolerant of tracking errors.
    *   **Examples:** Compound Parabolic Concentrators (CPCs).
    *   **Advantage:** Less stringent tracking requirements, can accept sunlight over a broader angular range, making them suitable for applications where precise tracking is difficult or expensive.

## 4. Key Features and Considerations for Concentrators

*   **Tracking Systems:** Concentrators, especially high-concentration ones, require tracking systems to keep the reflector pointed accurately at the sun. This can be single-axis (for parabolic troughs) or dual-axis (for parabolic dishes and heliostats).
*   **Optical Efficiency:** This refers to the percentage of incident solar radiation that reaches the absorber. It depends on factors like reflectivity of the mirrors, spillage (when concentrated radiation misses the absorber), and shadowing.
*   **Thermal Losses:** Like flat plate collectors, concentrators also experience thermal losses (convection and radiation) from the absorber. However, the higher temperatures achieved by concentrators lead to proportionally higher losses.
*   **Absorber Design:** The design of the absorber is critical to efficiently absorb concentrated sunlight and transfer heat to the working fluid while minimizing thermal losses. Selective coatings and evacuated enclosures are common.
*   **Cost:** Concentrators, particularly those requiring precise tracking and complex optical designs, can be more expensive to manufacture and install than flat plate collectors.

**Reference:**
*   **Solanki, Solar Photovoltaic Technology and Systems:** Discusses the importance of accurate tracking for concentrating PV systems.
*   **Goswami, Kreith, & Kreider:** Provides in-depth analysis of thermal losses and absorber design for concentrating collectors.

## 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary reason for using solar concentrators instead of flat plate collectors in some solar energy applications?
    a) To achieve lower operating temperatures
    b) To increase the collection area
    c) To achieve higher operating temperatures and concentration ratios
    d) To reduce tracking requirements

**Answer 1:** c) To achieve higher operating temperatures and concentration ratios. Concentrators are used to focus sunlight, increasing its intensity and allowing for higher temperatures, which are not achievable with flat plate collectors.

**Question 2:** Classify the following solar concentrators as either line focus or point focus:
    i) Parabolic Trough Collector
    ii) Parabolic Dish Collector
    iii) Central Receiver System
    iv) Linear Fresnel Reflector

**Answer 2:**
    i) Parabolic Trough Collector - **Line Focus**
    ii) Parabolic Dish Collector - **Point Focus**
    iii) Central Receiver System - **Point Focus**
    iv) Linear Fresnel Reflector - **Line Focus**

**Question 3:** Which type of concentrator is known for its ability to accept sunlight over a wider range of angles and does not form a real image of the sun?
    a) Parabolic Trough Collector
    b) Parabolic Dish Collector
    c) Compound Parabolic Concentrator (CPC)
    d) Central Receiver System

**Answer 3:** c) Compound Parabolic Concentrator (CPC). CPCs are non-imaging concentrators, making them less sensitive to tracking errors and capable of collecting diffuse radiation to some extent.

**Question 4:** Explain the difference between imaging and non-imaging concentrators and provide an example of each.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Imaging Concentrators:** Form a real image of the sun. They require precise tracking to ensure the image stays on the absorber. Examples include Parabolic Troughs and Parabolic Dishes.
*   **Non-Imaging Concentrators:** Do not form a real image of the sun. They collect and redirect sunlight over a wider angular range and are more tolerant of tracking errors. An example is the Compound Parabolic Concentrator (CPC).

**Question 5:** A solar collector has an aperture area of 10 m² and an absorber area of 0.5 m². Calculate its concentration ratio.
    a) 0.05
    b) 2
    c) 20
    d) 100

**Answer 5:** c) 20.
Concentration Ratio (CR) = Aperture Area / Absorber Area = 10 m² / 0.5 m² = 20.

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Concentrators are essential for high-temperature applications.**
*   **Concentration Ratio (CR) is a key parameter:** CR = Aperture Area / Absorber Area.
*   **Classification is based on geometry (line/point focus) and concentration ratio (low/medium/high).**
*   **Parabolic Troughs and Linear Fresnel Reflectors are line focus concentrators.**
*   **Parabolic Dishes and Central Receivers are point focus concentrators.**
*   **CPCs are non-imaging concentrators, offering wider acceptance angles and less stringent tracking.**
*   **Accurate tracking is crucial for imaging concentrators.**
*   **Concentrators increase the intensity of solar radiation, leading to higher temperatures but also potentially higher thermal losses.**
