# Industrial Tribology: Module 4 - Surface Treatments and Coatings

## Topic: Tribological Issues in Tool Wear and Lubrication in Manufacturing

This module focuses on how surface treatments and coatings, specifically heat treatments, surface hardening, and nitriding, influence tribological performance, particularly in the context of tool wear and lubrication in manufacturing processes.

---

### **1. Introduction to Tool Wear in Manufacturing**

**1.1 Definition of Tool Wear:**
Tool wear refers to the progressive removal of material from the surface of a cutting tool, mold, die, or any other manufacturing tool due to mechanical, chemical, or thermal interactions with the workpiece and the surrounding environment.

**1.2 Tribological Significance of Tool Wear:**
Tool wear is a primary tribological concern in manufacturing as it directly impacts:
*   **Tool Life:** The duration a tool can perform its function before replacement.
*   **Surface Finish of Workpiece:** Wear debris and irregular tool surfaces lead to poor workpiece quality.
*   **Dimensional Accuracy:** Wear changes tool geometry, affecting part dimensions.
*   **Cutting Forces and Power Consumption:** Increased wear necessitates higher forces, leading to increased energy usage.
*   **Production Costs:** Frequent tool replacement, scrap parts, and increased energy costs contribute significantly to manufacturing expenses.

**1.3 Types of Tool Wear:**
(Referencing Williams, 2005; Hutchings, 2017)

*   **Abrasive Wear:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Ploughing or cutting action by hard particles present in the workpiece material or introduced as contaminants.
    *   **Examples:** Machining of materials with hard inclusions (e.g., cast iron with graphite, composites), wear on grinding wheels.
*   **Adhesive Wear:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Formation and shearing of microscopic welds between contacting surfaces. Occurs due to surface cleanliness and high contact pressures.
    *   **Examples:** Galling or seizure in metal forming dies, built-up edge (BUE) formation in machining.
*   **Fatigue Wear:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Repetitive stress cycles during contact lead to crack initiation and propagation, resulting in material removal as flakes or particles.
    *   **Examples:** Wear on rolling element bearings, wear in high-cycle stamping operations.
*   **Corrosive Wear:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Chemical or electrochemical reactions between the tool surface and the environment (e.g., cutting fluid, atmosphere) accelerated by mechanical action.
    *   **Examples:** Fretting corrosion in oscillating tools, wear in aggressive chemical environments.
*   **Erosive Wear:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Impact by solid particles or high-velocity fluid streams.
    *   **Examples:** Wear in nozzles for spraying or injection molding, erosion by abrasive slurry.

**1.4 Factors Influencing Tool Wear:**
(Referencing Stachowiak & Batchelor, 2000; Bhushan, 2013)
*   **Material Properties:** Hardness, toughness, ductility, chemical reactivity of both tool and workpiece.
*   **Operating Conditions:** Cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, temperature, pressure, contact time.
*   **Lubrication:** Presence, type, and effectiveness of lubricants.
*   **Tool Geometry:** Rake angles, clearance angles, nose radius.
*   **Environment:** Presence of abrasive particles, corrosive agents, atmosphere.

### **2. Lubrication in Manufacturing Processes**

**2.1 Role of Lubrication:**
Lubrication is critical in manufacturing to:
*   **Reduce Friction:** Minimizing energy loss and heat generation.
*   **Reduce Wear:** Preventing direct contact between surfaces, especially under high loads.
*   **Cooling:** Dissipating heat generated by friction and deformation.
*   **Flushing:** Removing wear debris and contaminants.
*   **Corrosion Protection:** Preventing oxidation and chemical attack.

**2.2 Lubrication Regimes:**
(Referencing Hutchings, 2017; Lansdown, 2003)
*   **Boundary Lubrication:**
    *   **Description:** When lubricant film thickness is comparable to or less than the surface roughness of the contacting bodies. Direct surface contact occurs, but thin adsorbed layers of lubricant molecules provide protection.
    *   **Relevance:** Common in high-pressure, low-speed manufacturing operations like metal forming, where high loads can break down thicker films.
    *   **Additives:** Extreme Pressure (EP) additives are crucial in boundary lubrication to form protective layers at high temperatures.
*   **Mixed Lubrication:**
    *   **Description:** A transitional regime where both thick lubricant films and direct surface contact occur simultaneously.
    *   **Relevance:** Many metal cutting and forming operations operate in this regime.
*   **Hydrodynamic Lubrication:**
    *   **Description:** A continuous film of lubricant completely separates the surfaces, preventing direct contact. The relative motion of the surfaces generates pressure in the lubricant, supporting the load.
    *   **Relevance:** Less common in high-pressure manufacturing tools due to complex geometries and forces, but can be relevant in some rotating or sliding components.

**2.3 Types of Lubricants Used in Manufacturing:**
(Referencing Lansdown, 2003)
*   **Oils:** Mineral oils, synthetic oils (esters, silicones, polyalphaolefins).
*   **Greases:** Oils thickened with soaps or other agents.
*   **Solid Lubricants:** Graphite, MoS₂, PTFE (often incorporated into coatings).
*   **Water-Based Fluids (Coolants/Cutting Fluids):** Emulsions, semi-synthetics, synthetics. These provide cooling and some lubrication, often containing EP additives.

**2.4 Lubricant Selection Criteria:**
(Referencing Lansdown, 2003; CO4)
*   **Operating Temperature and Pressure:** Affects viscosity and film strength.
*   **Contact Speed:** Influences the ability to establish hydrodynamic films.
*   **Material Combination:** Chemical compatibility and surface interactions.
*   **Environmental Considerations:** Biodegradability, toxicity.
*   **Process Requirements:** Cooling capacity, flushing ability, residue.

### **3. Surface Treatments and Coatings for Wear and Lubrication Enhancement**

This section directly addresses the learning outcomes related to surface treatments and their impact on tribological issues, aligning with Course Outcome CO4.

**3.1 Heat Treatments:**
*   **Purpose:** To alter the microstructure and mechanical properties (hardness, toughness, strength) of the tool material, thereby influencing its wear resistance.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Hardening:** Increasing hardness through phase transformations (e.g., formation of martensite in steels).
    *   **Tempering:** Reducing brittleness and improving toughness after hardening by controlled heating.
    *   **Annealing:** Softening material to improve machinability or relieve stresses.
*   **Impact on Tribology:**
    *   **Increased Hardness:** Generally leads to improved resistance against abrasive and adhesive wear.
    *   **Toughness:** Crucial to prevent brittle fracture and chipping, especially in intermittent cutting operations.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Quenching and Tempering:** Standard heat treatment for many tool steels to achieve a balance of hardness and toughness for machining.
    *   **Case Hardening (brief mention here, detailed below):** Creating a hard outer layer while maintaining a tough core.

**3.2 Surface Hardening Techniques:**
These techniques create a hard surface layer on a less hard core, providing excellent wear resistance while retaining toughness.

*   **Carburizing:**
    *   **Description:** Diffusing carbon into the surface of low-carbon steels at high temperatures, followed by quenching and tempering.
    *   **Mechanism:** Increases carbon content in the surface layer, enabling it to be hardened to a high degree.
    *   **Tribological Benefit:** Significantly improves resistance to abrasive and adhesive wear.
    *   **Example:** Gears, shafts, high-wear components in machinery.
*   **Nitriding:**
    *   **Description:** Diffusing nitrogen into the surface of steels (containing nitride-forming elements like Cr, Mo, Al, V) at temperatures typically between 500-575°C. No subsequent quenching is usually required.
    *   **Mechanism:** Forms very hard nitride precipitates (e.g., iron nitrides, alloy nitrides) within the surface layer, creating a hard and wear-resistant case.
    *   **Tribological Benefit:**
        *   **Excellent Wear Resistance:** Particularly effective against abrasive and adhesive wear.
        *   **Improved Fatigue Strength:** Compressive residual stresses induced by nitriding enhance fatigue life.
        *   **Corrosion Resistance:** The surface layer formed can offer good resistance to mild corrosion.
        *   **Reduced Friction:** In some cases, the nitride layer can have a lower coefficient of friction.
    *   **Types of Nitriding:**
        *   **Gas Nitriding:** Involves placing parts in a furnace with a nitrogen-rich atmosphere (e.g., ammonia).
        *   **Salt Bath Nitriding (Liquid Nitriding):** Parts are immersed in a molten salt bath containing nitrogen compounds.
        *   **Plasma Nitriding (Ion Nitriding):** Uses a glow discharge (plasma) to deliver nitrogen to the surface in a vacuum.
    *   **Example:** Cutting tools (drills, milling cutters), engine components (crankshafts, camshafts), gears, dies. (Referencing Davis, 2001; Stachowiak & Batchelor, 2000)
*   **Flame Hardening & Induction Hardening:**
    *   **Description:** Rapid heating of the surface using a flame or induction coil, followed by quenching. Primarily for medium-carbon steels.
    *   **Mechanism:** Creates a shallow hardened layer (martensite) due to rapid heating and cooling.
    *   **Tribological Benefit:** Increases surface hardness for improved wear resistance.
    *   **Limitation:** Can cause distortion, and the depth of hardening is limited.
    *   **Example:** Machine tool slideways, cams, sprockets.

**3.3 Surface Coatings:**
Coatings are applied to the surface to provide specific tribological properties that the bulk material alone cannot achieve.

*   **Purpose:** To enhance wear resistance, reduce friction, provide corrosion protection, and improve the overall performance and life of the tool.
*   **Types of Coatings Relevant to Tool Wear and Lubrication:**
    *   **Hard Coatings (e.g., TiN, TiCN, Al₂O₃, CrN):**
        *   **Mechanism:** Applied via Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD) or Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) to form extremely hard, wear-resistant layers.
        *   **Tribological Benefit:** Significant reduction in abrasive and adhesive wear. Reduced cutting forces, improved surface finish, and extended tool life.
        *   **Lubrication Interaction:** Can reduce the need for external lubrication in some high-speed machining operations by lowering friction and preventing adhesion.
        *   **Example:** Coated carbide inserts for machining, drill bits, punches. (Referencing Davis, 2001; Stachowiak & Batchelor, 2000)
    *   **Lubricious Coatings (e.g., MoS₂, PTFE, WS₂):**
        *   **Mechanism:** Applied as coatings (often bonded or deposited) that provide low friction and self-lubricating properties.
        *   **Tribological Benefit:** Drastically reduce friction, minimizing wear and energy consumption. Crucial in applications where conventional lubrication is difficult or impossible.
        *   **Example:** Bearings, sliding components, molds for plastics and rubber.
    *   **Composite Coatings (e.g., Diamond-like Carbon (DLC) with Cr or W):**
        *   **Mechanism:** Combine the extreme hardness and low friction of DLC with improved adhesion and reduced residual stress from alloying elements.
        *   **Tribological Benefit:** Excellent wear resistance, low friction, and chemical inertness.
        *   **Example:** High-performance cutting tools, medical implants, automotive components.
    *   **Nitrided Coatings:**
        *   **Mechanism:** While nitriding is a diffusion process, it can be considered a surface treatment that creates a hard layer. The term "nitrided coating" might also refer to thin, hard nitride layers formed by PVD/CVD.
        *   **Tribological Benefit:** Enhanced hardness and wear resistance.

*   **LO6: Apply tribological knowledge in industrial applications:** Examples throughout (machining, forming, engine parts) demonstrate practical applications. (K3 - CO5)

**4.2 Highlighted Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Tool wear is a multi-faceted tribological problem** affecting productivity, quality, and cost in manufacturing.
*   **Understanding wear mechanisms (abrasive, adhesive, fatigue, corrosive)** is key to selecting appropriate solutions.
*   **Lubrication is vital** for reducing friction and wear, with different regimes (boundary, mixed, hydrodynamic) requiring different lubricant properties and additives.
*   **Surface treatments (heat treatment, surface hardening like nitriding, carburizing) and coatings** are the primary methods to enhance the tribological performance of manufacturing tools.
*   **Nitriding** offers excellent wear resistance, improved fatigue life, and good corrosion resistance due to the formation of hard nitrides.
*   **Hard coatings (TiN, TiCN)** significantly improve tool life by reducing abrasive and adhesive wear, often allowing for dry machining.
*   **Lubricious coatings** are essential where conventional lubrication is impractical.
*   **The choice of treatment/coating depends heavily on the specific manufacturing process, materials, and operating conditions.**

### **5. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**5.1 Multiple Choice Questions:**

1.  Which type of wear is characterized by the ploughing action of hard particles?
    a) Adhesive wear
    b) Corrosive wear
    c) Abrasive wear
    d) Fatigue wear
    *(Answer: c)*

2.  Nitriding primarily forms hard precipitates of what element in the surface layer of steel?
    a) Carbon
    b) Nitrogen
    c) Oxygen
    d) Sulphur
    *(Answer: b)*

3.  Which lubrication regime is characterized by direct surface contact, where adsorbed lubricant layers provide protection?
    a) Hydrodynamic lubrication
    b) Boundary lubrication
    c) Mixed lubrication
    d) Elastohydrodynamic lubrication
    *(Answer: b)*

4.  Which of the following coatings is primarily applied to reduce friction and provide self-lubrication properties?
    a) TiN
    b) Al₂O₃
    c) MoS₂
    d) TiCN
    *(Answer: c)*

**5.2 Short Answer Questions:**

1.  Briefly explain the main tribological benefits of nitriding compared to simple surface hardening (e.g., flame hardening).
    *(Answer: Nitriding forms hard nitride precipitates that offer superior wear resistance and also induces compressive residual stresses, improving fatigue life. Flame hardening primarily relies on martensite formation, which can be more brittle.)*

2.  What is the role of Extreme Pressure (EP) additives in lubricants? When are they most critical?
    *(Answer: EP additives react with metal surfaces at high temperatures and pressures to form sacrificial protective layers (e.g., sulphides, chlorides) that prevent direct metal-to-metal contact and seizure. They are most critical in boundary and mixed lubrication regimes.)*

3.  Describe two common manufacturing processes where advanced surface treatments or coatings are essential for tool performance.
    *(Answer:
    *   **High-speed machining:** Coated cutting tools (e.g., TiN coated carbide inserts) are essential to withstand high temperatures and wear, extending tool life and allowing for higher cutting speeds.
    *   **Metal forming (e.g., deep drawing, extrusion):** Dies and punches often require hard coatings (like TiN or CrN) or surface treatments (like nitriding) to resist abrasive and adhesive wear caused by high pressures and friction with the workpiece material.)*

**5.3 Problem-Solving Exercise:**

A manufacturer of automotive engine valves is experiencing premature wear on the valve stems, leading to increased friction and reduced engine efficiency. The stems are currently made of hardened steel.

**(a)** Identify the primary tribological mechanisms likely contributing to this wear.
**(b)** Suggest a suitable surface treatment or coating that could significantly improve the wear resistance and reduce friction of the valve stems. Justify your choice based on tribological principles and the function of a valve stem.
**(c)** Briefly explain how the proposed surface treatment/coating addresses the identified wear mechanisms.

**Solution:**

**(a) Likely Tribological Mechanisms:**
*   **Adhesive Wear:** Sliding contact between the valve stem and valve guide, especially at high temperatures and pressures, can lead to adhesion and material transfer.
*   **Abrasive Wear:** Presence of combustion by-products or fine particulate matter in the engine oil can act as abrasives, causing ploughing and cutting of the valve stem surface.
*   **Corrosive Wear:** Aggressive combustion products and high temperatures can lead to corrosive attack, which is exacerbated by the sliding motion.

**(b) Suggested Surface Treatment/Coating:**
A suitable treatment would be **Nitriding** (e.g., Gas Nitriding or Plasma Nitriding) or applying a **Diamond-Like Carbon (DLC) coating**.

*   **Justification for Nitriding:**
    *   **Excellent Wear Resistance:** The hard nitride layer (iron nitrides and alloy nitrides) provides superior resistance to both abrasive and adhesive wear.
    *   **Improved Fatigue Strength:** The compressive residual stresses induced by nitriding can increase the fatigue life of the valve stem, which experiences cyclic loading.
    *   **Corrosion Resistance:** The nitride layer offers good resistance to mild corrosive environments.
    *   **Lubricity:** The surface can exhibit reasonable lubricity, reducing friction.

*   **Justification for DLC Coating:**
    *   **Extremely Low Friction:** DLC coatings have exceptionally low coefficients of friction, significantly reducing wear and energy loss.
    *   **High Hardness and Wear Resistance:** They are very hard and resistant to abrasive wear.
    *   **Chemical Inertness:** Highly resistant to corrosive attack from engine fluids.

**(c) How the Proposed Treatment/Coating Addresses Wear Mechanisms:**

*   **Nitriding:**
    *   **Adhesive Wear:** The hard, stable nitride layer resists the formation and shearing of adhesive junctions.
    *   **Abrasive Wear:** The high hardness of the nitride layer prevents abrasive particles from cutting or ploughing the surface.
    *   **Corrosive Wear:** The surface layer provides a barrier against chemical attack.

*   **DLC Coating:**
    *   **Adhesive Wear:** The low friction and inert nature of DLC minimize adhesion.
    *   **Abrasive Wear:** The extreme hardness of DLC effectively resists abrasive particles.
    *   **Corrosive Wear:** The chemical inertness of DLC prevents corrosive reactions.
