# Industrial Tribology: Module 4 - Surface Treatments and Coatings

## Topic: Coatings: PVD, CVD, Thermal Spray Coatings, and Electroplating

### Introduction to Coatings in Tribology

Coatings play a crucial role in modifying the surface properties of engineering components to enhance their tribological performance. By applying thin layers of specialized materials, we can significantly improve wear resistance, reduce friction, prevent corrosion, and increase surface hardness. This module focuses on four major categories of coating technologies: Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD), Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD), Thermal Spray Coatings, and Electroplating.

**Key Concept:** Surface modification is a primary strategy in tribology to control the interaction between surfaces and their environment. Coatings are a powerful tool for this modification.

**Learning Outcomes Covered:**
*   Explain the principles and applications of PVD coatings.
*   Explain the principles and applications of CVD coatings.
*   Explain the principles and applications of Thermal Spray coatings.
*   Explain the principles and applications of Electroplating.
*   Select and Evaluate Coatings for specific tribological applications (CO4).
*   Apply tribological knowledge of coatings in industrial applications (CO5).

**Course Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1 (K2):** Understanding the fundamental principles of these coating techniques contributes to explaining the basic science behind tribological solutions.
*   **CO2 (K2):** While not directly about characterization, understanding how coatings are applied implies an awareness of the need for surface characterization to verify their quality and performance.
*   **CO3 (K2):** The effectiveness of coatings is often measured using wear measurement techniques.
*   **CO4 (K2):** This entire topic is directly aligned with selecting and evaluating lubricants and surface treatments.
*   **CO5 (K3):** Understanding the strengths and weaknesses of each coating type allows for their application in real-world industrial scenarios.

### 1. Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD) Coatings

**Definition:** PVD is a process where a thin solid film is deposited onto a substrate from a vapor phase. The vapor is generated by physical processes such as evaporation or sputtering.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Vacuum Environment:** PVD processes are carried out in a high vacuum to ensure purity and prevent contamination of the deposited film.
*   **Vapor Generation:** The source material (target) is vaporized.
*   **Transport:** The vaporized material travels to the substrate.
*   **Deposition:** The vapor condenses on the substrate, forming a thin film.
*   **Energetic Ion Bombardment:** Often, the substrate is bombarded with energetic ions to enhance film adhesion, density, and structure.

**Sub-types of PVD:**

**a) Evaporative Coating:**
    *   **Principle:** The source material is heated to its vaporization temperature, typically by electron bombardment or resistive heating, creating a vapor that condenses on the substrate.
    *   **Applications:** Primarily used for decorative coatings, mirrors, and optical coatings. Less common for high-performance tribological applications due to lower adhesion and film density compared to other PVD methods.
    *   **Reference:** While not the primary focus for tribology, the basic principle is described in general coating texts.

**b) Sputtering:**
    *   **Principle:** A plasma of inert gas ions (e.g., Argon) is created. These ions are accelerated towards a target material. When they strike the target, they dislodge atoms from the target, which then travel and deposit onto the substrate.
    *   **Types of Sputtering:**
        *   **DC Sputtering:** Used for conductive targets.
        *   **RF Sputtering:** Used for non-conductive (insulating) targets.
        *   **Magnetron Sputtering:** Uses magnetic fields to confine the plasma near the target, increasing the sputtering rate and efficiency. This is the most common and industrially relevant PVD method for tribological coatings.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Excellent for depositing hard coatings like TiN (Titanium Nitride), CrN (Chromium Nitride), AlTiN (Aluminum Titanium Nitride), TiAlN (Titanium Aluminum Nitride), DLC (Diamond-Like Carbon).
    *   **Advantages:**
        *   Good adhesion and film density.
        *   Can deposit a wide range of materials, including alloys and compounds.
        *   Lower substrate temperature compared to CVD, suitable for heat-sensitive materials.
        *   Excellent control over film thickness and composition.
    *   **Disadvantages:**
        *   Relatively slow deposition rates.
        *   Requires high vacuum.
        *   Limited to line-of-sight deposition (though advances in magnetron sputtering improve coverage).
    *   **Example:** TiN coatings on cutting tools (drills, milling cutters) to improve wear resistance and tool life.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Stachowiak & Batchelor (2000) likely discusses sputtering as a surface modification technique. Bhushan (2013) would also cover advanced coating methods including PVD.

**c) Ion-Assisted Deposition (IAD) / Ion Plating:**
    *   **Principle:** Combines sputtering or evaporation with a simultaneous ion beam bombardment of the growing film. This bombardment increases the kinetic energy of arriving species, promoting denser films, better adhesion, and controlled microstructures.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Enhances the performance of PVD coatings, especially for demanding applications.
    *   **Advantages:** Improved adhesion, density, hardness, and reduced internal stress.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Williams (2005) might detail ion plating as an advanced deposition technique.

**Important Points to Remember (PVD):**
*   Vacuum is essential.
*   Magnetron sputtering is the dominant PVD method for tribology.
*   Low substrate temperature makes it suitable for a wider range of materials.
*   Common tribological coatings: TiN, CrN, AlTiN, DLC.

### 2. Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) Coatings

**Definition:** CVD is a process where a chemical reaction between gaseous precursors at or near a heated substrate surface produces a solid deposit.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Gaseous Precursors:** Specific gases containing the elements or compounds to be deposited are introduced into a reaction chamber.
*   **Chemical Reaction:** The precursors react (thermally decomposed or react with each other) at elevated temperatures.
*   **Substrate Heating:** The substrate is heated to promote the chemical reaction and deposition.
*   **By-product Gases:** Gaseous by-products are removed from the chamber.

**Sub-types of CVD:**

**a) Atmospheric Pressure CVD (APCVD):**
    *   **Principle:** Performed at atmospheric pressure. Simpler equipment but can lead to lower film quality due to gas-phase reactions and poorer control.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Less common for high-performance tribological coatings compared to LPCVD.

**b) Low Pressure CVD (LPCVD):**
    *   **Principle:** Performed at reduced pressure. This minimizes gas-phase reactions, leading to more uniform and denser films with better step coverage.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Widely used for hard coatings like TiN, TiC (Titanium Carbide), TiCN (Titanium Carbonitride), and SiC (Silicon Carbide).
    *   **Advantages:**
        *   Excellent film quality, density, and uniformity.
        *   Good conformal coverage.
        *   Higher throughput possible due to larger batch sizes.
    *   **Disadvantages:**
        *   Requires higher substrate temperatures (typically 700-1000 °C) compared to PVD. This limits its use on heat-sensitive materials.
        *   More complex equipment and precursor handling.
    *   **Example:** TiN coatings on high-speed steel cutting tools to extend tool life. TiC coatings for abrasive wear resistance.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Stachowiak & Batchelor (2000) and Williams (2005) will detail CVD processes. Hutchings (2017) might provide context on materials deposited via CVD for wear resistance.

**c) Plasma-Enhanced CVD (PECVD):**
    *   **Principle:** Uses a plasma to activate the precursor gases, allowing deposition at lower temperatures than thermal CVD.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Useful for depositing amorphous hydrogenated carbon (a-C:H) films, SiN, and SiC at lower temperatures.
    *   **Advantages:** Lower deposition temperatures, good film properties.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can introduce hydrogen into the film, which may affect properties. Plasma uniformity can be a challenge.

**d) Reactive CVD:**
    *   **Principle:** One or more of the precursor gases are highly reactive with the substrate or other gases, promoting deposition. Often used for oxide or nitride coatings.

**Important Points to Remember (CVD):**
*   Relies on chemical reactions of gaseous precursors.
*   Higher substrate temperatures are generally required (except PECVD).
*   LPCVD is preferred for high-quality tribological coatings.
*   Common tribological coatings: TiN, TiC, TiCN, Al2O3 (Aluminum Oxide).

### 3. Thermal Spray Coatings

**Definition:** Thermal spray is a group of coating processes in which molten or semi-molten particles are propelled at high velocity onto a substrate surface, forming a coating.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Thermal Energy Source:** Used to heat and melt the coating material (e.g., flame, plasma arc, electric arc).
*   **Kinetic Energy:** Particles are accelerated towards the substrate.
*   **Deformation and Solidification:** Upon impact, the particles flatten (splat), solidify, and bond to the substrate and previously deposited particles, building up the coating layer by layer.
*   **Lamellar Structure:** Thermal spray coatings typically have a lamellar (layered) microstructure due to the splatting of molten particles.
*   **Porosity:** Can be a characteristic feature, influencing properties.

**Common Thermal Spray Processes:**

**a) Flame Spraying:**
    *   **Principle:** Uses a combustion flame (e.g., oxy-acetylene) to melt and propel the coating material, which is typically in wire or powder form.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Primarily for wear and corrosion resistance. Can deposit materials like steels, stainless steels, carbides (e.g., Tungsten Carbide), and ceramics.
    *   **Advantages:** Relatively simple and inexpensive equipment. Can coat large parts.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Lower particle velocities and temperatures compared to other thermal spray methods, leading to potentially lower bond strength and higher porosity.
    *   **Example:** Applying WC-Co (Tungsten Carbide-Cobalt) coatings on shafts, rolls, and cutting edges for wear resistance.

**b) Plasma Spraying:**
    *   **Principle:** Uses a high-temperature plasma jet (generated by an electric arc between electrodes in a gas) to melt and propel the powder feedstock.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Excellent for depositing a wide range of materials, including ceramics (e.g., Alumina, Zirconia), carbides, and high-melting-point alloys. Widely used for wear-resistant coatings in aerospace, automotive, and manufacturing.
    *   **Advantages:** Very high temperatures, capable of melting refractory materials. High particle velocities. Can produce dense coatings with good bond strength.
    *   **Disadvantages:** More complex and expensive equipment. Can be noisy and require significant fume extraction.
    *   **Example:** Alumina coatings on brake rotors for wear resistance and thermal management. Zirconia coatings for thermal barrier applications.
    *   **Reference:** Hutchings (2017) and Davis (2001) will likely detail thermal spray coatings and their tribological relevance.

**c) High-Velocity Oxy-Fuel (HVOF) Spraying:**
    *   **Principle:** Involves the combustion of fuel (e.g., kerosene, hydrogen, propane) and oxygen within a combustion chamber, creating a high-velocity, high-temperature gas stream that propels the powder feedstock. The gas temperature is lower than plasma but the particle velocity is very high.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Particularly effective for carbide coatings (e.g., WC-Co, WC-Co-Cr) and dense metallic coatings. Known for producing very hard, dense, and wear-resistant coatings with excellent bond strength and low porosity.
    *   **Advantages:** High particle velocities lead to good coating density and adhesion. Relatively lower oxide content compared to plasma spray for some materials. Lower operating temperature than plasma, reducing material degradation.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited to materials that can be processed as powders.
    *   **Example:** WC-Co coatings on hydraulic components, pump shafts, and valve components for severe wear environments.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Stachowiak & Batchelor (2000) and Williams (2005) should cover HVOF. Lansdown (2003) might discuss wear-resistant coatings applied via thermal spray in lubrication contexts.

**d) Electric Arc Spraying (Twin Wire Arc Spraying):**
    *   **Principle:** Two consumable wires of the coating material are fed into an electric arc, melting them. A stream of compressed air then atomizes the molten metal and propels the droplets onto the substrate.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Primarily for wear-resistant and corrosion-resistant coatings, especially metallic ones like zinc, aluminum, and alloys. Also used for hardfacing with steels and carbides.
    *   **Advantages:** High deposition rates, relatively low cost, simple operation.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Typically produces coatings with higher porosity and lower bond strength compared to HVOF or plasma spray.

**Important Points to Remember (Thermal Spray):**
*   Molten or semi-molten particles impact and build up the coating.
*   Coatings have a lamellar structure.
*   HVOF is excellent for hard, dense carbide coatings.
*   Plasma spray is versatile for ceramics and high-melting-point materials.
*   Porosity can be a factor influencing performance.

### 4. Electroplating (Electrochemical Deposition)

**Definition:** Electroplating is an electrochemical process used to deposit a thin layer of a metal onto a conductive substrate.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Electrochemical Cell:** Consists of an anode (positive electrode), a cathode (negative electrode, the substrate), and an electrolyte solution containing ions of the plating metal.
*   **DC Power Supply:** A direct current is applied, causing metal ions from the electrolyte to deposit onto the cathode (substrate).
*   **Anode:** Can be a soluble anode (made of the plating metal, which replenishes ions in the electrolyte) or an inert anode (which does not participate in the reaction).
*   **Electrolyte:** The solution containing the metal ions and other additives to control plating characteristics.

**Common Electroplated Metals and Tribological Relevance:**

**a) Chromium Plating (Hard Chrome):**
    *   **Principle:** Deposited from a chromic acid-based electrolyte.
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Extremely hard, wear-resistant, low-friction coating. Widely used on hydraulic cylinders, engine components, molds, cutting tools, and printing cylinders.
    *   **Advantages:** Excellent hardness (70-70 HRC equivalent), good corrosion resistance, low coefficient of friction (especially when lubricated). Can be deposited in relatively thick layers.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Brittle, prone to cracking under impact. Hexavalent chromium (Cr6+) plating is environmentally hazardous. Electroless nickel-chrome is an alternative.
    *   **Example:** Hard chrome plating on piston rods in hydraulic systems for wear resistance.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Williams (2005) and Davis (2001) will discuss hard chrome plating in detail. Lansdown (2003) might touch upon its use in tribological systems.

**b) Nickel Plating:**
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Electroplated Nickel:** Bright or dull nickel deposits.
        *   **Electroless Nickel (EN):** Deposited by a chemical reduction process, not requiring an external electric current. Offers more uniform thickness, even on complex shapes, and can be alloyed with phosphorus (Ni-P) or boron (Ni-B) for increased hardness and wear resistance.
    *   **Tribological Applications:**
        *   **Hard Chrome Alternative:** EN coatings, especially Ni-P with high phosphorus content, offer excellent hardness, wear resistance, and corrosion resistance.
        *   **Corrosion Protection:** Good for many environments.
        *   **Composite Coatings:** EN can be co-deposited with particles like SiC or PTFE for enhanced tribological properties.
    *   **Advantages:** Good wear and corrosion resistance (especially EN), uniform thickness (EN), can be post-treated (heat-treated) to further increase hardness.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Electroplated nickel can be less hard than hard chrome.
    *   **Example:** EN coatings on automotive engine components and industrial molds.
    *   **Reference:** Davis (2001) is a strong source for plating technologies and their applications.

**c) Cadmium Plating:**
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Excellent corrosion resistance and good anti-galling properties. Used in aerospace and marine environments.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Toxic and environmentally restricted. Often replaced by zinc-nickel alloys or other coatings.

**d) Zinc Plating:**
    *   **Tribological Applications:** Primarily for corrosion protection in mild environments. Less wear-resistant than chrome or nickel.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be less wear-resistant than other plating options.

**Important Points to Remember (Electroplating):**
*   Electrochemical deposition onto a conductive substrate.
*   Hard chrome is a very common and effective wear-resistant coating.
*   Electroless nickel offers uniformity and controllable hardness.
*   Environmental concerns (e.g., hexavalent chromium, cadmium) are important considerations.

### Selection and Evaluation of Coatings for Tribological Applications (CO4)

**Factors to Consider:**

1.  **Tribosystem Requirements:**
    *   **Operating Environment:** Temperature, humidity, presence of corrosive media, abrasives.
    *   **Load:** Light, moderate, severe.
    *   **Speed:** Sliding speed, impact speed.
    *   **Lubrication:** Dry contact, boundary lubrication, hydrodynamic lubrication.
    *   **Substrate Material:** Compatibility, maximum processing temperature.
    *   **Component Geometry:** Complex shapes might favor electroless plating or specific PVD/CVD geometries.
    *   **Desired Tribological Properties:** Low friction, high wear resistance, anti-galling, corrosion resistance.

2.  **Coating Properties:**
    *   **Hardness:** Crucial for resisting abrasive and adhesive wear.
    *   **Toughness/Brittleness:** Impacts resistance to chipping or cracking under shock loads.
    *   **Adhesion:** The strength of the bond between the coating and the substrate is paramount.
    *   **Porosity:** High porosity can lead to substrate corrosion or lubricant leakage.
    *   **Coefficient of Friction:** Influences energy loss and heat generation.
    *   **Oxidation Resistance:** Important for high-temperature applications.
    *   **Chemical Inertness:** Resistance to attack by the environment.

3.  **Cost:**
    *   Coating material cost.
    *   Process cost (equipment, operation, time).
    *   Coating thickness and lifespan.

**Evaluation Methods (CO2 & CO3):**

*   **Surface Characterization:**
    *   **Microscopy (SEM, Optical):** To examine surface topography, microstructure, defects, and adhesion interfaces.
    *   **Hardness Testing (Vickers, Rockwell):** To measure surface hardness.
    *   **Adhesion Testing (Scratch Test, Tape Test, Chevron Notching):** To quantify coating-substrate bond strength.
    *   **Thickness Measurement (Profilometry, X-ray Fluorescence):** To ensure the coating meets specifications.
    *   **Compositional Analysis (EDS, XPS):** To verify coating material and detect impurities.
*   **Tribological Testing:**
    *   **Pin-on-Disk/Ball-on-Disk:** To measure friction coefficient and wear rate under controlled sliding conditions.
    *   **Block-on-Ring:** Similar to pin-on-disk.
    *   **Reciprocating Wear Testers:** For simulating oscillatory motion.
    *   **Four-Ball Wear Tester:** To assess wear under high Hertzian stress.
    *   **Taber Abraser:** For abrasive wear resistance testing.

**Examples of Coating Selection:**

*   **Cutting Tools:** TiN, AlTiN, TiCN (PVD/CVD) for high hardness and wear resistance at elevated temperatures during machining.
*   **Hydraulic Cylinder Rods:** Hard Chrome or HVOF sprayed WC-Co for high wear resistance and low friction.
*   **Engine Piston Rings:** PVD coatings (e.g., CrN, DLC) or thermal spray coatings for wear and friction reduction.
*   **Bearings/Seals:** DLC (PVD) or ceramic coatings (Plasma Spray) for low friction and high wear resistance, especially in dry or low-lubrication conditions.
*   **Corrosion Protection with Wear Resistance:** Electroless Nickel, or PVD CrN, or HVOF sprayed stainless steels.

**Reference:** Stachowiak & Batchelor (2000) and Williams (2005) provide comprehensive overviews of these selection criteria and testing methods. Hutchings (2017) offers detailed insights into wear mechanisms and how coatings address them.

### Industrial Applications (CO5)

Coatings are ubiquitous in modern industry to solve tribological challenges:

*   **Automotive:** Engine components (pistons, rings, valves), transmission parts, brake systems, suspension components.
*   **Aerospace:** Turbine blades, landing gear components, actuators, bearings.
*   **Manufacturing:** Cutting tools, molds, dies, gears, bearings, pumps.
*   **Energy:** Power generation turbine components, drilling equipment, pipeline coatings.
*   **Medical Devices:** Orthopedic implants (e.g., TiN coatings on hip joints for wear resistance), surgical instruments.
*   **Printing and Paper Industry:** Rollers, cylinders for wear and corrosion resistance.

### Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** Which PVD technique is most commonly used for depositing hard tribological coatings like TiN and CrN?
    *   a) Evaporative Coating
    *   b) DC Sputtering
    *   c) Magnetron Sputtering
    *   d) Ion Plating

**Answer 1:** c) Magnetron Sputtering. It offers higher deposition rates and better film properties compared to basic sputtering or evaporation. Ion plating is a variation that enhances PVD properties.

**Question 2:** What is a key advantage of CVD coatings over PVD coatings in terms of substrate temperature?
    *   a) CVD requires significantly lower substrate temperatures.
    *   b) CVD typically requires higher substrate temperatures.
    *   c) Both processes operate at similar low temperatures.
    *   d) Substrate temperature is irrelevant for both processes.

**Answer 2:** b) CVD typically requires higher substrate temperatures. This is a major consideration when choosing between CVD and PVD, as CVD cannot be used on heat-sensitive materials.

**Question 3:** Which thermal spray process is best known for producing very hard, dense Tungsten Carbide-Cobalt coatings with excellent wear resistance and low porosity?
    *   a) Flame Spraying
    *   b) Plasma Spraying
    *   c) HVOF Spraying
    *   d) Electric Arc Spraying

**Answer 3:** c) HVOF Spraying. The high particle velocities and controlled combustion in HVOF are ideal for achieving dense carbide coatings.

**Question 4:** For a component requiring excellent wear resistance and a low coefficient of friction, especially in dry contact conditions, which coating would be a strong candidate?
    *   a) Zinc plating
    *   b) Hard Chrome plating
    *   c) Diamond-Like Carbon (DLC) (PVD)
    *   d) Electroplated Nickel

**Answer 4:** c) Diamond-Like Carbon (DLC). DLC coatings are renowned for their extremely low friction and high wear resistance, making them suitable for dry or low-lubrication tribosystems. Hard chrome also offers good wear resistance and lower friction than nickel or zinc, but DLC typically excels in low friction.

**Question 5:** Describe one significant tribological challenge addressed by applying a coating, and name a suitable coating technology and specific coating material for that challenge, justifying your choice.

**Answer 5:**
    *   **Tribological Challenge:** Severe abrasive wear on a component operating in a dusty environment.
    *   **Suitable Coating Technology:** Thermal Spraying (specifically HVOF).
    *   **Specific Coating Material:** Tungsten Carbide-Cobalt (WC-Co).
    *   **Justification:** WC-Co is extremely hard due to the presence of tungsten carbide particles. HVOF spraying provides high particle velocities and energy, leading to dense coatings with excellent adhesion and minimal porosity, which are critical for resisting abrasive particles and preventing lubricant loss (if any). The hard carbide phase directly combats abrasion, while the cobalt matrix provides some ductility and toughness.

### Important Points to Remember (Overall Topic)

*   **Coating Purpose:** Modify surface properties for improved tribological performance (wear, friction, corrosion).
*   **PVD:** Vacuum-based, physical processes (sputtering, evaporation). Lower substrate temps. Good for hard coatings like TiN, CrN, DLC.
*   **CVD:** Gas-phase chemical reactions at elevated temps. Good for hard coatings like TiN, TiC, TiCN. Higher substrate temps than PVD.
*   **Thermal Spray:** Molten/semi-molten particles impact substrate. Lamellar structure. HVOF excellent for WC-Co, Plasma versatile for ceramics.
*   **Electroplating:** Electrochemical deposition. Hard Chrome for high hardness/wear resistance. Electroless Nickel for uniform wear/corrosion resistance.
*   **Selection:** Consider operating conditions, substrate, desired properties, and cost.
*   **Evaluation:** Use surface characterization and tribological testing to verify performance.
*   **Environmental Impact:** Be mindful of hazardous materials (e.g., Cr6+, Cadmium).
