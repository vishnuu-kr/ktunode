# Industrial Tribology: Module 2 - Wear and Surface Examination Techniques

## Module Overview

This module delves into the critical aspect of **wear** in tribological systems, exploring its various forms and the fundamental techniques used to examine and understand the surfaces involved. Understanding wear mechanisms is crucial for predicting component lifespan, designing for durability, and selecting appropriate materials and surface treatments. This module aligns with **CO1** (explain fundamental principles of Tribology) by introducing a core tribological phenomenon and **CO2** (understand Surface characterisation techniques for tribological investigations) by detailing the methods used to analyze wear-induced surface changes.

---

## 2.1 Types of Wear

Wear is the progressive loss of material from a surface due to relative motion between surfaces in contact. It is a complex phenomenon influenced by numerous factors including load, speed, environment, and material properties.

### 2.1.1 Adhesive Wear

**Definition:** Adhesive wear occurs when microscopic junctions form between surfaces in relative motion, and these junctions are sheared or fractured during sliding. It is essentially a consequence of material transfer and removal due to strong intermolecular forces and localized plastic deformation.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Adhesion:** The tendency of dissimilar surfaces to bond to each other. This arises from attractive forces between atoms/molecules at the interface.
*   **Real Area of Contact:** The actual microscopic area where asperities of the two surfaces are in contact, which is significantly smaller than the apparent (nominal) area of contact.
*   **Junction Formation:** When asperities come into contact under load, they deform elastically and plastically, creating microscopic junctions.
*   **Shearing/Fracture of Junctions:** During relative motion, the weakest point of these junctions either shears within one of the materials or fractures at the interface, leading to material removal.
*   **Material Transfer:** In adhesive wear, material can be transferred from one surface to another. This is often seen as a mirror image of the worn area on the opposing surface.
*   **Third Bodies:** Contaminants or transferred material can act as "third bodies" that influence adhesion and subsequent wear.

**Mechanisms:**

1.  **Elastic and Plastic Deformation:** Under load, asperities deform.
2.  **Junction Formation:** High pressures at asperity contacts cause localized melting or cold welding (metallurgical bonding).
3.  **Shear:** Relative motion generates stresses that exceed the shear strength of the junctions.
4.  **Material Removal:** Fractured or sheared material detaches as wear debris.

**Factors Influencing Adhesive Wear:**

*   **Surface Energy:** Higher surface energy leads to stronger adhesion.
*   **Surface Hardness:** Softer materials are more prone to plastic deformation and junction formation.
*   **Load and Speed:** Higher loads and speeds generally increase adhesive wear.
*   **Surface Finish:** Smoother surfaces can sometimes lead to increased adhesion if there are fewer asperities to deform.
*   **Presence of Contaminants/Lubricants:** Lubricants reduce adhesion by forming a barrier layer.

**Examples:**

*   Scuffing or galling of engine components (pistons, cylinder liners) under boundary lubrication conditions.
*   Wear in unlubricated sliding components.
*   Cold welding of surfaces in vacuum environments.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Emphasize the role of atomic forces and the formation of strong metallurgical bonds at contact points. They discuss the "Archard's Law" for adhesive wear, which relates wear volume to load, distance, and a wear coefficient.
*   **Bhushan:** Highlights the importance of surface chemistry and the formation of transfer films, which can either protect or accelerate wear depending on their properties.
*   **Williams:** Discusses the contribution of real area of contact and the shear strength of the junctions in determining the extent of adhesive wear.
*   **Hutchings:** Explains that adhesive wear is dominant in vacuum or dry sliding conditions and can lead to significant material loss and surface damage.

### 2.1.2 Abrasive Wear

**Definition:** Abrasive wear occurs when hard particles or hard asperities on one surface slide against another, causing cutting, grooving, or plowing action, leading to material removal.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Hard Particles:** These can be embedded in one of the surfaces or present as contaminants in the environment or lubricant.
*   **Two-Body Abrasion:** Hard asperities on one surface (e.g., a rough rotating disc) scratch and remove material from a softer surface.
*   **Three-Body Abrasion:** Loose abrasive particles are introduced between two surfaces, acting as intermediaries to cause wear on both.
*   **Plowing:** The hard particle indents the surface and displaces material sideways.
*   **Cutting:** The hard particle penetrates the surface and removes a chip of material.
*   **Grooving/Scoring:** The repeated action of abrasive particles creates parallel grooves on the worn surface.

**Mechanisms:**

1.  **Penetration:** The abrasive particle penetrates the surface.
2.  **Deformation/Displacement:** Material is deformed around the particle.
3.  **Fracture/Chip Formation:** If the particle's penetration is deep enough and the material is brittle, a chip is removed. If it's more ductile, plowing occurs.

**Factors Influencing Abrasive Wear:**

*   **Hardness Difference:** A significant hardness difference between the abrasive particle and the surface being worn is crucial. The abrasive must be harder than the surface.
*   **Size and Shape of Abrasive Particles:** Larger, sharper particles are generally more aggressive.
*   **Concentration of Abrasive Particles:** Higher concentration leads to increased wear.
*   **Load and Sliding Speed:** Higher loads and speeds tend to increase abrasive wear.
*   **Angle of Attack:** The angle at which the abrasive particle interacts with the surface affects the wear mechanism (cutting vs. plowing).

**Examples:**

*   Wear of seals and bearings in dusty environments.
*   Cutting tools wearing down during machining.
*   Dirt or grit contaminating a lubricated system.
*   Sandpapering or polishing operations.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Detail the two main mechanisms: two-body and three-body abrasion, and the role of abrasive hardness and geometry.
*   **Bhushan:** Discusses the impact of abrasive particle morphology (size, shape, hardness) and their distribution on wear rates.
*   **Williams:** Provides insights into the forces involved in abrasive particle penetration and the geometry of the wear grooves formed.
*   **Hutchings:** Explains that abrasive wear is often the dominant wear mode when hard contaminants are present and can lead to rapid material removal.

### 2.1.3 Corrosive Wear

**Definition:** Corrosive wear is the synergistic effect of mechanical action and chemical attack on a surface, leading to material removal. The mechanical action often accelerates the chemical corrosion process.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Tribochemical Reactions:** Chemical reactions that occur at the sliding interface, often promoted by the elevated temperatures and mechanical stresses generated by friction.
*   **Surface Films:** Protective oxide or reaction layers can form on surfaces. Mechanical action can either remove these films, exposing fresh material to chemical attack, or it can break down passive films, leading to accelerated corrosion.
*   **Electrochemical Corrosion:** If an electrolyte is present (e.g., in a corrosive environment or due to lubricant breakdown products), electrochemical cells can form at the sliding interface, leading to anodic dissolution of material.
*   **Synergism:** The combined effect of wear and corrosion is greater than the sum of their individual effects.
*   **Wear Accelerated Corrosion:** Mechanical wear can expose fresh, reactive surfaces to corrosive environments, or it can remove protective passivation layers.

**Mechanisms:**

1.  **Chemical Attack:** The environment or lubricant reacts chemically with the surface material.
2.  **Mechanical Removal:** Sliding action removes the reaction products or the corroded layer.
3.  **Exposing Fresh Surface:** This action exposes more of the base material to further chemical attack, creating a cycle.

**Factors Influencing Corrosive Wear:**

*   **Corrosive Environment:** Presence of aggressive chemicals (acids, bases, salts, oxygen).
*   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures can accelerate chemical reactions.
*   **Load and Speed:** Can influence the rate of film removal and exposure of fresh surfaces.
*   **Material Properties:** Susceptibility of the material to corrosion.
*   **Lubricant Properties:** Presence of additives that can either promote or inhibit corrosion.

**Examples:**

*   Wear of engine components in contact with combustion byproducts.
*   Corrosion of gears in humid environments.
*   Wear of prosthetic joints in biological fluids.
*   Wear in hydraulic systems exposed to water contamination.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Introduce the concept of tribocorrosion and the interplay between electrochemical and mechanical processes.
*   **Bhushan:** Discusses the role of surface chemistry and the formation and removal of tribofilms, which can be a source of corrosive wear.
*   **Williams:** Explains how mechanical action can break down passive layers and accelerate electrochemical corrosion.
*   **Hutchings:** Highlights that corrosive wear is a complex process where chemical degradation and mechanical removal work together, often leading to significantly higher material loss than either process alone.

### 2.1.4 Surface Fatigue Wear

**Definition:** Surface fatigue wear occurs due to the repeated stress cycles applied to asperities at the contact interface, leading to the initiation and propagation of cracks, and eventual detachment of wear particles.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Cyclic Loading:** Repeated application of normal and tangential forces at the points of contact.
*   **Asperity Deformation:** Under load, asperities deform elastically and plastically.
*   **Stress Concentration:** High stresses occur at the tips and roots of asperities.
*   **Crack Initiation:** Repeated stressing can lead to the formation of microscopic cracks within the material.
*   **Crack Propagation:** These cracks grow with each subsequent stress cycle.
*   **Debris Detachment:** Eventually, detached wear particles are formed as pieces of the surface break off.
*   **Surface Fatigue:** Similar to bulk fatigue, but occurring at or near the surface due to contact stresses.

**Mechanisms:**

1.  **Contact Stress:** Real contact between surfaces involves high localized stresses at asperity contacts.
2.  **Cyclic Deformation:** Repeated sliding and normal loading causes cyclic stress and strain in the asperities.
3.  **Crack Nucleation:** Fatigue cracks initiate at points of high stress concentration, often below the surface (subsurface crack initiation) or at surface defects.
4.  **Crack Growth:** Cracks propagate through the material under repeated loading.
5.  **Particle Detachment:** When a crack reaches the surface or links up with other cracks, a piece of material is detached as wear debris.

**Factors Influencing Surface Fatigue Wear:**

*   **Contact Pressure/Load:** Higher loads increase the stress cycles.
*   **Surface Roughness:** Affects stress concentration.
*   **Material Properties:** Toughness, hardness, and fatigue strength are critical.
*   **Subsurface Microstructure:** Defects or inclusions can act as crack initiation sites.
*   **Lubrication:** Can influence the stress distribution and the crack propagation environment.
*   **Sliding Speed:** Can influence the rate of heat generation and stress cycles.

**Examples:**

*   Spalling or pitting of rolling element bearings.
*   Surface cracking and material detachment in gears and cams.
*   Wear on the track and ball interface in a ball bearing.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Discuss fatigue as a result of cyclic subsurface stresses caused by asperity interactions, leading to pit formation and spalling.
*   **Bhushan:** Explains how Hertzian contact stresses (and deviations from them due to roughness) can initiate subsurface cracks.
*   **Williams:** Focuses on the role of elastic and plastic deformation of asperities and the cyclic nature of the stresses.
*   **Hutchings:** Differentiates between surface crack initiation and subsurface crack initiation and their respective propagation paths leading to flake-like debris.

## 2.2 Techniques of Surface Examination

To understand the mechanisms of wear, it is essential to examine the surfaces of components before and after tribological testing. Surface examination techniques allow us to visualize surface topography, identify wear features, and analyze material transfer or removal.

### 2.2.1 Optical Microscopy

**Definition:** Optical microscopy (also known as light microscopy) uses visible light and a system of lenses to magnify images of specimens. It is a fundamental tool for initial surface inspection and identifying macroscopic wear features.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Magnification:** The ratio of the image size to the actual object size.
*   **Resolution:** The smallest distance between two points that can be distinguished as separate. Limited by the wavelength of light.
*   **Objective Lenses:** Lenses closest to the specimen, providing primary magnification.
*   **Eyepiece (Ocular Lens):** Magnifies the image formed by the objective lens.
*   **Bright-field Microscopy:** The most common mode, where light passes through the specimen.
*   **Dark-field Microscopy:** Useful for visualizing transparent or very smooth surfaces by illuminating them from the side, making defects or irregularities appear bright against a dark background.
*   **Nomarski Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) Microscopy:** Provides a 3D-like appearance of surface topography, highlighting subtle surface features and variations in height.

**How it works:**

Light from a source is focused onto or through the specimen. The transmitted or reflected light passes through a series of lenses that magnify the image, which is then viewed by the observer. For opaque materials (like worn surfaces), reflected light microscopy is used.

**Capabilities for Wear Analysis:**

*   **Visualization of macroscopic wear features:** Grooves, scratches, pits, cracks, and areas of material transfer can be observed.
*   **Surface texture assessment:** General roughness and the presence of distinct topographical features.
*   **Measurement of feature size:** Using calibrated reticles in the eyepiece or image analysis software.
*   **Identification of wear debris:** Observing particles that have detached from the surface.

**Limitations:**

*   **Limited resolution:** Typically around 0.2 micrometers ($\mu$m) due to the wavelength of visible light. Cannot resolve features smaller than this.
*   **Depth of field:** Relatively shallow, making it difficult to focus on very rough surfaces.
*   **Cannot directly analyze elemental composition or crystallographic structure.**

**Examples:**

*   Examining the shallow grooves on a surface due to mild abrasion.
*   Identifying the characteristic pitting in bearing raceways due to fatigue.
*   Observing large wear debris particles.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Cover basic principles of optical microscopy and its use in observing surface topography and wear mechanisms.
*   **Bhushan:** Mentions its utility for initial assessment and qualitative observation of wear scars and features.
*   **Williams:** Describes its role in the early stages of surface analysis and identification of gross damage.

### 2.2.2 Electron Microscopy

**Definition:** Electron microscopy uses a beam of electrons to scan or transmit through a specimen to create a magnified image. It offers much higher resolution and magnification than optical microscopy.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Electron Beam:** High-energy electrons are used instead of light.
*   **Electromagnetic Lenses:** Used to focus and control the electron beam.
*   **Higher Resolution:** Electrons have much shorter wavelengths than photons, allowing for resolution down to the nanometer (nm) or even sub-nanometer range.
*   **Higher Magnification:** Can achieve magnifications of millions of times.
*   **Vacuum Environment:** Specimens must be examined in a high vacuum to prevent scattering of electrons by air molecules.

#### 2.2.2.1 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

**Definition:** SEM scans a focused electron beam across the surface of a specimen. It detects various signals emitted from the surface (e.g., secondary electrons, backscattered electrons) to form an image of the topography and composition.

**How it works:**

1.  An electron gun generates a beam of electrons.
2.  The beam is focused by electromagnetic lenses and scanned across the specimen's surface in a raster pattern.
3.  As the electron beam interacts with the surface, it generates various signals:
    *   **Secondary Electrons (SE):** Low-energy electrons emitted from the outermost atomic layers. Highly sensitive to surface topography, providing detailed 3D-like images.
    *   **Backscattered Electrons (BSE):** High-energy electrons from the incident beam that are scattered back out of the specimen. Their yield is sensitive to the atomic number of the elements, allowing for compositional contrast (heavier elements appear brighter).
    *   **X-rays:** Characteristic X-rays are emitted by the specimen, allowing for elemental analysis (Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy - EDS or Wavelength Dispersive Spectroscopy - WDS).

**Capabilities for Wear Analysis:**

*   **High-resolution imaging of surface topography:** Revealing fine details of wear features like micro-cracks, abrasive wear grooves, and fatigue pits.
*   **Elemental analysis (EDS/WDS):** Identifying the chemical composition of wear debris, transferred material, or surface layers, helping to determine wear mechanisms (e.g., presence of transferred material from the opposing surface, oxidation products).
*   **Imaging of fine wear debris particles.**
*   **Observation of fracture surfaces.**

**Limitations:**

*   **Requires conductive specimens:** Non-conductive samples need to be coated with a thin layer of conductive material (e.g., gold, carbon), which can obscure very fine surface details.
*   **Vacuum environment:** Samples must be stable in a vacuum.
*   **Limited depth of field for extremely rough surfaces compared to some profilometry techniques.**

**Examples:**

*   Observing the fine chisel-like edges of abrasive wear particles.
*   Identifying subsurface crack initiation sites in fatigue wear.
*   Analyzing the chemical composition of wear debris to determine if it's oxidized.
*   Visualizing the smooth, polished surface of a component that has undergone severe adhesive wear.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Provide detailed descriptions of SEM operation and its application in tribology, particularly for analyzing wear surfaces and debris.
*   **Bhushan:** Emphasizes the importance of SEM-EDS for elemental analysis to understand tribochemical reactions and material transfer.
*   **Williams:** Discusses SEM's role in revealing the fine morphology of wear scars and wear particles.
*   **Hutchings:** Highlights SEM as a primary tool for detailed examination of wear surfaces, enabling identification of specific wear mechanisms.

#### 2.2.2.2 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

**Definition:** TEM uses a beam of electrons that is transmitted *through* an ultra-thin specimen. It is used to study the internal structure of materials, including microstructural defects and crystal structures, at very high resolution.

**How it works:**

1.  A highly focused electron beam passes through an extremely thin specimen (typically < 100 nm).
2.  Electrons that are transmitted through the specimen are focused by electromagnetic lenses to form an image.
3.  Variations in the electron beam intensity, due to scattering and diffraction by the specimen's internal structure, form the image.

**Capabilities for Wear Analysis:**

*   **Ultra-high resolution imaging of crystallographic defects:** Dislocations, grain boundaries, and precipitates within the material near the wear surface can be observed.
*   **Analysis of the microstructure of wear debris:** Understanding the formation mechanisms of very fine debris.
*   **Electron Diffraction:** Can be used to determine the crystallographic orientation and phase identification of very small areas on the surface or within debris.

**Limitations:**

*   **Specimen preparation is extremely demanding and time-consuming:** Requires ultra-thinning (e.g., ion milling, electropolishing), which can introduce artifacts.
*   **Limited to very thin samples or very small particles.**
*   **Does not directly image surface topography of bulk samples.**

**Examples:**

*   Observing the dislocation structure within a wear track formed by severe plastic deformation.
*   Analyzing the crystal structure of wear debris particles.
*   Investigating the nature of sub-surface microstructural changes due to fatigue.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Mention TEM for studying the substructure of materials and fine wear debris, but it's generally less common for routine surface wear examination than SEM.
*   **Bhushan:** Briefly touches upon TEM for analyzing the internal structure of wear debris.
*   **Williams:** May refer to TEM for detailed microstructural investigations related to wear mechanisms.

### 2.2.3 Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

**Definition:** AFM is a type of scanning probe microscopy that maps surface topography by "feeling" the surface with a very sharp tip mounted on a cantilever. It provides extremely high-resolution 3D surface profiles.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Sharp Tip:** A microscopic tip (radius typically a few nanometers) attached to a flexible cantilever.
*   **Cantilever Deflection:** As the tip scans the surface, forces between the tip and the surface cause the cantilever to deflect.
*   **Deflection Detection:** A laser beam is reflected off the back of the cantilever onto a photodetector to measure the deflection.
*   **Feedback Loop:** A feedback system maintains a constant cantilever deflection (or constant tip-surface separation), allowing the system to track the surface topography and generate a height map.
*   **Operating Modes:**
    *   **Contact Mode:** The tip is in continuous contact with the surface. Can exert forces that may affect soft or fragile surfaces.
    *   **Tapping Mode (Intermittent Contact Mode):** The cantilever oscillates at or near its resonant frequency. The tip intermittently "taps" the surface. This reduces lateral forces and is suitable for softer or more delicate surfaces.
    *   **Non-contact Mode:** The tip oscillates above the surface, sensing long-range forces. Offers the least tip-surface interaction but can be sensitive to contamination.

**Capabilities for Wear Analysis:**

*   **Extremely high-resolution 3D surface profiling:** Can measure features at the nanometer and sub-nanometer scale.
*   **Quantitative measurement of surface roughness:** Providing precise parameters like Ra, Rq, and peak heights.
*   **Direct measurement of wear scar depth and profile:** Allows for accurate quantification of material loss in nanoscale wear tests.
*   **Visualization of crack initiation and propagation at the nanoscale.**
*   **Analysis of surface films and their thickness.**
*   **Can operate in air or liquid environments:** Useful for studying wear in lubricated or corrosive conditions without vacuum.
*   **Can perform force spectroscopy:** Measuring adhesion forces between the tip and surface.

**Limitations:**

*   **Scan area is typically small (micrometers).**
*   **Scan speed is relatively slow.**
*   **Tip convolution:** The finite size of the tip can distort the imaging of very sharp features or narrow valleys.
*   **Susceptible to vibrations.**

**Examples:**

*   Measuring the depth of nanoscale wear tracks generated in micro-tribology experiments.
*   Quantifying the roughness change of a surface after polishing.
*   Observing the formation of tribofilms or wear debris layers with nanometer precision.
*   Studying the initial stages of crack formation on a highly stressed surface.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Stachowiak & Batchelor:** Discuss AFM as a crucial technique for nanoscale surface characterization and tribological measurements.
*   **Bhushan:** Highlights AFM's ability to measure surface topography with nanometer precision, enabling quantification of wear at the micro and nanoscale.
*   **Williams:** May mention AFM for its advanced capabilities in surface metrology.
*   **Hutchings:** Emphasizes AFM's role in understanding the fundamental mechanisms of friction and wear at the nanoscale.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Wear is a complex phenomenon** influenced by multiple factors, and often a combination of wear mechanisms occurs simultaneously.
*   **Surface examination is crucial** for identifying the dominant wear mechanism and understanding component degradation.
*   **Optical microscopy** provides a good starting point for initial surface inspection.
*   **SEM** offers higher resolution and the ability to perform elemental analysis, crucial for understanding material transfer and chemical changes.
*   **TEM** is used for in-depth microstructural analysis and studying very fine debris.
*   **AFM** provides unparalleled nanoscale 3D surface topography and roughness measurements, essential for micro- and nanotribology.
*   The choice of surface examination technique depends on the scale of features to be observed, the information required (topography, composition, microstructure), and the nature of the specimen.

## Practice Questions

**Question 1:** Differentiate between two-body and three-body abrasive wear, providing an example for each.
**Answer:**
*   **Two-body abrasive wear:** Occurs when the abrasive is fixed to one of the surfaces. Example: A rough grinding wheel (abrasive fixed to the wheel) abrading a softer material.
*   **Three-body abrasive wear:** Occurs when abrasive particles are free to move between the surfaces. Example: Sand or grit particles trapped between a rotating shaft and a stationary seal.

**Question 2:** Explain the synergistic effect in corrosive wear. Which surface examination technique would be most useful for identifying the chemical composition of corrosion products on a worn surface?
**Answer:**
The synergistic effect in corrosive wear means that the combination of mechanical action and chemical attack leads to a higher rate of material removal than the sum of the individual effects. Mechanical action can remove protective passive layers or expose fresh material, while the corrosive environment attacks this freshly exposed surface. This cycle leads to accelerated wear.
**Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) with Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)** would be most useful for identifying the chemical composition of corrosion products on a worn surface.

**Question 3:** A critical component in a high-speed bearing shows signs of surface degradation characterized by the formation of small pits and eventual flaking of material. Which type of wear is most likely responsible? What surface examination technique would you primarily use to confirm this and observe the early stages of damage?
**Answer:**
The formation of pits and flaking due to repeated stress cycles is characteristic of **surface fatigue wear**.
To confirm this and observe the early stages of damage, **Optical Microscopy** could be used for initial assessment of pits and flakes. For a more detailed observation of the pit morphology, crack initiation sites (possibly subsurface), and the microstructural changes, **Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)** would be the primary technique. If very fine cracks or subsurface features need to be investigated, **Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)** might be considered, although it's less common for routine surface wear analysis. For precise nanoscale depth measurements of pits, **Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)** would be ideal.

**Question 4:** What is the primary advantage of using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) over Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) for characterizing the topography of a very soft, polymeric wear surface?
**Answer:**
The primary advantage of AFM over SEM for a soft polymeric surface is its ability to perform imaging with significantly lower tip-surface interaction forces, especially when used in **Tapping Mode** or **Non-contact Mode**. SEM, while providing high resolution, relies on electron beam interaction which can sometimes cause charging or surface damage on soft, non-conductive materials, and the necessary conductive coating can alter the surface. AFM's ability to map topography with minimal force disturbance makes it ideal for preserving the integrity of delicate surfaces. Additionally, AFM provides direct 3D height information with nanometer resolution, whereas SEM's 3D impression is derived from secondary electron intensity.

**Question 5:** List three key factors that influence the rate of abrasive wear.
**Answer:**
1.  **Hardness of the abrasive particles relative to the surface.**
2.  **Size and shape of the abrasive particles.**
3.  **Load applied.**
4.  **Sliding speed.**
5.  **Concentration of abrasive particles.**
(Any three of these are acceptable).
