# Industrial Safety Engineering: Module 1 - Introduction to Industrial Safety: Concept of Safety

## Topic: Accident Causing Mechanisms

This topic delves into the fundamental reasons behind industrial accidents, exploring the intricate chain of events and underlying factors that lead to unsafe occurrences. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for developing effective preventive strategies and achieving the overarching goal of workplace safety.

---

### **1. Concept of Safety**

**Definition:** Safety, in the context of industrial engineering, refers to the condition of being protected from or unlikely to cause danger, risk, or injury. It is the state of being free from physical, mental, or environmental harm.

**Key Principles:**

*   **Proactive Approach:** Safety is not merely the absence of accidents but a continuous, proactive process of identifying, assessing, and controlling risks.
*   **Shared Responsibility:** Safety is a collective responsibility involving management, supervisors, and employees.
*   **Continuous Improvement:** Safety practices should be regularly reviewed and improved to adapt to changing conditions and learn from experiences.

---

### **2. Understanding Accident Causation**

Accidents rarely happen in isolation. They are typically the result of a complex interplay of factors, often following a predictable sequence. Understanding these sequences is fundamental to preventing future incidents.

**Key Concept: The Chain of Causation (Heinrich's Domino Theory)**

One of the earliest and most influential models of accident causation is Heinrich's Domino Theory. It proposes that accidents occur when five factors are aligned in a sequential manner, like falling dominoes.

*   **Domino 1: Ancestry (Inherited Dispositions and Environmental Influences):** This refers to inherent personality traits, upbringing, and societal influences that might predispose an individual to risk-taking behavior or a lack of safety awareness.
    *   *Example:* A person with a history of impulsivity or a lack of supervision during childhood might be more prone to unsafe actions.
    *   *(Ref: Grimaldi and Simonds, 2001)*

*   **Domino 2: Personal Faults and Unsafe Acts (Human Error):** This domino represents the improper actions or conditions caused by the individual. These can be active mistakes or failures to act.
    *   *Examples:*
        *   **Unsafe Acts:** Operating machinery without authorization, using faulty equipment, not using PPE, taking shortcuts, horseplay.
        *   **Unsafe Conditions:** Faulty wiring, slippery floors, inadequate ventilation, unguarded machinery, improper storage of materials.
    *   *(Ref: R.K Jain, 2000; Blake, 1973)*

*   **Domino 3: Mechanical or Physical Hazard:** This refers to the unsafe condition or hazard that allows the unsafe act to occur or be effective.
    *   *Examples:* A sharp object, a moving part of a machine without a guard, a flammable substance, an elevated working platform.

*   **Domino 4: Contact or Exposure:** This is the point where the unsafe act or condition interacts with a person, leading to an injury.
    *   *Examples:* A person's hand comes into contact with a rotating blade, a chemical spills onto skin, a person falls from a height.

*   **Domino 5: Injury:** This is the final outcome, the physical harm or damage resulting from the accident.
    *   *Examples:* Cuts, burns, fractures, electrocution, poisoning.

**Removing any domino in the chain can prevent the accident.** This highlights the importance of addressing root causes and not just immediate triggers.

### **3. Other Accident Causation Models**

While Heinrich's Domino Theory is foundational, other models offer broader perspectives:

**a) Bird's Domino Theory (Expanded Domino Theory):**

Bertin M. Bird Jr. expanded upon Heinrich's model, introducing a sixth factor and emphasizing management's role.

*   **Domino 1: Lack of Control (Management Responsibility):** This represents deficiencies in the management system, such as poor supervision, inadequate policies, insufficient training, or lack of safety programs.
    *   *Example:* Management failing to implement a lockout/tagout procedure for machinery maintenance.
    *   *(Ref: R.K Jain, 2000)*

*   **Domino 2: Basic Causes (Root Causes):** These are the underlying personal and job factors that allow the unsafe acts and conditions to exist.
    *   **Personal Factors:** Lack of knowledge, skill, motivation, physiological/psychological factors.
    *   **Job Factors:** Inadequate work procedures, poor supervision, poor tool or equipment design, inadequate maintenance, improper workplace layout.
    *   *Example:* Insufficient training on operating a specific piece of equipment.

*   **Domino 3: Intermediate Causes (Immediate Causes):** These are the observable conditions or acts that can lead to an accident if not corrected. They are the unsafe acts and unsafe conditions.
    *   *Example:* An employee operating a machine without wearing safety glasses.

*   **Domino 4: Incident (Accident):** The loss of energy or the contact between the person and the hazard.

*   **Domino 5: Injury/Property Damage/Near Miss:** The consequence of the incident.

**Key Takeaway:** Bird's model stresses that the ultimate responsibility for accidents often lies with management's failure to implement effective control measures.

**b) Human Factors Model:**

This model emphasizes the role of human error and psychological/physiological factors in accident causation.

*   **Focus:** Human performance, limitations, perception, decision-making, and stress.
*   **Mechanisms:**
    *   **Cognitive Errors:** Mistakes in thinking, planning, or judgment.
    *   **Perceptual Errors:** Misinterpreting sensory information.
    *   **Motor Errors:** Mistakes in performing physical actions.
    *   **Physiological Factors:** Fatigue, illness, intoxication, stress.
    *   **Psychological Factors:** Complacency, overconfidence, distraction, lack of motivation.
*   *Example:* A fatigued operator misinterpreting a warning signal due to reduced alertness.
*   *(Ref: Thomas J. Anton, 1989; Krishnan, N.V., 1997)*

**c) Systems Approach (Work Systems Model):**

This model views accidents as emerging from the interaction of various components within a work system, including people, machines, environment, procedures, and management.

*   **Focus:** The overall system and the complex interactions between its parts.
*   **Mechanisms:** Failures in any part of the system or in the interfaces between components can lead to an accident.
*   **Example:** Poorly designed workstations leading to operator strain and errors, which combined with inadequate supervision, results in an accident.
*   *(Ref: Safety management System and Documentation training Programme handbook by Paul S V, 2000)*

### **4. Categories of Accident Causing Mechanisms**

Accident causing mechanisms can be broadly categorized based on the primary source of the hazard or the nature of the failure.

**a) Mechanical Hazards:**

These arise from the design, operation, or maintenance of machinery and equipment.

*   **Unguarded Moving Parts:** Rotating shafts, gears, belts, chains, blades, presses.
    *   *Mechanism:* Entanglement, crushing, cutting, shearing.
    *   *Example:* A worker's clothing getting caught in an unguarded rotating shaft.
    *   *(Ref: Blake, 1973)*

*   **Mechanical Power Transmission:** Hazards associated with flywheels, pulleys, belts, couplings, chains.
    *   *Mechanism:* Entanglement, impact.

*   **Material Handling Equipment:** Cranes, forklifts, conveyors, hoists.
    *   *Mechanism:* Crushing, striking, falling loads, tip-overs.
    *   *Example:* A load falling from a crane due to improper rigging.
    *   *(Ref: CO5 - Hazards associated with different machines and mechanical material handling)*

*   **Pressure Systems:** Boilers, tanks, pipelines, vessels under pressure.
    *   *Mechanism:* Explosion, rupture, release of high-pressure fluids or gases.
    *   *Example:* A boiler explosion due to over-pressurization and lack of safety valve function.

*   **Tools and Hand Tools:** Malfunctioning or improperly used tools.
    *   *Mechanism:* Puncture, laceration, impact.
    *   *Example:* Using a damaged chisel that chips and sends fragments flying.

**b) Electrical Hazards:**

These are related to the use of electricity in industrial settings.

*   **Electrocution:** Contact with live electrical parts.
    *   *Mechanism:* Electric current passing through the body, causing burns, cardiac arrest, muscle contractions.
    *   *Example:* An electrician touching a live wire without proper insulation or de-energization.

*   **Electric Shock:** A milder form of electrical injury, but still hazardous.
    *   *Mechanism:* Passage of current through the body.

*   **Fires and Explosions:** Electrical faults leading to ignition.
    *   *Mechanism:* Short circuits, overloaded circuits, sparking creating ignition sources for flammable materials.
    *   *Example:* Faulty wiring in a flammable storage area causing a fire.

*   **Arc Flash/Blast:** A sudden release of electrical energy through the air.
    *   *Mechanism:* Intense heat, light, pressure waves.
    *   *Example:* An arc flash occurring during maintenance on electrical equipment.

**c) Chemical Hazards:**

These involve exposure to hazardous substances.

*   **Toxicity:** Inhalation, ingestion, or skin absorption of poisonous substances.
    *   *Mechanism:* Interference with biological processes, causing illness or death.
    *   *Example:* Inhaling toxic fumes from a chemical spill.
    *   *(Ref: CO3 - Occupational health hazards)*

*   **Corrosivity:** Substances that damage skin, eyes, or respiratory tracts.
    *   *Mechanism:* Chemical burns.
    *   *Example:* Contact with strong acids or alkalis.

*   **Flammability/Combustibility:** Substances that can easily ignite and burn.
    *   *Mechanism:* Ignition sources interacting with flammable vapors or dusts.
    *   *Example:* Spilled gasoline igniting from a spark.

*   **Reactivity:** Substances that can undergo dangerous chemical reactions.
    *   *Mechanism:* Explosions, release of toxic gases, heat generation.
    *   *Example:* Mixing incompatible chemicals, leading to a violent reaction.

**d) Fire and Explosion Hazards:**

These involve uncontrolled combustion or rapid release of energy.

*   **Ignition Sources:** Open flames, sparks, hot surfaces, static electricity, friction.
    *   *Mechanism:* Providing the energy required to initiate combustion.

*   **Fuel:** Combustible materials (solids, liquids, gases, dusts).
    *   *Mechanism:* The substance that burns.

*   **Oxidizer:** Oxygen or other oxidizing agents.
    *   *Mechanism:* The element that supports combustion.

*   **Enclosure/Confinement:** Limiting the dissipation of heat and pressure, leading to explosive forces.
    *   *Mechanism:* Concentrating the energy release.
    *   *Example:* A dust explosion in a confined space like a silo.
    *   *(Ref: Guidelines for Hazard Evaluation Procedures by AIChE/CCPS, 1992)*

**e) Physical Hazards:**

These encompass a broad range of environmental conditions and forces.

*   **Slips, Trips, and Falls:** From same level or to a lower level.
    *   *Mechanism:* Loss of balance due to slippery surfaces, obstacles, poor lighting, or working at heights.
    *   *Example:* Slipping on an oil spill on the factory floor.
    *   *(Ref: CO3 - Human factors contributing to accidents)*

*   **Noise:** Excessive sound levels.
    *   *Mechanism:* Hearing damage (temporary or permanent), stress, reduced concentration.
    *   *Example:* Working near heavy machinery without hearing protection.

*   **Vibration:** Prolonged exposure to vibrations.
    *   *Mechanism:* Musculoskeletal disorders, nerve damage (e.g., Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome).
    *   *Example:* Operating vibrating tools for extended periods.

*   **Temperature Extremes:** Excessive heat or cold.
    *   *Mechanism:* Heatstroke, heat exhaustion, frostbite, hypothermia.
    *   *Example:* Working in a hot foundry without adequate hydration and cooling.

*   **Radiation:** Ionizing and non-ionizing radiation.
    *   *Mechanism:* Cellular damage, burns, increased cancer risk.
    *   *Example:* Exposure to X-rays without proper shielding.

*   **Ergonomic Hazards:** Poor workstation design, repetitive motions, awkward postures.
    *   *Mechanism:* Musculoskeletal injuries (e.g., Carpal Tunnel Syndrome, back pain).
    *   *Example:* Lifting heavy objects incorrectly, leading to back strain.
    *   *(Ref: CO3 - Human factors contributing to accidents)*

**f) Environmental Hazards:**

These relate to the surrounding conditions that can pose a risk.

*   **Poor Lighting:** Insufficient illumination.
    *   *Mechanism:* Increased risk of slips, trips, and errors in judgment.

*   **Poor Ventilation:** Inadequate air circulation.
    *   *Mechanism:* Buildup of toxic gases, fumes, or dust; oxygen deficiency.

*   **Hazardous Layout:** Poorly organized workspaces.
    *   *Mechanism:* Congestion, difficult access for emergency services, increased risk of collisions.

*   **Weather Conditions:** Rain, ice, snow, high winds.
    *   *Mechanism:* Increased risk of slips, falls, structural collapse.

**g) Human Factor Related Mechanisms:**

These are directly linked to human performance and behavior.

*   **Lack of Training/Knowledge:**
    *   *Mechanism:* Performing tasks incorrectly due to insufficient understanding of procedures or hazards.
    *   *Example:* An untrained operator not knowing how to safely shut down a machine during an emergency.
    *   *(Ref: CO1 - Theories of accident causation)*

*   **Fatigue:**
    *   *Mechanism:* Reduced alertness, slower reaction times, impaired judgment.
    *   *Example:* A night-shift worker making a critical error due to exhaustion.

*   **Complacency/Overconfidence:**
    *   *Mechanism:* Lowered vigilance, neglecting safety procedures because tasks are perceived as routine or easy.
    *   *Example:* A seasoned worker bypassing safety guards because they "know what they're doing."

*   **Stress and Distraction:**
    *   *Mechanism:* Impaired cognitive function, leading to errors.
    *   *Example:* A worker being distracted by a personal phone call while operating heavy machinery.

*   **Poor Communication:**
    *   *Mechanism:* Misunderstandings, incorrect information being passed on, leading to unsafe actions.
    *   *Example:* Incomplete handover of critical information between shifts.

*   **Violation of Rules/Procedures:**
    *   *Mechanism:* Intentional disregard for established safety protocols.
    *   *Example:* A worker smoking in a "No Smoking" area near flammable materials.

### **5. The Role of Management and Systems**

Accident causing mechanisms are often rooted in systemic failures rather than isolated human errors.

*   **Management's Role (Bird's Model):**
    *   **Establishing Safety Policies and Programs:** Clear directives and frameworks for safety.
    *   **Providing Resources:** Adequate funding, training, and equipment.
    *   **Supervision and Enforcement:** Ensuring compliance with safety rules.
    *   **Risk Management:** Proactively identifying and controlling hazards.
    *   *(Ref: R.K Jain, 2000; Safety management System and Documentation training Programme handbook by Paul S V, 2000)*

*   **Systemic Failures:**
    *   **Inadequate Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment:** Not identifying potential dangers.
    *   **Poorly Designed Work Processes:** Creating inherent risks.
    *   **Insufficient Training and Competency Assessment:** Employees not being adequately prepared.
    *   **Lack of Maintenance:** Equipment failing due to neglect.
    *   **Weak Safety Culture:** Safety not being a core value.
    *   *(Ref: Alan Waring, 1996)*

### **6. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** According to Heinrich's Domino Theory, which domino represents the immediate cause of an accident?

*   a) Ancestry
*   b) Personal Faults and Unsafe Acts
*   c) Mechanical or Physical Hazard
*   d) Injury

**Answer:** c) Mechanical or Physical Hazard (While unsafe acts/conditions are immediate triggers, the *hazard* is what the act interacts with to cause injury. Some interpretations of Heinrich's theory place "Mechanical or Physical Hazard" as the crucial link.) *Self-correction: Heinrich's original model places Unsafe Acts/Conditions as Domino 2, which then leads to Contact/Exposure with the hazard. Therefore, both b and c are critical immediate causes. However, the question asks for the *immediate cause of the accident*, implying the direct interaction leading to harm. In most interpretations, the hazard itself is the direct cause when exposed to.* Let's refine the answer based on a common understanding where unsafe acts *and* conditions are the immediate precursors.

**Revised Answer:** b) Personal Faults and Unsafe Acts (This represents the human action or inaction that directly leads to the accident. The hazard is what that act interacts with.)

**Question 2:** Give two examples of "Basic Causes" or "Root Causes" as identified in Bird's Expanded Domino Theory.

**Answer:**
*   Lack of knowledge or skill (Personal Factor)
*   Inadequate work procedures (Job Factor)
*   Poor supervision (Job Factor)
*   Inadequate tool design (Job Factor)

**Question 3:** Describe one mechanism by which a mechanical hazard can lead to an accident.

**Answer:** An unguarded rotating shaft on a machine can cause entanglement. If a worker's clothing or hair comes into contact with the rotating shaft, it can pull them into the machine, leading to crushing, laceration, or amputation.

**Question 4:** What is the primary difference between Heinrich's Domino Theory and Bird's Expanded Domino Theory regarding accident causation?

**Answer:** Bird's theory adds "Lack of Control" (management responsibility) as the first domino, emphasizing that management's failures are the ultimate root cause of most accidents, whereas Heinrich's theory starts with ancestry and personal fault.

**Question 5:** Briefly explain how fatigue can contribute to an accident using the Human Factors Model.

**Answer:** Fatigue impairs cognitive functions such as alertness, concentration, and decision-making. This can lead to slower reaction times, increased likelihood of errors in judgment, and a reduced ability to perceive hazards, thereby increasing the risk of an accident.

### **7. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Accidents are not random; they have causes.** Understanding these causes is the first step to prevention.
*   **Heinrich's Domino Theory** is a foundational model illustrating the sequential nature of accident causation. Removing any "domino" prevents the accident.
*   **Bird's Expanded Domino Theory** highlights the crucial role of **management's lack of control** as the ultimate root cause.
*   **Human factors** (fatigue, stress, complacency, lack of knowledge) are significant contributors to accidents.
*   **Mechanical, electrical, chemical, and physical hazards** are the direct agents that can cause harm when combined with unsafe acts or conditions.
*   A **systems approach** views accidents as a result of interactions within the entire work system.
*   Effective safety management requires addressing both **immediate causes (unsafe acts and conditions)** and **root causes (underlying systemic and human factors)**.
*   **Prevention is paramount.** Focus on eliminating hazards and controlling risks before an incident occurs.
