# INDUSTRIAL SAFETY ENGINEERING - Module 4: Safety issues in Machines: Machinery Safeguard

## Topic: Material Handling - Classification - Safety Consideration - Manual and Mechanical Handling - Maintenance of Common Elements (Wire Rope, Chains, Slings, Hooks, Clamps)

This module focuses on the critical safety aspects of material handling in industrial environments, encompassing both manual and mechanical methods, and the essential maintenance of associated equipment. Understanding these principles is vital for preventing accidents, protecting worker health, and ensuring efficient operations.

---

### 1. Introduction to Material Handling and its Importance

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Material Handling:** The movement, protection, storage, and control of materials and products throughout manufacturing, warehousing, distribution, consumption, and disposal. (Blake, 1973)
*   **Purpose of Material Handling:** To reduce costs, improve efficiency, increase throughput, enhance product quality, and improve working conditions.
*   **Scope:** Encompasses the entire lifecycle of materials, from raw materials to finished goods and waste.

**Importance in Industrial Safety:**

*   Material handling is a significant source of workplace injuries, particularly back injuries, strains, sprains, and amputations. (Jain, 2000)
*   Improper handling can lead to damage to materials, equipment, and the work environment, resulting in financial losses and downtime.
*   Effective material handling systems contribute to overall safety by minimizing manual exertion, reducing the need for hazardous movements, and organizing workspaces.

### 2. Classification of Material Handling Systems

Material handling systems can be broadly classified based on the method of handling:

#### 2.1. Manual Material Handling (MMH)

**Definition:** Involves the physical exertion by a worker to move, lift, carry, push, or pull materials.

**Methods:**

*   **Lifting:** Raising an object from a lower to a higher position.
*   **Carrying:** Transporting an object from one place to another.
*   **Pushing/Pulling:** Moving an object horizontally using physical force.
*   **Reaching/Twisting:** Movements involving extending arms or rotating the torso.

**Safety Considerations for Manual Material Handling:** (Anton, 1989; Grimaldi & Simonds, 2001)

*   **Ergonomic Principles:** Designing tasks to fit the worker, rather than forcing the worker to fit the task. This includes considering:
    *   **Weight of the load:** Lighter loads are safer.
    *   **Frequency of handling:** Less frequent handling is safer.
    *   **Distance of movement:** Shorter distances are safer.
    *   **Posture:** Maintaining neutral postures (avoiding excessive bending, twisting, reaching).
    *   **Grip:** Ensuring good grip on the load.
    *   **Force required:** Minimizing the effort needed.
*   **Risk Assessment:** Identifying tasks that pose a high risk of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs).
*   **Training:** Educating workers on proper lifting techniques, body mechanics, and the risks associated with MMH.
*   **Work Design:**
    *   **Reducing load weight:** Breaking down large loads into smaller ones.
    *   **Minimizing handling frequency:** Automating or mechanizing frequent tasks.
    *   **Improving posture:** Providing adequate space and improving access.
    *   **Using aids:** Employing hand trucks, trolleys, or conveyors where appropriate.
*   **Personal Factors:** Age, physical condition, previous injuries, and fatigue can all influence an individual's susceptibility to injury.

**Examples:**

*   Lifting boxes from a pallet.
*   Carrying tools or materials between workstations.
*   Pushing a cart laden with goods.
*   Manually loading/unloading trucks.

#### 2.2. Mechanical Material Handling (MMH)

**Definition:** Utilizes mechanical aids and equipment to move, lift, store, and control materials.

**Types of Mechanical Handling Equipment:**

*   **Conveyors:** Continuous moving devices to transport materials.
    *   **Belt Conveyors:** Flat belts for transporting bulk materials or packaged goods.
    *   **Roller Conveyors:** Series of rollers for moving boxes or containers.
    *   **Screw Conveyors:** Rotating helical shaft for moving bulk solids.
    *   **Overhead Conveyors:** Suspended systems for transporting parts or finished goods.
*   **Cranes and Hoists:** Lifting and lowering heavy loads, and moving them horizontally.
    *   **Overhead Cranes:** Bridge cranes moving along runways.
    *   **Jib Cranes:** Rotating boom cranes.
    *   **Electric Hoists:** Motorized lifting devices.
    *   **Manual Hoists:** Hand-operated lifting devices.
*   **Lift Trucks (Forklifts):** Powered industrial trucks for lifting and moving loads.
    *   **Counterbalanced Forklifts:** Most common type.
    *   **Reach Trucks:** For narrow aisles.
    *   **Pallet Trucks:** For moving pallets.
*   **Industrial Trucks/Tractors:** For towing or pushing loads.
*   **Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) and Robotic Systems:** For automated material movement.
*   **Pneumatic Conveying Systems:** Using air pressure to move bulk materials.

**Safety Considerations for Mechanical Material Handling:** (Jain, 2000; Grimaldi & Simonds, 2001; Blake, 1973)

*   **Equipment Selection:** Choosing the right equipment for the job based on load capacity, material type, and environment.
*   **Operator Training and Certification:** Ensuring operators are properly trained, competent, and certified for the specific equipment they operate (especially forklifts and cranes).
*   **Preventive Maintenance:** Regular inspection and maintenance of all mechanical handling equipment to prevent failures.
*   **Load Capacity:** Adhering strictly to the load capacity limits of the equipment. Overloading is a major cause of accidents.
*   **Stability:** Ensuring the stability of the load and the equipment. Proper load placement and securing are crucial.
*   **Clear Operating Areas:** Maintaining clear pathways, ensuring adequate visibility, and separating pedestrian and vehicle traffic.
*   **Guarding:** Ensuring all moving parts of the machinery are adequately guarded.
*   **Emergency Stop Mechanisms:** Availability and functionality of emergency stop buttons.
*   **Load Attachment Devices:** Safe and appropriate use of slings, hooks, clamps, and other rigging equipment.
*   **Environmental Factors:** Considering factors like floor conditions, lighting, noise, and the presence of hazardous materials.
*   **Lockout/Tagout:** Implementing lockout/tagout procedures during maintenance and repair.

**Examples:**

*   Using a conveyor belt to move parts on an assembly line.
*   Lifting heavy machinery with an overhead crane.
*   Transporting pallets of raw materials with a forklift.
*   Using a hoist to position a component during assembly.

### 3. Safety Considerations in Material Handling Operations

Beyond the specific types of handling, several overarching safety considerations apply to all material handling activities.

**Key Principles:** (Jain, 2000; Grimaldi & Simonds, 2001; Anton, 1989)

*   **Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment:** Systematically identifying potential hazards (e.g., falling objects, crushing, entanglement, slips, trips, falls) and assessing their risks. Tools like Job Safety Analysis (JSA) or Hazard and Operability Studies (HAZOP) can be useful.
*   **Safe Work Procedures (SWPs):** Developing and enforcing documented procedures for all material handling tasks.
*   **Training and Competency:** Comprehensive training for all personnel involved in material handling, including operators, riggers, and supervisors.
*   **Supervision:** Effective supervision to ensure adherence to safety procedures and prompt correction of unsafe practices.
*   **Housekeeping:** Maintaining clean, organized, and clutter-free work areas to prevent slips, trips, and falls, and to ensure clear access for material movement.
*   **Signage and Markings:** Using clear signage for walkways, hazards, load capacities, and operational instructions.
*   **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Ensuring appropriate PPE (e.g., safety shoes, gloves, helmets, high-visibility clothing) is used by workers. (CO4)
*   **Emergency Preparedness:** Having clear emergency procedures in place for incidents involving material handling equipment or injuries.
*   **Communication:** Effective communication among workers and supervisors regarding material movement plans, potential hazards, and operational status.
*   **Workplace Design:** Designing the workplace layout to facilitate safe material flow, minimize congestion, and provide adequate space for movement.

### 4. Maintenance of Common Material Handling Elements

Proper and regular maintenance of the components used in material handling is crucial to prevent failures that can lead to accidents.

#### 4.1. Wire Rope

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Wire Rope Construction:** Composed of several strands of wire twisted around a core. Different constructions (e.g., 6x19, 6x37) offer varying flexibility and strength.
*   **Purpose:** Used in hoists, cranes, slings, and other lifting applications.

**Inspection and Maintenance:** (Jain, 2000; Blake, 1973)

*   **Regular Visual Inspections:** Before each use (daily) and periodic in-depth inspections.
*   **Key Inspection Points:**
    *   **Broken Wires:** Look for broken wires, especially in outer strands. A general rule is to retire the rope if more than a specified number of wires are broken in a rope lay.
    *   **Kinking/Crushing:** Deformation of the rope structure, which can significantly reduce its strength.
    *   **Corrosion:** Rusting, particularly in the core or between strands, weakens the rope.
    *   **Wear:** Reduction in rope diameter due to abrasion.
    *   **Bird Caging:** Strands or wires separating due to torsional stress.
    *   **Heat Damage:** Discoloration indicating exposure to excessive heat.
    *   **Proper Lubrication:** Maintains flexibility and prevents corrosion.
*   **Retirement Criteria:** Establish clear criteria for when a wire rope must be replaced, based on the number of broken wires, reduction in diameter, kinking, crushing, etc. (Consult manufacturer's guidelines and relevant standards like ASME).
*   **Proper Storage:** Store wire rope on reels, protected from the elements and mechanical damage.

**Alignment with Course Outcomes:**

#### 4.2. Chains

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Chain Types:** Proof coil, alloy steel, machine, machine safety. Alloy steel chains are generally preferred for lifting due to higher strength and durability.
*   **Purpose:** Used in slings, hoists, and lifting assemblies.

**Inspection and Maintenance:** (Jain, 2000; Blake, 1973)

*   **Regular Visual Inspections:** Before each use and periodic detailed inspections.
*   **Key Inspection Points:**
    *   **Stretching/Elongation:** Chains can stretch over time, reducing their strength. Measure the length between links.
    *   **Wear:** Abrasion on the surfaces of the links.
    *   **Twisting:** Bent or twisted links can indicate overload or improper use.
    *   **Cracked/Broken Links:** Obvious signs of damage.
    *   **Corrosion/Pitting:** Weakens the metal.
    *   **Deformed Links:** Due to impact or overload.
*   **Retirement Criteria:** Chains should be retired if there is a noticeable stretch, wear exceeding a specified percentage of the original link diameter, or visible damage.
*   **Load Ratings:** Never exceed the rated load capacity of the chain.
*   **Proper Storage:** Keep chains clean, dry, and free from damage.

**Alignment with Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO5:** Describes various hazards associated with different machines and mechanical material handling. (Chain failure during lifting operations is a significant hazard.)

#### 4.3. Slings

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Sling Types:**
    *   **Wire Rope Slings:** Flexible, strong, and durable.
    *   **Chain Slings:** Strong, durable, and resistant to abrasion and heat.
    *   **Synthetic Rope Slings (Nylon, Polyester):** Lightweight, flexible, non-damaging to loads, but sensitive to heat, UV, and chemicals.
    *   **Webbing Slings:** Flat fabric slings, usually polyester or nylon, good for delicate loads.
*   **Purpose:** Connect the load to the lifting device (e.g., hook, shackle).

**Inspection and Maintenance:** (Jain, 2000; AIChE/CCPS, 1992)

*   **Pre-Use Inspection:** Essential for all sling types before each use.
*   **Key Inspection Points (General):**
    *   **Cut/Abrasion:** Damage to the outer layers or core of the sling.
    *   **Kinks/Twists:** Deformation of the sling structure.
    *   **Heat/Chemical Damage:** Discoloration, melting, or degradation of the material.
    *   **Knots:** Improperly tied knots weaken slings.
    *   **Core Exposure:** Visible internal fibers or wires.
    *   **End Fittings:** Damage to eyes, hooks, or other connecting hardware.
*   **Specific Sling Type Inspections:**
    *   **Wire Rope Slings:** As per wire rope inspection points.
    *   **Chain Slings:** As per chain inspection points.
    *   **Synthetic/Webbing Slings:** Check for excessive fuzzing, pilling, cuts, heat damage, chemical damage, UV degradation, and broken stitches in the eyes.
*   **Load Rating Tags:** Ensure tags are present and legible, indicating the sling's capacity.
*   **Retirement Criteria:** Slings must be immediately removed from service if any significant damage is observed. Specific criteria vary by material and manufacturer.
*   **Proper Use:** Avoid dragging slings, using them with sharp edges without protection, and exceeding rated capacities.

**Alignment with Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO5:** Describes various hazards associated with different machines and mechanical material handling. (Slings are critical rigging components; their failure can cause catastrophic accidents.)

#### 4.4. Hooks

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Types:** Lifting hooks, grab hooks, eye hooks, shank hooks.
*   **Material:** Typically made of forged steel.
*   **Purpose:** Connect slings or other rigging to the load or lifting device.

**Inspection and Maintenance:** (Jain, 2000; Blake, 1973)

*   **Regular Visual Inspection:** Before each use.
*   **Key Inspection Points:**
    *   **Opening of the Hook:** The throat opening should not be deformed beyond a specified percentage (e.g., 10-15%) of the original opening.
    *   **Cracks/Grooves:** Inspect for any cracks, especially at the root of the threads or wear on the bearing surface.
    *   **Twisting:** Deformation of the hook itself.
    *   **Worn Shoulder:** The bearing surface where the hook rotates should not be excessively worn.
    *   **Proper Latch:** If equipped with a latch, ensure it functions correctly and closes securely.
*   **Retirement Criteria:** Hooks with cracks, excessive deformation, or worn shoulders must be retired.
*   **Load Concentration:** Avoid loading hooks on the point; always use the bowl of the hook.
*   **Proper Rigging:** Ensure loads are properly centered in the hook's bowl.

**Alignment with Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO5:** Describes various hazards associated with different machines and mechanical material handling. (Hook failure or improper use is a direct hazard in lifting operations.)

#### 4.5. Clamps

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Types:** Plate clamps (vertical, horizontal), beam clamps, drum clamps, vacuum clamps.
*   **Purpose:** Used to securely grip and lift loads that cannot be easily rigged with slings or hooks.

**Inspection and Maintenance:** (Jain, 2000; AIChE/CCPS, 1992)

*   **Regular Visual Inspection:** Before each use.
*   **Key Inspection Points:**
    *   **Gripping Surfaces:** Check for wear, damage, or contamination that could affect grip.
    *   **Spring Mechanisms:** Ensure springs are intact and provide adequate tension.
    *   **Locking Mechanisms:** Verify that any locking devices function correctly.
    *   **Structural Integrity:** Inspect the clamp body for cracks or deformation.
    *   **Jaw Alignment:** Ensure jaws close properly and evenly.
    *   **Load Rating Tag:** Ensure the tag is present and legible.
*   **Retirement Criteria:** Clamps with damaged gripping surfaces, faulty mechanisms, or structural defects must be retired.
*   **Proper Application:** Use clamps only for the type of material and orientation for which they are designed.
*   **Load Security:** Always ensure the clamp is securely engaged with the load before lifting. Perform a test lift with a reduced load if unsure.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1 (CO5):**
Describe three common hazards associated with the operation of forklifts and suggest at least two preventive measures for each hazard.

**Answer:**
*   **Hazard:** Tipping over (e.g., due to uneven ground, high speeds, turning sharply, overloading).
    *   **Prevention:**
        1.  Operate at safe speeds, especially when turning or on inclines.
        2.  Ensure loads are centered and within capacity, and forks are tilted back.
*   **Hazard:** Collisions with pedestrians or other vehicles (e.g., due to blind spots, poor visibility, excessive speed).
    *   **Prevention:**
        1.  Maintain clear visibility and use horns at intersections or when approaching corners.
        2.  Establish designated pedestrian walkways and forklift operating zones.
*   **Hazard:** Dropped loads (e.g., due to unstable loads, improper load securing, forklift malfunction).
    *   **Prevention:**
        1.  Ensure loads are properly stacked, secured, and within forklift capacity.
        2.  Regularly inspect and maintain the forklift's mast, hydraulics, and load-bearing components.

**Question 2 (CO1, CO3):**
A warehouse worker frequently lifts heavy boxes from the floor to a high shelf. This task is repetitive and requires bending and twisting. Discuss the potential occupational health hazards this worker faces and recommend ergonomic interventions to reduce the risk of injury.

**Answer:**
*   **Occupational Health Hazards:**
    *   **Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs):** Specifically, lower back injuries (strains, sprains, disc herniation), shoulder pain, and knee problems due to repetitive bending, lifting, and twisting.
    *   **Overexertion Injuries:** Strains and sprains from lifting loads that are too heavy or handled with poor technique.
    *   **Fatigue:** Increased likelihood of errors and accidents when tired.
*   **Ergonomic Interventions:**
    1.  **Mechanical Aids:** Introduce a pallet jack or forklift to move boxes closer to the shelf, or use a drum lifter or small hoist to lift boxes to a more accessible height.
    2.  **Task Redesign:**
        *   Lower the shelves to a more ergonomic working height.
        *   Use trolleys to bring boxes to the lifting area.
        *   Break down heavy loads into smaller, lighter units.
    3.  **Training:** Provide comprehensive training on proper lifting techniques (e.g., bend knees, keep back straight, lift with legs, avoid twisting).
    4.  **Work Rotation:** Rotate workers through different tasks to reduce the duration and frequency of exposure to this high-risk activity.
    5.  **Team Lifting:** For very heavy loads, implement a policy of team lifting with clear communication and coordination.

**Question 3 (CO5):**
You are inspecting a chain sling used for lifting heavy steel beams. You notice that one of the links appears slightly stretched compared to others, and there is some minor abrasion on the surface of several links. What is your assessment, and what action should you take?

**Answer:**
*   **Assessment:** The observed stretched link and abrasion indicate wear and potential elongation of the chain. Stretching beyond a specified limit significantly reduces the chain's load-carrying capacity and increases the risk of failure. Abrasion also reduces the cross-sectional area of the metal, weakening the link.
*   **Action:** This chain sling is **unsafe** and must be **immediately removed from service**. It should be tagged with "Do Not Use" and sent for proper disposal or repair if the damage is minor and can be rectified according to manufacturer specifications and industry standards. Do not attempt to use it for lifting any load.

**Question 4 (CO4):**
When working with wire rope slings, what types of damage should you look for during pre-use inspections, and what is the significance of each?

**Answer:**
During pre-use inspections of wire rope slings, look for:
*   **Broken Wires:** These reduce the strength of the rope. A significant number of broken wires in a short length or within a single strand is a critical sign of wear or damage.
*   **Kinking/Crushing:** These deformations permanently distort the rope structure and severely weaken it, making it prone to further failure.
*   **Corrosion (Rusting):** Weakens the rope by reducing the metal's cross-sectional area and can hide internal damage.
*   **Wear (Reduced Diameter):** Abrasion on the outer wires reduces the rope's strength. A noticeable reduction in diameter indicates significant wear.
*   **Bird Caging:** This indicates torsional stress, where the outer wires spiral outwards, weakening the rope and making it susceptible to unraveling.
*   **Heat Damage:** Discoloration indicates exposure to temperatures that can affect the metal's strength.

The significance of each is that any of these can compromise the sling's integrity, potentially leading to the failure of the sling and the dropped load, causing severe injury or fatality.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Hierarchy of Controls:** Always prioritize eliminating or substituting hazardous material handling methods before relying on PPE.
*   **Ergonomics:** Crucial for manual material handling to prevent musculoskeletal disorders.
*   **Mechanical Advantage:** Mechanical handling aims to provide mechanical advantage, but the equipment itself introduces new hazards requiring stringent safety measures.
*   **"Right Tool for the Job":** Select material handling equipment and rigging gear appropriate for the specific load and task.
*   **Load Capacity is Paramount:** Never exceed the rated load capacity of any equipment or rigging component.
*   **Inspection is Non-Negotiable:** Regular and thorough inspections of all material handling equipment and components are vital.
*   **Training is Key:** Ensure all personnel involved are adequately trained and competent.
*   **Maintenance is Proactive Safety:** Preventive maintenance programs are essential to prevent equipment failures.
*   **Communication:** Clear communication protocols are vital for coordinating material movement and preventing accidents.
*   **Housekeeping:** A clean and organized workplace significantly reduces material handling hazards.
