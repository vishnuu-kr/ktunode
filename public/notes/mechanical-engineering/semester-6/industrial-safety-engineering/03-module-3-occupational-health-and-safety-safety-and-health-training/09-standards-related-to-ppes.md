# Industrial Safety Engineering - Module 3: Occupational Health and Safety - Safety and Health Training

## Topic: Standards Related to Personal Protective Equipment (PPEs)

This module focuses on ensuring a safe and healthy working environment. A critical aspect of this is the appropriate use and selection of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE). This topic delves into the various standards and guidelines that govern the design, manufacturing, testing, and use of PPE to protect workers from hazards.

*   Identify common occupational health hazards and their sources.
*   Explain the importance of training in occupational health and safety.
*   **Explain the purpose and importance of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE).**
*   **Describe the process of selecting appropriate PPE for different tasks and hazards.**
*   **Identify key international and national standards related to various types of PPE.**
*   **Discuss the limitations of PPE and the need for a hierarchical approach to hazard control.**
*   **Understand the role of training in ensuring the effective use and maintenance of PPE.**
*   **Evaluate the safety performance related to PPE usage and identify relevant indicators.**

### **1. Introduction to Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)**

**Definition:** Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) refers to any equipment worn or held by a worker to protect them from one or more risks to their health or safety while working.

**Purpose of PPE:**
*   To prevent or minimize injury and illness to workers.
*   To act as a last line of defense when other control measures are insufficient or not feasible.
*   To protect specific body parts from identified hazards.

**References:**
*   **Jain, R.K. (2000).** *Industrial Safety, Health and Environment management systems.* Emphasizes PPE as a component of a comprehensive safety system.
*   **Grimaldi and Simonds (2001).** *Safety Management.* Discusses PPE as a vital element in hazard control strategies.
*   **Anton, Thomas J. (1989).** *Occupational Safety and Health Management.* Highlights the importance of selecting and using PPE correctly.

---

### **2. Hierarchy of Controls and the Role of PPE**

It is crucial to understand that PPE is the *last resort* in the hierarchy of controls. The most effective methods for hazard control are:

1.  **Elimination:** Removing the hazard entirely.
2.  **Substitution:** Replacing the hazard with a less hazardous alternative.
3.  **Engineering Controls:** Isolating people from the hazard (e.g., ventilation systems, machine guarding).
4.  **Administrative Controls:** Changing the way people work (e.g., work rotation, safety procedures, training).
5.  **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Protecting the worker directly.

**Important Point:** Reliance solely on PPE without implementing higher levels of control is generally ineffective and can lead to a false sense of security.

**Reference:**
*   **Waring, Alan (1996).** *Safety management system.* Underscores the importance of the hierarchy of controls.

---

### **3. Standards Related to PPEs: Why are they Important?**

Standards provide a benchmark for the quality, performance, and safety of PPE. They ensure that PPE:

*   Offers adequate protection against specific hazards.
*   Is designed for comfort and usability.
*   Is durable and performs as expected.
*   Is manufactured consistently.
*   Facilitates fair trade and international recognition.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Performance Standards:** Define the minimum requirements PPE must meet to be effective against specific hazards (e.g., impact resistance, chemical permeation).
*   **Certification Marks:** Indicate that a product has been tested and meets specific standards (e.g., CE marking, ANSI Z87.1).

### **4. Major International and National Standards for PPE**

While specific standards can vary by region and industry, here are some widely recognized bodies and their relevant standards:

**4.1. International Organization for Standardization (ISO)**

ISO develops and publishes international standards across various sectors, including safety. While ISO might not have specific product standards for *all* PPE, it develops standards for management systems and testing methodologies that can be applied.

**4.2. European Standards (EN)**

European Norms (EN) are widely adopted across the European Union. For PPE, the relevant directive is the **PPE Regulation (EU) 2016/425**. This regulation categorizes PPE into three risk classes:

*   **Category I:** Minimal risks (e.g., gardening gloves).
*   **Category II:** Intermediate risks (e.g., safety footwear, high-visibility clothing).
*   **Category III:** High risks (e.g., respiratory protective equipment, fall arrest systems, chemical protection suits).

*   **Examples of EN Standards:**
    *   **EN 397:** Specification for protective helmets for industrial use.
    *   **EN 166:** Personal eye-protection – Specifications.
    *   **EN 345 (now replaced by EN ISO 20345):** Safety footwear for professional use.
    *   **EN 133:** Respiratory protective devices – Filtering half masks to protect against particles – Requirements, testing and marking.
    *   **EN ISO 11611:** Protective clothing for use in welding and allied processes.
    *   **EN ISO 11612:** Protective clothing – Against heat and flame.

**4.3. American National Standards Institute (ANSI)**

ANSI accredits standards developing organizations and approves American National Standards. Many US standards are developed by specific organizations accredited by ANSI.

*   **Examples of ANSI Accredited Standards:**
    *   **ANSI Z89.1:** Requirements for industrial head protection (helmets).
    *   **ANSI Z87.1:** Practice for occupational and educational eye and face protection.
    *   **ANSI Z133.1:** Safety requirements for arboricultural operations (includes PPE for tree care).
    *   **ANSI/ISEA 107:** High-visibility public safety apparel.
    *   **ANSI/ISEA 125:** Performance testing of general purpose hand protection.

**4.4. National Fire Protection Association (NFPA)**

NFPA standards are primarily focused on fire safety but also include standards for protective clothing used by firefighters and other emergency responders.

*   **Example:**
    *   **NFPA 1971:** Standard on Protective Ensembles for Structural Fire Fighting and Proximity Fire Fighting.

**4.5. Other National Standards**

Many countries have their own national standards bodies that often harmonize with international standards (like ISO) or regional standards (like EN). Examples include:

*   **British Standards (BS):** Often align with EN standards.
*   **Indian Standards (IS):** Developed by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS).
    *   **IS 2975:** Code of practice for protection of workers against heavy duty electric shock.
    *   **IS 9974 (Part 1 & 2):** Safety helmets for construction work.
    *   **IS 4151:** Specification for protective gloves for electrical purposes.

**Reference:**
*   **Blake, Ronald P. (1973).** *Industrial safety.* While an older text, it discusses the foundational need for standardized safety equipment.

### **5. Selection of Appropriate PPE**

Effective PPE selection is crucial and involves a systematic approach.

**Key Steps in PPE Selection:**

1.  **Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment:**
    *   Thoroughly identify all potential hazards in the workplace (e.g., chemical splashes, falling objects, noise, extreme temperatures, biological agents, electricity, confined spaces).
    *   Assess the level of risk associated with each hazard (probability and severity of harm).
    *   **Referencing:**
        *   **AIChE/CCPS (1992).** *Guidelines for Hazard Evaluation Procedures.* This is a critical resource for systematic hazard identification and risk assessment, which forms the basis for PPE selection.

2.  **Task Analysis:**
    *   Understand the specific tasks workers perform.
    *   Identify how workers are exposed to the identified hazards during these tasks.

3.  **Matching PPE to Hazards and Tasks:**
    *   Select PPE that is specifically designed to protect against the identified hazards.
    *   Ensure the PPE is suitable for the task's duration and intensity.
    *   Consider compatibility with other PPE being worn.

4.  **Considering User Factors:**
    *   **Fit and Comfort:** PPE must fit properly and be comfortable to wear for extended periods to encourage consistent use. Ill-fitting PPE can be ineffective or create new hazards.
    *   **Dexterity and Vision:** Some PPE can impair dexterity or vision. Select PPE that minimizes these impairments while still providing adequate protection.
    *   **Communication:** Certain PPE (e.g., respirators, hearing protection) can impede communication.
    *   **Skin Sensitivities/Allergies:** Consider if a worker has any sensitivities to materials used in PPE.

5.  **Checking Standards Compliance:**
    *   Ensure the chosen PPE meets relevant national and international standards. Look for certification marks.

6.  **Consultation:**
    *   Involve workers in the selection process. Their input on comfort, fit, and practicality is invaluable.

**Example:**
*   **Hazard:** Grinding metal, producing sparks and flying debris.
*   **Task:** Operating a grinder.
*   **Required PPE:** Safety glasses or goggles meeting **ANSI Z87.1** or **EN 166** standards, possibly a face shield for additional protection. Hearing protection (earplugs or earmuffs) meeting appropriate **ANSI** or **EN** standards for noise levels. Work gloves for grip and protection from minor cuts. Safety footwear.

### **6. Types of PPE and Associated Standards**

Here's a breakdown of common PPE types and relevant standards:

**6.1. Head Protection (Safety Helmets)**

*   **Hazards:** Falling objects, impact from stationary objects, electrical hazards, falling from height.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN 397:** Industrial helmets (impact, penetration, flame resistance, electrical properties).
    *   **ANSI Z89.1:** Industrial head protection (Type I – impact to crown, Type II – impact to crown and sides; Class E – electrical, Class G – electrical, Class C – conductive).
    *   **IS 9974 (Part 1 & 2):** Safety helmets for construction work.

**6.2. Eye and Face Protection**

*   **Hazards:** Flying particles, dust, liquid splashes, chemical fumes, radiant energy (UV/IR), laser beams, impact.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN 166:** Personal eye protection – Specifications (optical quality, impact resistance, splash protection, UV/IR filters).
    *   **ANSI Z87.1:** Occupational and educational eye and face protection (impact rating: High Velocity Impact; Chemical splash, dust resistance).
    *   **EN 170:** UV filters – Functional requirements.
    *   **EN 172:** Personal eye protection – Solar protection for industrial use.
    *   **EN 175:** Equipment for eye and face protection during welding and similar operations.

**6.3. Hearing Protection**

*   **Hazards:** Noise-induced hearing loss.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN 352:** Hearing protectors (earplugs, earmuffs). Standards specify Noise Reduction Rating (NRR) or Single Number Rating (SNR).
    *   **ANSI S3.19:** Hearing Protection Devices – Mfrs. Method of Measurement of Insertion Attenuation.
    *   **ANSI S12.6:** Methods for measuring the insertion loss of hearing protection devices.

**6.4. Respiratory Protective Equipment (RPE)**

*   **Hazards:** Dusts, mists, fumes, gases, vapors, oxygen deficiency.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN 143/EN 149:** Filtering half masks (FFP1, FFP2, FFP3).
    *   **EN 14387:** Chemical filters and filter cartridges.
    *   **EN 12941/EN 12942:** Powered filtering devices.
    *   **NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health - USA) Approved Respirators:** A key certification for respirators in the US, often linked to **42 CFR Part 84**.

**6.5. Hand Protection (Gloves)**

*   **Hazards:** Cuts, abrasions, punctures, chemicals, heat, cold, biological agents, electricity.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN 388:** Protective gloves against mechanical risks (resistance to abrasion, blade cut, tear, puncture).
    *   **EN 374:** Protective gloves against chemicals and micro-organisms.
    *   **EN 407:** Protective gloves against thermal risks (heat and/or flame).
    *   **EN 511:** Protective gloves against cold.
    *   **IS 4151:** Specification for protective gloves for electrical purposes.

**6.6. Foot Protection (Safety Footwear)**

*   **Hazards:** Falling objects, punctures, slips, electrical hazards, chemicals, heat, cold.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN ISO 20345:** Personal protective equipment – Safety footwear with protective toecaps (SB, S1, S1P, S2, S3 classes).
    *   **EN ISO 20346:** Personal protective equipment – Protective footwear with toecaps.
    *   **EN ISO 20347:** Personal protective equipment – Occupational footwear.
    *   **ASTM F2413:** Standard Specification for Performance Requirements for Protective Footwear.

**6.7. Body Protection**

*   **Hazards:** Heat, flame, chemicals, cuts, abrasions, poor visibility, biological agents, electrical arc flash.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN ISO 11612:** Protective clothing against heat and flame.
    *   **EN ISO 11611:** Protective clothing for use in welding and allied processes.
    *   **EN 14605:** Protective clothing against liquid chemicals – spray-tight (Type 3) and splash-tight (Type 4).
    *   **EN 13034:** Protective clothing against liquid chemicals – limited splash-type (Type 6).
    *   **EN ISO 20471:** High-visibility clothing.
    *   **ANSI/ISEA 107:** High-visibility safety apparel.
    *   **NFPA 70E:** Standard for Electrical Safety in the Workplace (for arc flash protective clothing).

**6.8. Fall Protection (Fall Arrest Systems)**

*   **Hazards:** Falling from height.
*   **Key Standards:**
    *   **EN 361:** Full body harnesses.
    *   **EN 353-1:** Guided type fall arresters including an engineering line.
    *   **EN 353-2:** Guided type fall arresters on a flexible anchor line.
    *   **EN 355:** Energy absorbers.
    *   **EN 362:** Connectors.
    *   **ANSI Z359 Series:** Fall Arrest Systems.

**Reference:**
*   **Anton, Thomas J. (1989).** *Occupational Safety and Health Management.* Provides an overview of various PPE types and their applications.
*   **Jain, R.K. (2000).** *Industrial Safety, Health and Environment management systems.* Integrates PPE standards within a broader management framework.

### **7. Training, Use, and Maintenance of PPE**

Even the best PPE is ineffective if not used or maintained correctly.

**7.1. Training Requirements:**

Workers must be trained on:
*   **Why PPE is necessary:** The hazards they are protected from.
*   **When PPE is required:** Specific tasks and conditions.
*   **What PPE to use:** Proper selection for the task.
*   **How to properly don, doff, adjust, and wear PPE:** Correct fit and positioning.
*   **The limitations of PPE:** What it cannot protect against.
*   **How to inspect, care for, maintain, and store PPE:** Ensuring its continued effectiveness.
*   **The meaning of warning labels and certification marks.**
*   **Troubleshooting common issues with PPE.**

**Reference:**
*   **Paul S.V. (2000).** *Safety management System and Documentation training Programme handbook.* Emphasizes the documentation and training aspects of safety management, including PPE.

**7.2. Proper Use:**
*   Wear PPE consistently whenever the hazard is present.
*   Ensure it is worn correctly and fits properly.
*   Do not modify PPE unless specifically permitted by the manufacturer and standards.

**7.3. Inspection and Maintenance:**
*   **Pre-use Inspection:** Visually check for damage, wear, tears, cracks, chemical degradation, or missing parts before each use.
*   **Regular Maintenance:** Follow manufacturer's instructions for cleaning, disinfection, and repair.
*   **Storage:** Store PPE in a clean, dry place away from direct sunlight, heat, or corrosive materials.
*   **Record Keeping:** Maintain records of inspections, maintenance, and replacements.

**7.4. Replacement:**
*   Replace PPE immediately if it is damaged, worn out, or no longer meets performance requirements.
*   Follow the manufacturer's recommended service life or replacement schedule.

### **8. Safety Performance and Indicators Related to PPE**

Monitoring the effectiveness of PPE programs is crucial.

**8.1. Safety Performance:**
*   The extent to which the PPE program contributes to preventing injuries and illnesses.
*   This includes compliance rates, effectiveness of training, and the overall reduction in hazard exposure.

**8.2. Safety Performance Indicators (SPIs) for PPE:**

*   **Lagging Indicators (Measure past performance):**
    *   **Injury/Illness Rates involving PPE:** Number of incidents where PPE was a contributing factor (either failed, was not used, or was used incorrectly).
    *   **Near Misses related to PPE:** Incidents where an injury was averted, but PPE was involved (e.g., a guard failing, a worker not wearing required PPE).
    *   **Workers' Compensation Claims:** Claims related to injuries that PPE should have prevented.

*   **Leading Indicators (Measure current activities and predict future performance):**
    *   **PPE Compliance Rate:** Percentage of observed workers wearing the correct PPE for their task.
    *   **PPE Availability:** Percentage of necessary PPE readily available to workers.
    *   **PPE Inspection/Maintenance Records:** Completion rates for scheduled inspections and maintenance.
    *   **Training Completion Rates:** Percentage of workers who have completed required PPE training.
    *   **Worker Feedback on PPE:** Surveys or feedback mechanisms to gauge satisfaction, comfort, and perceived effectiveness of PPE.
    *   **PPE Audit Results:** Findings from systematic checks of PPE programs.

**Reference:**
*   **Grimaldi and Simonds (2001).** *Safety Management.* Discusses the importance of measuring safety performance through various indicators.

---

### **9. Limitations of PPE**

*   **Can be uncomfortable:** Leading to reduced compliance.
*   **Can restrict movement, vision, or hearing:** Potentially creating new hazards.
*   **Provides a false sense of security:** If not used in conjunction with other controls.
*   **Requires proper maintenance and replacement:** Degradation can occur over time.
*   **Subject to user error:** Incorrect use can render it ineffective.
*   **Limited protection against certain complex hazards:** Like arc flash or high-level chemical exposure, where specialized systems might be needed.

---

### **Key Points to Remember:**

*   **PPE is the last line of defense.** Always prioritize elimination, substitution, engineering, and administrative controls.
*   **Standards are essential** for ensuring the quality, performance, and safety of PPE.
*   **Thorough hazard assessment is the foundation** for selecting appropriate PPE.
*   **Proper fit, comfort, and training are critical** for effective PPE use and compliance.
*   **Regular inspection, maintenance, and timely replacement** are vital to ensure PPE remains effective.
*   **Monitor safety performance** related to PPE using both lagging and leading indicators.

### **Practice Questions and Exercises:**

**Question 1 (CO4, K2):** Explain the primary purpose of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) in an industrial setting and why it is considered the last line of defense.

**Answer:** The primary purpose of PPE is to protect individual workers from specific hazards in their work environment when those hazards cannot be eliminated or controlled by other means. It is the last line of defense because it protects the worker directly, rather than eliminating the hazard itself or modifying the work environment. Higher-level controls (elimination, substitution, engineering, administrative) are more effective in preventing exposure and are therefore prioritized.

**Question 2 (CO4, K3):** A worker is assigned to a task involving painting with a solvent-based paint in a well-ventilated area. Identify at least three potential hazards and suggest appropriate PPE, referencing relevant standard types.

**Answer:**
*   **Hazards:**
    1.  **Inhalation of solvent vapors:** (Health hazard)
    2.  **Skin contact with paint/solvent:** (Health hazard, potential irritation or absorption)
    3.  **Eye irritation/damage from splashes:** (Physical hazard)
*   **Appropriate PPE:**
    1.  **Respiratory Protection:** A respirator with organic vapor cartridges (e.g., meeting **EN 14387** for filters or **NIOSH-approved** organic vapor respirators).
    2.  **Hand Protection:** Chemical-resistant gloves, suitable for organic solvents (e.g., nitrile or neoprene, meeting **EN 374**).
    3.  **Eye Protection:** Safety goggles with indirect ventilation for splash protection (e.g., meeting **ANSI Z87.1** or **EN 166**).

**Question 3 (CO4, K2):** List five key elements that must be included in training for workers on the use of PPE.

**Answer:**
1.  Why PPE is necessary (specific hazards).
2.  When PPE is required (specific tasks/conditions).
3.  How to properly don, doff, adjust, and wear PPE.
4.  The limitations of the PPE.
5.  How to inspect, care for, maintain, and store PPE.

**Question 4 (CO1, K2 & CO4, K2):** Discuss how the hierarchy of controls influences the decision to use PPE and explain why over-reliance on PPE can be problematic.

**Answer:** The hierarchy of controls dictates that PPE should only be used when hazards cannot be adequately controlled through elimination, substitution, engineering controls, or administrative controls. This means PPE is the last resort. Over-reliance on PPE is problematic because:
*   It can create a false sense of security, leading to less rigorous application of higher-level controls.
*   PPE can be uncomfortable, leading to non-compliance or incorrect usage.
*   PPE has inherent limitations and can fail or degrade if not properly maintained.
*   It does not eliminate the hazard itself, meaning exposure can still occur if PPE is breached or not used.

**Question 5 (CO4, K2):** What is the difference between a lagging and a leading safety performance indicator, and provide one example of each related to PPE?

**Answer:**
*   **Lagging Indicator:** Measures past performance and is typically a result of actions taken. It indicates *what happened*.
    *   *Example:* Number of reported incidents where a worker suffered an injury because their PPE failed or was not worn.
*   **Leading Indicator:** Measures current activities and aims to predict future performance. It indicates *what is being done* to prevent incidents.
    *   *Example:* Percentage of workers observed wearing the correct PPE for their task during a safety audit (PPE Compliance Rate).
