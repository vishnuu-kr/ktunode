### 3. Introduction to Drilling Machines

Drilling is a machining process that uses a rotating cutting tool to create cylindrical holes in a workpiece. Drilling machines are versatile machine tools capable of performing various operations beyond simple hole creation.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Hole Creation:** The primary function is to produce holes of a specific diameter and depth.
*   **Chip Formation:** Drilling involves the removal of material in the form of chips.
*   **Tool Rotation and Feed:** The cutting tool rotates, and the workpiece (or the tool) is fed axially into the workpiece.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Hajra Choudhury & Roy, "Elements of Workshop Technology Vol-II Machine Tools":** Provides a foundational understanding of drilling machine types and their working principles.
*   **Ghosh & Mallik, "Manufacturing Science":** Offers insights into the mechanics of chip formation during drilling.

### 4. Operations Performed on Drilling Machines

Drilling machines are not limited to just drilling holes. They can perform a variety of related operations:

*   **Drilling:** The fundamental operation of creating a hole.
*   **Reaming:** Enlarging an existing hole to a precise size with a smooth finish. Reamers have multiple cutting edges that remove a small amount of material.
    *   **Purpose:** To improve hole accuracy and surface finish.
    *   **Tool:** Reamer (multi-fluted)
*   **Boring:** Enlarging an existing hole to a larger diameter with greater accuracy. Boring is typically done on pre-drilled or cored holes.
    *   **Purpose:** To achieve a precise larger diameter and improved concentricity.
    *   **Tool:** Boring bar with a single point cutting tool.
*   **Counterboring:** Creating a larger diameter recess at the opening of a drilled hole, allowing a screw head to sit flush or below the surface.
    *   **Purpose:** To recess screw heads.
    *   **Tool:** Counterbore tool (similar to a drill but with a pilot to guide it into the existing hole).
*   **Countersinking:** Creating a conical enlargement at the opening of a drilled hole, typically for flat-head screws.
    *   **Purpose:** To recess flat-head screw heads.
    *   **Tool:** Countersink tool (conical shape).
*   **Spot Facing:** Machining a shallow flat surface on a rough casting or forging to provide a clean seating surface for bolt heads or nuts.
    *   **Purpose:** To create a clean, flat surface for component seating.
    *   **Tool:** Spot facer (similar to a counterbore but often with a larger diameter and a pilot).
*   **Tapping:** Cutting internal threads into a pre-drilled hole.
    *   **Purpose:** To create internal threads for fasteners.
    *   **Tool:** Tap (threaded cutting tool).
*   **Gun Drilling:** A specialized technique for drilling deep, small-diameter holes. It uses a special drill bit and a high-pressure coolant system.
    *   **Purpose:** Deep hole drilling, often in barrel manufacturing or hydraulic components.
    *   **Tool:** Gun drill.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Kalpakjian & Schmid, "Manufacturing Engineering and Technology":** Provides detailed descriptions and illustrations of these operations.
*   **Chapman, "Workshop Technology":** Offers practical explanations and common applications of each operation.

**Example:**

If you need to mount a component with flat-head screws, you would first drill the hole, then use a countersink tool to create a conical recess for the screw head.

### 5. Drill Bit Nomenclature

Understanding the different parts of a twist drill bit is crucial for effective drilling.

**Key Components of a Twist Drill:**

*   **Point Angle:** The angle of the conical cutting tip. The standard point angle is 118°. For harder materials, a smaller angle (e.g., 90°) is used for better cutting action. For softer materials, a larger angle (e.g., 135°) is preferred.
*   **Lip:** The cutting edge at the periphery of the drill point.
*   **Chisel Edge:** The blunt edge at the center of the drill point, formed by the intersection of the two helixes. It has a rubbing action and contributes to the cutting force.
*   **Land:** The cylindrical surface of the drill helix, relieved to prevent rubbing against the hole wall.
*   **Body:** The cylindrical portion of the drill between the land and the web.
*   **Helix Angle:** The angle of the spiral groove along the body of the drill. It affects chip curl and evacuation.
*   **Flute:** The spiral groove that provides a channel for chip removal and allows coolant to reach the cutting lips.
*   **Web:** The solid metal core of the drill, which thickens towards the shank.
*   **Web Thickness:** The thickness of the web at the chisel edge.
*   **Shank:** The part of the drill that is held in the drill chuck or spindle.
    *   **Straight Shank:** Common for smaller diameter drills.
    *   **Taper Shank:** Used for larger diameter drills, providing a more rigid connection.
*   **Tang:** The tapered end of a taper shank, which fits into a driving slot in the drill sleeve or socket to prevent the drill from slipping.
*   **Neck:** A reduced diameter section on the shank, often found on larger drills to prevent interference with the workpiece or chuck.

### 6. Process Parameters in Drilling

The quality of the hole and the efficiency of the drilling operation are significantly influenced by several process parameters:

*   **Cutting Speed (v):** The peripheral speed of the drill bit at its outer diameter. It is usually expressed in meters per minute (m/min).
    *   **Formula:** $v = (\pi \times D \times N) / 1000$, where:
        *   $v$ = Cutting speed (m/min)
        *   $D$ = Drill diameter (mm)
        *   $N$ = Spindle speed (rpm)
*   **Spindle Speed (N):** The rotational speed of the drill bit, measured in revolutions per minute (rpm).
    *   **Relationship with Cutting Speed:** Higher cutting speed requires higher spindle speed (for a given diameter).
*   **Feed Rate (f):** The axial distance the drill advances into the workpiece per revolution of the drill. It is usually expressed in millimeters per revolution (mm/rev).
    *   **Impact:** Affects chip thickness, surface finish, and tool life.
*   **Depth of Cut (d):** In drilling, the depth of cut is essentially the depth of the hole being drilled. When considering additional operations like counterboring, it refers to the depth of the counterbored section.
*   **Coolant/Lubricant:** Used to reduce cutting temperature, lubricate the cutting edges, and flush away chips.
    *   **Types:** Water-based coolants, oil-based coolants, synthetic coolants.
    *   **Function:** Reduces friction, prevents tool wear, improves surface finish.
*   **Drill Material:** The material of the drill bit itself affects its cutting ability and durability (e.g., High-Speed Steel (HSS), Cobalt HSS, Carbide).
*   **Workpiece Material:** The hardness, strength, and thermal properties of the material being drilled significantly influence the optimal parameters.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Hajra Choudhury & Roy, "Elements of Workshop Technology Vol-II Machine Tools":** Provides tables of recommended cutting speeds and feed rates for various materials and drill sizes.
*   **Kalpakjian & Schmid, "Manufacturing Engineering and Technology":** Discusses the influence of each parameter on the machining process and tool life.

**Example:**

For drilling a 10mm diameter hole in mild steel with an HSS drill bit, a typical cutting speed might be 30 m/min. The spindle speed would then be calculated as:

$N = (v \times 1000) / (\pi \times D) = (30 \times 1000) / (\pi \times 10) \approx 955$ rpm.
A typical feed rate might be 0.15 mm/rev.

**Important Point to Remember:**

Selecting the correct cutting speed and feed rate is a balance between achieving a reasonable cutting time, obtaining a good surface finish, and maximizing tool life. Always consult machining handbooks or manufacturer recommendations for specific materials and tools.

### 7. Machining Time Calculations

Calculating machining time is essential for production planning, costing, and scheduling.

**Basic Formula for Machining Time (Tm):**

$T_m = (L / f_m) + T_t$

Where:

*   $T_m$ = Total Machining Time (minutes)
*   $L$ = Total length of cut (mm)
*   $f_m$ = Feed rate per minute (mm/min)
*   $T_t$ = Tool approach/retract time or any auxiliary time (minutes)

**Breakdown for Drilling:**

*   **Total Length of Cut (L):** This includes the actual depth of the hole plus any approach or over-travel of the drill.
    *   $L = d_{hole} + a + o$, where:
        *   $d_{hole}$ = Depth of the hole (mm)
        *   $a$ = Drill approach distance (mm) - This is the distance the drill travels before the cutting lips engage the material, effectively half the point angle. For a standard 118° drill, $a \approx 0.3 \times D$.
        *   $o$ = Over-travel (mm) - The distance the drill travels beyond the workpiece to ensure the hole is fully cut.
*   **Feed Rate per Minute ($f_m$):** This is the axial feed rate in mm/min.
    *   $f_m = f \times N$, where:
        *   $f$ = Feed rate per revolution (mm/rev)
        *   $N$ = Spindle speed (rpm)
*   **Tool Approach/Retract Time ($T_t$):** This accounts for the time taken to position the tool, start the spindle, and retract the tool. In simple calculations, it might be considered negligible or estimated based on the machine's capabilities.

**Putting it together for Drilling:**

$T_m = \frac{(d_{hole} + 0.3D)}{ (f \times N)} + T_t$

**Example Calculation:**

Calculate the machining time to drill a 20 mm deep hole of 10 mm diameter in mild steel.
Given:
*   Drill diameter ($D$) = 10 mm
*   Depth of hole ($d_{hole}$) = 20 mm
*   Feed rate per revolution ($f$) = 0.15 mm/rev
*   Spindle speed ($N$) = 955 rpm
*   Assume tool approach ($a$) $\approx 0.3 \times 10 = 3$ mm
*   Assume over-travel ($o$) = 2 mm
*   Assume auxiliary time ($T_t$) = 0.5 minutes (for spindle start/stop, positioning)

1.  **Calculate Total Length of Cut (L):**
    $L = d_{hole} + a + o = 20 + 3 + 2 = 25$ mm

2.  **Calculate Feed Rate per Minute ($f_m$):**
    $f_m = f \times N = 0.15 \text{ mm/rev} \times 955 \text{ rpm} = 143.25$ mm/min

3.  **Calculate Machining Time ($T_m$):**
    $T_m = (L / f_m) + T_t = (25 \text{ mm} / 143.25 \text{ mm/min}) + 0.5 \text{ min}$
    $T_m \approx 0.175 \text{ min} + 0.5 \text{ min} = 0.675$ minutes

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Hajra Choudhury & Roy, "Elements of Workshop Technology Vol-II Machine Tools":** Provides a dedicated chapter on machining time calculations with examples for various operations.
*   **Ghosh & Mallik, "Manufacturing Science":** Covers the theoretical aspects of calculating machining time based on cutting parameters.

**Important Point to Remember:**

Machining time calculations are estimates. Actual time can vary due to factors like operator skill, machine rigidity, and variations in workpiece material.

### 8. Cutting Forces in Drilling

The forces generated during drilling are critical for machine tool design, power requirement calculation, and tool life estimation.

**Primary Forces:**

*   **Thrust Force ($F_x$ or $P_z$):** The axial force required to push the drill into the workpiece. This is a significant force that can cause deflection and vibrations.
*   **Torque ($T$ or $M_t$):** The rotational force that drives the drill. It is required to overcome the cutting resistance of the material and friction.
*   **Radial Force ($F_y$ or $P_r$):** A smaller force that acts perpendicular to the axial feed, caused by the chisel edge and the helix angle. It can contribute to hole runout.

**Factors Affecting Cutting Forces:**

*   **Material Properties:** Hardness, tensile strength, and ductility of the workpiece material.
*   **Drill Geometry:** Point angle, helix angle, web thickness, and rake angle of the cutting lips.
*   **Cutting Parameters:** Cutting speed, feed rate, and depth of cut.
*   **Coolant:** The presence and type of coolant can influence friction and, consequently, forces.
*   **Tool Wear:** As the drill wears, cutting forces tend to increase.

**Empirical Formulas (Simplified Representations):**

Various empirical formulas exist to estimate cutting forces. One common approach relates the forces to the material's shear strength ($\tau_s$) and the cross-sectional area being cut.

*   **Approximate Torque ($T$):**
    $T \approx K_t \times f^a \times D^b \times \tau_s$ (This is a simplified conceptual representation, actual formulas are more complex and involve empirical constants derived from experimental data).
    A more practical approach considers the torque required for the main cutting edges and the chisel edge.
*   **Approximate Thrust Force ($F_x$):**
    $F_x \approx K_p \times f^c \times D^d \times \tau_s$

Where:
*   $K_t, K_p$ are empirical constants.
*   $a, b, c, d$ are exponents depending on material and drilling conditions.
*   $\tau_s$ is the shear strength of the material.

**Power Consumption:**

The power required for drilling is directly related to the torque and the spindle speed.

*   **Cutting Power ($P_c$):**
    $P_c = (2 \pi N T) / 60,000$ (in kW), where T is in N-m.
    Alternatively, using cutting speed: $P_c = (v \times F_c) / (1000 \times 60)$ where $F_c$ is the tangential cutting force.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Ghosh & Mallik, "Manufacturing Science":** Provides detailed theoretical analysis and empirical formulas for cutting forces in drilling.
*   **Kalpakjian & Schmid, "Manufacturing Engineering and Technology":** Discusses the mechanics of chip formation and force generation in drilling.

**Important Point to Remember:**

Cutting forces are crucial for selecting the appropriate machine tool, determining the power required, and estimating the tool life. They are also a key factor in understanding the forces that cause vibration and affect hole accuracy.

**Question 2 (CO2, K3):**
Calculate the machining time required to drill a hole of 15 mm diameter and 30 mm depth in mild steel. The recommended cutting speed is 35 m/min, and the feed rate is 0.2 mm/rev. Assume a drill approach of 0.3 times the drill diameter and an over-travel of 2 mm. The time taken for spindle start/stop and tool positioning is 0.75 minutes.

**Answer:**
**Given:**
*   $D = 15$ mm
*   $d_{hole} = 30$ mm
*   $v = 35$ m/min
*   $f = 0.2$ mm/rev
*   $a \approx 0.3 \times D = 0.3 \times 15 = 4.5$ mm
*   $o = 2$ mm
*   $T_t = 0.75$ min

**Steps:**
1.  **Calculate Spindle Speed (N):**
    $N = (v \times 1000) / (\pi \times D)$
    $N = (35 \times 1000) / (\pi \times 15) \approx 737$ rpm

2.  **Calculate Total Length of Cut (L):**
    $L = d_{hole} + a + o = 30 + 4.5 + 2 = 36.5$ mm

3.  **Calculate Feed Rate per Minute ($f_m$):**
    $f_m = f \times N = 0.2 \text{ mm/rev} \times 737 \text{ rpm} = 147.4$ mm/min

4.  **Calculate Machining Time ($T_m$):**
    $T_m = (L / f_m) + T_t$
    $T_m = (36.5 \text{ mm} / 147.4 \text{ mm/min}) + 0.75 \text{ min}$
    $T_m \approx 0.248 \text{ min} + 0.75 \text{ min} = 0.998$ minutes

**Machining Time $\approx 1.0$ minute.**

**Question 4 (Cutting Forces Concept):**
What are the main types of cutting forces encountered during drilling? Briefly explain the factors that influence these forces.

**Answer:**
The main types of cutting forces are:
1.  **Thrust Force:** The axial force pushing the drill into the material.
2.  **Torque:** The rotational force driving the drill.
3.  **Radial Force:** A minor force acting perpendicular to the axis of the drill.

**Factors influencing these forces include:**
*   Workpiece material properties (hardness, strength).
*   Drill geometry (point angle, helix angle).
*   Cutting parameters (cutting speed, feed rate).
*   Tool wear.
*   Use of coolant.

### 10. Important Points to Remember:

*   Drilling machines are versatile and can perform multiple hole-related operations.
*   The geometry of the drill bit, especially the point angle, is crucial for effective drilling.
*   Process parameters (cutting speed, feed rate) must be carefully selected based on the workpiece material and drill type.
*   Machining time calculations are essential for production planning.
*   Cutting forces are vital for machine tool design, power estimation, and understanding process stability.
*   The use of coolant is critical for reducing temperature, improving lubrication, and flushing chips, thereby extending tool life and improving surface finish.
*   Achieving precise hole dimensions and desired fits will rely on selecting appropriate drilling parameters and potentially follow-up operations like reaming or boring, as well as accurate measurement (linking to Metrology aspects).
