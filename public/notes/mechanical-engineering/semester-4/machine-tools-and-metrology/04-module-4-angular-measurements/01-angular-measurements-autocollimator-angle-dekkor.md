# Machine Tools and Metrology: Module 4 - Angular Measurements

This module focuses on advanced methods for measuring and setting angles with high precision, specifically exploring the principles and applications of the Autocollimator and the Angle Dekkor.

## Module 4: Angular Measurements - Autocollimator, Angle Dekkor

### 1. Introduction to Angular Measurement and Its Importance

*   **Definition:** Angular measurement is the process of determining the angle between two intersecting lines or planes.
*   **Significance:**
    *   Crucial in manufacturing for ensuring the accuracy of parts with inclined surfaces, bevels, chamfers, and angular features.
    *   Essential for aligning machine tool components (e.g., spindle axis, table rotation) to achieve desired precision in operations like milling, grinding, and turning.
    *   Vital in metrology for calibrating angular measuring instruments and verifying the angular accuracy of manufactured components.
    *   Plays a key role in optical alignment and surface flatness testing.
*   **Course Outcome Alignment:** CO1 (Describe various machine tool operations) and CO4 (Identify the uses of various advanced measuring instruments).

### 2. Autocollimator: Principles and Applications

**2.1. What is an Autocollimator?**

*   An autocollimator is a high-precision optical instrument used for measuring small angles of deviation. It acts as a visual alignment tool and an angular measuring device.
*   It consists of an eyepiece, a collimator lens, and a target (graticule). The collimator lens produces a parallel beam of light, which is then reflected off a mirror or a reflective surface on the workpiece. The reflected beam returns to the autocollimator and is viewed through the eyepiece.
*   **Key Concept:** It utilizes the principle of reflecting a parallel beam of light from a precisely controlled source and analyzing its return path to detect angular deviations.

**2.2. Working Principle:**

1.  **Light Source and Collimation:** A light source illuminates a graticule (a reticle with crosshairs or a scale). This light passes through a collimator lens, which converts the diverging light rays into a parallel beam.
2.  **Reflection:** This parallel beam is directed towards a flat, polished surface (usually a mirror attached to the workpiece or the workpiece itself if sufficiently reflective).
3.  **Return Beam:** The parallel beam reflects off the surface. If the surface is perfectly perpendicular to the incoming beam, the reflected beam will travel back along the same path.
4.  **Image Formation and Observation:** The reflected beam re-enters the autocollimator. The collimator lens refocuses these parallel rays. The eyepiece magnifies the image of the graticule as seen through the returning beam.
5.  **Angle Measurement:**
    *   If the reflecting surface is perfectly aligned (normal to the incident beam), the image of the graticule will be seen in its normal position within the eyepiece.
    *   If the reflecting surface is tilted by an angle $\theta$, the reflected beam will be deviated by an angle $2\theta$. This angular deviation causes the image of the graticule to shift its position in the field of view of the eyepiece.
    *   The scale in the eyepiece, or an external scale with a photographic recording or electronic detector, is used to measure this shift, which is directly proportional to the angular deviation ($2\theta$).

**2.3. Components of an Autocollimator:**

*   **Objective Lens (Collimator Lens):** Produces a parallel beam of light.
*   **Eyepiece:** Used to view the graticule and its reflected image.
*   **Graticule (Reticle):** A precision-etched scale or crosshairs within the instrument, used as a reference.
*   **Light Source:** Illuminates the graticule.
*   **Mirror/Reflective Surface:** Attached to or part of the workpiece to reflect the beam.
*   **Mounting Mechanism:** For stable positioning and adjustment.

**2.4. Types of Autocollimators:**

*   **Visual Autocollimator:** The operator visually observes the graticule shift through the eyepiece.
*   **Electronic/Digital Autocollimator:** Uses photodetectors or CCD cameras to capture the position of the reflected graticule, providing digital readouts and data logging capabilities.

**2.5. Key Features and Specifications:**

*   **Angular Range:** The maximum angle that can be measured.
*   **Resolution/Sensitivity:** The smallest angular change that can be detected.
*   **Accuracy:** The overall precision of the measurement.
*   **Working Distance:** The optimal distance between the autocollimator and the reflecting surface.
*   **Beam Diameter:** The diameter of the collimated beam.

**2.6. Applications of Autocollimators:**

*   **Machine Tool Alignment:**
    *   Checking and setting the squareness of machine tool slideways (e.g., ensuring the table moves perpendicular to the spindle axis).
    *   Verifying the alignment of rotary tables and indexing heads.
    *   Testing the straightness and flatness of machine tool beds and guideways.
    *   Measuring spindle runout and tilt.
*   **Optical Testing:**
    *   Measuring the flatness of optical surfaces (e.g., mirrors, lenses).
    *   Testing the parallelism and squareness of optical components.
    *   Aligning lasers and optical systems.
*   **Precision Engineering:**
    *   Calibrating other angular measuring instruments.
    *   Setting precise angles in precision assemblies.
    *   Measuring the angular displacement of components.
*   **Surface Plate Calibration:** Checking the flatness and level of granite surface plates.

**2.7. Advantages:**

*   High precision for small angle measurements.
*   Non-contact measurement, avoiding distortion of delicate parts.
*   Can measure over relatively long distances.
*   Versatile for various alignment tasks.

**2.8. Limitations:**

*   Requires a clean, flat, and highly reflective surface on the workpiece.
*   Sensitive to ambient vibrations and air currents.
*   Visual autocollimators are subjective and prone to operator fatigue.
*   Limited to measuring small angles.
*   Can be expensive.

### 3. Angle Dekkor: Principles and Applications

**3.1. What is an Angle Dekkor?**

*   The Angle Dekkor is a specialized optical instrument, similar in principle to an autocollimator but often designed for specific angular settings and measurements, particularly in manufacturing environments. It's essentially an instrument for setting and checking precise angles.
*   It typically uses a projection system to display an image of a calibrated graticule onto a screen or a workpiece.
*   **Key Concept:** It projects a precise angular scale or pattern, allowing for the visual setting and verification of angles relative to a reference.

**3.2. Working Principle:**

1.  **Light Source and Projection System:** A light source illuminates a graticule that contains precise angular markings or geometric patterns.
2.  **Projection:** This illuminated graticule is projected through a system of lenses and mirrors.
3.  **Angular Setting:** The instrument is set up such that the projected pattern is aligned with a reference line or surface on the workpiece.
4.  **Measurement/Setting:** The Angle Dekkor is often used to:
    *   **Set angles:** By adjusting the workpiece or the instrument until a specific angular marking on the projected graticule aligns with a reference feature.
    *   **Measure angles:** By observing the position of a feature on the workpiece relative to the projected angular scale.
    *   **Check angles:** Verifying if an existing angle on a part matches a specified angular setting.

**3.3. Components (Typical):**

*   **Illuminated Graticule:** Contains a precise angular scale or reference pattern.
*   **Projection Optics:** Lenses and mirrors to project the graticule image.
*   **Base/Stand:** For stable positioning.
*   **Adjustment Mechanisms:** For fine positioning and aiming.
*   **Screen (Optional):** Some models might have a built-in screen for viewing.

**3.4. Applications of Angle Dekkor:**

*   **Setting and Checking Angles on Machine Tools:**
    *   Setting the angle of a dividing head or rotary table.
    *   Checking the angle of tool heads on lathes or milling machines.
    *   Setting the swivel angle of machine tool components.
*   **Manufacturing of Angled Parts:**
    *   Used in workshops for setting up operations to produce parts with specific bevels, chamfers, or angled surfaces.
    *   Checking the angles of machined features during production.
*   **Calibration:**
    *   Used in the calibration of other angular measuring devices like inclinometers or protractors.
*   **Inspection:**
    *   Quickly verifying if components meet angular specifications.

**3.5. Advantages:**

*   Relatively simple to operate for angle setting and checking.
*   Provides a clear visual indication of the angle.
*   Can be used in workshop environments.
*   More direct for setting known angles compared to some autocollimator applications.

**3.6. Limitations:**

*   Precision is generally lower than a high-end autocollimator for minute angle measurements.
*   Accuracy depends on the quality of the projected graticule and the operator's ability to align it.
*   Primarily for visual comparison and setting, not for precise numerical measurement over long distances without a reference.
*   May require a suitable surface for projection or alignment.

**3.7. Reference Material:**

*   **Workshop Technology by Chapman W. A. J.:** Similar to Hajra Choudhury, this will provide practical context.

### 4. Comparison: Autocollimator vs. Angle Dekkor

| Feature           | Autocollimator                               | Angle Dekkor                                   |
| :---------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------- |
| **Primary Use**   | Measuring small angular deviations, alignment | Setting and checking specific angles           |
| **Principle**     | Reflection of a parallel beam of light       | Projection of an angular scale/pattern         |
| **Precision**     | Very High (for small deviations)             | Moderate to High (for setting/checking)        |
| **Measurement Type** | Indirect (measures deviation of reflected beam) | Direct (visual comparison with projected scale) |
| **Surface Req.**  | Highly reflective surface (mirror)           | Can be used with less reflective surfaces      |
| **Applications**  | Machine tool alignment, optical testing      | Angle setting on machines, workshop tasks      |
| **Complexity**    | Can be complex, requires precise setup       | Generally simpler operation                    |

### 5. Importance in Machine Tools and Manufacturing

*   **Machine Tool Alignment (CO1, CO4):**
    *   **Squareness:** Autocollimators are crucial for ensuring that the linear motion of one axis is perfectly perpendicular to another (e.g., spindle axis perpendicular to table axis). This guarantees that operations like milling produce accurate right angles.
    *   **Straightness and Flatness:** Used to check if slideways are straight and level, preventing errors in positioning and machining.
    *   **Spindle Accuracy:** Measuring tilt and runout of a spindle.
*   **Precision Angle Setting (CO4):** Angle Dekkors, and autocollimators in specific configurations, are used to accurately set the angles of machine tool components, rotary tables, and cutting tools. This is vital for producing parts with precise bevels, tapers, and angular features.
*   **Quality Control (CO4):** Both instruments are used in quality control to inspect manufactured parts for angular accuracy, ensuring they meet design specifications.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
An autocollimator is used to check the squareness of a machine tool's cross-slide to its main slideway. A mirror is attached to the cross-slide. When the main slideway is perfectly square, the image of the autocollimator's graticule appears at a certain position. If the cross-slide is found to be out of square by $0.05$ mm at a distance of $500$ mm from the autocollimator's objective lens, what is the angular deviation measured by the autocollimator in seconds of arc?

**Answer 1:**
*   The deviation on the scale is caused by a linear shift in the image.
*   Imagine a right-angled triangle where the adjacent side is the distance from the autocollimator to the mirror ($500$ mm) and the opposite side is the observed linear shift.
*   The observed linear shift is $0.05$ mm.
*   The angle of deviation measured by the autocollimator is $2\theta$.
*   Using trigonometry, $\tan(\theta) \approx \theta$ (in radians) for small angles.
*   The observed deviation in the autocollimator's field of view is related to the shift and distance.
*   The problem states a linear shift of $0.05$ mm *at a distance of $500$ mm*. This implies that the autocollimator scale is calibrated to show a certain linear shift corresponding to an angular deviation.
*   Let's assume the autocollimator's resolution is described by its ability to detect a linear shift. If a shift of $\Delta y$ is observed at distance $D$, the angle $\phi$ subtended at the autocollimator is $\tan(\phi) = \Delta y / D$.
*   In autocollimator readings, typically a specific linear shift on the reticle scale corresponds to a known angular value. For example, a common reading is in mm per meter, which can be converted to seconds.
*   Let's reinterpret the question: If the cross-slide is out of square by $0.05$ mm *linear displacement* at $500$ mm, this means the surface itself is angled such that at $500$ mm distance from the pivot point (which is effectively the mirror location if the pivot is on the slideway), there's a $0.05$ mm deviation.
*   The angle of deviation of the surface $\theta$ is such that $\tan(\theta) = \text{deviation} / \text{distance}$.
*   If we assume the $0.05$ mm is the *observed shift in the autocollimator eyepiece*, and the autocollimator's scale is calibrated such that a $1$ mm shift at $1$ meter distance represents a certain angle.
*   A common calibration unit for autocollimators is seconds of arc per millimeter of shift at a given distance. For instance, a common unit is 20 arc-seconds per millimeter at 1 meter.
*   Let's assume the $0.05$ mm is the measured shift on the autocollimator's graticule, and the instrument is calibrated to read $1$ unit of shift at $1$ meter for a specific angle.
*   If $1$ mm shift at $1$ meter ($1000$ mm) corresponds to an angle $\alpha$.
*   Then $0.05$ mm shift at $0.5$ meters ($500$ mm) corresponds to an angle $\beta$.
*   The angle $\phi$ measured by the autocollimator is directly proportional to the observed shift.
*   The question is a bit ambiguous about whether $0.05$ mm is the observed shift or the actual geometric deviation. Let's assume $0.05$ mm is the *linear shift observed on the autocollimator's graticule*.
*   Typical autocollimator scales might show a shift in mm at a standard distance (e.g., 1 meter). If the autocollimator has a scale where 1 division = 0.01 mm and the reading is 5 divisions, and the autocollimator is calibrated as 1 mm shift at 1 meter = 20 arc-seconds, then:
    *   Observed shift = $5 \times 0.01$ mm = $0.05$ mm.
    *   Angular deviation per mm at 1 meter = 20 arc-seconds/mm.
    *   Total angular deviation = $0.05$ mm $\times$ 20 arc-seconds/mm = $1$ arc-second.
    *   This is the deviation of the reflected beam ($2\theta$).
    *   The angular error of the surface $\theta$ would be $0.5$ arc-seconds.
*   **Let's reconsider the phrasing:** "out of square by $0.05$ mm at a distance of $500$ mm from the autocollimator's objective lens". This phrasing suggests a direct geometric measurement related to the workpiece's flatness or squareness.
*   If the surface itself is tilted by angle $\theta$, then at distance $D=500$ mm, the deviation on that surface would be $d = D \tan(\theta)$.
*   If the deviation $d = 0.05$ mm, then $\tan(\theta) = 0.05 \text{ mm} / 500 \text{ mm} = 0.0001$.
*   For small angles, $\theta \approx \tan(\theta)$ in radians.
*   $\theta \approx 0.0001$ radians.
*   To convert radians to seconds of arc: $1 \text{ radian} = (180/\pi) \times 3600 \text{ seconds of arc} \approx 206265 \text{ seconds of arc}$.
*   So, $\theta \approx 0.0001 \times 206265 \text{ arc-seconds} \approx 20.63 \text{ arc-seconds}$.
*   The autocollimator measures the deviation of the reflected beam, which is $2\theta$.
*   Therefore, the measured angle by the autocollimator would be $2 \times 20.63 \text{ arc-seconds} = 41.26 \text{ arc-seconds}$.
*   **Common Autocollimator Interpretation:** Autocollimators are often used to measure deviations of a surface from a reference plane. If the autocollimator measures an angular deviation, it's directly what it is calibrated to show. A common specification is resolution per angular unit. For example, a reading of 1 arc-second means the surface is tilted by 1 arc-second.
*   **Assuming the question means the autocollimator *reads* an angle corresponding to a linear deviation:** A common spec for autocollimators is that a shift of 1mm on the graticule at 1 meter distance corresponds to 20 arc-seconds.
*   If the autocollimator shows a shift of $0.05$ mm *on its own scale* (which is typically magnified and at a standard viewing distance), and the instrument is calibrated to show 1 arc-second per micron of deviation at 1 meter.
*   Let's use the first interpretation as it's more direct geometric application. The deviation of $0.05$ mm at $500$ mm from the autocollimator's objective lens implies a tilt. The autocollimator measures the angle of this tilt.
*   **The most straightforward interpretation:** The question is asking what the autocollimator measures. If the mirror is tilted by angle $\theta$, the reflected beam deviates by $2\theta$. The problem states the setup is "out of square by $0.05$ mm at a distance of $500$ mm". This phrasing implies the *error* creates a $0.05$ mm deviation at that distance.
*   So, the angle of the surface $\theta$ is such that $\tan(\theta) = 0.05/500 = 0.0001$.
*   The autocollimator measures $2\theta$.
*   $\tan(2\theta) = \frac{2 \tan(\theta)}{1 - \tan^2(\theta)} = \frac{2 \times 0.0001}{1 - (0.0001)^2} \approx 0.0002$.
*   So, $2\theta \approx 0.0002$ radians.
*   $2\theta \approx 0.0002 \times 206265 \text{ arc-seconds} \approx 41.25 \text{ arc-seconds}$.
*   **Final Answer based on standard interpretation of autocollimator application:** The autocollimator measures the deviation of the reflected beam. If the workpiece surface is tilted by $\theta$, the beam reflects at $2\theta$. The question is phrased as an error of $0.05$ mm at $500$ mm. This geometric error corresponds to an angle $\theta$. The autocollimator measures $2\theta$.
*   Angle of surface error $\theta = \arctan(0.05/500) = \arctan(0.0001)$.
*   Autocollimator reading $2\theta = 2 \arctan(0.0001)$.
*   Using calculator: $2 \times \arctan(0.0001) \approx 0.0002$ radians.
*   $0.0002 \text{ radians} \times \frac{180}{\pi} \times 3600 \text{ arc-seconds/radian} \approx 41.25 \text{ arc-seconds}$.

**Question 2:**
Describe two distinct applications of an autocollimator in a machine tool setting. (CO1, CO4)

**Answer 2:**
1.  **Checking the Squareness of Machine Tool Slideways:** An autocollimator can be used to verify that the movement of one axis (e.g., the cross-slide) is perfectly perpendicular to the movement of another axis (e.g., the main bed slideway). A mirror is mounted on the moving slide, and the autocollimator is fixed on the stationary one. If the slideways are not square, the reflected beam from the mirror will be deviated, and this deviation can be measured by the autocollimator, indicating the angular error. This ensures that operations like milling produce accurate right angles.
2.  **Verifying Spindle Alignment/Tilt:** An autocollimator can be used to measure the angular deviation (tilt) of a machine tool spindle axis relative to a reference line. A mirror is mounted on the spindle, and the autocollimator is positioned to observe the reflected beam. Any tilt in the spindle axis will cause a detectable shift in the autocollimator's graticule, allowing for precise measurement and correction of spindle alignment for critical operations like precision grinding or boring.

**Question 3:**
When would you prefer to use an Angle Dekkor over an Autocollimator for setting an angle? (CO4)

**Answer 3:**
You would prefer to use an Angle Dekkor when the primary task is to *set* a specific, predetermined angle on a machine tool or workpiece, rather than measuring a small deviation from a reference. For example:
*   **Setting a dividing head:** To achieve a specific angle for indexing a workpiece.
*   **Setting the angle of a swivel table:** For a milling operation that requires a precise angled cut.
*   **Checking an existing angle against a standard:** If you have a known angle to replicate or verify.

While an autocollimator is excellent for measuring very small deviations (e.g., flatness errors, out-of-squareness), an Angle Dekkor is often more straightforward for direct visual alignment and setting of larger, known angles due to its projected scale. The Angle Dekkor's projected pattern directly displays the angles, making it easier for an operator to align a component to a specific angular setting.

**Question 4:**
What is the fundamental principle behind the operation of an autocollimator? (Knowledge Level: K1)

**Answer 4:**
The fundamental principle behind the operation of an autocollimator is the **reflection of a parallel beam of light**. It uses a collimator lens to produce a parallel beam that is directed towards a mirror or reflective surface on the workpiece. This beam is reflected back, and the angular deviation of the reflected beam, caused by any tilt in the workpiece surface, is measured by observing the shift of a graticule image in the eyepiece. The instrument relies on the optical law that if a mirror is rotated by an angle $\theta$, the reflected beam rotates by $2\theta$.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Autocollimator:** Measures *small angular deviations* by reflecting a parallel beam of light. Requires a *reflective surface*. Key principle: deviation of reflected beam is *twice* the angle of the reflecting surface. Essential for *alignment* and *flatness testing*.
*   **Angle Dekkor:** Used for *setting and checking* precise angles. Projects an *angular scale* for visual alignment. Simpler for direct angle setting.
*   **Accuracy:** Autocollimators offer very high precision for small angle measurements. Angle Dekkors are accurate for their intended use of setting/checking angles.
*   **Applications:** Both instruments are vital for achieving precision in machine tool operations and manufacturing quality control, directly contributing to CO1 and CO4.
*   **Reflection vs. Projection:** Understand the core difference in their working principles.
