# Module 2: Demand Forecasting in Supply Chain - Aggregate Planning in Supply Chain

## 1. Introduction to Aggregate Planning

Aggregate planning is a crucial process in supply chain management that aims to balance supply and demand over a medium-term horizon (typically 3-12 months). It involves making decisions about aggregate production rates, inventory levels, and workforce size to meet anticipated demand while minimizing costs.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Aggregate:** Refers to planning at a high level, grouping products into families or categories rather than focusing on individual SKUs. This simplifies the planning process.
*   **Planning Horizon:** The time period for which the plan is developed. For aggregate planning, this is typically medium-term.
*   **Capacity:** The maximum output a firm can achieve. This can be influenced by workforce size, machine capacity, and operating hours.
*   **Demand:** The anticipated need for products or services.
*   **Costs:** Various costs are associated with aggregate planning, including production costs, inventory holding costs, backorder costs, hiring/firing costs, and overtime costs.

**Why is Aggregate Planning Important?**

*   **Balances Supply and Demand:** Helps avoid stockouts or excess inventory by aligning production with expected demand.
*   **Cost Minimization:** Identifies the most cost-effective way to meet demand by considering trade-offs between different production and workforce strategies.
*   **Improved Resource Utilization:** Optimizes the use of resources such as labor, machinery, and facilities.
*   **Enhanced Customer Service:** By meeting demand, it leads to better customer satisfaction.
*   **Foundation for Detailed Planning:** Provides a framework for more detailed production scheduling and resource allocation.

**Relationship to Demand Forecasting:**

Aggregate planning relies heavily on **demand forecasts**. Accurate demand forecasts are essential for developing realistic and effective aggregate plans. The process of aggregate planning often involves refining and adjusting initial demand forecasts based on capacity constraints and cost considerations.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

## 2. Components of Aggregate Planning

Aggregate planning decisions typically revolve around two main areas: **demand-side management** and **supply-side management**.

### 2.1. Demand-Side Management

This involves influencing demand to align it with supply capacity. Common strategies include:

*   **Pricing:** Offering discounts during off-peak periods or premium pricing during peak periods to shift demand.
*   **Promotions:** Running marketing campaigns and promotions to stimulate demand during slower periods.
*   **Backorders:** Accepting customer orders that cannot be fulfilled immediately due to stockouts, promising delivery at a later date.
*   **Tarrifs/Pre-empts:** Offering discounts for early orders or charging penalties for late orders.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

### 2.2. Supply-Side Management

This involves adjusting the supply capacity to meet demand. Common strategies include:

*   **Workforce Level Changes:**
    *   **Hiring:** Increasing the workforce to meet higher demand.
    *   **Firing/Layoffs:** Reducing the workforce during periods of lower demand.
*   **Workforce Utilization Changes:**
    *   **Overtime:** Increasing working hours for the existing workforce.
    *   **Undertime/Reduced Hours:** Decreasing working hours when demand is low.
*   **Inventory Buffers:** Building up inventory during periods of low demand to meet demand during peak periods.
*   **Outsourcing/Subcontracting:** Using external suppliers to produce goods or services when internal capacity is insufficient.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

## 3. Strategies for Aggregate Planning

Firms can adopt various strategies to implement aggregate planning. These strategies often involve trade-offs between costs and flexibility.

### 3.1. Chase Strategy

*   **Definition:** This strategy aims to match production exactly to demand in each period by adjusting workforce levels and/or production rates.
*   **How it works:**
    *   If demand increases, hire more workers or increase production hours.
    *   If demand decreases, lay off workers or reduce production hours.
*   **Pros:**
    *   Minimizes inventory holding costs.
    *   Avoids obsolescence of inventory.
*   **Cons:**
    *   High costs associated with frequent hiring and firing (recruitment, training, severance).
    *   Potential for labor morale issues and reduced productivity due to workforce instability.
    *   Can be difficult to implement if labor availability is constrained.
*   **Best suited for:** Situations where inventory holding costs are very high, and labor costs are relatively low or flexible.

**Example:** A seasonal business like a ski resort might hire temporary staff during the winter season and lay them off during the summer.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

### 3.2. Level Strategy (Stable Workforce, Variable Output)

*   **Definition:** This strategy maintains a stable workforce and production rate throughout the planning horizon. Any variations in demand are absorbed by adjusting inventory levels.
*   **How it works:**
    *   Produce at a constant rate, often based on the average demand over the planning horizon.
    *   Build inventory when demand is below the production rate.
    *   Use inventory to meet demand when demand exceeds the production rate.
*   **Pros:**
    *   Stable workforce leads to higher productivity and morale.
    *   Lower costs associated with hiring and firing.
    *   Consistent production can improve operational efficiency.
*   **Cons:**
    *   High inventory holding costs when demand is low.
    *   Risk of stockouts or backorders if demand surges unexpectedly and inventory is depleted.
    *   Potential for obsolescence of inventory.
*   **Best suited for:** Situations where inventory holding costs are manageable, and there is a need for a stable workforce and consistent production.

**Example:** A large appliance manufacturer might aim to produce a consistent number of units each month, regardless of short-term demand fluctuations, and then use inventory to smooth out supply.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

### 3.3. Mixed Strategy (Hybrid Approach)

*   **Definition:** This strategy combines elements of the chase and level strategies to achieve a balance between costs and flexibility.
*   **How it works:** Firms may use a combination of:
    *   Maintaining a core stable workforce.
    *   Utilizing overtime and undertime to manage moderate demand variations.
    *   Building some inventory.
    *   Potentially outsourcing for extreme demand peaks.
*   **Pros:**
    *   Offers a good balance between cost and flexibility.
    *   Can adapt to a wider range of demand patterns.
*   **Cons:**
    *   Requires careful planning to determine the optimal mix of strategies.
    *   May involve some level of inventory holding and workforce fluctuation costs.
*   **Best suited for:** Most organizations, as it allows for a pragmatic approach to managing supply chain operations.

**Example:** A retail company might maintain a stable workforce for the majority of the year, use overtime during peak seasons (like holidays), and build some inventory in anticipation of these peaks.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

## 4. Developing an Aggregate Plan: Methods and Techniques

Several methods can be used to develop an aggregate plan. These range from simple graphical and heuristic methods to more complex mathematical optimization techniques.

### 4.1. Graphical and Heuristic Methods

These methods are intuitive and easy to implement, often involving visual aids or simple decision rules.

*   **Graphical Method:**
    *   Plot the forecasted demand for each period.
    *   Plot cumulative demand.
    *   Determine a production rate (e.g., average demand or a level rate).
    *   Calculate inventory levels by subtracting production from demand.
    *   Calculate costs associated with different production rates, workforce changes, and inventory.
    *   Iterate to find a plan that minimizes costs.

*   **Heuristic Methods:** These use simple rules or "rules of thumb" to guide decisions. Examples include:
    *   **Lot-for-Lot:** Produce exactly what is needed for each period.
    *   **Fixed Period Requirements:** Produce enough to cover demand for a fixed number of periods.
    *   **Minimum Cost Method:** Aims to find the most cost-effective lot size.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

### 4.2. Mathematical Optimization Methods

These methods use mathematical models to find the optimal solution that minimizes total cost.

*   **Linear Programming (LP):**
    *   **Definition:** A mathematical technique used to determine the best outcome in a mathematical model whose requirements are represented by linear relationships.
    *   **Application in Aggregate Planning:** LP models can be formulated to minimize total costs (production, inventory, hiring, firing, overtime) subject to constraints such as demand, capacity, and inventory balance.
    *   **Decision Variables:** Production rate, workforce level, inventory level, overtime hours for each period.
    *   **Objective Function:** Minimize total cost.
    *   **Constraints:**
        *   Demand must be met (production + beginning inventory - ending inventory = demand).
        *   Workforce capacity limits.
        *   Inventory capacity limits.
        *   Non-negativity constraints.

**Example of an LP Formulation:**

Let:
*   $D_t$ = Demand in period $t$
*   $P_t$ = Production in period $t$
*   $I_t$ = Inventory at the end of period $t$
*   $W_t$ = Workforce level in period $t$
*   $O_t$ = Overtime hours in period $t$
*   $H_t$ = Hiring cost in period $t$
*   $F_t$ = Firing cost in period $t$
*   $C_P$ = Cost of production per unit
*   $C_I$ = Inventory holding cost per unit per period
*   $C_O$ = Overtime cost per unit
*   $C_H$ = Hiring cost per worker
*   $C_F$ = Firing cost per worker
*   $R$ = Regular production rate per worker per period
*   $RO$ = Overtime production rate per worker per period

**Objective Function:**
Minimize $\sum_{t=1}^{N} (C_P \cdot P_t + C_I \cdot I_t + C_O \cdot (\text{units produced by overtime})_t + C_H \cdot (\text{new hires})_t + C_F \cdot (\text{layoffs})_t)$

**Constraints:**

1.  **Demand Constraint:** $P_t + I_{t-1} - I_t = D_t$ for each period $t$.
2.  **Production Capacity (Regular):** $P_t \le R \cdot W_t$ for each period $t$.
3.  **Production Capacity (Overtime):** $(\text{units produced by overtime})_t \le RO \cdot W_t$ for each period $t$. (Note: This can also be modeled as total production being $P_t + (\text{units produced by overtime})_t \le (R+RO) \cdot W_t$).
4.  **Workforce Change:** $W_t = W_{t-1} + (\text{new hires})_t - (\text{layoffs})_t$ for each period $t$.
5.  **Non-negativity:** All variables $\ge 0$.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning; Simchi-Levi et al., Chapter 3: Inventory Management and Planning)**

*   **Other Optimization Techniques:**
    *   **Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP):** Used when decisions involve discrete quantities (e.g., number of workers to hire/fire).
    *   **Simulation:** Used to evaluate the performance of different aggregate plans under uncertainty.

## 5. Challenges in Aggregate Planning

*   **Inaccurate Demand Forecasts:** The quality of the aggregate plan is directly dependent on the accuracy of the demand forecasts.
*   **Uncertainty in Capacity:** Factors like machine breakdowns, employee absenteeism, and supplier reliability can impact actual capacity.
*   **Cost Trade-offs:** Balancing competing costs (inventory vs. production changes) can be complex.
*   **External Factors:** Economic conditions, competitor actions, and regulatory changes can influence demand and capacity.
*   **Product Families:** Defining appropriate product families for aggregation can be challenging, especially with product proliferation.
*   **Implementation Issues:** Resistance to change, lack of coordination between departments, and inadequate information systems can hinder effective implementation.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

## 6. Linking Aggregate Planning to Other Supply Chain Processes

Aggregate planning is not an isolated activity. It needs to be closely linked with other supply chain processes:

*   **Demand Forecasting:** As discussed, aggregate planning is driven by demand forecasts.
*   **Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP):** Aggregate planning is a core component of S&OP, which aligns sales forecasts with operational plans.
*   **Master Production Scheduling (MPS):** The aggregate plan provides the input for the MPS, which details production plans for specific product families.
*   **Material Requirements Planning (MRP):** MPS drives MRP, which determines the raw materials and components needed.
*   **Capacity Requirements Planning (CRP):** The aggregate plan informs CRP to ensure that sufficient capacity is available.
*   **Inventory Management:** Aggregate plans determine target inventory levels.
*   **Workforce Planning:** Decisions on hiring, firing, and overtime are direct outputs of aggregate planning.

**(Ref: Chopra & Kalra, Chapter 12: Aggregate Planning)**

## 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**

Explain the primary goal of aggregate planning in a supply chain. What are the main strategies used to achieve this goal, and what are the trade-offs associated with each?

**Answer:**
The primary goal of aggregate planning is to balance supply and demand over a medium-term horizon (3-12 months) while minimizing costs. The main strategies are:

1.  **Chase Strategy:** Matches production to demand by adjusting workforce levels.
    *   *Trade-offs:* Minimizes inventory but incurs high hiring/firing costs and can lead to workforce instability.
2.  **Level Strategy:** Maintains a stable workforce and production rate, using inventory to buffer demand fluctuations.
    *   *Trade-offs:* Stable workforce and lower hiring/firing costs but incurs high inventory holding costs and risks of stockouts.
3.  **Mixed Strategy:** Combines elements of both chase and level strategies.
    *   *Trade-offs:* Offers a balance between cost and flexibility but requires careful planning and can still involve some inventory and workforce costs.

**Question 2 (Problem-Solving - Simplified):**

A company forecasts the following demand for product "X" over the next four months:

| Month | Forecasted Demand |
| :---- | :---------------- |
| 1     | 500 units         |
| 2     | 700 units         |
| 3     | 900 units         |
| 4     | 600 units         |

The company has a starting inventory of 100 units.
*   Regular production cost: $10 per unit
*   Inventory holding cost: $2 per unit per month
*   Hiring cost: $100 per worker
*   Firing cost: $150 per worker
*   Each worker can produce 100 units per month at regular time.
*   The company starts with 6 workers.

**Task:** Develop an aggregate plan using a **level strategy** and calculate the total cost. Assume no overtime or subcontracting is allowed for this exercise.

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Calculate Average Demand**
Total Demand = 500 + 700 + 900 + 600 = 2700 units
Average Demand = 2700 units / 4 months = 675 units per month

**Step 2: Determine Production Rate (Level Strategy)**
Since we are using a level strategy, the production rate will be constant. To meet demand and manage inventory, the company should aim to produce at least the average demand. Let's assume production is set at 675 units per month.

**Step 3: Determine Workforce Level**
Since each worker produces 100 units/month, the number of workers needed for a production of 675 units is:
Workforce = 675 units / 100 units/worker = 6.75 workers.
Since we can't have fractions of workers, we'll need to round up to 7 workers to meet the production target consistently.

**Step 4: Calculate Production and Inventory for Each Month**

*   **Starting Workforce:** 7 workers
*   **Production per month:** 7 workers * 100 units/worker = 700 units

| Month | Forecasted Demand | Starting Inventory | Production | Ending Inventory | Units Produced by Overtime | Units Produced by Subcontract |
| :---- | :---------------- | :----------------- | :--------- | :--------------- | :-------------------------- | :---------------------------- |
| 1     | 500               | 100                | 700        | 100 + 700 - 500 = 300 | 0                           | 0                             |
| 2     | 700               | 300                | 700        | 300 + 700 - 700 = 300 | 0                           | 0                             |
| 3     | 900               | 300                | 700        | 300 + 700 - 900 = 100 | 0                           | 0                             |
| 4     | 600               | 100                | 700        | 100 + 700 - 600 = 200 | 0                           | 0                             |

**Step 5: Calculate Total Costs**

*   **Production Costs:** 700 units/month * 4 months * $10/unit = 2800 units * $10 = $28,000
*   **Inventory Holding Costs:**
    *   Month 1: 300 units * $2/unit = $600
    *   Month 2: 300 units * $2/unit = $600
    *   Month 3: 100 units * $2/unit = $200
    *   Month 4: 200 units * $2/unit = $400
    *   Total Inventory Holding Cost = $600 + $600 + $200 + $400 = $1,800
*   **Hiring Costs:**
    *   Starting workforce = 6 workers. Need 7 workers.
    *   Hiring cost = (7 - 6) * $100 = 1 worker * $100 = $100
*   **Firing Costs:** No firings in this plan. = $0

**Total Cost = Production Costs + Inventory Holding Costs + Hiring Costs**
Total Cost = $28,000 + $1,800 + $100 = **$29,900**

**(Note: A chase strategy would likely involve different costs due to hiring/firing. A mixed strategy would consider overtime.)**

## 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Aggregate planning bridges the gap between strategic supply chain design and detailed operational scheduling.**
*   **Demand forecasts are the bedrock of any aggregate plan.**
*   **The choice of strategy (chase, level, mixed) depends on cost structures, product characteristics, and market dynamics.**
*   **Linear Programming is a powerful tool for finding optimal aggregate plans when costs and constraints are well-defined.**
*   **Effective aggregate planning requires cross-functional collaboration (sales, marketing, operations, finance).**
*   **Aggregate planning is an iterative process that should be reviewed and updated regularly.**
