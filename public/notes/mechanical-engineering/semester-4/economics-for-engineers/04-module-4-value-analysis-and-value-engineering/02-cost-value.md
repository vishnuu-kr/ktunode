# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS

## Module 4: Value Analysis and Value Engineering

### Topic: Cost Value

---

### **1. Introduction to Cost Value**

This topic focuses on understanding the relationship between cost and value, a fundamental concept in both Value Analysis (VA) and Value Engineering (VE). It's about maximizing the value received for the cost incurred, leading to more efficient and competitive products and services.

**Key Concept:** Value is not simply the absence of cost, nor is cost the only determinant of value. Value is a perception that changes with the needs and expectations of the user.

**Learning Outcome Covered:** This topic directly addresses the ability to "Make use of the possibilities of value analysis and value engineering" (CO4), as understanding cost-value is the bedrock of these methodologies.

---

### **2. Defining Value**

In the context of engineering economics and VA/VE, value is defined in various ways, but a widely accepted definition is:

**Definition:** **Value = Function / Cost**

This simple equation highlights that value can be increased by:
*   Increasing the function (performance, quality, utility) of a product or service, while keeping the cost constant.
*   Decreasing the cost of a product or service, while keeping the function constant.
*   Increasing the function AND decreasing the cost simultaneously.

**Textbook Reference:** This definition is a cornerstone of value analysis and engineering and is consistently emphasized in works like those by Park (2001) and Thuesen & Fabrycky (1966).

**Examples:**

*   **Mobile Phone:** A smartphone with advanced features (camera, processing power, battery life) offers higher function than a basic feature phone. If both cost the same, the smartphone has higher value. Conversely, if a competitor offers a smartphone with similar features at a lower price, they are offering higher value.
*   **Car Engine:** An engine that is more fuel-efficient and produces more power (higher function) while consuming less fuel and having lower manufacturing costs (lower cost) represents higher value.

**Important Point to Remember:** Value is subjective and depends on the perspective of the stakeholder (customer, manufacturer, etc.).

---

### **3. Understanding Cost**

To effectively analyze value, we need to understand the different types of costs involved in a product's lifecycle.

**Types of Costs:**

*   **Direct Costs:** Costs directly attributable to the production of a specific product or service.
    *   **Direct Material Costs:** Cost of raw materials, components, etc.
    *   **Direct Labor Costs:** Wages paid to workers directly involved in production.
*   **Indirect Costs (Overheads):** Costs not directly tied to a specific product but necessary for overall operations.
    *   **Manufacturing Overheads:** Factory rent, utilities, machinery depreciation, indirect labor (supervisors, maintenance).
    *   **Selling & Administrative Overheads:** Marketing, sales staff, office rent, salaries of administrative personnel.
*   **Lifecycle Costs:** The total cost associated with a product from its inception to its disposal. This includes:
    *   Research & Development (R&D) Costs
    *   Design Costs
    *   Manufacturing Costs
    *   Marketing & Sales Costs
    *   Distribution Costs
    *   Operating Costs (for the user)
    *   Maintenance & Support Costs
    *   Disposal/Recycling Costs

**Textbook Reference:** Paneerselvam (2012) and Khan (2011) provide detailed discussions on various cost classifications relevant to engineering economic decisions.

**Example:**
Consider a manufactured chair.
*   **Direct Costs:** Wood, screws, labor of the carpenter assembling the chair.
*   **Indirect Costs (Manufacturing):** Rent for the workshop, electricity for tools, salary of the workshop supervisor.
*   **Indirect Costs (Selling & Admin):** Advertising the chair, salary of the salesperson, rent for the showroom.
*   **Lifecycle Costs:** Design of the chair, manufacturing, shipping to a retailer, warranty service, and eventually, the cost of disposing of it.

**Key Concept:** Understanding lifecycle costs is crucial for true value analysis, as focusing only on manufacturing costs can lead to suboptimal decisions regarding durability or maintenance. (Blank & Tarquin, 7th Edition).

### **4. Understanding Function**

Function refers to what a product or service *does* for the user. It's the purpose or utility it serves. Functions can be categorized as:

**Types of Functions:**

*   **Basic Function:** The primary purpose or fundamental use of the product/service. This is the essential reason for its existence.
*   **Secondary Function:** Additional features, capabilities, or qualities that enhance the basic function or provide added convenience, aesthetic appeal, or performance.

**Textbook Reference:** Park (2001) emphasizes the importance of identifying and analyzing functions as the first step in VA/VE.

**Examples:**

*   **Hammer:**
    *   **Basic Function:** To drive nails.
    *   **Secondary Functions:** To pry nails, to provide a striking surface for other tools, aesthetic design of the handle.
*   **Ballpoint Pen:**
    *   **Basic Function:** To write.
    *   **Secondary Functions:** Comfortable grip, clip for attaching to a pocket, aesthetic appearance, refillability.
*   **Car:**
    *   **Basic Function:** To transport people from one place to another.
    *   **Secondary Functions:** Air conditioning, entertainment system, safety features (airbags, ABS), comfort (leather seats), aesthetic design.

**Important Point to Remember:** Clearly identifying and differentiating between basic and secondary functions is critical. Often, cost reduction efforts focus on secondary functions that don't significantly impact the basic utility for the user, thus increasing value. (Newman & Lavelle, 2002).

### **5. Cost-Value Relationship and its Importance**

The core of VA/VE is to systematically analyze and improve the cost-value relationship.

**Key Principles:**

*   **Cost Reduction vs. Value Improvement:**
    *   **Cost Reduction:** Simply lowering costs without considering the impact on function can lead to a decrease in value (e.g., using cheaper materials that break easily).
    *   **Value Improvement:** Aims to increase value by either enhancing function at the same cost or reducing cost while maintaining or improving function.
*   **Customer Perspective:** The ultimate measure of value is whether it meets the customer's needs and expectations at a given price.
*   **Life Cycle Costing:** Considering the total cost over the product's life is essential for a comprehensive value analysis.
*   **Functional Cost Analysis:** Breaking down costs by function helps identify areas where costs are disproportionately high for the value delivered.

**Textbook Reference:** Thuesen & Fabrycky (1966) and Park (2001) extensively discuss how economic principles guide decisions to improve value through cost-function optimization.

**Examples:**

*   **Packaging:** A company might reduce the thickness of its product packaging to save material costs. If this leads to increased product damage during shipping (reduced function/reliability), the value may decrease despite the cost saving. However, if a lighter, more sustainable packaging material offers equal protection at a lower cost, it's a clear value improvement.
*   **Software Features:** Adding a complex, rarely used feature to a software product increases development and maintenance costs. If this feature doesn't significantly enhance the user's experience or solve a critical problem (limited functional benefit), it reduces the overall value.

**CO4 Alignment:** Understanding this relationship is fundamental to using VA/VE possibilities to solve business problems, as it provides the framework for identifying improvement opportunities.

---

### **6. Tools and Techniques for Cost-Value Analysis**

Several techniques are used within VA/VE to analyze and improve the cost-value equation. While a full exploration of VA/VE methodologies is beyond this specific topic, understanding the underlying cost-value analysis is key.

*   **Functional Cost Analysis (FCA):** A systematic method of identifying and quantifying the functions of a product or service and the costs associated with each function. This helps pinpoint areas of "high cost/low function" or "low cost/high function."
*   **Brainstorming and Creative Thinking:** Generating ideas for alternative ways to achieve the required functions at a lower cost.
*   **Benchmarking:** Comparing the product's cost-value against competitors or industry best practices.
*   **Pareto Analysis (80/20 Rule):** Focusing efforts on the 20% of functions or cost drivers that contribute to 80% of the problem or value.

**Textbook Reference:** Blank & Tarquin (7th Edition) and Park (2001) introduce these techniques as practical applications of engineering economic principles.

### **7. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**
Define "Value" in the context of Value Analysis and Value Engineering. Explain the equation used and how value can be increased.

**Answer 1:**
Value is defined as **Value = Function / Cost**.
Value can be increased by:
1.  Increasing Function while keeping Cost constant.
2.  Decreasing Cost while keeping Function constant.
3.  Increasing Function and decreasing Cost simultaneously.

**Question 2:**
A company is considering using a cheaper adhesive to assemble its electronic devices, which would reduce material costs by $0.50 per unit. However, customer complaints about product failures due to weak adhesion have increased by 15% since the change.
*   Identify the basic and secondary functions of the adhesive in this context.
*   Discuss whether this change represents an improvement in value. Justify your answer using the Value = Function / Cost concept.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Basic Function of the adhesive:** To securely bond components together, ensuring the device functions as intended.
*   **Secondary Function of the adhesive:** Potentially to reduce manufacturing cost (from the company's perspective) or contribute to product aesthetics (if visible).
*   **Analysis:**
    *   **Cost:** Decreased by $0.50 per unit.
    *   **Function:** Decreased due to increased product failures (weak adhesion). This directly impacts the product's reliability and performance, which are key functional aspects for the customer.
    *   **Value:** The decrease in function (increased failures) likely outweighs the decrease in cost. This means **Value = Reduced Function / Reduced Cost** is likely less than the original value. Therefore, this change does *not* represent an improvement in value, and may even represent a decrease in value from the customer's perspective.

**Question 3:**
Consider a car door. List its possible basic and secondary functions. How could a Value Engineering approach be applied to improve its cost-value relationship?

**Answer 3:**
*   **Basic Functions:**
    *   Provide access to the car interior.
    *   Enclose the car interior from the outside environment.
    *   Provide structural integrity to the car body.
*   **Secondary Functions:**
    *   Aesthetics (design, paint finish).
    *   Comfort (armrest, storage compartment).
    *   Safety (impact resistance, side-impact beams, airbag deployment).
    *   Convenience (power windows, central locking).
    *   Aerodynamics.
*   **VE Application for Cost-Value Improvement:**
    *   **Analyze each function:** Is the current cost for each function justified by its contribution to the overall value?
    *   **Reduce Cost of Secondary Functions:** Can the door be made lighter without compromising safety or access? Can the aesthetics be achieved with less expensive materials or processes? Can storage compartments be simplified?
    *   **Improve Function (if cost-effective):** Could a minor increase in cost for a different material lead to significant weight reduction, improving fuel efficiency (a key customer value)? Can safety features be integrated more cost-effectively?
    *   **Example Idea:** Using high-strength, low-weight aluminum or composite materials for the door panel instead of traditional steel could reduce weight (improve fuel efficiency/performance function) even if the initial material cost is slightly higher, as the overall lifecycle value might increase.

### **8. Highlighting Important Points to Remember**

*   **Value is the core concept:** Always focus on **Value = Function / Cost**.
*   **Customer is king:** Value is defined by the customer's needs and perception.
*   **Functions are paramount:** Thoroughly identify and understand both basic and secondary functions.
*   **Lifecycle costs matter:** Don't get fixated only on immediate manufacturing costs.
*   **Seek win-win:** Aim to improve value by enhancing function, reducing cost, or both.
*   **Cost reduction alone is not value improvement.**
