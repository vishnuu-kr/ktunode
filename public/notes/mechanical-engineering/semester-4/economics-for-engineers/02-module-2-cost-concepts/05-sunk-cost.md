# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS

## Module 2: Cost Concepts

### Topic: Sunk Cost

---

**Learning Outcomes:**

*   Understand the definition and nature of sunk costs.
*   Distinguish sunk costs from relevant costs in decision-making.
*   Analyze the impact of sunk costs on engineering and business decisions.
*   Apply the principle of ignoring sunk costs in practical scenarios.

---

**Introduction:**

In engineering economics, understanding different types of costs is crucial for making sound financial and strategic decisions. Sunk costs represent a specific category of cost that often leads to flawed decision-making if not properly understood and managed. This topic will define sunk costs, explain why they are irrelevant to future decisions, and provide examples to illustrate their impact.

---

### 1. Definition of Sunk Cost

*   **Definition:** A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of future decisions or actions. It is an expenditure that has already happened and is gone forever.

*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   **Irrecoverable:** Once spent, the money is gone and cannot be retrieved.
    *   **Past Expenditure:** Sunk costs relate to decisions made in the past.
    *   **Irrelevant to Future Decisions:** Because they cannot be changed or recovered, sunk costs should not influence future choices.

---

### 2. Sunk Cost vs. Relevant Cost

*   **Relevant Costs:** These are future costs that differ among the alternative courses of action being considered. They are the costs that are pertinent to the decision at hand.
*   **Irrelevant Costs:** These are costs that do not change regardless of the decision made. Sunk costs are a primary example of irrelevant costs. Other irrelevant costs include those that are the same for all alternatives.

*   **The Core Principle:** When making a decision, engineers and managers should focus solely on **relevant costs** and ignore **sunk costs**.

---

### 3. Why Sunk Costs Are Irrelevant to Future Decisions

*   **Focus on Future:** Decisions are about the future. Sunk costs are about the past. Focusing on past expenditures does not alter the potential outcomes of future choices.
*   **Opportunity Cost:** By continuing to invest in a project or venture based on past sunk costs, one might be foregoing a more profitable or efficient future opportunity.
*   **Emotional Bias (Sunk Cost Fallacy):** People often have a psychological tendency to continue investing in something because they have already invested a significant amount of resources (time, money, effort) into it, even if it's no longer a rational decision. This is known as the "sunk cost fallacy" or "escalation of commitment."

---

### 4. Examples of Sunk Costs in Engineering and Business

*   **Example 1: Software Development**
    *   A company has spent \$1 million developing a new software product.
    *   Market research now indicates that the product is unlikely to be successful.
    *   The \$1 million is a sunk cost.
    *   **Decision:** Should the company continue investing more money to complete and launch the software, or abandon the project?
    *   **Correct Approach:** The decision should be based on the *future* costs and *future* benefits of completing the software versus the costs and benefits of abandoning it. The initial \$1 million should be ignored. If the future costs of completion outweigh the future expected revenues, the project should be abandoned, regardless of how much has already been spent.

*   **Example 2: Equipment Purchase and Obsolescence**
    *   An engineering firm bought a specialized machine for \$200,000 five years ago.
    *   The machine has been depreciated and has a book value of \$50,000.
    *   A new, more efficient machine is available for \$150,000.
    *   The old machine can be sold for \$10,000 as scrap.
    *   **Analysis:**
        *   The \$200,000 purchase price is a sunk cost.
        *   The \$50,000 book value is an accounting concept and not a cash flow relevant to the *future* decision.
        *   The relevant costs and benefits for buying the new machine are:
            *   **Cost of new machine:** \$150,000
            *   **Salvage value of old machine (opportunity cost of keeping it):** \$10,000
            *   **Net cash outflow for new machine:** \$150,000 - \$10,000 = \$140,000
        *   The decision to buy the new machine should be based on whether the future benefits (increased efficiency, reduced operating costs) of the new machine justify this \$140,000 net investment, not on the original \$200,000 cost or the current book value.

*   **Example 3: Research and Development (R&D)**
    *   A pharmaceutical company has spent \$50 million on R&D for a new drug.
    *   Clinical trials are ongoing, and there's a significant chance the drug won't receive regulatory approval or won't be commercially viable.
    *   **Decision:** Continue funding the R&D or terminate the project?
    *   **Correct Approach:** The \$50 million is sunk. The decision should be based on projected future costs to bring the drug to market versus the projected future revenues and market share, considering the risk of failure.

---

### 5. Incorporating Sunk Cost Concepts in Decision-Making (Linking to Course Outcomes)

*   **CO2: Develop decision making capability by applying concepts relating to costs and revenue...**
    *   Sunk cost is a critical concept for making rational financial decisions. By understanding that sunk costs are irrelevant, engineers can avoid emotional biases and focus on the economic viability of future options. This leads to better allocation of resources.
*   **CO4: Make use of the possibilities of value analysis and engineering, and solve simple business problems using break even analysis, cost benefit analysis and capital budgeting techniques.**
    *   In **Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)**, only future costs and benefits are considered. Sunk costs are explicitly excluded.
    *   In **Capital Budgeting**, when evaluating investment proposals, the initial outlay for a project is a future cost. However, any money already spent on preliminary studies or designs for that project should not be factored into the decision of whether to proceed with the *main* investment.
    *   **Value Engineering** often involves re-evaluating a product or process to reduce costs while maintaining or improving functionality. If a certain component or process has already been designed and costed, but a cheaper alternative is identified, the costs already incurred in the previous design phase are sunk. The decision should then focus on the future cost savings from the new design.

---

### 6. Key Points to Remember

*   **Sunk costs are past costs that cannot be recovered.**
*   **Always ignore sunk costs when making future decisions.**
*   **Focus on future incremental costs and benefits.**
*   **Be aware of the sunk cost fallacy (escalation of commitment) and resist it.**
*   **Relevant costs are future costs that differ between alternatives.**

### 8. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
A company invested \$500,000 in a research project to develop a new product. After spending the money, they realized the market demand for the product is much lower than anticipated. The project requires an additional \$200,000 to complete. If the projected future revenue from the product is \$400,000, should the company proceed with the project? Explain your reasoning.

**Answer:**
The \$500,000 already spent is a sunk cost and is irrelevant to the decision. The decision should be based on the future costs and future benefits.
*   Future Cost: \$200,000
*   Future Benefit (Projected Revenue): \$400,000
Since the future benefit (\$400,000) is greater than the future cost (\$200,000), the company *should* proceed with the project. The decision is based on the marginal profitability, not on recouping past losses.

**Question 2:**
An engineering firm purchased a specialized testing rig for \$150,000 three years ago. Its book value is currently \$75,000. They can sell it today for \$20,000 as scrap. A new, more advanced testing rig that will save \$30,000 per year in operating costs compared to the old rig is available for \$100,000. Assuming a 5-year project life for the new rig and ignoring depreciation and taxes for simplicity, should the firm buy the new rig?

**Answer:**
*   **Sunk Cost:** The original \$150,000 purchase price is a sunk cost. The book value of \$75,000 is also irrelevant to the *cash flow* decision.
*   **Relevant Cost of New Rig:** The cost of the new rig is \$100,000.
*   **Opportunity Cost of Keeping Old Rig:** The \$20,000 salvage value that can be obtained by selling the old rig is the opportunity cost of keeping it. This amount should be subtracted from the cost of the new rig.
*   **Net Investment in New Rig:** \$100,000 (cost of new) - \$20,000 (salvage of old) = \$80,000.
*   **Total Savings over 5 Years:** \$30,000/year * 5 years = \$150,000.
*   **Decision:** The net investment in the new rig is \$80,000. The total savings generated by the new rig over its life is \$150,000. Since the total savings (\$150,000) are significantly greater than the net investment (\$80,000), the firm should buy the new testing rig. The original \$150,000 and the \$75,000 book value are irrelevant to this decision.
