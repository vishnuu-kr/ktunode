# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts

## Topic: Explicit and Implicit Cost

This topic delves into the fundamental components of cost that engineers must understand for effective decision-making in projects and business operations. Recognizing the difference between explicit and implicit costs is crucial for accurate cost analysis, profitability assessment, and resource allocation.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

#### 1. Cost Concepts: An Overview

In engineering and business, cost refers to the monetary value of resources used to produce a good or service, or to undertake a project. Engineers are often involved in estimating, managing, and minimizing costs to ensure project feasibility and profitability.

*   **Cost:** The expenditure incurred in acquiring or using goods or services.
*   **Economic Cost:** The sum of explicit and implicit costs, representing the true cost of any activity or decision. This is often more relevant for economic decision-making than accounting cost alone. (Referenced from Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury).

#### 2. Explicit Cost (Accounting Cost / Out-of-Pocket Cost)

Explicit costs are direct, out-of-pocket payments made by a firm or an individual for the use of resources. These are the costs that are easily quantifiable, recorded in accounting statements, and involve a direct transfer of money.

*   **Definition:** Payments made to external parties for the use of resources.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Involve actual monetary transactions.
    *   Easily identifiable and measurable.
    *   Recorded in the firm's financial accounts (e.g., income statement, balance sheet).
    *   Also known as accounting costs or out-of-pocket costs.
*   **Examples for Engineers:**
    *   **Wages and Salaries:** Payments to employees, including engineers, technicians, and administrative staff.
    *   **Raw Material Costs:** Expenditure on materials used in manufacturing or construction.
    *   **Rent:** Payment for the use of office space, factory buildings, or land.
    *   **Utilities:** Costs of electricity, water, gas, and internet for operations.
    *   **Machinery and Equipment Purchase/Lease:** Costs associated with acquiring or renting necessary equipment.
    *   **Interest Payments:** Costs incurred on loans taken for project financing.
    *   **Transportation Costs:** Expenses for moving materials, equipment, or finished goods.
    *   **Marketing and Sales Expenses:** Costs related to promoting and selling products or services.

    *   **(From Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam, 2012):** Explicit costs are the most obvious costs that appear on financial statements and are considered when calculating profit in the accounting sense.

#### 3. Implicit Cost (Opportunity Cost / Imputed Cost)

Implicit costs represent the value of resources owned by the firm or individual that are used in the production process, but for which no direct monetary payment is made. These costs are not typically recorded in accounting statements but are crucial for economic decision-making. They represent the *opportunity cost* of using these resources in a particular way rather than their next best alternative.

*   **Definition:** The value of foregone opportunities when resources owned by the firm are used for a specific purpose.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Do not involve direct monetary transactions.
    *   Represent the value of sacrificed alternatives.
    *   Not recorded in standard accounting statements.
    *   Crucial for evaluating the true profitability of a venture and making strategic decisions.
    *   Also known as opportunity costs or imputed costs.
*   **Examples for Engineers:**
    *   **Owner's Salary/Foregone Income:** If an engineer who owns a consulting firm works full-time in the firm, the salary they *could have earned* working for another company is an implicit cost. (Referenced from Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury).
    *   **Return on Owner's Capital:** If an entrepreneur invests their own capital into a project, the return they *could have earned* by investing that capital elsewhere (e.g., in stocks or bonds) is an implicit cost. This is also referred to as the "normal profit" necessary to keep the entrepreneur in business.
    *   **Use of Owner's Property:** If a firm uses a building owned by the founder without paying rent, the rent that could have been earned by leasing the building to a third party is an implicit cost.
    *   **Owner's Time (non-salaried):** Time spent by the owner on activities that don't have a direct salary attached but contribute to the business's operations.
    *   **(From Engineering Economics by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky, 1966):** The concept of opportunity cost is central to economic analysis. Even when no cash changes hands, the sacrifice of an alternative use of a resource constitutes a real cost.

#### 4. Economic Cost vs. Accounting Cost

The distinction between explicit and implicit costs leads to the concept of economic cost.

*   **Accounting Cost:** The sum of all explicit costs incurred by a firm.
    *   *Accounting Cost = Sum of Explicit Costs*
*   **Economic Cost:** The sum of both explicit and implicit costs. It represents the total sacrifice made to employ resources.
    *   *Economic Cost = Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs*

*   **Profit:**
    *   **Accounting Profit:** Revenue - Accounting Cost
    *   **Economic Profit:** Revenue - Economic Cost = Revenue - (Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs)
    *   **Normal Profit:** The minimum level of profit needed to compensate the entrepreneur for their time, effort, and risk, including the opportunity cost of their capital. Economic profit is profit above and beyond normal profit.

*   **Relevance for Engineers:**
    *   When evaluating the feasibility of a new project, comparing it against alternatives, or making pricing decisions, using economic cost (including implicit costs) provides a more accurate picture of the true cost and potential profitability.
    *   Ignoring implicit costs can lead to decisions that appear profitable on paper but are actually less advantageous than foregone alternatives. (Referenced from Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park, 2001).

### Examples Illustrating Explicit and Implicit Costs:

**Scenario 1: A Small Engineering Consulting Firm Started by an Engineer**

*   **Engineer's Investment:** The engineer invests $50,000 of their own savings into the firm.
*   **Engineer's Salary:** The engineer pays themselves a salary of $60,000 per year.
*   **Office Rent:** The firm rents an office space for $1,000 per month ($12,000 annually).
*   **Equipment:** The firm buys computer equipment for $10,000.
*   **Opportunity Cost of Capital:** The engineer could have invested their $50,000 savings in a mutual fund that yields 8% per year.
*   **Foregone Salary Opportunity:** The engineer could have worked for another company as a senior engineer, earning $80,000 per year.

**Analysis:**

*   **Explicit Costs:**
    *   Engineer's Salary: $60,000
    *   Office Rent: $12,000
    *   Computer Equipment (Depreciation/Amortization - assuming this is how it's treated for cost): Let's say $2,000/year.
    *   **Total Explicit Costs:** $60,000 + $12,000 + $2,000 = $74,000

*   **Implicit Costs:**
    *   Opportunity Cost of Capital: 8% of $50,000 = $4,000
    *   Foregone Salary Opportunity: $80,000 (This is the *opportunity cost* of the engineer's own time, which is higher than the salary they pay themselves. The $60,000 is an explicit cost paid *to themselves*, but the true opportunity cost of their labor is $80,000).
    *   **Total Implicit Costs:** $4,000 + $80,000 = $84,000

*   **Economic Cost:** $74,000 (Explicit) + $84,000 (Implicit) = $158,000

*   **If the firm's total revenue is $160,000:**
    *   **Accounting Profit:** $160,000 - $74,000 = $86,000
    *   **Economic Profit:** $160,000 - $158,000 = $2,000

    *   In this case, the firm is making an accounting profit, but a very small economic profit. The engineer is earning more than their foregone salary and the return on their capital. If the economic profit were negative, the engineer would be better off financially by closing the firm and pursuing their next best alternative.

**Scenario 2: A Manufacturing Plant Using Owned Land**

*   A manufacturing company owns a plot of land worth $1,000,000.
*   The company uses this land for its factory operations.
*   The company could have rented out this land to another business for $50,000 per year.

**Analysis:**

*   **Explicit Costs:** All direct payments for factory operation (labor, materials, utilities, depreciation of buildings and machinery, etc.).
*   **Implicit Cost:** The $50,000 annual rental income foregone by using the land for its own factory. This is the opportunity cost of using the land.

### Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:**
Differentiate between explicit and implicit costs. Provide two examples of each from an engineering project perspective.

**Answer:**
*   **Explicit Costs:** Direct monetary payments made to external parties. Examples:
    1.  Cost of specialized equipment purchased for a construction project.
    2.  Wages paid to construction workers and supervisors.
*   **Implicit Costs:** Opportunity costs of using resources owned by the entity. Examples:
    1.  The interest income foregone by using the company's own retained earnings to fund a new R&D project instead of investing it in financial markets.
    2.  The value of using an existing company-owned workshop for a new prototype development, when that workshop could have been leased out to generate rental income.

**Question 2:**
A chemical engineering firm is considering a new process optimization project.
*   The project requires purchasing new sensors for $10,000.
*   The project team will use an existing company laboratory, which could have been rented out to another lab for $5,000 per year.
*   The lead engineer on the project will forgo overtime work at their current job, which pays $8,000 per year.
*   The project will incur electricity costs of $1,000.

Calculate the explicit costs, implicit costs, and total economic cost for the first year of the project.

**Answer:**
*   **Explicit Costs:**
    *   New Sensors: $10,000
    *   Electricity Costs: $1,000
    *   **Total Explicit Costs:** $10,000 + $1,000 = $11,000
*   **Implicit Costs:**
    *   Foregone Laboratory Rental: $5,000
    *   Foregone Overtime Work: $8,000
    *   **Total Implicit Costs:** $5,000 + $8,000 = $13,000
*   **Total Economic Cost (for the first year):** Total Explicit Costs + Total Implicit Costs = $11,000 + $13,000 = $24,000

**Question 3:**
Why is understanding implicit costs important for engineers when making investment decisions or evaluating project profitability?

**Answer:**
Understanding implicit costs is crucial because they represent the true cost of using resources, including the value of forgone alternatives. For engineers involved in project evaluation and investment decisions, ignoring implicit costs can lead to an overestimation of profitability and an underestimation of the true cost of a project. This can result in suboptimal decisions, such as choosing a project that appears profitable but is less beneficial than another opportunity that was not fully considered due to a narrow focus on explicit (accounting) costs. Economic profit, which accounts for both explicit and implicit costs, provides a more accurate measure of whether a project is truly value-creating. (Referenced from Engineering Economics and analysis by Donald G. Newman, Jerome P. Lavelle, 2002).

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Explicit costs are about money paid out; implicit costs are about value forgone.**
*   **Accounting profit is based on explicit costs, while economic profit is based on total economic costs (explicit + implicit).**
*   **Implicit costs are also known as opportunity costs.**
*   **For sound economic decision-making, engineers must consider both explicit and implicit costs.**
*   **Ignoring implicit costs can lead to a false sense of profitability and poor resource allocation.**
*   **Normal profit is the minimum return required to keep a business operating, covering all implicit costs.**
*   **Engineering projects often involve opportunities for using existing company resources, where implicit costs (like foregone rental income or alternative uses of engineer's time) are significant.**
