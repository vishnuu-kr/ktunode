## Topic: Metallurgy - Specimen Preparation, Etching & Microscopic Study of Steel, Cast iron and Brass and Grain Size Measurement

### 1. Introduction to Metallurgy in Manufacturing

Metallurgy is the science and engineering of metals. Understanding the microstructure of metals is crucial in manufacturing as it directly influences their properties, such as strength, hardness, ductility, and wear resistance. This knowledge allows us to select appropriate materials for specific applications and optimize manufacturing processes.

**Key Concept:** **Microstructure** refers to the structure of a metal at a microscopic level, including the arrangement of grains, phases, and any inclusions.

**Relevance to Manufacturing Technology Lab (Lathe Operations):**
*   **Material Selection:** Knowing the microstructure helps in choosing the right material (steel, cast iron, brass) for a particular workpiece to be machined on a lathe.
*   **Machining Parameters:** The microstructure affects how a material behaves during cutting (cutting forces, tool wear, surface finish).
*   **Quality Control:** Microscopic examination can reveal defects that might arise during manufacturing or heat treatment, impacting the final product's performance.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO5:** Gain knowledge on the structure, properties, testing and applications of ferrous and non ferrous metals. (Knowledge Level: K3) - *This topic directly addresses the structure and testing of ferrous (steel, cast iron) and non-ferrous (brass) metals.*
*   **CO1:** The students can operate different machine tools with understanding of work holders and operating principles to produce different part features to the desired quality. (Knowledge Level: K3) - *Understanding material properties through metallurgy aids in achieving the desired quality.*

### 2. Specimen Preparation for Metallographic Study

The goal of specimen preparation is to reveal the true microstructure of the metal for examination under a microscope. This involves several steps, each critical for obtaining accurate results.

**Objective:** To produce a flat, scratch-free, and representative surface of the material.

#### 2.1. Sectioning (Cutting)

*   **Purpose:** To obtain a small, manageable sample from the bulk material.
*   **Method:**
    *   **Abrasive Cut-off Wheels:** Commonly used for cutting metal samples. The wheel selection is important to minimize heat generation and deformation.
    *   **Hacksaws:** For softer materials or smaller samples.
*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   **Minimize Heat:** Excessive heat can alter the microstructure, especially in heat-treated materials. Use coolant where possible.
    *   **Minimize Deformation:** Avoid mechanical deformation that can introduce artifacts.
    *   **Sample Size:** The sample size should be appropriate for mounting and subsequent grinding/polishing.

#### 2.2. Mounting

*   **Purpose:** To provide a stable holder for small or irregularly shaped specimens, making them easier to handle during grinding and polishing.
*   **Types of Mounting:**
    *   **Hot Mounting (Compression Mounting):** The specimen is placed in a die with a thermosetting or thermoplastic resin (e.g., Bakelite, phenolic resins) and subjected to heat and pressure.
        *   *Example:* A small steel pin is placed in a mounting press with Bakelite powder, and under pressure and heat, it forms a solid, cylindrical mount.
    *   **Cold Mounting (Room Temperature Curing):** The specimen is placed in a mold, and a liquid resin (epoxy or acrylic) is poured over it. The resin cures at room temperature.
        *   *Advantage:* Suitable for heat-sensitive materials.
*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   Ensure good contact between the specimen and the mounting material.
    *   The mount should be uniform and free of voids.

#### 2.3. Grinding

*   **Purpose:** To remove the damaged layer from sectioning and to create a flat surface.
*   **Method:** Sequential use of abrasive papers of progressively finer grit sizes.
    *   Starts with coarse grit papers (e.g., 120 grit) to remove saw marks and rapidly flatten the surface.
    *   Progresses to finer grit papers (e.g., 240, 320, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1200 grit).
*   **Technique:**
    *   Grind in one direction on each paper.
    *   Rotate the specimen by 90 degrees between each grit size. This ensures that the scratches from the previous grit are removed.
    *   Wash the specimen and the abrasive paper thoroughly between steps to prevent carry-over of coarser grit particles.
*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   Apply consistent pressure.
    *   Avoid overheating.
    *   Ensure all scratches from the previous grit are removed before moving to the next finer grit.

#### 2.4. Polishing

*   **Purpose:** To remove the fine scratches left by the final grinding step and produce a mirror-like finish.
*   **Method:** Use of polishing cloths mounted on rotating wheels, along with fine abrasive suspensions.
    *   **Coarse Polishing:** Typically uses diamond paste (e.g., 6 micron, 3 micron) on a firm cloth.
    *   **Fine Polishing:** Uses finer abrasive suspensions like alumina (e.g., 1 micron, 0.3 micron, 0.05 micron) on softer cloths.
    *   **Final Polishing (Optional):** For very delicate microstructures or specific applications, a colloidal silica suspension might be used.
*   **Technique:**
    *   Apply a small amount of abrasive suspension to the polishing cloth.
    *   Polish for a short duration on each cloth.
    *   Clean the specimen thoroughly between different abrasive sizes.
*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   Use appropriate lubrication (often water-based or oil-based lubricants are used with the abrasive pastes/suspensions).
    *   Avoid excessive polishing time, which can cause relief between different phases or grains.
    *   Ensure the specimen surface is kept flat.

#### 2.5. Cleaning

*   **Purpose:** To remove all residual abrasive particles and polishing lubricants.
*   **Method:** Thorough washing with water, followed by rinsing with alcohol (e.g., ethanol or isopropyl alcohol) and drying with a gentle stream of hot air.
*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   Cleanliness is paramount to avoid contamination that can obscure the microstructure.

**Important Point to Remember:** Each step in specimen preparation is cumulative. Errors or incomplete removal of previous scratches will be visible in the final polished surface and will hinder microscopic observation.

### 3. Etching

*   **Purpose:** To selectively attack different microstructural constituents (phases, grain boundaries, inclusions) of the prepared metal surface, revealing the microstructure for microscopic observation. Etching makes the differences in reflectivity or topography of these features visible.
*   **Mechanism:** Etching solutions are chemically reactive and selectively dissolve or discolor certain phases or grain boundaries more readily than others.
*   **Common Etchants and Their Applications:**

    *   **Steel:**
        *   **Nital (Nitric acid in ethanol):** Most common etchant for plain carbon and low-alloy steels. It attacks ferrite more readily than pearlite, outlining the grain boundaries and revealing the pearlite structure.
            *   *Concentration:* Typically 2-5% HNO₃ in 95% ethanol.
            *   *Time:* Short etching times (seconds to a minute), depending on the steel and desired effect.
        *   **Picral (Picric acid in ethanol):** Used for steels, especially for revealing tempered martensite and bainite.
        *   **Marble's Etch:** For stainless steels, especially austenitic stainless steels.

    *   **Cast Iron:**
        *   **Nital:** Can be used for gray and ductile cast irons, revealing graphite flakes/nodules and the matrix structure (ferrite, pearlite).
        *   **Alkaline Picrate:** Effective for revealing different phases in cast irons, including phosphide eutectic.
        *   **Beilby Layer Removal Etchants:** Some etchants are designed to remove the Beilby layer (a very fine, deformed layer produced during polishing) more effectively.

    *   **Brass (Copper-Zinc Alloy):**
        *   **Ammonium Hydroxide and Hydrogen Peroxide:** A common etchant for brass. It attacks the zinc-rich phase more readily, revealing grain boundaries and the distribution of phases (e.g., alpha and alpha-beta phases in certain brasses).
        *   **Ferric Chloride:** Also used for copper alloys, including brass.

*   **Etching Procedure:**
    1.  Immerse the polished specimen in the etchant or swab the surface with a cotton ball soaked in the etchant.
    2.  Observe the surface as it etches. The time is critical.
    3.  When the desired contrast is achieved, rinse the specimen immediately with water and then alcohol.
    4.  Dry gently with a stream of hot air.

*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   **Etchant Concentration and Time:** Must be optimized for the specific material and microstructure. Over-etching can obscure details, while under-etching will not reveal the microstructure.
    *   **Safety:** Etching solutions are often corrosive or hazardous. Always wear appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) like gloves and safety glasses, and work in a well-ventilated area or fume hood.
    *   **Consistency:** Maintain consistent etching times and procedures for reproducible results.

*   **Workshop Technology Part I by W. A. J. Chapman (ELBS & Edward Arnold Publishers, 1972):** May touch upon the importance of material structure in workshop processes.

### 4. Microscopic Study of Steel, Cast Iron, and Brass

*   **Purpose:** To examine the revealed microstructure and identify different phases, grain structures, and any defects.
*   **Equipment:** **Metallurgical Microscope** (also known as an **Epi-illuminating Microscope** or **Reflected Light Microscope**).
    *   This microscope uses light reflected off the polished and etched surface of the specimen, unlike biological microscopes that use transmitted light.
    *   Key components include: light source, objective lenses, eyepiece, stage, focus knobs.
*   **Magnification:** Metallographic examination typically uses magnifications ranging from 100x to 2000x.

#### 4.1. Microstructure of Steel

*   **Constituents:**
    *   **Ferrite (α-iron):** Body-centered cubic (BCC) structure. Soft, ductile, magnetic. Appears light under a microscope after etching.
    *   **Austenite (γ-iron):** Face-centered cubic (FCC) structure. Stable at higher temperatures. Non-magnetic.
    *   **Cementite (Fe₃C):** Iron carbide. Hard, brittle. Appears as a lamellar or globular structure within pearlite, or as a network along grain boundaries.
    *   **Pearlite:** A lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite, formed from the eutectoid decomposition of austenite. Appears as parallel plates (lamellae) under the microscope. Can be coarse pearlite (formed at slower cooling rates) or fine pearlite (formed at faster cooling rates).
    *   **Bainite:** A feathery or acicular (needle-like) microstructure formed at temperatures between pearlite and martensite transformation ranges.
    *   **Martensite:** A hard, brittle, acicular microstructure formed by rapid cooling of austenite. Its formation is diffusionless.
    *   **Slag Inclusions:** Non-metallic impurities, often appearing as irregular dark or stringy shapes.
*   **What to Observe:**
    *   **Grain Size:** The size of the ferrite or austenite grains.
    *   **Phase Distribution:** How ferrite, pearlite, cementite, etc., are arranged.
    *   **Presence of Inclusions:** Non-metallic particles.
    *   **Defects:** Cracks, voids, decarburization.

*   **Example:** In a slowly cooled plain carbon steel, you would expect to see ferrite grains and lamellar pearlite. As carbon content increases, the amount of pearlite increases, and the lamellae become finer.

#### 4.2. Microstructure of Cast Iron

*   **Constituents:**
    *   **Graphite:** Carbon in the form of flakes (gray cast iron), nodules or spheroids (ductile/nodular cast iron), or cementite (white cast iron). This is the most distinguishing feature.
        *   *Gray Cast Iron:* Graphite is in the form of flakes. This reduces ductility and strength but improves machinability and damping capacity.
        *   *Ductile Cast Iron (Nodular Iron):* Graphite is in the form of spheres or nodules. This significantly improves ductility and toughness.
        *   *Malleable Cast Iron:* Graphite is in the form of irregular lumps or temper carbon, formed by heat treating white cast iron.
    *   **Matrix:** Can be ferrite, pearlite, or a mixture of both.
    *   **Cementite (Iron Carbide):** Present in white cast iron, contributing to its extreme hardness and brittleness. Also present in the pearlite matrix of other cast irons.
    *   **Phosphide Eutectic:** A hard, brittle phase containing iron and iron phosphide, often appearing as a network in irons with higher phosphorus content.
*   **What to Observe:**
    *   **Form of Graphite:** Flakes, nodules, or temper carbon.
    *   **Distribution of Graphite:** How the graphite is spread throughout the matrix.
    *   **Matrix Structure:** The composition of the ferrite and pearlite matrix.
    *   **Presence of Cementite:** Especially in white cast iron.

*   **Example:** In gray cast iron, you will see ferrite and/or pearlite matrix with graphite flakes dispersed within. The shape and size of these flakes influence the properties.

#### 4.3. Microstructure of Brass (Copper-Zinc Alloy)

*   **Constituents:**
    *   **Alpha (α) Phase:** A solid solution of zinc in copper (FCC structure). Ductile and malleable.
    *   **Beta (β) Phase:** A body-centered cubic (BCC) solid solution, harder and stronger than alpha. Stable at higher temperatures and quenched into solid solution.
    *   **Alpha-Beta (α+β) Phases:** In certain brasses (e.g., Naval brass, Muntz metal), both alpha and beta phases coexist at room temperature, forming a two-phase structure. This provides a good balance of strength and ductility.
    *   **Gamma (γ) Phase:** A complex FCC solid solution, very hard and brittle, formed at higher zinc concentrations (above ~46% Zn).
    *   **Dezincification:** Selective removal of zinc, leaving behind a porous copper structure, can occur in some environments.
*   **What to Observe:**
    *   **Grain Boundaries:** The boundaries between individual grains of the alpha or beta phase.
    *   **Phase Distribution:** The relative amounts and arrangement of alpha and beta phases (if present).
    *   **Twin Bands:** Lines within grains, common in FCC metals like copper and brass, indicating a change in orientation within the grain.
    *   **Porosity or Inclusions:** Any voids or foreign particles.

*   **Example:** A cartridge brass (70% Cu, 30% Zn) might show an equiaxed (roughly equal in all directions) grain structure of the alpha phase. A brass with higher zinc content might exhibit an alpha-beta structure with characteristic grain shapes.

**Important Point to Remember:** The correct choice of etchant and etching time is critical to reveal the specific features of interest in each material's microstructure.

### 5. Grain Size Measurement

*   **Purpose:** Grain size is a significant microstructural parameter that influences mechanical properties. Finer grain sizes generally lead to higher strength and toughness (Hall-Petch relationship).
*   **Methods:**

    1.  **Interception Method (Hatch-Jeffries Method):**
        *   **Procedure:** Overlay a test circle with known length (or a known number of test lines) onto the micrograph. Count the number of grain boundaries that intersect the test lines or circle.
        *   **Formula:**
            *   For a test circle of diameter D, the average grain diameter (d) is approximately:
                `d = D / (M * P)`
                where:
                *   D = Diameter of the test circle (in the micrograph)
                *   M = Magnification of the micrograph
                *   P = Average number of grain boundary intersections per unit length of test line/circle perimeter. (In the Hatch-Jeffries method, P is often approximated as the average number of intercepts).
            *   Alternatively, using a test grid with N lines of length L, the average number of intercepts (N_i) is counted. The average grain diameter (d) is then:
                `d = 2 * L_actual / (M * N_i)`
                where L_actual is the actual length of the test lines.
        *   **ASTM E112 Standard:** This is the standard for determining average grain size. It provides detailed procedures for counting intercepts and converting them to an ASTM grain size number.

    2.  **Comparison Method (Heyn's Intercept Method):**
        *   **Procedure:** Overlay a standard chart of grain patterns with different sizes onto the micrograph. Visually compare the microstructure to the patterns on the chart to find the closest match. The chart is usually marked with ASTM grain size numbers.
        *   **ASTM Grain Size Number (n):** This is a measure of grain fineness. A higher number indicates a finer grain size. The relationship between the number of grains per square inch (N) at 100x magnification and the ASTM number (n) is given by:
            `N = 2^(n-1)`
            or
            `n = log₂(N) + 1`
        *   **Example:** If at 100x magnification, there are approximately 128 grains per square inch, the ASTM grain size number is approximately `log₂(128) + 1 = 7 + 1 = 8`.

*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   **Magnification:** Ensure the magnification is appropriate for the grain size being measured.
    *   **Representative Area:** Measure in several representative areas of the specimen to obtain an average grain size.
    *   **Accuracy:** The interception method is generally more accurate but also more time-consuming than the comparison method.
    *   **ASTM Standard:** Adherence to ASTM E112 or equivalent standards is crucial for consistent and comparable results.

### 6. Summary and Key Takeaways

*   **Specimen Preparation** is a multi-step process (sectioning, mounting, grinding, polishing, cleaning) to reveal the true microstructure without introducing artifacts.
*   **Etching** uses chemical reagents to selectively attack microstructural features, making them visible under a microscope. The choice of etchant and time is critical.
*   **Microscopic Study** involves examining the prepared and etched surface using a metallurgical microscope to identify phases, grain structure, and defects.
*   **Steel** microstructures consist of ferrite, pearlite, cementite, martensite, etc., with grain size being a key factor.
*   **Cast Iron** is characterized by the form of its graphite (flakes, nodules, temper carbon) and the surrounding matrix.
*   **Brass** microstructures are determined by the phases present (alpha, beta, alpha-beta) and their distribution.
*   **Grain Size Measurement** is crucial for understanding material properties and is typically done using comparison or interception methods, often quantified by the ASTM grain size number.

### 7. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary purpose of etching in metallography?
**Answer:** The primary purpose of etching is to selectively attack different microstructural constituents (phases, grain boundaries) of a metal surface, thereby revealing the microstructure and making it visible for microscopic examination.

**Question 2:** Which etchant is commonly used for plain carbon steels and what does it reveal?
**Answer:** Nital (Nitric acid in ethanol) is commonly used for plain carbon steels. It attacks ferrite more readily than pearlite, thereby revealing the grain boundaries and the structure of pearlite (lamellar ferrite and cementite).

**Question 3:** Describe the difference between the microstructure of gray cast iron and ductile cast iron.
**Answer:** Gray cast iron has graphite in the form of flakes, which are distributed within the metallic matrix (ferrite and/or pearlite). Ductile cast iron has graphite in the form of spheres or nodules, which significantly improves its ductility and toughness compared to gray cast iron.

**Question 4:** If a metallographic specimen exhibits a mirror-like finish but is not etched, what would you observe under the microscope?
**Answer:** Without etching, you would observe a bright, featureless surface (except for any residual polishing scratches or inclusions). The different phases and grain boundaries would not be distinguishable due to similar reflectivity and topography.

**Question 5:** How does the ASTM grain size number relate to the fineness of the grain structure?
**Answer:** The ASTM grain size number is inversely related to the fineness of the grain structure. A higher ASTM grain size number (e.g., 7 or 8) indicates a finer grain size, meaning there are more grains per unit area. A lower number (e.g., 3 or 4) indicates a coarser grain size.

**Question 6:** What are the potential problems of excessive heat during the sectioning of a metal specimen for metallography?
**Answer:** Excessive heat during sectioning can alter the microstructure of the metal, especially for heat-treated materials. It can lead to tempering, phase transformations, or recrystallization, thus creating an inaccurate representation of the original material's microstructure.

**Question 7:** Explain the principle behind the 90-degree rotation technique during grinding.
**Answer:** The 90-degree rotation technique during grinding between successive grit sizes ensures that the scratches from the previous, coarser grit are completely removed. If you ground in the same direction, the deeper scratches from the coarser grit would remain visible even after polishing with finer abrasives, obscuring the true microstructure. Rotating by 90 degrees allows the finer grit to cut perpendicular to the previous scratches, effectively removing them.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Cleanliness is paramount** throughout the entire specimen preparation process.
*   **Minimize deformation and heat** during sectioning and grinding.
*   **Progressive reduction in abrasive grit size** is essential for achieving a scratch-free surface.
*   **Etchant selection and etching time** are crucial for revealing specific microstructural features.
*   **Safety precautions** must be followed when handling etching chemicals.
*   **Understanding the characteristic microstructures** of different materials is key to their identification and analysis.
*   **Grain size significantly impacts mechanical properties.**
