### 1. Introduction to Arc Welding

Arc welding is a fusion welding process that uses an electric arc to generate the heat necessary to melt and join metals. The arc is formed between an electrode and the workpiece. This process is widely used due to its versatility, strength of welds, and ability to join a variety of metals.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Electric Arc:** A sustained electrical discharge between two conductors. In arc welding, it's between the electrode and the workpiece.
*   **Electrode:** A conductor that supplies the electric current and filler material (in some cases) to the weld joint. Electrodes can be consumable (melt into the weld) or non-consumable (do not melt).
*   **Filler Metal:** Additional metal added to the weld joint to fill the gap and provide strength.
*   **Shielding:** Protecting the molten weld pool from atmospheric contamination (oxygen, nitrogen) which can weaken the weld. This is achieved through flux, shielding gas, or a combination.
*   **Molten Weld Pool:** The liquid metal formed by melting the base metals and potentially the filler metal during welding.
*   **Solidification:** The process where the molten weld pool cools and solidifies to form a solid weld joint.

### 2. Types of Arc Welding Processes

For this exercise, we will focus on **Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW)**, also known as **Stick Welding**, which is a common and versatile process.

**Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW):**

*   **Process:** Uses a consumable electrode coated in flux. The flux melts and decomposes to create a shielding gas and slag, protecting the weld pool.
*   **Electrode:** Composed of a metal core wire and a flux coating.
*   **Equipment:** Welding power source (AC or DC), electrode holder, welding cables, electrode, work clamp.

**Reference:**

*   **Production Technology by HMT (Tata McGraw Hill, 2017):** Likely discusses SMAW as a primary welding process.

### 3. Welding Joints and Positions

Two fundamental joint types will be explored:

#### 3.1 Butt Welding

*   **Definition:** A joint where two workpieces are placed edge-to-edge or end-to-end in the same plane.
*   **Purpose:** To create a continuous surface by joining two separate pieces.
*   **Applications:** Joining plates for pipelines, structural components, tanks, etc.
*   **Preparation:** Edges might need preparation (e.g., beveling for thicker materials) to ensure full penetration and a strong weld. For thin M.S. sheets, a square edge butt joint is common.

**Diagrammatic Representation (Conceptual):**

```
       +-------+
       |       |
       |       |  <- Workpiece 1
       +-------+
             |
             |  <- Weld Seam
             |
       +-------+
       |       |
       |       |  <- Workpiece 2
       +-------+
```

**Key Considerations for Butt Welding M.S. Sheets:**

*   **Edge Preparation:** For thin sheets (e.g., 1.5mm to 3mm), a square butt joint is usually sufficient. For thicker sheets, a V-groove or U-groove preparation might be needed to ensure complete fusion.
*   **Root Gap:** A small gap (e.g., 1-3 mm) between the edges can aid penetration and prevent burn-through.
*   **Alignment:** Accurate alignment of the edges is crucial for a straight and strong weld.

#### 3.2 Lap Welding

*   **Definition:** A joint where two workpieces overlap each other, and the weld is made along the edge of one or both pieces.
*   **Purpose:** To join two pieces by overlapping their surfaces.
*   **Applications:** Sheet metal fabrication, joining plates where surface continuity is not critical, attaching stiffeners.

**Diagrammatic Representation (Conceptual):**

```
       +-------+
       |       |
       |       |  <- Workpiece 1
       +-------+
         /
        /  <- Overlap Zone
       /
+-------+
|       |
|       |  <- Workpiece 2
+-------+
```

**Weld Types in Lap Joints:**

*   **Fillet Weld:** The most common type in lap joints, made along the edge of the overlapping pieces.

**Key Considerations for Lap Welding M.S. Sheets:**

*   **Overlap:** The extent of overlap influences the strength of the joint. Typically, an overlap of 2-3 times the sheet thickness is recommended.
*   **Weld Placement:** Welds can be made on one edge or both edges of the overlap.
*   **Distortion:** Lap joints can be prone to distortion due to heat input. Careful welding sequence and technique are important.

**Reference:**

*   **Production Technology by HMT (Tata McGraw Hill, 2017):** This textbook will likely cover different types of welding joints and their applications.
*   **Workshop Technology Part I by W. A. J. Chapman (ELBS & Edward Arnold Publishers, 1972):** Might illustrate basic joint preparations and welding positions.

### 4. Welding Parameters and Control

Achieving a good weld requires careful control of several parameters.

**Key Welding Parameters:**

*   **Welding Current (Amperage):**
    *   **Effect:** Controls the heat input. Higher current means more heat.
    *   **Selection:** Depends on the electrode type and diameter, material thickness, and joint type.
    *   **Too Low:** Poor fusion, incomplete penetration, slag inclusions.
    *   **Too High:** Burn-through, excessive spatter, warping, porosity.
    *   **Example:** For 3mm M.S. sheet with a 2.5mm electrode, a current around 70-90 Amps might be suitable.
*   **Voltage (Arc Voltage):**
    *   **Effect:** Affects the arc length and width of the weld bead. Higher voltage generally leads to a wider, flatter bead.
    *   **Selection:** Generally related to the electrode type and current.
*   **Electrode Angle:**
    *   **Effect:** Influences the penetration and bead shape.
    *   **Drag Angle:** Electrode is pulled in the direction of welding. Common for SMAW.
    *   **Push Angle:** Electrode is pushed against the direction of welding. Less common for SMAW.
*   **Travel Speed:**
    *   **Effect:** Controls the size of the weld bead and the time for solidification.
    *   **Too Fast:** Narrow bead, poor penetration, incomplete fusion.
    *   **Too Slow:** Wide bead, excessive heat input, potential for burn-through and sagging.

**Important Point to Remember:** The correct combination of these parameters is crucial for obtaining a strong, defect-free weld. Refer to electrode manufacturer's recommendations for initial parameter settings.

**Reference:**

*   **Production Technology by HMT (Tata McGraw Hill, 2017):** This book will provide detailed information on the influence of welding parameters on weld quality.

### 5. Welding Procedure for M.S. Sheets

**General Steps for Butt and Lap Welding using SMAW:**

1.  **Safety First:**
    *   Wear appropriate Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Welding helmet with correct shade lens, welding gloves, leather apron, safety shoes, flame-retardant clothing.
    *   Ensure good ventilation to avoid inhaling fumes.
    *   Keep flammable materials away from the welding area.
    *   Understand the operation of the welding machine and emergency procedures.

2.  **Preparation of Workpieces:**
    *   **Cleaning:** Thoroughly clean the M.S. sheets using a wire brush and degreaser to remove rust, paint, oil, and any other contaminants from the welding area. Contaminants can lead to weld defects.
    *   **Joint Preparation (Butt Weld):** For thin M.S. sheets, ensure edges are square and clean. A small root gap can be beneficial.
    *   **Joint Preparation (Lap Weld):** Clean the overlapping surfaces. Ensure the overlap is adequate.

3.  **Setting up the Welding Machine:**
    *   Connect the work clamp securely to the workpiece or welding table, ensuring good electrical contact.
    *   Insert the electrode into the electrode holder.
    *   Set the welding machine to the appropriate current (Amperage) and polarity (for DC, usually DCEP - electrode positive). Consult electrode packaging or reference charts for recommended settings based on electrode size and material thickness.

4.  **Striking the Arc:**
    *   **Scratching Method:** Similar to striking a match, lightly scratch the electrode tip across the workpiece surface.
    *   **Tapping Method:** Lower the electrode to the workpiece and tap it gently.
    *   Once the arc is struck, maintain a consistent arc length.

5.  **Performing the Weld:**
    *   **Butt Welding:**
        *   Start welding a short distance from the edge and move towards it, or start on the edge and move across.
        *   Maintain a consistent travel speed and electrode angle.
        *   Keep the arc focused on the leading edge of the molten pool.
        *   For longer welds, restart the arc slightly ahead of the crater from the previous run.
    *   **Lap Welding:**
        *   Start at one end of the overlap.
        *   Move the electrode along the edge of the top plate at the desired travel speed and angle.
        *   Maintain a consistent arc length and electrode angle to create a uniform fillet weld.

6.  **Finishing the Weld:**
    *   When reaching the end of the weld, slow down slightly and form a crater.
    *   Fill the crater to prevent cracking.
    *   Break the arc.

7.  **Post-Weld Cleaning:**
    *   After the weld has cooled sufficiently, use a chipping hammer to remove the slag from the weld bead.
    *   Use a wire brush to clean the weld area.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Electrode Angle:** For SMAW, a slight drag angle (pulling the electrode) is generally preferred.
*   **Arc Length:** Maintaining a consistent arc length is critical. Too long an arc causes porosity and spatter; too short an arc can cause the electrode to stick or "short out."
*   **Travel Speed:** A steady travel speed results in a uniform bead width and height.
*   **Root Penetration (Butt Weld):** Ensure the weld metal fuses the root of the joint.
*   **Undercut:** Avoid undercut, which is a groove melted into the base metal next to the weld toe.

**Reference:**

*   **Production Technology by HMT (Tata McGraw Hill, 2017):** Likely details the step-by-step procedures for various welding operations.
*   **Workshop Technology Part I by W. A. J. Chapman (ELBS & Edward Arnold Publishers, 1972):** Provides practical guidance on performing welding tasks.

### 6. Weld Defects and Inspection

Understanding common weld defects is crucial for producing quality welds. Visual inspection is the primary method in a lab setting.

**Common Weld Defects:**

*   **Porosity:** Cavities within the weld metal caused by trapped gas.
    *   **Causes:** Inadequate shielding, damp electrodes, contaminated base metal.
*   **Slag Inclusion:** Trapped slag within the weld metal.
    *   **Causes:** Incomplete slag removal between passes, improper electrode manipulation.
*   **Undercut:** A groove melted into the base metal adjacent to the weld toe.
    *   **Causes:** Excessive current, incorrect electrode angle, excessive travel speed.
*   **Incomplete Fusion:** Lack of fusion between the weld metal and the base metal, or between successive weld beads.
    *   **Causes:** Insufficient heat input, improper joint preparation, too fast travel speed.
*   **Incomplete Penetration:** Weld metal does not extend through the thickness of the joint.
    *   **Causes:** Insufficient current, too large a root gap, incorrect electrode angle.
*   **Wormholes:** Large, elongated cavities caused by gas escaping through a solidifying weld pool.
    *   **Causes:** Similar to porosity but more severe.
*   **Spatter:** Small droplets of molten metal ejected from the arc that adhere to the base metal surface.
    *   **Causes:** Excessive current, improper electrode angle, wrong polarity.
*   **Distortion:** Warping or deformation of the workpiece due to uneven heating and cooling.

**Visual Inspection Techniques:**

*   **Weld Bead Appearance:** Should be uniform in width and height, with a smooth profile.
*   **Undercut:** Check for any depressions along the edges of the weld.
*   **Porosity:** Small, pin-hole like cavities on the surface.
*   **Slag:** Visually apparent trapped slag.
*   **Alignment:** Check for proper alignment of the joined pieces.

### 7. Practical Exercises and Questions

**Exercise 1: Butt Welding of M.S. Sheets**

**Objective:** To practice butt welding two M.S. sheets of approximately 1.5mm or 2mm thickness.

**Procedure:**

1.  Take two M.S. sheets of size 100mm x 50mm x 2mm.
2.  Clean the edges of both sheets thoroughly.
3.  Align the edges of the sheets to form a butt joint. You may use clamps or tack welds to hold them in position. A small root gap (1-2mm) is recommended.
4.  Set up the SMAW machine with an appropriate electrode (e.g., E6013, 2.5mm diameter) and current setting (e.g., 70-85 Amps).
5.  Strike the arc and weld the joint from one end to the other, maintaining a consistent arc length, electrode angle (slight drag), and travel speed.
6.  If necessary, weld from both sides for better penetration and to minimize distortion.
7.  After cooling, chip off the slag and clean the weld with a wire brush.
8.  Visually inspect the weld for defects like undercut, porosity, or incomplete penetration.

**Exercise 2: Lap Welding of M.S. Sheets**

**Objective:** To practice lap welding two M.S. sheets of approximately 1.5mm or 2mm thickness.

**Procedure:**

1.  Take two M.S. sheets of size 100mm x 50mm x 2mm.
2.  Clean the surfaces that will overlap and the edges to be welded.
3.  Overlap one sheet over the other by about 20-25mm.
4.  Secure the sheets in position.
5.  Set up the SMAW machine with an appropriate electrode (e.g., E6013, 2.5mm diameter) and current setting (e.g., 70-85 Amps).
6.  Strike the arc at one end of the overlap and run a fillet weld along the edge of the top sheet, maintaining a consistent arc length, electrode angle, and travel speed. Aim for a weld bead that is approximately the thickness of the sheet.
7.  Repeat the weld on the other edge of the overlap for a stronger joint, if required by the instructor.
8.  After cooling, chip off the slag and clean the weld with a wire brush.
9.  Visually inspect the weld for defects like undercut, incomplete fusion, or excessive convexity/concavity of the weld bead.

**Practice Questions:**

1.  **What is the primary function of the flux coating on an SMAW electrode?**
    *   **Answer:** The flux coating provides shielding gas to protect the molten weld pool from atmospheric contamination, and it forms slag that covers the molten metal to prevent rapid cooling and oxidation, and it also adds alloying elements.
2.  **Describe the difference between butt welding and lap welding.**
    *   **Answer:** Butt welding joins two pieces edge-to-edge in the same plane, while lap welding joins two pieces by overlapping them.
3.  **What can happen if the welding current is too low for butt welding M.S. sheets?**
    *   **Answer:** Low current can lead to poor fusion, incomplete penetration, and slag inclusions, resulting in a weak weld.
4.  **Explain the importance of cleaning the base metal before welding.**
    *   **Answer:** Cleaning removes contaminants like rust, oil, paint, and dirt, which can cause weld defects such as porosity, inclusions, and poor fusion, leading to a weak and brittle weld.
5.  **Name three common visual defects found in arc welds.**
    *   **Answer:** Porosity, undercut, slag inclusion, incomplete penetration, incomplete fusion.
6.  **What is the purpose of a root gap in a butt weld?**
    *   **Answer:** A root gap can aid in achieving full penetration by allowing the molten metal to flow to the root of the joint and prevent burn-through on thin materials.
7.  **How does travel speed affect the weld bead?**
    *   **Answer:** Too fast a travel speed results in a narrow bead with poor penetration. Too slow a travel speed results in a wide bead with excessive heat input and potential for sagging or burn-through.
