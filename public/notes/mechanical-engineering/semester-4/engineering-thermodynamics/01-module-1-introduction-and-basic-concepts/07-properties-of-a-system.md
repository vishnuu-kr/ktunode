### 1. Introduction to Thermodynamics and Systems

Thermodynamics is the science that deals with energy and its transformations. It governs the behavior of energy in various forms and its relationship with matter.

**System:** A thermodynamic system is any region of space or quantity of matter that is being studied or analyzed. The boundaries of the system are defined by the problem context.

*   **Surroundings:** Everything outside the system.
*   **Boundary:** The real or imaginary surface that separates the system from its surroundings.

**Types of Systems:**

1.  **Closed System:**
    *   **Definition:** A system where mass cannot cross its boundary, but energy (in the form of heat and work) can.
    *   **Example:** A piston-cylinder device containing a gas. Mass (gas) is contained within, but heat can enter or leave, and work can be done by or on the gas. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 1)
2.  **Open System (Control Volume):**
    *   **Definition:** A system where both mass and energy can cross its boundary.
    *   **Example:** A turbine, a pump, a boiler, or a nozzle. Mass flows into and out of these devices, and energy is exchanged. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 1)
3.  **Isolated System:**
    *   **Definition:** A system where neither mass nor energy can cross its boundary.
    *   **Example:** A perfectly insulated and sealed container. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 1)

**Important Point:** The choice of system boundary is crucial in thermodynamic analysis and is determined by the problem at hand.

### 2. Thermodynamic Properties

A **property** is a characteristic or attribute of a system that can be observed or measured. Properties are used to describe the state of a system.

**Examples:** Pressure ($P$), Temperature ($T$), Volume ($V$), Mass ($m$), Internal Energy ($U$), Enthalpy ($H$), Entropy ($S$).

**Key Characteristics of Properties:**

*   **State Function:** The value of a property depends only on the current state of the system, not on the path taken to reach that state. This is a fundamental concept in thermodynamics. (Moran & Shapiro, Ch. 1)
*   **Point Function:** Similar to state functions, properties are point functions, meaning their value is determined by the specific state.

#### 2.1 Types of Properties:

Properties are classified into two main categories:

1.  **Intensive Properties:**
    *   **Definition:** Properties that are **independent of the mass** of the system. They do not change when the system is divided into smaller parts.
    *   **Characteristics:** Do not depend on the extent of the system.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   Temperature ($T$)
        *   Pressure ($P$)
        *   Density ($\rho$)
        *   Specific volume ($v = V/m$)
        *   Specific internal energy ($u = U/m$)
        *   Specific enthalpy ($h = H/m$)
        *   Specific entropy ($s = S/m$)
        *   Color, hardness, boiling point, melting point.
    *   **Example:** If you have a cup of water at 25°C, and you take a spoonful of it, both the cup and the spoonful of water are still at 25°C. Temperature is an intensive property. (Nag, Ch. 2)

2.  **Extensive Properties:**
    *   **Definition:** Properties that are **dependent on the mass** or size of the system. Their values change when the system is divided into smaller parts.
    *   **Characteristics:** Depend on the extent of the system.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   Mass ($m$)
        *   Volume ($V$)
        *   Total internal energy ($U$)
        *   Total enthalpy ($H$)
        *   Total entropy ($S$)
        *   Weight ($W$)
    *   **Example:** If you have 1 liter of water, its mass is approximately 1 kg. If you take 0.5 liters of water, its mass is 0.5 kg. Mass is an extensive property. (Nag, Ch. 2)

**Important Point:** An extensive property can be converted into an intensive property by dividing it by mass or some other convenient property (like moles). The resulting property is called a **specific property**.

#### 2.2 Specific Properties:

*   **Definition:** An extensive property per unit mass.
*   **Notation:** Often denoted by lowercase letters (e.g., $v$ for specific volume, $u$ for specific internal energy).
*   **Formula:** Specific Property = Extensive Property / Mass
    *   $v = V/m$ (Specific volume)
    *   $u = U/m$ (Specific internal energy)
    *   $h = H/m$ (Specific enthalpy)
    *   $s = S/m$ (Specific entropy)
*   **Significance:** Specific properties are intensive and are very useful because they are independent of the system's size and can be readily found in property tables for common substances. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 3)

### 3. Thermodynamic State and Equilibrium

#### 3.1 Thermodynamic State:

*   **Definition:** The condition of a system at a particular time, described by a set of its properties.
*   **Requirement:** For a simple compressible system, the state is completely specified by two independent intensive properties. For more complex systems, more properties might be needed. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 2)
*   **Example:** A gas in a container is defined by its pressure, temperature, and specific volume. If these properties are known, the state of the gas is known.

#### 3.2 Equilibrium:

*   **Definition:** A state where a system is stable and no changes are occurring within it or between it and its surroundings. A system in equilibrium is uniform in its properties throughout.
*   **Types of Equilibrium:** For a system to be in thermodynamic equilibrium, it must satisfy three conditions:
    1.  **Thermal Equilibrium:** No temperature difference exists within the system or between the system and its surroundings. Temperature is uniform. (Nag, Ch. 2)
    2.  **Mechanical Equilibrium:** No pressure difference exists within the system or between the system and its surroundings, except for those due to gravity. Pressure is uniform (ignoring gravitational effects). (Nag, Ch. 2)
    3.  **Phase Equilibrium:** If multiple phases are present (e.g., liquid and vapor), the mass of each phase remains constant over time. (Nag, Ch. 2)
    4.  **Chemical Equilibrium:** The chemical composition of the system does not change with time. No chemical reactions are occurring. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 2)

*   **Equilibrium State:** A state in which a system is in thermodynamic equilibrium. Properties are well-defined and uniform.

#### 3.3 Non-Equilibrium States:

*   **Definition:** States where the system is not in a state of thermodynamic equilibrium. Properties may vary from point to point within the system.
*   **Processes:** Thermodynamic processes that occur rapidly or involve sudden changes in conditions often involve non-equilibrium states. (Moran & Shapiro, Ch. 1)
*   **Quasi-equilibrium (Quasi-static) Process:**
    *   **Definition:** An idealized process that occurs so slowly and so gently that the system remains infinitesimally close to an equilibrium state at all times.
    *   **Significance:** Allows for the definition of properties like pressure and temperature during the process and is essential for analyzing many thermodynamic processes. Most theoretical analyses assume quasi-equilibrium processes. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 2)
    *   **Example:** Slowly compressing a gas in a cylinder by adding weights one by one, allowing the gas to adjust to the new pressure and temperature after each weight is added.

### 4. Thermodynamic Cycles

*   **Definition:** A sequence of thermodynamic processes that starts and ends with the same system state.
*   **Significance:** Many practical thermodynamic devices (like engines and refrigerators) operate on cycles. Analyzing cycles allows us to understand the net effect of energy transfer and transformation. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 1)
*   **Example:** The vapor power cycle (Rankine cycle) used in power plants, or the vapor-compression refrigeration cycle. In these cycles, a working fluid undergoes a series of processes (e.g., evaporation, compression, condensation, expansion) and returns to its initial state.

### 5. Property Tables and Diagrams

*   **Purpose:** To provide accurate values of thermodynamic properties for various substances under different conditions (temperature, pressure).
*   **Property Tables:**
    *   Contain tabulated values of properties like temperature, pressure, specific volume, internal energy, enthalpy, and entropy for common substances (e.g., water, refrigerants, ideal gases).
    *   Often divided into:
        *   **Saturated Tables:** For substances undergoing phase change (e.g., saturated liquid, saturated vapor). Properties are dependent on a single independent variable (either saturation temperature or saturation pressure).
        *   **Superheated Tables:** For substances in a single phase above their saturation temperature at a given pressure. Properties depend on two independent intensive variables (e.g., temperature and pressure).
        *   **Compressed Liquid Tables:** For substances in a single phase below their saturation temperature at a given pressure. Often approximated by saturated liquid properties. (Cengel & Boles, Ch. 3)
    *   **Reference Books:** Cengel & Boles, P.K. Nag, Moran & Shapiro, and Sonntag et al. all provide extensive property tables in their appendices.

*   **Property Diagrams:**
    *   Graphical representations of the relationship between properties.
    *   **Common Diagrams:**
        *   **T-s Diagram (Temperature-Entropy):** Useful for analyzing the energy transfer in terms of heat and entropy.
        *   **P-v Diagram (Pressure-Specific Volume):** Useful for visualizing work done by or on a system during processes.
        *   **h-s Diagram (Enthalpy-Entropy or Mollier Diagram):** Particularly useful for analyzing processes in turbines and compressors. (Nag, Ch. 2)
    *   **Significance:** Diagrams help visualize the path of a process and understand the changes in properties. They also help identify different phases of a substance.

**Important Point:** When using property tables, it's crucial to identify the **phase** of the substance and the **independent properties** that define its state.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Classify the following properties as intensive or extensive:
a) Mass
b) Temperature
c) Density
d) Volume
e) Specific enthalpy
f) Pressure

**Answer 1:**
a) Mass: **Extensive**
b) Temperature: **Intensive**
c) Density: **Intensive**
d) Volume: **Extensive**
e) Specific enthalpy: **Intensive**
f) Pressure: **Intensive**

**Question 2:**
A rigid tank contains 5 kg of saturated water vapor at 100°C. Determine the specific volume and volume of water in the tank. (Assume the specific volume of saturated vapor at 100°C is $1.673 \, m^3/kg$).

**Answer 2:**
Given:
Mass ($m$) = 5 kg
Temperature ($T$) = 100°C
Specific volume of saturated vapor ($v_g$) at 100°C = $1.673 \, m^3/kg$

*   **Specific Volume:** Since the substance is saturated water vapor at 100°C, its specific volume is $v_g$.
    Specific Volume ($v$) = $1.673 \, m^3/kg$ (This is an intensive property)

*   **Volume:** The total volume of the water in the tank is the product of its mass and specific volume.
    Volume ($V$) = $m \times v$
    $V = 5 \, kg \times 1.673 \, m^3/kg$
    $V = 8.365 \, m^3$ (This is an extensive property)

**Question 3:**
Explain the conditions required for a system to be in thermodynamic equilibrium.

**Answer 3:**
For a system to be in thermodynamic equilibrium, it must satisfy all of the following:
1.  **Thermal Equilibrium:** Uniform temperature throughout the system and with the surroundings.
2.  **Mechanical Equilibrium:** Uniform pressure throughout the system and with the surroundings (ignoring gravitational effects).
3.  **Phase Equilibrium:** No net change in the mass of each phase present.
4.  **Chemical Equilibrium:** No net chemical reaction or change in composition.

**Question 4:**
Differentiate between a closed system and an open system. Provide an example for each.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Closed System:** Mass cannot cross the boundary, but energy can.
    *   **Example:** A sealed pot of boiling water on a stove. Heat can enter the pot, and steam can escape if the lid is not perfectly sealed, but the primary mass is contained. However, a *perfectly sealed* pot would be closer to a closed system where only energy transfer occurs. A better example is a piston-cylinder device containing a fixed amount of gas.
*   **Open System (Control Volume):** Both mass and energy can cross the boundary.
    *   **Example:** A steam turbine. Steam enters the turbine, does work, and exits. Heat can also be lost or gained.

### 7. Important Points to Remember:

*   **Properties define the state.** The state of a simple system is fixed by two independent intensive properties.
*   **Intensive properties are independent of mass; extensive properties are dependent on mass.**
*   **Specific properties (extensive property per unit mass) are intensive.**
*   **Thermodynamic equilibrium is a prerequisite for defining a state.**
*   **Quasi-equilibrium processes are idealized but crucial for analysis.**
*   **Property tables and diagrams are essential tools for obtaining property values.**
*   The selection of the system boundary is a critical first step in any thermodynamic analysis.
