### 1. Introduction to Nuclear Fuel Reprocessing

**Key Concept:** Nuclear fuel reprocessing is the chemical separation and recovery of valuable components from spent nuclear fuel.

**Purpose of Reprocessing:**

*   **Resource Conservation:** Recovering fissile materials (Uranium and Plutonium) from spent fuel for reuse in new fuel. This reduces the need for mining fresh uranium. (CO3, K2)
*   **Waste Management:** Separating reusable materials from high-level radioactive waste, thereby reducing the volume and changing the characteristics of the waste requiring disposal. (CO3, K2)
*   **Nuclear Proliferation Safeguards:** Controlling the movement and accounting of fissile materials (Plutonium) is crucial for preventing their diversion for weapons purposes. (CO3, K2)

**Spent Nuclear Fuel:**

*   Contains unburnt Uranium (primarily U-238).
*   Contains Plutonium (produced by neutron capture in U-238).
*   Contains fission products (highly radioactive elements formed during nuclear fission).
*   Contains minor actinides (e.g., Neptunium, Americium, Curium), also produced by neutron capture.

**Important Note:** Reprocessing is a complex process involving highly radioactive materials and requires stringent safety measures and international safeguards.

### 2. Solvent Extraction: General Principles

**Key Concept:** Solvent extraction is a separation technique based on the difference in solubility of various components between two immiscible liquid phases – typically an aqueous phase and an organic phase.

**Process:**

1.  **Dissolution:** Spent fuel is typically dissolved in an acid (e.g., nitric acid) to create an aqueous solution containing the uranium, plutonium, fission products, and actinides.
2.  **Extraction:** The aqueous solution is brought into contact with an organic solvent containing a specific extractant. The extractant selectively binds to the desired elements (e.g., Uranium and Plutonium), transferring them from the aqueous phase to the organic phase.
3.  **Stripping:** The organic phase containing the extracted elements is then contacted with a different aqueous solution (stripping solution). This solution is designed to reverse the extraction process, transferring the desired elements back into a new aqueous phase, leaving impurities behind in the organic phase.
4.  **Purification:** Multiple stages of extraction and stripping are used to achieve high purity of the recovered materials.

**Textbook Reference:** *Engineering Physics* by R K Gupta & S L Gaur often covers separation techniques in their chapters on nuclear chemistry. *Nuclear reactor engineering* by Dr G Vaidyanathan would also detail the chemical processes involved in fuel reprocessing.

### 3. Solvent Extraction Processes

#### 3.1 PUREX Process (Plutonium Uranium Redox Extraction)

**Key Concept:** The PUREX process is the most widely used solvent extraction method for reprocessing spent nuclear fuel from light-water reactors (LWRs).

**Extractant:** Tributyl phosphate (TBP) dissolved in a diluent (e.g., kerosene or dodecane).

**Process Steps:**

1.  **Fuel Dissolution:** Decladded spent fuel is dissolved in nitric acid.
2.  **Partitioning:** This crucial step separates Uranium and Plutonium from the fission products and other actinides.
    *   **Uranium and Plutonium Extraction:** TBP in the organic phase selectively extracts U(VI) and Pu(IV) from the nitric acid solution.
    *   **Fission Product and Actinide Removal:** Fission products and minor actinides remain in the aqueous phase.
3.  **Separation of Uranium and Plutonium (Separation Cycle):**
    *   **Plutonium Reduction:** Plutonium is chemically reduced from its extractable Pu(IV) state to an unextractable Pu(III) state using a reducing agent (e.g., ferrous sulfamate or hydroxylamine nitrate).
    *   **Plutonium Stripping:** Pu(III) is then stripped from the organic phase into a fresh aqueous nitric acid solution.
    *   **Uranium Stripping:** The remaining Uranium (U(VI)) is stripped from the organic phase using a dilute nitric acid solution.
4.  **Purification Cycles:** The recovered Uranium and Plutonium streams undergo further cycles of extraction and stripping to achieve the required purity.

**Output:** Purified Uranium (as uranyl nitrate) and Purified Plutonium (as plutonyl nitrate). These can be converted into oxides for fabrication into new fuel elements (e.g., Mixed Oxide - MOX fuel).

**Important Note:** The PUREX process is highly efficient but requires careful control of oxidation states to separate Uranium and Plutonium.

#### 3.2 UREX Process (Uranium Extraction)

**Key Concept:** The UREX process is designed to extract Uranium from spent fuel while leaving most of the transuranic elements (Plutonium and minor actinides) in the aqueous waste stream.

**Purpose:** To facilitate partitioning of transuranics for either direct disposal or advanced partitioning and transmutation strategies.

**Extractant:** Butyl tributyl phosphate (BTBP) or other specialized extractants.

**Process Steps:**

1.  **Fuel Dissolution:** Similar to PUREX, spent fuel is dissolved in nitric acid.
2.  **Uranium Extraction:** The UREX process uses an extractant that selectively extracts Uranium in its hexavalent state (U(VI)).
3.  **Transuranic Element Management:** Most of the Plutonium and minor actinides are retained in the aqueous phase, along with the fission products.

**Advantages:**

*   Avoids co-decontamination of Plutonium and Uranium, simplifying downstream processing if transuranic separation is the primary goal.
*   Can be a precursor step for advanced fuel cycles where transuranic partitioning is a key feature.

**Disadvantages:**

*   Does not recover Plutonium for reuse, which is a primary driver for reprocessing in many traditional fuel cycles.

#### 3.3 TRUEX Process (Transuranic Extraction)

**Key Concept:** The TRUEX process is designed to remove long-lived transuranic elements (Plutonium and minor actinides) from high-level radioactive waste, leaving behind most of the fission products in the aqueous stream.

**Purpose:** To reduce the radiotoxicity and thermal load of the waste requiring geological disposal, and potentially to facilitate transmutation of the removed transuranics.

**Extractant:** A mixture of a phosphine oxide (e.g., di-octylphenylphosphonic acid - HDOPPA or CMPO) and a phase modifier (e.g., tri-n-butyl phosphate - TBP) dissolved in a diluent.

**Process Steps:**

1.  **Waste Preparation:** The high-level liquid waste from processes like PUREX is typically denitrated and adjusted to specific chemical conditions.
2.  **TRU Extraction:** The TRUEX extractant selectively complexes and extracts the transuranic elements from the aqueous waste.
3.  **Fission Product Retention:** Most fission products remain in the aqueous phase.
4.  **TRU Stripping:** The transuranics are stripped from the organic phase using a suitable stripping solution (e.g., dilute acid with complexing agents).

**Output:** A concentrated stream of transuranic elements, which can then be further processed for transmutation or conditioning for disposal.

**Important Note:** TRUEX is often applied to the waste streams generated by PUREX or directly to spent fuel after initial dissolution, depending on the overall fuel cycle strategy.

### 4. Pyroprocessing

**Key Concept:** Pyroprocessing is an electrochemical separation process carried out at high temperatures (typically 400-700°C) in a molten salt or molten metal medium. It is particularly suited for reprocessing spent fuels from fast reactors and advanced fuel cycles.

**Advantages:**

*   **Compact Process:** Can achieve separation in fewer steps compared to solvent extraction.
*   **High Throughput:** Suitable for reprocessing large quantities of fuel.
*   **Handles High Burnup Fuels:** Less susceptible to degradation by radiation compared to organic solvents.
*   **Integral Transuranic Separation:** Can co-extract Uranium, Plutonium, and minor actinides, facilitating their management.

**Disadvantages:**

*   **High Temperatures:** Requires specialized equipment and safety considerations.
*   **Corrosion:** Molten salts and metals can be corrosive to materials.
*   **Limited Fission Product Separation:** Fission products are generally not separated as efficiently as in solvent extraction, leading to a more complex waste stream.

**Textbook Reference:** *Nuclear reactor engineering* by Dr G Vaidyanathan would provide details on advanced fuel cycles and reprocessing techniques like pyroprocessing.

#### 4.1 Electrolysis in Pyroprocessing

**Key Concept:** Electrolysis is a process where an electric current is used to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction, typically used for the separation of metals and other elements.

**Process in Pyroprocessing:**

1.  **Molten Salt/Metal Bath:** Spent fuel elements (often in metallic form or dissolved in a molten salt) are immersed in a molten electrolyte (e.g., a mixture of molten salts like LiCl-KCl).
2.  **Electrode Arrangement:** Anode and cathode are placed in the molten electrolyte.
3.  **Electrochemical Dissolution (Anodic Dissolution):** The spent fuel is placed at the anode. Under the influence of the applied electric potential, the metallic components of the fuel (Uranium, Plutonium, minor actinides) are oxidized and dissolve into the molten electrolyte as metal ions.
    *   Example: $\text{U} \rightarrow \text{U}^{n+} + ne^{-}$
4.  **Electrowinning (Cathodic Deposition):** The dissolved metal ions are then transferred to the cathode. By controlling the applied potential, specific metal ions can be selectively reduced and deposited onto the cathode as pure metals or alloys.
    *   Example: $\text{U}^{n+} + ne^{-} \rightarrow \text{U}$ (deposited on cathode)

**Separation Achieved:**

*   **Actinide Separation:** Uranium, Plutonium, and minor actinides can be separated from fission products and structural materials.
*   **Fission Product Partitioning:** While less efficient than solvent extraction, some separation of fission products can be achieved through differences in their electrochemical potentials. Noble metals (like Palladium, Rhodium, Ruthenium) often separate as solids at the anode.

**Example Applications:**

*   **Integral Fast Reactor (IFR) Fuel Cycle:** Pyroprocessing was a key component of the IFR concept for reprocessing metallic fuel.
*   **Advanced Fuel Cycles:** Used for reprocessing fuels from fast breeder reactors and molten salt reactors.

**Important Note:** Electrolysis in pyroprocessing allows for the consolidation and separation of actinides from spent fuel in a highly integrated manner.

### 5. Reprocessing and Spent Fuel Management (CO5, K3)

**Evaluation of Options:**

*   **Once-Through Fuel Cycle:** Spent fuel is directly sent for disposal without reprocessing. This is simpler in terms of initial management but leads to higher volumes of radioactive waste and less efficient utilization of nuclear fuel resources.
*   **Reprocessing (e.g., PUREX):**
    *   **Pros:** Recovers valuable fissile materials, reduces the volume of high-level waste requiring disposal, can enable closed fuel cycles.
    *   **Cons:** Technically complex, expensive, raises proliferation concerns due to the separation of Plutonium, generates intermediate-level waste streams.
*   **Advanced Reprocessing (e.g., UREX, TRUEX, Pyroprocessing):**
    *   **Pros:** Tailored to specific fuel cycles (e.g., fast reactors), can facilitate advanced waste management strategies like transmutation. Pyroprocessing offers a more compact and robust alternative for certain fuel types.
    *   **Cons:** Often more specialized and less mature than PUREX. Pyroprocessing's waste stream can be more challenging to manage due to less efficient fission product separation.

**CO5 Alignment:** Understanding these different reprocessing methods allows for an evaluation of their suitability as spent fuel management strategies, considering resource utilization, waste characteristics, and proliferation risks.

### 6. Key Points to Remember

*   **Reprocessing aims to recover fissile materials (U, Pu) and manage waste.**
*   **Solvent extraction utilizes differential solubility between liquid phases for separation.**
*   **PUREX is the dominant solvent extraction process for LWR fuels.**
*   **PUREX separates U and Pu from fission products and uses redox chemistry to separate U from Pu.**
*   **UREX primarily extracts Uranium, leaving transuranics in the waste.**
*   **TRUEX targets the removal of transuranic elements from waste.**
*   **Pyroprocessing uses high temperatures and electrochemistry for separation, suitable for fast reactor fuels.**
*   **Electrolysis in pyroprocessing involves anodic dissolution and cathodic deposition.**
*   **The choice of reprocessing technology depends on the fuel type, reactor design, and overall fuel cycle strategy.**
*   **Reprocessing has economic, resource, and proliferation implications.**

### 7. Practice Questions & Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary extractant used in the PUREX process, and what are its main advantages and disadvantages?

**Answer:** The primary extractant in the PUREX process is Tributyl phosphate (TBP) dissolved in a diluent.
*   **Advantages:** It is effective in extracting U(VI) and Pu(IV), relatively stable, and has been extensively studied and implemented.
*   **Disadvantages:** TBP can degrade under high radiation fields, and it forms complexes with fission products, requiring multiple cycles for efficient separation.

**Question 2:** Briefly explain the difference in purpose between the PUREX and UREX processes.

**Answer:**
*   **PUREX** aims to recover both Uranium and Plutonium from spent fuel for reuse.
*   **UREX** aims to extract Uranium while leaving the majority of transuranic elements (Plutonium and minor actinides) in the aqueous waste stream, often as a precursor step for advanced waste management.

**Question 3:** In pyroprocessing, what is the role of electrolysis?

**Answer:** Electrolysis is used in pyroprocessing to selectively separate metal ions dissolved in a molten electrolyte. The spent fuel components are dissolved at the anode (anodic dissolution), and then specific metal ions are reduced and deposited at the cathode (electrowinning) by controlling the applied electric potential.

**Question 4:** Name one advantage and one disadvantage of pyroprocessing compared to solvent extraction.

**Answer:**
*   **Advantage:** Pyroprocessing is generally more robust for high burnup fuels and can be more compact.
*   **Disadvantage:** Pyroprocessing typically does not achieve as high a degree of fission product separation as solvent extraction, leading to a more complex waste stream.

**Question 5:** What is the main motivation for reprocessing spent nuclear fuel from a resource perspective? (Relates to CO3)

**Answer:** The main motivation from a resource perspective is to recover unburnt Uranium and produced Plutonium, which are fissile materials that can be reused to fabricate new nuclear fuel, thus conserving natural uranium resources and extending the lifespan of nuclear energy.
