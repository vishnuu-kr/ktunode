# Engineering Entrepreneurship and IPR - Module 2: Problem and Solution Canvas Preparation

## Topic: Prioritisation and Selection

Welcome, everyone! In Module 2, we've been delving into the crucial steps of preparing a problem and solution canvas. We've explored how to identify problems, understand customer needs, and begin to conceptualize solutions. Today, we're going to tackle a vital part of this process: **Prioritisation and Selection**. Think of it as sifting through a mountain of potential ideas and choosing the ones with the most promise. This is where we start turning raw potential into a focused strategy, directly impacting our ability to create successful engineering ventures.

This topic is absolutely central to our course outcomes. Specifically, it helps us achieve **CO2: Develop a framework for identifying, curating, and validating engineering-based business ideas**. Without effective prioritisation, we might spread ourselves too thin or chase ideas that won't lead anywhere. It also lays the groundwork for **CO3: Learn essential tools for understanding product-market fit and customer needs**, because our prioritisation decisions are directly informed by how well a problem/solution pair resonates with the market. Ultimately, this skill is essential for **CO4: Create a comprehensive business plan**, as a well-prioritised list of problems and solutions forms the core of any viable business.

### Why Prioritise? The Art of Saying 'No'

As engineers, we're trained to solve problems, and often, we can see *many* potential problems and solutions. This is a great strength, but it can also be a weakness if not managed. Imagine you're a software engineer who sees a flaw in a popular app, a need for a new type of drone, and a way to improve local traffic flow – all brilliant ideas! But you only have so much time, resources, and energy.

This is where **prioritisation** comes in. It's the process of evaluating multiple opportunities and deciding which ones are most important, most feasible, and most likely to succeed. It’s about making informed choices and, importantly, learning to say "no" to good ideas so you can say "yes" to the *best* ideas. This is a core principle in entrepreneurship, echoed in books like *The Innovator's DNA*, which talks about the importance of focusing your efforts.

### Selecting the "Right" Problem: Beyond Just a Pain Point

We've discussed identifying problems as the first step. Now, how do we choose *which* problem to focus on? Not all problems are created equal in the eyes of a potential customer, or an investor.

Think about it: a minor annoyance like a slightly slow-loading website might be a problem, but is it a **significant pain point**? A customer is unlikely to pay for a solution to a problem that doesn't genuinely bother them. We need to look for problems that:

*   **Are Urgent:** Customers feel the need to solve them *now*.
*   **Are Frequent:** They occur often enough to be a recurring nuisance.
*   **Are Severe:** They have a significant negative impact on the customer's life, business, or goals.

This relates directly to **CO3** – understanding customer needs. A problem that is deeply felt by a significant number of customers is far more valuable to address than a trivial inconvenience.

**Example:** Let's say you're an engineering student passionate about sustainability. You might notice that people have to carry multiple bulky reusable bags to the supermarket. That's a problem. But what if you also notice that a significant portion of grocery waste comes from poor refrigeration in rural areas, leading to spoilage and economic loss for farmers? The latter is likely a more severe, urgent, and impactful problem, even if the former is more immediately visible to you.

### Evaluating Potential Solutions: Feasibility Meets Desirability

Once we've identified and prioritised a problem, we start thinking about solutions. Again, we might brainstorm several ways to solve that problem. How do we choose the best solution to pursue? This is where we bring in concepts from **CO2** and **CO3**.

We need to evaluate our potential solutions based on a few key criteria. I often think of this in terms of a Venn diagram, where we're looking for the sweet spot where these three things overlap:

1.  **Desirability (Customer Need):** Does the solution actually solve the customer's problem in a way they will appreciate and pay for? This is where your customer discovery and validation efforts from earlier in the module are crucial. We need to ask: *Do people want this?*
2.  **Feasibility (Technical & Resource):** Can we actually build this solution with our engineering skills and available resources (time, money, equipment)? This is our core engineering competency, drawing from your knowledge in books like *The Engineering Handbook*. We must ask: *Can we build this?*
3.  **Viability (Business & Market):** Is there a sustainable business model around this solution? Can we make money from it? Does the market support it? This connects to **CO4** and involves understanding market size and competitive landscape. We need to ask: *Can we sell this profitably?*

**The "Why" Behind the Solution (Connecting to *Start with Why*)**

Simon Sinek, in *Start with Why*, emphasizes that people don't buy *what* you do; they buy *why* you do it. This is critical for prioritisation. When evaluating solutions, ask:

*   Does this solution align with our core mission or vision?
*   Does it solve a problem we are truly passionate about solving?

Choosing a solution that resonates with your deeper purpose makes it easier to stay motivated through the inevitable challenges of entrepreneurship. If your "why" is to improve healthcare access, a solution that tackles a critical diagnostic gap will be more compelling than a minor convenience feature, even if both are technically feasible.

### Frameworks for Prioritisation: Making Tough Choices

So, how do we actually *do* this prioritisation? There are several helpful frameworks, and understanding these will be key for your exams.

#### 1. Impact vs. Effort Matrix

This is a simple yet powerful tool. You plot potential solutions or features on a 2x2 grid:

*   **High Impact, Low Effort:** These are your "quick wins" – highly desirable and easy to implement. *Definitely prioritise these!*
*   **High Impact, High Effort:** These are significant projects that require substantial investment but offer big rewards. These are often your core product offerings, requiring careful planning.
*   **Low Impact, Low Effort:** These are things you might do if you have spare capacity, but they shouldn't be a focus.
*   **Low Impact, High Effort:** *Avoid these like the plague!* They consume resources without significant return.

**Example:** Imagine you're developing a smart irrigation system for agriculture.
*   **Quick Win:** A mobile app alert for when the system needs calibration (high impact on efficiency, low effort to build an alert feature).
*   **Big Project:** Developing a novel sensor that accurately measures soil moisture at multiple depths (high impact on water saving, but high R&D effort).
*   **Low Priority:** Adding a social media sharing feature for crop yield data (low impact on the core problem, relatively low effort but not core to value).
*   **Avoid:** A complex AI prediction model for rainfall that requires massive datasets and processing power, but only offers marginal improvements over existing weather forecasts (high effort, low added impact).

This directly helps with **CO2** by curating ideas based on their potential value and implementation cost.

#### 2. Weighted Scoring Model

For more complex decisions, you can assign weights to different criteria and score each option. This adds objectivity to the process.

*   **Define Criteria:** What's important for your venture? (e.g., Market Size, Technical Feasibility, Competitive Advantage, Revenue Potential, Alignment with Vision, Regulatory Hurdles).
*   **Assign Weights:** Give each criterion a percentage that reflects its importance (totaling 100%).
*   **Score Each Option:** For each potential problem/solution, score it against each criterion (e.g., on a scale of 1-5).
*   **Calculate Weighted Score:** Multiply the score for each criterion by its weight, then sum them up for a total score.

**Example:** Let's say you're choosing between two engineering business ideas:
*   **Idea A:** A novel drone delivery service for remote medical supplies.
*   **Idea B:** An improved algorithm for predicting machine failure in factories.

You might set criteria and weights:
*   Market Size (30%)
*   Technical Feasibility (25%)
*   Regulatory Hurdles (20%)
*   Revenue Potential (25%)

You'd then score each idea against these. Idea A might have a larger market but face significant regulatory hurdles, while Idea B might have a smaller but more accessible market with higher technical feasibility. The weighted score helps you see which idea objectively ranks higher based on your defined priorities. This is a more analytical approach, directly feeding into **CO4** for business plan development.

#### 3. Risk Assessment

Every entrepreneurial endeavor involves risk. Prioritisation often involves assessing and mitigating risks associated with different problems and solutions. As engineers, we're good at identifying potential failure points.

*   **Technical Risk:** Can we actually build it? What are the chances of failure?
*   **Market Risk:** Will customers adopt it? Is the market large enough?
*   **Financial Risk:** Can we fund it? What's the payback period?
*   **Operational Risk:** Can we deliver it consistently and efficiently?

When prioritising, you'll often lean towards solutions with manageable or mitigable risks, or those where the potential reward significantly outweighs the risk. This is fundamental to **CO2** and **CO3**.

**Connecting to Intellectual Property (IPR)**

While prioritisation might seem purely business-focused, it has strong IPR implications. When evaluating solutions, consider:

*   **Patentability:** Does your solution offer something novel and inventive that could be patented? (Referencing books like *Indian Patent Law* by P. Narayanan or *Intellectual Property Rights* by Prabuddha Ganguli). Pursuing solutions with strong IP potential can create a significant competitive advantage and barrier to entry.
*   **Freedom to Operate:** Are you infringing on existing patents with your proposed solution? This can heavily influence feasibility.
*   **Trade Secrets:** Are there aspects of your solution that are better kept as trade secrets rather than publicly disclosed via patents?

When you prioritise a solution that has strong IP protection potential, you're not just choosing a technical path; you're also building a defensible business. This is a key consideration for **CO1** and **CO4**.

**Common Pitfalls to Avoid During Prioritisation:**

*   **Chasing "Shiny Objects":** Getting distracted by new, exciting ideas without properly evaluating them against your core strategy.
*   **Analysis Paralysis:** Spending too much time trying to find the *perfect* prioritisation criteria, delaying actual progress. Sometimes, a "good enough" decision made with imperfect information is better than no decision.
*   **Ignoring Market Feedback:** Prioritising based solely on internal opinions rather than what customers have validated. Always tie your prioritisation back to customer needs and market signals.
*   **Underestimating Effort or Overestimating Impact:** Be realistic in your assessments. This is where teamwork and diverse perspectives are invaluable.

### Summarising the Core Takeaway

Prioritisation and selection are not about finding a single "right" answer. They are about making the *best possible choices* given your current understanding of the problem, the customer, the technology, and the market. It's an iterative process that will evolve as you learn more.

**Remember this:** Effective prioritisation means focusing your limited engineering talent and resources on the opportunities that have the greatest potential for impact and success. It's about strategic decision-making that underpins your entire entrepreneurial journey, from the initial problem canvas to the final business plan.

## Sample Questions and Answers

**1. Conceptual Question:** Explain why prioritisation is a crucial skill for engineering entrepreneurs, even though engineers are often trained to solve all identified problems.

**Answer:** Engineering entrepreneurs, unlike employees in a large organization, typically operate with limited resources (time, money, personnel). While engineers are adept at problem-solving, attempting to address every identified problem simultaneously would lead to a dilution of effort, inefficiency, and a higher risk of failure. Prioritisation allows entrepreneurs to focus their limited resources on the most impactful problems and their most viable solutions, thereby increasing the chances of achieving product-market fit, securing funding, and building a sustainable business. It embodies the entrepreneurial discipline of saying "no" to good ideas to focus on the best ones, as discussed in texts like *The Innovator's DNA*. This focus directly contributes to achieving **CO2** and **CO4**.

**2. Application Question:** You are developing a new smart home device. You've identified three potential problems your device could solve:
    a) Reducing electricity bills by optimizing appliance usage.
    b) Enhancing home security through anomaly detection.
    c) Providing convenience by automating daily routines.

Using the Impact vs. Effort Matrix, how would you approach prioritising which problem to focus on for your initial product launch? Provide a brief rationale for each quadrant.

**Answer:**
The Impact vs. Effort Matrix helps us plot potential solutions/problems based on their perceived customer impact (desirability) and the resources/complexity required for implementation (feasibility).

*   **High Impact, Low Effort:** This would be the ideal starting point. For instance, if there's a simple software update that can significantly optimize appliance usage (e.g., smarter scheduling of high-consumption devices based on peak/off-peak electricity rates) and this feature is highly desired by energy-conscious consumers, it's a "quick win." You'd launch this first.
*   **High Impact, High Effort:** This represents the core of your product vision. For example, developing sophisticated anomaly detection algorithms for home security that can distinguish between a pet and an intruder might have high impact (enhanced safety) but requires significant R&D and robust hardware. These are your major development projects that will likely form the main product offering but need careful planning and funding.
*   **Low Impact, Low Effort:** These are minor features that are easy to implement but don't solve a critical problem or offer substantial value. Perhaps an aesthetic customization option for the device's casing. These can be considered later or if resources permit, but shouldn't be the initial focus.
*   **Low Impact, High Effort:** These are tasks to be avoided. For example, trying to integrate a complex, niche automation routine that only a tiny fraction of users would ever use, and which requires significant engineering effort. This would drain resources without a proportional return.

**Rationale for Prioritisation:** You would ideally start by tackling the "High Impact, Low Effort" problems to gain initial traction and market validation. Then, focus on the "High Impact, High Effort" problems which represent the core value proposition, while deferring or discarding "Low Impact" tasks. This approach ensures that early efforts are efficient and that the most critical customer needs are addressed first, supporting **CO2** and **CO3**.

**3. Exam-Oriented Question:** A startup is developing a new type of water purification system for rural areas. They have identified the following potential features, each addressing a specific aspect of water scarcity and quality:

    I. A portable, solar-powered unit for individual households.
    II. A community-level filtration plant that can serve multiple villages.
    III. A smartphone app that monitors local water quality and predicts shortages.

Using the principles of prioritisation and selection discussed in Module 2, how would you guide this startup in choosing which feature to develop first? Consider technical feasibility, market demand, and potential for intellectual property. (Relates to CO2, CO3, CO1).

**Answer:**
To guide the startup in prioritising these features, we need to evaluate each against key criteria:

*   **Feature I: Portable, Solar-Powered Unit:**
    *   **Market Demand:** Likely high, as individual households are directly affected by water scarcity and would appreciate a personal, self-sufficient solution. This aligns with understanding customer needs (**CO3**).
    *   **Technical Feasibility:** This could be complex, requiring expertise in solar energy, filtration technology, and robust, durable design for rural environments. It's a significant engineering challenge.
    *   **IP Potential:** Novel filtration mechanisms, efficient solar integration, or unique design elements could be patentable, offering a strong competitive advantage (**CO1**).
    *   **Risk:** High upfront development cost, potential for damage in rough environments, and need for user training.

*   **Feature II: Community-Level Filtration Plant:**
    *   **Market Demand:** Potentially very high if it addresses a widespread community problem, but requires community buy-in and perhaps government partnerships, making the market access more complex.
    *   **Technical Feasibility:** Scalability of filtration technology and robust infrastructure design are key. May be less portable but more efficient in terms of water output per unit of effort.
    *   **IP Potential:** Patents on the plant design, specific filtration stages, or operational efficiency could be explored.
    *   **Risk:** Requires significant capital investment, logistical challenges in installation and maintenance, and reliance on community or governmental support.

*   **Feature III: Smartphone App:**
    *   **Market Demand:** Demand might be conditional on widespread smartphone penetration and reliable local data availability. Its direct impact on immediate water availability might be less tangible than a physical unit.
    *   **Technical Feasibility:** App development is generally more feasible for a tech-oriented startup. However, gathering and processing accurate real-time water quality and prediction data from rural sensors could be challenging.
    *   **IP Potential:** Algorithms for prediction, data processing, or unique user interface elements might be protectable as IP, though often software patents have specific criteria.
    *   **Risk:** Dependency on infrastructure (phones, data), accuracy of data sources, and ensuring the app translates to actionable improvements for users.

**Prioritisation Guidance:**

A common entrepreneurial approach, aligning with **CO2** and **CO3**, is to **start with the solution that offers the most direct customer value, is most feasible to develop and validate quickly, and has clear IP potential.**

1.  **Consider Feature I (Portable Unit):** This often represents a strong starting point. It addresses individual needs directly, allowing for focused customer feedback and validation. If the engineering challenge is manageable with the startup's current capabilities, and IP is achievable, this could be the priority. It allows for a clear Minimum Viable Product (MVP).
2.  **Consider Feature III (App):** If smartphone penetration is high and reliable data sources can be secured cheaply, the app could be a lower-cost entry point, potentially acting as a complementary service to the physical unit or as a standalone information service. It might be an easier initial validation.
3.  **Feature II (Community Plant):** This is likely the most capital-intensive and complex to implement and validate. It might be a longer-term vision or a later-stage expansion once the core technology and market are proven with smaller-scale solutions.

**Recommendation:** The startup should conduct **customer validation** for all three aspects. If households express a strong desire and willingness to pay for the portable unit, and the technical hurdles are surmountable, focusing on Feature I as an MVP would be a prudent first step. Simultaneously, they could explore the feasibility of data for the app, and reserve the community plant for a later phase. This iterative approach ensures learning and reduces risk, a key tenet of building a viable business, as emphasized in *Business Model Generation*.
