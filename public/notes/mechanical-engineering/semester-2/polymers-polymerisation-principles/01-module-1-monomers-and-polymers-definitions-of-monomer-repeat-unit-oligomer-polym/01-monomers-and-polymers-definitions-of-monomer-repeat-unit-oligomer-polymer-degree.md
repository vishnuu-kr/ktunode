# Module 1: Monomers and Polymers - Building Blocks and Giant Molecules

Welcome to our journey into the fascinating world of polymers! In this first module, we're going to lay the foundation for everything we'll learn. We'll be diving into the very essence of what polymers are, where they come from, and how they're represented. Think of this as understanding the alphabet and basic sentence structures before we start writing entire novels. Our goal is to grasp the fundamental definitions and concepts that will allow us to explore the complexities of polymer science later on.

By the end of this module, you should be able to clearly define key terms like monomer and polymer, understand how we represent these structures, and appreciate the different types and properties that make polymers so incredibly versatile. This directly ties into our **Course Outcome 1 (CO1): Explain the structural requirements of polymerisation** and **Course Outcome 2 (CO2): Illustrate structural formula of simple plastic and rubber materials**. We’ll also touch upon concepts that will be crucial for later modules, like understanding molecular weights, which links to **CO3**.

Let's start with the absolute basics.

## 1.1 The Building Blocks: Monomers

Imagine you're building a very long chain, maybe out of LEGO bricks or even just paper clips linked together. The individual LEGO brick or the single paper clip is like a **monomer**.

**Definition:** A **monomer** (from Greek, *mono* meaning 'one' and *meros* meaning 'part') is a small molecule that can react with other similar molecules to form a much larger molecule, a polymer. These small molecules are the fundamental units that repeat throughout the polymer chain.

Think about something as simple as water. While we don't typically think of it this way in chemistry class, the water molecule ($H_2O$) could theoretically be considered a "monomer" in the context of forming ice crystals, where many water molecules arrange themselves in a repeating pattern. However, in polymer science, we're usually dealing with organic molecules with specific reactive groups that allow them to link up.

**Key Characteristics of Monomers:**
*   **Small Molecular Size:** They are relatively simple molecules.
*   **Reactivity:** They possess specific chemical groups (functional groups) that enable them to bond with other monomers. This reactivity is the *structural requirement* for polymerization, directly relating to **CO1**.

**Examples:**
*   **Ethylene:** The simplest olefin ($C_2H_4$). It's a gas at room temperature and is the monomer for polyethylene, one of the most common plastics in the world.
*   **Vinyl Chloride:** ($CH_2=CHCl$). This is the monomer for polyvinyl chloride (PVC), used in pipes, window frames, and electrical insulation.
*   **Styrene:** ($CH_2=CH(C_6H_5)$). The monomer for polystyrene, found in disposable cups, packaging foam (Styrofoam), and CD cases.

Remember, it's the **ability to join together** repeatedly that makes a molecule a monomer in the context of polymer science.

## 1.2 The Repeating Pattern: Repeat Units

When monomers link together to form a polymer, they don't just randomly attach. They join in a specific way, repeating a particular structural unit over and over. This repeating structural unit derived from the monomer is called the **repeat unit**.

**Definition:** A **repeat unit** is the smallest group of atoms that, when repeated sequentially, forms the polymer chain. It is essentially the "monomer residue" after polymerization has occurred.

Let's go back to our LEGO chain analogy. If you used red bricks to make a chain, each red brick is a monomer. When you snap them together, the repeating element in the chain is still that single red brick. The difference is that in the polymer, the monomer has undergone a chemical reaction to link to its neighbors, so it might have slightly altered bonding.

**Example:**
*   **Polyethylene:** Formed from the monomer ethylene ($CH_2=CH_2$). During polymerization, the double bond in ethylene breaks, and the monomers link up head-to-tail. The repeat unit is thus $-CH_2-CH_2-$.
    *   Monomer: $CH_2=CH_2$
    *   Polymerization: $n(CH_2=CH_2) \rightarrow -(CH_2-CH_2)_n-$
    *   Repeat Unit: $-CH_2-CH_2-$

*   **Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC):** Formed from vinyl chloride ($CH_2=CHCl$).
    *   Monomer: $CH_2=CH(Cl)$
    *   Polymerization: $n(CH_2=CH(Cl)) \rightarrow -(CH_2-CH(Cl))_n-$
    *   Repeat Unit: $-CH_2-CH(Cl)-$

The repeat unit is what we usually see drawn when representing a polymer chain. It’s the fundamental building block that is *repeated*.

## 1.3 The Intermediate Stage: Oligomers

What if our LEGO chain is not super long, but just a few bricks long? We have a few monomers linked together, but not enough to be considered a "polymer" in the common sense of having very high molecular weight and distinct macroscopic properties. This intermediate stage is called an **oligomer**.

**Definition:** An **oligomer** (from Greek, *oligos* meaning 'few') is a molecule made up of a small number of linked monomer units. Typically, oligomers contain between 2 and about 10-20 repeat units.

Oligomers are important because they can sometimes have properties different from both the monomers and the high molecular weight polymers. They can also be precursors to polymers or can be used as additives.

**Examples:**
*   **Dimers:** Two monomers linked together.
*   **Trimers:** Three monomers linked together.
*   **Tetramers:** Four monomers linked together.

When you see terms like "dimerization" or "trimerization," it refers to the formation of these short chains. If you were to synthesize polyethylene, you might first form some short chains (oligomers) before reaching very long chains (polymers).

## 1.4 The Giant Molecules: Polymers

Now, let's talk about the main event: the **polymer**.

**Definition:** A **polymer** (from Greek, *poly* meaning 'many' and *meros* meaning 'part') is a large molecule composed of many repeating structural units (repeat units), which are linked together by covalent chemical bonds.

The term "many" is key here. Polymers are often called **macromolecules** (large molecules) because they can have molecular weights ranging from thousands to millions of grams per mole. This enormous size gives them unique properties that are very different from their small-molecule monomers.

Think about rubber bands. A single molecule of rubber is incredibly long, like a microscopic piece of spaghetti. This long chain nature is what allows it to stretch and return to its original shape. A single ethylene molecule ($C_2H_4$) is a gas. Polyethylene, made from millions of ethylene molecules linked together, can be a flexible film, a rigid plastic pipe, or a tough fiber, depending on how the chains are arranged and their length. This is a prime example of how properties emerge from the **structure** and **size**, directly relevant to **CO1** and **CO2**.

**Analogy:** Imagine a single string of beads versus a huge necklace made of thousands of those beads. The single bead (monomer) has very different properties from the entire necklace (polymer).

## 1.5 How Long is "Many"? Degree of Polymerization (DP)

We've established that polymers are made of "many" repeat units. But how do we quantify "many"? That's where the **degree of polymerization (DP)** comes in.

**Definition:** The **degree of polymerization (DP)** is the number of repeat units in a polymer molecule.

For a polymer like polyethylene with the repeat unit $-CH_2-CH_2-$, a DP of 1000 means there are 1000 such units linked together, giving a molecule with a repeat unit backbone of approximately 2000 carbon atoms.

**Relationship with Molecular Weight:**
The molecular weight of a polymer molecule ($M$) is directly related to its DP and the molecular weight of the repeat unit ($M_0$):

$M = DP \times M_0$

For example, if the repeat unit molecular weight for polyethylene is about 28 g/mol (2 carbons + 4 hydrogens), a polymer with DP = 1000 would have a molecular weight of roughly $1000 \times 28 = 28,000$ g/mol.

**Why is DP important?** The DP significantly influences the physical properties of a polymer. Generally, as DP increases, properties like tensile strength, viscosity, and toughness also increase. This is because longer chains can entangle more effectively with each other, creating a stronger network. This understanding of how chain length affects properties is crucial for tailoring materials for specific applications.

## 1.6 Representing Polymers: Structural Formulas

How do we draw these long chains? We use structural formulas, but with a shorthand to indicate the repeating nature.

**General Representation:**
We show the repeat unit enclosed in parentheses, with a subscript 'n' outside the parentheses, indicating that this unit repeats 'n' times. The bonds extending from the ends of the parentheses represent the continuation of the chain in both directions.

*   **Polyethylene:** $-(CH_2-CH_2)_n-$
*   **Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC):** $-(CH_2-CH(Cl))_n-$
*   **Polystyrene:** $-(CH_2-CH(C_6H_5))_n-$ (where $C_6H_5$ represents the phenyl ring)

**Block and Graft Copolymers:**
Sometimes polymers are made from more than one type of monomer. If blocks of one monomer are joined to blocks of another, we call it a **block copolymer**. If side chains of one monomer are attached to the backbone of another, it's a **graft copolymer**. We represent these by showing the different repeat units linked together.

*   **Block Copolymer (ABAB type):** $-(A)_m-(B)_p-$ where A and B are different repeat units, and m and p are the number of repeat units of each type.
*   **Graft Copolymer:** A backbone chain with side chains attached.

Understanding these representations is vital for **CO2**, where you'll be illustrating structural formulas.

## 1.7 The "Sticky Points": Functionality

What determines how many connections a monomer can make? This is its **functionality**.

**Definition:** The **functionality (f)** of a molecule is the number of reactive sites or functional groups it possesses that can participate in polymerization reactions.

Think of it as the number of "hands" a monomer has available to hold onto other monomers or growing chains.

**Types of Functionality:**

*   **Unifunctional Monomer (f=1):** Has only one reactive site. These monomers can form chain ends but cannot form a polymer chain themselves. They are often used as "chain stoppers" or modifiers.
    *   Example: Methyl chloride ($CH_3Cl$). The chlorine atom is a potential reactive site, but only one.

*   **Bifunctional Monomer (f=2):** Has two reactive sites. These are the workhorses of polymer formation, capable of linking monomers end-to-end to build linear polymer chains. This is fundamental to **CO1**.
    *   Example: Ethylene ($CH_2=CH_2$). The double bond can open up at two points, allowing it to link with other ethylene molecules.
    *   Example: Adipic acid and Hexamethylenediamine, which react to form Nylon 6,6. Adipic acid has two carboxylic acid groups (-COOH), and hexamethylenediamine has two amine groups (-NH2). Each of these functional groups can react.

*   **Polyfunctional Monomer (f > 2):** Has three or more reactive sites. These monomers create cross-linking points, leading to branched or network polymers.
    *   Example: Glycerol. It has three hydroxyl (-OH) groups. If used in polyesterification, it can lead to branched chains or even a cross-linked network.
    *   Example: Pentaerythritol. It has four hydroxyl groups.

## 1.8 Special Cases: Bi-functional and Poly-functional Systems

Let's elaborate on these key functional systems.

### 1.8.1 Bi-functional Systems (f=2)

When all monomers involved in a polymerization are bifunctional, the resulting polymer chains are typically **linear** or **branched**, but not highly cross-linked. This is because each monomer can only connect to two other monomers, extending the chain in a linear fashion.

**Examples:**
*   **Addition Polymerization:** Monomers like ethylene, propylene, vinyl chloride, styrene, etc., are bifunctional due to their double bonds. When these double bonds open, they create two points of attachment.
    *   Ethylene $\rightarrow$ Polyethylene (Linear)
    *   Propylene $\rightarrow$ Polypropylene (Linear, with side branches)
*   **Step-Growth Polymerization:** Monomers with two functional groups at opposite ends of the molecule react.
    *   Diol + Diacid $\rightarrow$ Polyester (e.g., PET)
    *   Diamino + Diacid $\rightarrow$ Polyamide (e.g., Nylon)

These systems are the most common for producing thermoplastics – materials that can be melted and reshaped. The linear or lightly branched structure allows chains to slide past each other upon heating.

### 1.8.2 Poly-functional Systems (f > 2)

When monomers with functionality greater than two are involved, things get more complex.

**Effect of Poly-functionality:**
*   **Branching:** If a monomer with f=3 or f=4 is incorporated into a chain, it acts as a branching point. Imagine a T-junction in a road; a monomer with f=3 is like that.
*   **Cross-linking:** If multiple branching points connect to each other, or if a monomer with f>2 reacts with multiple chains, it creates **cross-links**. These are covalent bonds between polymer chains.

**Types of Polymers Formed:**
*   **Branched Polymers:** Chains have side branches, but they are still individual chains.
*   **Network Polymers (or Cross-linked Polymers):** Form a three-dimensional, giant network structure where all chains are interconnected. These are typically thermosetting polymers. Once formed, they cannot be melted or reshaped because the cross-links prevent chain movement.

**Examples:**
*   **Vulcanization of Rubber:** Natural rubber is based on isoprene, which is bifunctional. Vulcanization involves heating rubber with sulfur. The sulfur atoms form cross-links between the rubber chains, dramatically improving its strength, elasticity, and temperature resistance. This is a classic example of using a polyfunctional agent (sulfur) to create cross-links.
*   **Epoxy Resins:** Often formed from monomers that are polyfunctional, leading to highly cross-linked thermoset materials used in adhesives and coatings.
*   **Phenol-Formaldehyde Resins (Bakelite):** Formed from phenol (which has multiple reactive sites on the aromatic ring) and formaldehyde. These are classic thermosetting plastics.

The presence of cross-links means that poly-functional systems lead to materials with significantly different properties – often higher strength, rigidity, and thermal stability, but they lose their melt processability. This is a critical distinction in polymer classification.

## 1.9 Why Polymers? Advantages and Disadvantages

Polymers are everywhere! From the clothes we wear and the food packaging we use to the car parts and medical implants. Why are they so dominant in modern materials science? Let's look at their pros and cons.

### 1.9.1 Advantages of Polymers

*   **Low Density:** Polymers are generally much less dense than metals, ceramics, or glass. This makes them ideal for applications where weight reduction is important (e.g., aircraft, automobiles, sporting goods). Think about how much lighter a plastic water bottle is compared to a glass one.
*   **Ease of Processing:** Polymers can be easily molded, extruded, cast, and formed into complex shapes using relatively low temperatures and pressures compared to metals. This makes manufacturing cost-effective. Processes like injection molding are revolutionary because of polymers.
*   **Corrosion Resistance:** Unlike many metals, polymers are highly resistant to corrosion and chemical attack from acids, bases, and salts. This makes them excellent for pipes, containers, and protective coatings.
*   **Electrical and Thermal Insulation:** Most polymers are excellent electrical insulators (which is why they're used for wire coatings) and good thermal insulators.
*   **Versatility and Tunable Properties:** By changing the monomer, altering the molecular weight, controlling the architecture (linear, branched, cross-linked), or adding fillers and additives, we can tailor the properties of polymers to suit almost any application. From soft, flexible elastomers to rigid, high-strength engineering plastics. This ability to "tune" properties is a huge advantage.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** For many applications, polymers are significantly cheaper to produce than materials like metals or ceramics.

### 1.9.2 Disadvantages of Polymers

*   **Low Strength and Stiffness (compared to metals):** While some engineering polymers are very strong, generally, polymers are not as strong or stiff as metals. Their mechanical properties can also be highly dependent on temperature.
*   **Low Service Temperature:** Many common polymers soften or degrade at relatively low temperatures compared to metals or ceramics. This limits their use in high-temperature environments.
*   **Degradation:** Polymers can be susceptible to degradation by UV radiation, heat, oxygen, and certain chemicals, leading to a loss of mechanical properties over time. Think about how plastic garden furniture can become brittle after years in the sun.
*   **Flammability:** Many polymers are flammable and can produce toxic fumes when burned. Flame retardants are often added to mitigate this.
*   **Solubility/Swelling:** Some polymers can dissolve or swell in certain solvents, which can be a disadvantage if they need to withstand exposure to chemicals.
*   **Environmental Concerns:** The persistence of many synthetic polymers in the environment (e.g., plastic waste) is a significant global challenge, leading to increased focus on recycling and biodegradable polymers.

Understanding these trade-offs is crucial when selecting a material for a specific application.

## 1.10 Sorting Them Out: Classification of Polymers

Polymers can be classified in several ways, based on their origin, their structure, their thermal behavior, or their polymerization mechanism. Knowing these classifications helps us predict their properties and processing methods.

### 1.10.1 Classification Based on Origin

This is a straightforward division based on where the polymer comes from.

*   **Natural Polymers:** Polymers that occur in nature.
    *   **Examples:** Proteins (e.g., silk, wool, collagen), nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), polysaccharides (starch, cellulose, chitin), natural rubber, lignin.
    *   These are often complex and are the building blocks of life.
*   **Synthetic Polymers:** Polymers that are synthesized artificially in laboratories or industrial processes.
    *   **Examples:** Polyethylene (PE), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), Polystyrene (PS), Polypropylene (PP), Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), Nylon, Polycarbonates.
    *   These are the materials we most commonly encounter as plastics, rubbers, and fibers.
*   **Semi-synthetic Polymers:** Polymers that are derived from natural polymers but have been chemically modified.
    *   **Examples:** Cellulose acetate (used in photographic film, textiles), Vulcanized rubber.

### 1.10.2 Classification Based on Structure

This classification focuses on the arrangement of the polymer chains.

*   **Linear Polymers:** Monomers are joined end-to-end in long, straight chains. These chains can still be flexible and may entangle.
    *   **Examples:** High-density polyethylene (HDPE), Nylon, PVC.
*   **Branched Polymers:** Polymer chains have side branches extending from the main backbone. These branches can affect chain packing and density.
    *   **Example:** Low-density polyethylene (LDPE) is a classic example, where branching prevents tight packing, making it less dense and more flexible than HDPE.
*   **Cross-linked Polymers / Network Polymers:** Adjacent polymer chains are joined together by covalent bonds (cross-links) formed through the reaction of polyfunctional monomers or during processing. This creates a three-dimensional network.
    *   **Examples:** Vulcanized rubber, Bakelite, Epoxy resins.
    *   These are typically thermosetting materials.

### 1.10.3 Classification Based on Thermal Behavior

This is a very practical classification, as it dictates how polymers are processed and their behavior under heat.

*   **Thermoplastics:** These polymers soften and melt when heated and solidify when cooled. This process is reversible and can be repeated. They consist of linear or branched molecules with weak intermolecular forces (like van der Waals forces or dipole-dipole interactions) between the chains. The chains can slide past each other upon heating.
    *   **Processing:** Injection molding, extrusion, blow molding.
    *   **Examples:** Polyethylene (PE), Polypropylene (PP), Polystyrene (PS), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), Nylon.
    *   This is often asked about in exams: "How do you distinguish between thermoplastics and thermosets?" The key is reversibility of softening.
*   **Thermosetting Polymers (Thermosets):** These polymers undergo irreversible chemical changes upon heating, forming a rigid, infusible, and insoluble network structure. Once they are formed into a shape and cured (often by heat or a catalyst), they cannot be remelted or reshaped. They contain extensive cross-linking.
    *   **Processing:** Compression molding, reaction injection molding.
    *   **Examples:** Phenolic resins (Bakelite), Epoxy resins, Polyurethanes, Vulcanized rubber.
    *   Their advantage is higher thermal stability and mechanical strength, but they are difficult to process once cured.

### 1.10.4 Classification Based on Monomer Type

This classification is based on the type of monomer used and the mechanism of polymerization.

*   **Addition Polymers:** Formed by the addition of monomers to one another without the loss of any atoms or molecules. The repeat unit has the same atoms as the monomer. These polymers are typically formed from monomers with double or triple bonds.
    *   **Mechanism:** Often involves chain growth.
    *   **Monomers:** Olefins (ethylene, propylene, styrene, vinyl chloride), dienes.
    *   **Examples:** Polyethylene, Polypropylene, PVC, Polystyrene.
*   **Condensation Polymers:** Formed by the reaction between monomers with two or more functional groups, where a small molecule (like water, ammonia, or HCl) is eliminated as a byproduct. The repeat unit is not the same as the monomer; it's a modified monomer.
    *   **Mechanism:** Often involves step growth.
    *   **Monomers:** Diols, diacids, diamines, amino acids.
    *   **Examples:** Polyesters (like PET from ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid, with water elimination), Polyamides (like Nylon 6,6 from adipic acid and hexamethylenediamine, with water elimination), Polycarbonates, Polyurethanes.

Understanding these different classification schemes provides a framework for understanding the vast diversity of polymeric materials and their properties. For exams, being able to classify polymers based on these criteria is essential. For instance, you might be asked to compare the properties of a thermoplastic like PE with a thermoset like Bakelite, and the classification based on thermal behavior is the key to explaining their differences.

This covers the fundamental definitions and concepts of monomers and polymers. We've defined our building blocks, how they link up, how we represent them, and how their structure leads to macroscopic properties. We've also looked at why polymers are so important and how we categorize them. These are the absolute cornerstones of polymer science.

## Sample Questions and Answers

Here are some practice questions to test your understanding of Module 1.

**1. Conceptual Question:**
Explain the relationship between a monomer, a repeat unit, and the degree of polymerization (DP). Use polyethylene as an example.

**Answer:**
A **monomer** is the small molecule that can react to form a polymer. For polyethylene, the monomer is ethylene ($CH_2=CH_2$).
During polymerization, the double bond in ethylene breaks, and the monomers link together. The **repeat unit** is the structural unit that is repeated throughout the polymer chain. For polyethylene, the repeat unit is $-CH_2-CH_2-$.
The **degree of polymerization (DP)** is the number of these repeat units in a single polymer molecule. So, a polyethylene chain with a DP of 5000 consists of 5000 repeat units of $-CH_2-CH_2-$. The molecular weight of the polymer molecule is approximately $DP \times M_0$, where $M_0$ is the molecular weight of the repeat unit.

---

**2. Exam-Oriented Question:**
A polymer sample has the following structural representation: $-(CH_2-CH(Cl))_n-$.
(a) Identify the monomer.
(b) What type of polymerization is likely involved?
(c) Is this a thermoplastic or a thermosetting polymer? Justify your answer.

**Answer:**
(a) The repeat unit is $-(CH_2-CH(Cl))-$. By reversing the polymerization process (breaking the single bonds and forming a double bond), we can identify the monomer as vinyl chloride, $CH_2=CH(Cl)$.

(b) This polymer is likely formed by **addition polymerization**. The repeat unit contains the same atoms as the monomer, and the mechanism involves the addition of monomer units without the loss of any small molecules. The presence of a double bond in the monomer is characteristic of addition polymerization.

(c) This is most likely a **thermoplastic** polymer. Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is a common example that fits this structure. PVC is known to soften and melt upon heating and can be molded into various shapes, indicating it does not form an extensive, irreversible cross-linked network typical of thermosets. The structure suggests linear chains with relatively weak intermolecular forces between them, allowing for reversible melting.

---

**3. Application-Based Question:**
Why is vulcanized rubber considered a thermosetting polymer, while polyethylene is considered a thermoplastic polymer? Relate your answer to their structures and the concept of functionality.

**Answer:**
**Vulcanized Rubber:** Natural rubber (polyisoprene) is a linear polymer. Vulcanization involves heating rubber with sulfur, which acts as a **polyfunctional agent** (f > 2). Sulfur atoms form **cross-links** between the polymer chains. These cross-links create a three-dimensional network structure. Once formed, this network is rigid and cannot be melted or reshaped by heating because the covalent cross-links restrict chain movement. This irreversible transformation upon heating classifies it as a **thermosetting polymer**.

**Polyethylene:** Polyethylene is formed from the addition polymerization of ethylene, a **bifunctional** monomer (f=2). This leads to primarily linear polymer chains with only very minor branching (in LDPE) and no significant cross-linking. The forces between these linear chains are weak van der Waals forces. Upon heating, these forces are overcome, allowing the chains to slide past each other, causing the polymer to soften and melt. This melting and solidification process is **reversible**. Therefore, polyethylene is classified as a **thermoplastic polymer**.

---

**4. Definition Recall Question:**
Define **oligomer** and give an example of how it might be relevant in polymer synthesis.

**Answer:**
An **oligomer** is a molecule composed of a small, finite number of linked monomer units, typically ranging from two to around ten or twenty repeat units. For example, if ethylene monomers are polymerized, a dimer would be $-CH_2-CH_2-CH_2-CH_2-$ (formed from two ethylene units). Oligomers can be relevant in polymer synthesis as intermediate products, as sometimes they are formed before reaching high molecular weight polymers, or they can be intentionally synthesized for specific applications, like oligomeric surfactants or specialty coatings.

---

**5. Comparison Question:**
Compare and contrast **addition polymerization** and **condensation polymerization** in terms of monomer loss and the resulting repeat unit.

**Answer:**
**Addition Polymerization:**
*   **Monomer Loss:** No atoms or small molecules are lost during the polymerization process. The monomer simply adds to the growing chain.
*   **Repeat Unit:** The repeat unit has the same chemical composition as the monomer. For example, ethylene ($C_2H_4$) forms polyethylene with the repeat unit $-CH_2-CH_2-$.
*   **Monomer Type:** Typically involves monomers with double or triple bonds.

**Condensation Polymerization:**
*   **Monomer Loss:** A small molecule, such as water ($H_2O$), methanol ($CH_3OH$), or hydrochloric acid ($HCl$), is eliminated as a byproduct during the reaction between monomers.
*   **Repeat Unit:** The repeat unit is different from the monomer because a portion of the monomer is lost as the byproduct. For example, Nylon 6,6 is formed from adipic acid ($HOOC-(CH_2)_4-COOH$) and hexamethylenediamine ($H_2N-(CH_2)_6-NH_2$). The repeat unit contains both segments, and water is eliminated.
*   **Monomer Type:** Involves monomers with two or more reactive functional groups (e.g., -OH, -COOH, -NH2).

This covers the core concepts for Module 1. Keep these definitions and distinctions clear, as they are the bedrock for all subsequent learning in polymers.
