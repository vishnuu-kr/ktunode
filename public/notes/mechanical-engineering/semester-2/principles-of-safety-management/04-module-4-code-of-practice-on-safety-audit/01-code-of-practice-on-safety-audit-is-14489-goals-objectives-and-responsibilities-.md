# PRINCIPLES OF SAFETY MANAGEMENT

## MODULE 4: CODE OF PRACTICE ON SAFETY AUDIT

### Topic: Code of Practice on Safety Audit – IS 14489

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into a crucial aspect of safety management: the **Code of Practice on Safety Audit**, specifically as outlined in **IS 14489**. Think of a safety audit as a health check-up for your organization's safety system. Just like you go to a doctor to ensure your body is functioning well, a safety audit checks if your safety processes are healthy, effective, and compliant. This standard, IS 14489, provides a structured framework for conducting these vital assessments.

Our journey today will cover the fundamental reasons *why* we conduct safety audits, *what* we aim to achieve, *who* is responsible, *how* we go about it, and some of the key tools and techniques used, like Job Safety Analysis (JSA) and non-conformity reporting. This module directly links to our Course Outcomes, particularly CO1 (understanding safety concepts and duties), CO2 (explaining safety organization and accident prevention), CO3 (evaluating safety performance), and CO4 (analyzing job safety and accident investigation). So, let's get started!

### 1. Goals, Objectives, and Responsibilities of a Safety Audit

Before we talk about the "how," let's understand the "why" and "who."

#### 1.1 Goals of a Safety Audit

The overarching **goal** of a safety audit is to ensure that an organization's safety management system is effective, efficient, and compliant with all relevant laws, regulations, and internal policies. It’s not just about finding faults; it’s about promoting continuous improvement in safety performance. Think of it as a strategic tool to keep your organization not only safe but also responsible and sustainable.

*   **Assurance:** To provide management with assurance that the safety management system is working as intended.
*   **Improvement:** To identify areas of weakness and opportunities for improvement in safety practices and procedures.
*   **Compliance:** To verify adherence to legal requirements, industry standards (like IS 14489 itself!), and organizational safety policies.
*   **Risk Reduction:** To proactively identify and mitigate potential hazards and risks before they lead to incidents.

#### 1.2 Objectives of a Safety Audit

While goals are broad, objectives are specific and measurable. A safety audit aims to:

*   **Evaluate the effectiveness of the existing safety management system:** Is it just on paper, or is it actually working in practice? Are people following the rules? Are the controls in place actually preventing harm?
*   **Identify potential hazards and risks:** This involves looking for unsafe conditions (e.g., faulty equipment, poor lighting) and unsafe behaviors (e.g., not wearing PPE, improper lifting techniques).
*   **Assess compliance with legal and regulatory requirements:** Are we meeting our statutory obligations? This is critical to avoid fines and legal trouble.
*   **Review safety policies and procedures:** Are they up-to-date, relevant, and clearly communicated?
*   **Assess the adequacy of safety training and awareness programs:** Do employees understand the risks and how to protect themselves?
*   **Evaluate the effectiveness of accident investigation and reporting:** Are we learning from past incidents?
*   **Measure safety performance against established benchmarks:** How are we doing compared to our own past performance or industry best practices?

**Remember this:** Safety audits are a proactive measure. They are about *preventing* accidents and ill health, not just reacting to them. This aligns with CO1, where we understand the fundamental concepts of safety and the proactive nature of safety management.

#### 1.3 Responsibilities in a Safety Audit

Who is involved? It's a team effort!

*   **Top Management:** Ultimately responsible for providing resources, setting the safety policy, and demonstrating commitment. They receive the audit reports and ensure corrective actions are taken. Their commitment is the bedrock of a successful safety program.
*   **Safety Officer/Department:** Often plays a key role in planning, conducting, and following up on audits. They are the technical experts. This relates directly to CO1, as we learn about the duties of a safety officer.
*   **Auditors (Internal/External):** The individuals who perform the audit. They need to be competent, objective, and skilled in auditing techniques. External auditors bring an independent perspective, while internal auditors have a deeper understanding of the organization's context.
*   **Auditees (Employees, Supervisors, Department Heads):** Those being audited. They are expected to cooperate with the audit process, provide information, and implement corrective actions. Their participation is crucial for the audit to be meaningful.

### 2. Audit Methodology: The "How To" of Safety Audits

Now, let's get into the practical steps of conducting a safety audit, as guided by IS 14489.

#### 2.1 The Audit Process: A Step-by-Step Approach

A well-structured audit follows a logical progression. Think of it like planning a journey: you need a destination, a map, and a vehicle.

**Phase 1: Planning and Preparation**
This is arguably the most critical phase. If you don't plan well, you won't audit well.

*   **Define the Scope and Objectives:** What areas will be audited? What specific aspects of the safety system are we focusing on? (e.g., machine guarding, chemical handling, emergency preparedness). This is tailored to the organization's specific needs and risks.
*   **Develop an Audit Plan:** This is your roadmap. It includes the audit schedule, the areas to be covered, the criteria against which performance will be measured (e.g., legislation, standards, company policies), and the audit team members.
*   **Assemble the Audit Team:** Select competent individuals with the necessary knowledge and skills.
*   **Communicate the Audit Plan:** Inform the relevant departments and individuals about the upcoming audit, its purpose, and the schedule. This avoids surprises and fosters cooperation.

**Phase 2: Conducting the Audit (Fieldwork)**
This is where the auditors go into action.

*   **Opening Meeting:** The audit team meets with management and relevant personnel to officially start the audit, reconfirm the scope and objectives, and outline the methodology.
*   **Data Collection:** This is the core of the fieldwork. Auditors gather information through:
    *   **Document Review:** Examining safety manuals, policies, procedures, training records, accident reports, inspection logs, etc.
    *   **Interviews:** Talking to employees at all levels, supervisors, and managers to understand their perceptions and practices regarding safety. This gives you the "ground truth."
    *   **Observation:** Physically observing work activities, workplace conditions, and the use of safety equipment. This is where you see if procedures are actually followed. For example, are workers wearing their safety glasses when operating machinery?
    *   **Measurement and Sampling:** Taking readings (e.g., noise levels, light intensity) or collecting samples where appropriate.
*   **Evaluation:** Auditors compare the collected data against the established audit criteria (laws, standards, policies).

**Phase 3: Reporting and Follow-up**
The audit isn't over when you leave the site.

*   **Audit Closing Meeting:** The audit team presents its preliminary findings to management and relevant personnel. This is an opportunity for clarification.
*   **Preparation of the Audit Report:** A formal document detailing the audit findings, including strengths, areas for improvement, and non-conformities. This report must be clear, concise, and objective.
*   **Distribution of the Audit Report:** The report is shared with relevant stakeholders, especially top management.
*   **Corrective Action Planning and Implementation:** Management is responsible for developing and implementing action plans to address the identified non-conformities and recommendations. This is where the audit’s value is realized!
*   **Audit Follow-up:** The audit team or designated personnel verify that the corrective actions have been implemented effectively. This closes the loop and ensures accountability.

This structured approach ensures that the audit is thorough, systematic, and leads to tangible improvements, directly contributing to CO3 (estimating safety performance) and CO4 (analyzing job safety).

### 3. Executing the Audit: Techniques and Tools

How do auditors actually gather the information needed during the fieldwork phase? IS 14489 emphasizes a combination of methods.

*   **Checklists:** Pre-prepared lists of questions or items to be verified. They ensure consistency and cover all essential aspects. While useful for systematic coverage, they shouldn't limit the auditor's scope for observation and inquiry.
*   **Interviews:** As mentioned, a crucial tool. Good interviewing skills are essential – asking open-ended questions, listening actively, and probing for details. For instance, instead of asking "Is the machine safe?", ask "Tell me about the safety features on this machine and how you use them."
*   **Observation of Work Practices:** Simply watching how work is done. This can reveal deviations from procedures or unsafe habits. Imagine observing a worker operating a forklift – are they using their seatbelt? Are they looking before moving?
*   **Document Review:** The paper trail is important. Are safety training records up-to-date? Is the chemical inventory properly managed?

Think of it like a detective: they examine clues (documents), talk to witnesses (interviews), and observe the scene (workplace observations).

### 4. Job Safety Analysis (JSA)

Now, let's zoom in on a key tool often used *during* safety audits and as a proactive safety measure: Job Safety Analysis, or JSA. This is directly relevant to CO4, which focuses on analyzing job safety.

#### 4.1 What is JSA?

JSA is a systematic process for identifying hazards associated with a specific job or task, determining the risks involved, and then recommending controls to eliminate or reduce those risks. It breaks down a job into a series of steps, identifies hazards at each step, and specifies the safest way to perform the job.

**Analogy:** Imagine you're going to hike a new, challenging trail. You wouldn't just start walking. You'd look at a map, identify potential dangers (steep drops, loose rocks, weather changes), and pack appropriate gear (sturdy shoes, water, first-aid kit). JSA is the safety equivalent for any job.

#### 4.2 Steps in Performing a JSA

The process is quite straightforward:

1.  **Select the Job to Analyze:** Choose jobs that are hazardous, have a history of incidents, are new or modified, or are complex.
2.  **Break Down the Job into Steps:** List the sequence of actions required to complete the job from start to finish. Be specific! For example, for "changing a light bulb":
    *   Get a new bulb and ladder.
    *   Position the ladder.
    *   Climb the ladder.
    *   Remove the old bulb.
    *   Install the new bulb.
    *   Descend the ladder.
    *   Put away equipment.
3.  **Identify Hazards:** For each step, ask: "What could go wrong here?" Think about:
    *   **Physical Hazards:** Slips, trips, falls, falling objects, electrical hazards, sharp objects, machinery.
    *   **Chemical Hazards:** Exposure to toxic or corrosive substances.
    *   **Biological Hazards:** Exposure to bacteria, viruses.
    *   **Ergonomic Hazards:** Repetitive motions, awkward postures.
    *   **Psychosocial Hazards:** Stress, fatigue.
    *   *Example for "Position the ladder":* Ladder slipping (unstable surface, improper angle), ladder tipping (uneven ground, insufficient bracing), striking passing personnel.
4.  **Determine Controls:** For each identified hazard, what can be done to prevent it? Controls should follow the hierarchy of controls:
    *   **Elimination:** Remove the hazard entirely (e.g., use a different, safer method).
    *   **Substitution:** Replace the hazard with something less hazardous (e.g., use a less toxic chemical).
    *   **Engineering Controls:** Isolate people from the hazard (e.g., machine guards, ventilation systems).
    *   **Administrative Controls:** Change the way people work (e.g., safe work procedures, training, permits to work, job rotation).
    *   **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):** Protect the worker with equipment (e.g., safety glasses, gloves, hard hats). PPE is the last line of defense.
    *   *Example controls for "Ladder slipping":* Ensure ladder is on firm, level ground; use a ladder with anti-slip feet; secure the top of the ladder; have a spotter.
5.  **Document and Train:** Record the JSA and train the employees performing the job on the identified hazards and required controls. The JSA becomes part of the safe operating procedure.

**Key takeaway:** JSA is a powerful tool for analyzing tasks and implementing preventive measures, a core concept in understanding accident causation and prevention methods (CO1, CO2).

### 5. Safety Surveys

Another vital tool used in auditing and proactive safety management is the safety survey. While JSA focuses on specific jobs, safety surveys often look at the broader workplace environment and systems.

#### 5.1 What is a Safety Survey?

A safety survey is a systematic examination of the workplace to identify existing and potential hazards, unsafe conditions, and unsafe work practices. It's often a more general assessment than a JSA, covering physical aspects of the workplace, equipment, and the overall safety program.

**Analogy:** Think of a home inspection before buying a house. The inspector looks at the foundation, roof, plumbing, electrical systems – the overall structure and its potential issues. A safety survey is similar, but for the workplace.

#### 5.2 Types of Safety Surveys

*   **General Workplace Survey:** A broad assessment of housekeeping, lighting, ventilation, noise levels, emergency exits, fire safety, and general orderliness.
*   **Equipment-Specific Survey:** Focused examination of particular machinery or equipment to ensure it's properly maintained, guarded, and operated safely.
*   **Process-Specific Survey:** Looking at a particular manufacturing process or operational procedure to identify associated hazards.
*   **Behavioral Observation Survey:** Focusing on observing and recording worker behaviors to identify unsafe practices and provide feedback. This links closely to understanding safety psychology and motivational theories (CO2).

#### 5.3 Conducting a Safety Survey

The process typically involves:

*   **Planning:** Defining the scope (e.g., entire facility, a specific department), the survey route, and the criteria for evaluation.
*   **Execution:** Walking through the designated areas, observing conditions, talking to workers, and taking notes. Auditors might use checklists or a more free-form approach depending on the survey's objective.
*   **Documentation:** Recording all findings, including hazards, unsafe acts, and good safety practices observed. Photos can be very helpful.
*   **Reporting:** Compiling the findings into a report that highlights key issues and recommends corrective actions.

Safety surveys are excellent for identifying those "hidden" hazards that might not be obvious when looking at individual jobs, contributing to a comprehensive understanding of workplace safety (CO1).

### 6. Non-Conformity Reporting (NCR)

When an audit or survey identifies a deviation from established standards, policies, or regulations, this deviation is formally reported. This is where Non-Conformity Reporting comes in.

#### 6.1 What is a Non-Conformity?

A **non-conformity (NC)** is the **non-fulfillment of a specified requirement**. In the context of a safety audit, it means something is not being done according to the rules, procedures, or best practices that are supposed to be in place.

*   **Example:** A procedure states that all employees entering the machine shop must wear safety shoes. An auditor observes an employee entering the shop without safety shoes. This is a non-conformity.

#### 6.2 The Non-Conformity Report (NCR)

An NCR is a formal document that records details of a non-conformity. It's the tool that signals a problem needs attention and action. A typical NCR includes:

*   **Date of Identification:** When the NC was found.
*   **Description of Non-Conformity:** A clear, factual statement of what is wrong.
*   **Location/Area:** Where the NC was observed.
*   **Requirement Not Met:** The specific standard, policy, or regulation that was violated.
*   **Evidence:** Observations, interview notes, photos, or document references supporting the NC.
*   **Assigned Responsibility:** Who is responsible for correcting the NC.
*   **Corrective Action Plan:** What steps will be taken to fix the problem.
*   **Target Completion Date:** When the corrective action should be finished.
*   **Verification:** Confirmation that the corrective action was effective.
*   **Closure:** Formal closing of the NCR once verified.

**Why are NCRs important?** They provide a traceable record of safety issues and the actions taken to resolve them. This helps in monitoring the effectiveness of the safety management system over time and is essential for continuous improvement, directly supporting CO3 and CO4 in terms of compliance and performance assessment.

**Think of it this way:** If safety is about building a strong structure, NCRs are like the reports from building inspectors that identify cracks or structural weaknesses. Without these reports and the subsequent repairs, the structure is at risk.

### Summary and Key Takeaways

*   **Safety Audits** are systematic evaluations to ensure the effectiveness and compliance of a safety management system.
*   Their **goals** are assurance, improvement, compliance, and risk reduction.
*   **Objectives** are specific targets like evaluating system effectiveness and identifying hazards.
*   **Responsibilities** are shared, with top management playing a crucial role.
*   The **audit methodology** involves planning, fieldwork (document review, interviews, observation), and reporting/follow-up.
*   **JSA** breaks down jobs to identify and control hazards at each step.
*   **Safety Surveys** provide a broader assessment of workplace hazards and conditions.
*   **Non-Conformity Reports (NCRs)** formally document deviations and trigger corrective actions.

Mastering these concepts will equip you to understand and contribute to effective safety management within any organization. They are the practical tools that turn safety policy into safe practice.

### Sample Questions with Answers

**Q1. What is the primary goal of a safety audit as per IS 14489?**

**Answer:** The primary goal of a safety audit is to ensure that an organization's safety management system is effective, efficient, and compliant with all relevant laws, regulations, and internal policies, ultimately promoting continuous improvement in safety performance.

**Reasoning:** This directly addresses the foundational purpose of audits, focusing on assurance and improvement rather than just finding faults.

**Q2. Briefly explain the role of top management in the safety audit process.**

**Answer:** Top management is responsible for providing resources for the audit, setting the overall safety policy, demonstrating commitment to the audit process, and ensuring that corrective actions identified in audit reports are implemented effectively. They are ultimately accountable for the organization's safety performance.

**Reasoning:** This highlights the crucial leadership role in driving the safety culture and ensuring audits lead to meaningful change, aligning with the responsibility aspect of the topic.

**Q3. Describe the sequence of steps involved in conducting a Job Safety Analysis (JSA).**

**Answer:** The steps in a JSA are:
1.  **Select the Job to Analyze:** Identify the task to be assessed.
2.  **Break Down the Job into Steps:** List the sequential actions.
3.  **Identify Hazards:** For each step, determine what could go wrong.
4.  **Determine Controls:** For each hazard, specify preventive measures (following the hierarchy of controls).
5.  **Document and Train:** Record the JSA and educate workers on its findings.

**Reasoning:** This question tests the understanding of a key tool discussed. Providing the steps in the correct order demonstrates recall and comprehension, linking to CO4.

**Q4. "A safety audit is only about finding faults." Do you agree? Justify your answer with reference to the goals of a safety audit.**

**Answer:** No, I do not agree. While identifying non-conformities (faults) is a part of a safety audit, it is not the sole purpose. The primary goals of a safety audit, as outlined by IS 14489, include **assurance** (providing management with confidence in the safety system), **improvement** (identifying areas for enhancement), **compliance** (verifying adherence to legal and organizational standards), and **risk reduction** (proactively mitigating hazards). Therefore, audits are a proactive and developmental process aimed at building a robust safety culture and performance, not just a fault-finding exercise.

**Reasoning:** This question requires critical thinking and application of learned concepts. It forces a nuanced understanding of the audit's purpose beyond simply finding negatives, directly addressing the "goals" section and connecting to the proactive nature of safety management (CO1).

**Q5. What is a Non-Conformity Report (NCR) and why is it a critical component of the audit follow-up process?**

**Answer:** A Non-Conformity Report (NCR) is a formal document that records the details of a deviation from a specified requirement (e.g., a safety procedure, legal standard, or company policy) identified during an audit or inspection. It is critical to the audit follow-up process because it provides a traceable record of safety issues, details the required corrective actions, assigns responsibility, and sets a timeline for resolution. Verifying the implementation and effectiveness of these corrective actions, documented via NCRs, ensures that identified problems are addressed and closed out, thus driving continuous improvement in the safety management system.

**Reasoning:** This question focuses on the administrative but crucial outcome of an audit. Explaining what an NCR is and its role in the corrective action cycle demonstrates understanding of the process and its link to improvement, relevant to CO3 and CO4.
