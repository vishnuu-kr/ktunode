## CIVIL ENGINEERING DRAFTING LAB - MODULE 8: DRAW PLAN, SECTION, AND ELEVATION OF A TWO-STORIED FRAMED BUILDING

**(Topic: Draw plan, section and elevation of two-storied framed building from the given line sketch)**

Hello everyone! Welcome back to our Civil Engineering Drafting Lab. Today, we embark on a crucial and exciting journey: understanding how to translate a simple line sketch into a fully detailed architectural drawing of a two-storied framed building. This isn't just about lines on paper; it's about visualizing and communicating the very essence of a structure. Think of yourselves as architects and engineers, bringing a building to life through your drawings.

This module directly aligns with several of our course objectives. Specifically, it will help us **CO1: Illustrate ability to organize civil engineering drawings systematically and professionally** by focusing on the systematic representation of a building. We'll also touch upon **CO2: Illustrate the detailing of building components like doors, windows, roof trusses etc.** as we derive these from the sketch. Most importantly, this topic is key for **CO3: Develop the sketch of plan, front elevation and sectional elevation from line diagram** and also sets the foundation for **CO4: Draft the plan elevation and sectional views of the residential buildings, industrial buildings, and framed structures using software** later on. Understanding the manual process first is vital, as it builds the conceptual framework for CAD software.

### Understanding the Building Sketch: The Foundation of Our Drawing

So, what exactly is this "line sketch" we're talking about? Imagine a very basic representation of a building, perhaps drawn by an architect in the initial concept stage. It might show the overall dimensions, the position of walls, doors, windows, and maybe even the basic layout of floors. It's like a rough blueprint, giving us the essential information without all the intricate details.

Our task is to take this foundational sketch and elaborate on it to create professional, detailed drawings. This involves understanding what each line *represents* in the real world.

### Key Concepts: Bringing the Sketch to Life

Before we dive into drawing, let's refresh some fundamental concepts.

*   **Framed Structure:** This is a building where the loads are carried by a skeleton of structural elements – typically columns, beams, and slabs. Unlike load-bearing wall construction, where walls themselves support the floors and roof, in a framed structure, the frame takes the brunt of the load and transfers it to the foundation. Think of it like a human body: the bones (columns and beams) provide the support, and the walls are more like cladding. Our two-storied building will be of this type.
*   **Plan:** The plan is a horizontal slice through a building, usually taken about 1.2 meters (or 4 feet) above the finished floor level. It shows the layout of rooms, walls, doors, windows, stairs, and other features as seen from above. For our two-storied building, we’ll need a **Ground Floor Plan** and a **First Floor Plan**.
*   **Elevation:** An elevation drawing is a view of the building from the outside, showing its exterior appearance. We typically draw the **Front Elevation** (the main facade), but elevations of other sides (side elevation, rear elevation) are also common. They show the heights of different elements, the position and design of windows and doors, and the overall architectural style.
*   **Section:** A section drawing is like slicing the building vertically. It reveals the internal construction and arrangement of elements that would otherwise be hidden. A **Sectional Elevation** is particularly useful. It shows an elevation view, but with a part of the building cut away to reveal the internal structure, like how floors are supported, the arrangement of beams and columns, and the thickness of walls. This is crucial for understanding the building's construction.

### Decoding the Line Sketch: What to Look For

When you're given a line sketch, your first job is to interpret it accurately. You need to identify:

1.  **Overall Dimensions:** Get a feel for the building's size.
2.  **Wall Lines:** These define the rooms and external boundaries. Pay attention to the thickness implied by double lines or by context.
3.  **Door and Window Symbols:** Even in a sketch, these are usually indicated. You need to know their size and location.
4.  **Staircase Location:** This is a critical element in a multi-storied building.
5.  **Column Locations:** In a framed structure, the columns are the vertical supports. Their position is vital.
6.  **Beam Indications:** Beams connect columns and support slabs.
7.  **Slab Thickness (Implied):** The sketch might not explicitly show slab thickness, but you'll infer it based on standard practices or provided details.

### Step-by-Step Process: From Sketch to Detailed Drawings

Let's break down the process, imagining we have a typical line sketch in front of us. We'll be referencing guidelines from books like "Building Drawing and Detailing" by Dr. Balagopal T.S. Prabhu and Shah, Kale, and Patki's "Building Drawing With An Integrated Approach to Built Environment."

#### 1. Preparing the Drawing Sheet and Title Block

First things first, a professional drawing needs a professional presentation. This means setting up your drawing sheet correctly with a well-defined title block. The title block, as per IS 962:1989 (Reaffirmed 2022), contains essential information: name of the project, drawing number, scale, date, drafter's name, and reviewer's name. This is our **CO1** in action – organization and professionalism.

#### 2. Drawing the Ground Floor Plan

*   **Establish Grid Lines:** In a framed structure, a grid system (usually labeled with letters and numbers) helps in accurately locating columns and beams. Mark these on your drawing based on the sketch.
*   **Draw Outer Walls:** Using the overall dimensions from the sketch and an appropriate scale (e.g., 1:50 or 1:100), draw the external walls. Remember, in a framed structure, these are often non-load-bearing partitions, but their positions are dictated by the layout.
*   **Draw Internal Walls:** Similarly, draw the internal partition walls. Pay close attention to the dimensions provided or implied in the sketch for room sizes.
*   **Locate Columns:** Based on the grid and the sketch, accurately position the columns. Columns are usually represented by solid squares or rectangles in the plan.
*   **Indicate Door and Window Openings:** Draw the door and window openings within the wall lines. Show the swing of the doors (usually a quarter circle). The sizes of doors and windows should be consistent with typical building requirements as per National Building Code of India (NBC) and Kerala Building Rules (Local municipal rules will also be relevant here, as mentioned in your reference books).
*   **Show Staircase:** Draw the staircase accurately, indicating the direction of travel, number of steps, riser height, and tread width. The landing also needs to be clearly shown.
*   **Add Dimensions:** Crucially, add clear dimensions to all walls, openings, and significant features. This is non-negotiable for a professional drawing. Think about it: if you were a contractor, how would you build it without dimensions?
*   **Labeling:** Label rooms (e.g., Living Room, Bedroom), and indicate the finished floor level (FFL) and ground level (GL).

#### 3. Drawing the First Floor Plan

This is largely similar to the ground floor plan, but with some key differences:

*   **Slab:** The first floor plan will show the slab of the first floor. In a framed structure, this slab is supported by beams.
*   **Staircase Continuation:** The staircase will continue from the ground floor to the first floor.
*   **Internal Layout:** The internal partition walls might differ from the ground floor, so draw them as per the sketch.
*   **Balconies and Overhangs:** If the sketch shows any, these will be clearly represented.
*   **Add Dimensions and Labels:** Similar to the ground floor plan, meticulous dimensioning and labeling are essential.

#### 4. Drawing the Front Elevation

The elevation shows the building as if you are looking at it from the front.

*   **Establish Ground Line:** Start with a ground line.
*   **Project Heights:** Project the heights of the ground floor, first floor, parapet wall (if any), and roof from your floor plans. You'll need to assume standard floor-to-floor heights if not provided, say 3 meters for each floor.
*   **Draw Walls:** Draw the external walls up to the required heights.
*   **Show Doors and Windows:** Accurately draw the doors and windows in their correct positions and sizes, as seen from the front.
*   **Architectural Features:** Add any balconies, decorative elements, or other features visible from the front.
*   **Roof Line:** Show the profile of the roof.
*   **Add Grid Lines and Levels:** It's good practice to show the grid lines and relevant levels (like FFL of ground floor, FFL of first floor, parapet top).
*   **Hatching:** You might need to hatch certain elements to indicate materials, like brickwork or concrete.

#### 5. Drawing a Sectional Elevation (e.g., Section AA)

This is where we reveal the internal workings. We'll cut through the building along a specific line (say, section line 'AA' marked on the plans).

*   **Cutting Plane:** Imagine a cutting plane passing through the building, typically through rooms and openings, and crucially, through the staircase.
*   **Draw the Cut Line:** On the plan, you'll mark the line of section (AA) and also indicate the direction of view.
*   **Project Visible and Cut Elements:**
    *   **Cut Elements:** Anything the cutting plane passes through is shown in solid line and often sectioned (hatched) to indicate material. This includes the floor slab, beams, columns, and foundations.
    *   **Visible Elements:** Elements beyond the cutting plane but visible in the direction of view are shown in solid lines, but without sectioning. This might include doors, windows on the side, or parts of walls not directly cut.
*   **Show Structural Details:** This is the most important part. You'll clearly depict:
    *   **Foundation:** Show the footing, foundation walls, and plinth beam.
    *   **Columns:** Show the columns extending from the foundation through the floors.
    *   **Beams:** Show the beams supporting the slabs and connecting the columns.
    *   **Slabs:** Indicate the thickness of the ground floor slab and the first-floor slab.
    *   **Staircase Construction:** The cut will show the steps, landing, and supporting structure.
    *   **Wall Thickness:** Clearly show the thickness of external and internal walls.
*   **Add Levels and Dimensions:** Mark all significant levels (GL, FFLs) and dimensions necessary to understand the vertical construction.
*   **Labeling:** Label all the structural elements clearly (e.g., Column, Beam, Slab, Footing, Staircase).

**An everyday analogy for sectioning:** Imagine you have a layered cake. To see what's inside – the layers of sponge, cream, and frosting – you cut a slice. The sectional elevation is that slice, showing you the building's internal structure.

### Key Considerations and Exam Focus

*   **Scale:** Always maintain a consistent and appropriate scale. For building plans and elevations, 1:50 or 1:100 are common.
*   **Line Weights:** Use different line weights to differentiate between visible lines, hidden lines, construction lines, and center lines. This enhances clarity.
*   **Proportions:** Even if exact dimensions aren't given for everything in the sketch, ensure the proportions of doors, windows, and rooms are realistic and consistent. Your reference books and NBC will guide you on typical dimensions.
*   **Consistency:** The plan, elevations, and sections must be consistent with each other. A window shown on the plan must appear in the elevation and also be accounted for in the section if it falls along the cutting plane.
*   **Detailing:** While you're starting from a sketch, the aim is to produce detailed drawings. This means showing openings, finishes (implied through hatching), and structural connections where necessary for understanding.
*   **Common Pitfalls:** Forgetting to add dimensions, inconsistent representation of elements between views, incorrect hatching, and poor line quality. Always double-check your work!

Remember, the goal of these drawings is to communicate the design and construction intent clearly and unambiguously. Your drawings are your building's story.

### Sample Questions and Answers

Let's test our understanding with a couple of questions, just like you might see in an exam.

**Question 1 (Conceptual):** Explain the primary purpose of a sectional elevation in building drawing and how it differs from an elevation.

**Answer:**
A sectional elevation is a drawing that shows the internal construction of a building by cutting through it vertically along a specified line. Its primary purpose is to reveal hidden structural elements like foundations, beams, slabs, and the arrangement of floors and walls. This allows for a clear understanding of how the building is put together.

In contrast, a regular elevation shows the *external* appearance of the building from a specific viewpoint (front, side, or rear). It depicts facades, doors, windows, and external architectural features but hides the internal construction.

**Question 2 (Exam-Oriented - Application):** A line sketch shows a two-storied framed residential building with a staircase on the north side. When drawing the sectional elevation along the East-West axis, what key structural elements would you expect to see *cut* and therefore *sectioned* (hatched)?

**Answer:**
When drawing a sectional elevation along the East-West axis, any structural element that the cutting plane intersects will be shown as *cut* and *sectioned*. Based on the description of a two-storied framed building, these would typically include:

1.  **External Walls:** The east and west external walls, if the section line passes through them.
2.  **Internal Walls:** Any internal partition walls that lie directly on the section line.
3.  **Columns:** Any columns located on the East-West axis that the cutting plane passes through.
4.  **Beams:** Beams supporting the ground floor slab and the first-floor slab if they are intersected by the plane.
5.  **Slabs:** The ground floor slab and the first-floor slab would be cut and shown sectioned.
6.  **Staircase:** If the section line passes through the staircase, the steps, landing, and supporting beams would be sectioned.
7.  **Foundation:** The foundation elements directly below the cut walls, columns, and slabs, such as the footing and plinth beam, would also be sectioned.

Elements that are *visible* beyond the cutting plane but not intersected, like a window on the north side (if the section is East-West and the staircase is also on the north) or a door on the south side, would be shown in solid lines without hatching.
