## Module 4: Draw Elevation, Section and Detailing of Connections for Steel Roof Truss

### Topic: Drawing Elevation, Section and Detailing of Connections for Steel Roof Truss

Welcome, everyone! In this module, we're going to delve into the exciting world of steel roof trusses. Think of a roof truss as the skeleton of your roof – it's what gives it its shape and strength, distributing loads effectively to the supporting walls. Our focus today is on translating that structural skeleton into clear, comprehensive drawings, which is a crucial skill for any civil engineer. This directly ties into our **Course Outcomes**:

*   **CO1 (K2):** We'll learn how to organize these drawings systematically and professionally, just like you'd present a well-thought-out proposal.
*   **CO2 (K2):** This module is all about detailing those critical components – the members of the truss and, more importantly, *how they connect*.
*   **CO3 (K3):** We'll be working from conceptual ideas to detailed drawings, essentially developing sketches into precise graphical representations.
*   **CO4 (K3):** While we might not be using software extensively in this specific session, the principles we learn here are foundational for using CAD software to draft complex structures.

Our understanding of these concepts is built upon the excellent guidance found in textbooks like **"Building Drawing and Detailing" by Dr. Balagopal T.S. Prabhu** and **"Building Drawing With An Integrated Approach to Built Environment" by Shah, M.G., Kale, C. M. and Patki, S.Y.** We'll also be keeping in mind the standards set by the **National Building Code of India (NBC)** and relevant state building rules, ensuring our drawings are practical and compliant.

### 1. Understanding the Steel Roof Truss: The Basics

Before we start drawing, let's get a solid grasp of what a steel roof truss is and why we draw it the way we do.

#### What is a Truss?

A truss is essentially a framework of interconnected members, typically arranged in a triangular pattern. Why triangles? Because a triangle is a geometrically stable shape. If you try to deform a triangle, its angles must change, which requires elongation or compression of its sides. Unlike squares or rectangles, which can easily be pushed into a parallelogram, a triangle holds its form. This inherent stability is what makes trusses so effective at carrying loads over long spans without needing excessive intermediate supports. Think of a bridge or a simple bicycle frame – they rely on triangulation for their strength.

#### Components of a Steel Roof Truss

A typical steel roof truss consists of several key members:

*   **Top Chord:** This is the uppermost member, usually under compression. It defines the pitch of the roof.
*   **Bottom Chord:** The lowermost member, typically under tension. It forms the ceiling line of the space below.
*   **Web Members:** These are the internal members connecting the top and bottom chords. They include:
    *   **Struts:** Members that are primarily under compression.
    *   **Ties:** Members that are primarily under tension.
    *   **Rafters:** Members that form the sloping top chord.
    *   **Principal Rafter:** The main sloping member of the top chord.
    *   **Queen Post / King Post:** Vertical members connecting the top and bottom chords, depending on the truss type.
    *   **Verticals and Diagonals:** The web members that create the triangular patterns.

#### Why Draw Trusses?

Drawing is not just about creating pretty pictures. For structural components like trusses, drawings are the universal language of construction. They convey:

*   **Geometry:** The exact size, shape, and arrangement of each member.
*   **Member Sizes:** The specific cross-section (e.g., angle sections, channel sections, I-beams) of each component.
*   **Connections:** This is arguably the most critical part. How are these members joined together? This dictates the strength and integrity of the entire truss.
*   **Details:** Fasteners (bolts, rivets), weld details, clearances, and any other specific instructions needed for fabrication and erection.

This directly relates to **CO1** and **CO2** – we need to organize and detail these components professionally.

### 2. Drawing the Elevation of a Steel Roof Truss

The elevation view shows the truss as you would see it from the side. It's like looking at the "face" of the truss.

#### What to Show in an Elevation Drawing:

*   **Overall Dimensions:** Span, height, pitch.
*   **Member Layout:** The precise arrangement of the top chord, bottom chord, and web members.
*   **Member Designations:** Each member should be clearly identified with its size and type (e.g., "2 ISA 75x75x8" for two equal angle sections of a specific size).
*   **Panel Points:** These are the points where members connect. They are crucial for load transfer.
*   **Connections:** While detailed connection drawings are separate, the elevation should indicate where connections occur.
*   **Connections to Supports:** How the truss sits on the main building structure.

**Relatable Example:** Imagine you're building a simple A-frame shed. The elevation drawing would show the outline of the shed's roof from the front or side – how tall it is, how wide, and the slope of the roof. It would also show the criss-cross pattern of the wood beams (or in our case, steel members) that make up the roof structure.

**Exam Tip:** When asked to draw an elevation, always start with the main outline (top and bottom chords) and then add the web members systematically, ensuring all panel points are clear. Pay attention to the symmetry of the truss, as most common trusses are symmetrical.

### 3. Drawing the Section of a Steel Roof Truss

A section view cuts through the truss and shows the internal arrangement of members, particularly where they intersect or overlap. This is vital for understanding how members are joined in three dimensions.

#### What to Show in a Section Drawing:

*   **Cutting Plane:** The location of the section cut should be clearly indicated on the elevation drawing.
*   **Internal Members:** How the web members connect to the chords.
*   **Connection Details:** You'll often see gusset plates (steel plates used to join members) and the pattern of bolts or welds.
*   **Member Overlap:** How different members are positioned relative to each other.

**Relatable Example:** Think back to our A-frame shed. If you were to slice through the roof structure horizontally in the middle, a section view would show you the cross-sections of the beams you cut through. For a steel truss, it would show you a steel angle section cut in half, or a steel plate. This is where you see how the bolts pass *through* the steel members.

**Connecting to CO3 (K3):** Drawing a section requires you to visualize cutting through the 3D structure and projecting what you see onto a 2D plane. This is a key part of developing a sketch into a precise drawing.

### 4. Detailing of Connections Between Members

This is where the "rubber meets the road" in truss design and drafting. A strong truss is only as strong as its weakest connection. These details are critical for structural integrity and are a major focus in our learning, aligning perfectly with **CO2 (K2)**.

#### Types of Connections:

Steel members in a truss are typically connected using either **bolted connections** or **welded connections**.

##### a) Bolted Connections:

*   **Gusset Plates:** These are thin steel plates that act as a common connecting surface for several members. Members butt against the gusset plate and are fastened to it with bolts.
*   **Bolts:** High-strength bolts are commonly used. The arrangement of bolts (number, pattern, diameter, pitch – distance between bolt centers, gauge – distance between rows of bolts) is crucial.
*   **Clearances:** Proper spacing between bolts and from the edge of the plate is essential to prevent tearing of the steel or stress concentrations.
*   **Holes:** The size of the bolt holes relative to the bolt diameter is also specified.

**Key Concepts in Bolted Connections (Referencing Prabhu, Shah et al.):**

*   **End-Bearing and Shear Failure:** Bolts can fail by shearing (cutting through) or by the plate yielding or bearing against the bolt. The design accounts for these.
*   **Gusset Plate Thickness:** This depends on the forces acting on it and the number of bolts it needs to accommodate.
*   **Bolt Pitch and Gauge:** These are not arbitrary. They ensure adequate load transfer and prevent premature failure. For instance, a closer pitch can lead to shear failure of the steel between holes, while too wide a pitch might not distribute the load effectively. The NBC and IS codes provide specific guidelines for these values.

**Relatable Example:** Imagine you're assembling furniture with screws. The screws are like bolts, and the pieces of wood you're joining are like steel members. The larger metal plate you might use to reinforce a corner is like a gusset plate. The way you drill holes and the spacing of those holes and screws significantly impact how strong that joint is.

**Exam Tip:** When detailing a bolted connection, show the gusset plate, the members connecting to it, and the bolt pattern clearly. Indicate the bolt size and material grade. Common bolt patterns include *staggered* and *diamond* patterns, which are used to optimize load distribution.

##### b) Welded Connections:

*   **Fillet Welds:** These are the most common type, forming a triangular cross-section and placed in the angle between two members.
*   **Groove Welds:** Used when members are joined end-to-end or at an angle where a butt joint is required.
*   **Weld Size:** Specified by the *throat thickness*, which is the effective depth of the weld.
*   **Weld Length:** The actual length of the weld applied.
*   **Weld Type:** Continuous or intermittent welds. Intermittent welds are used to save material and reduce residual stresses but are placed strategically.

**Key Concepts in Welded Connections:**

*   **Weld Strength:** Determined by the weld material, throat thickness, and length.
*   **Residual Stresses and Distortion:** Welding generates heat, which can cause stresses and distortion in the steel members. Proper welding procedures and sequences help minimize these.
*   **Weld Access Holes:** Sometimes, small notches are cut in members where welds are made to ensure complete fusion and prevent stress concentrations.

**Relatable Example:** Think of joining two pieces of metal with a soldering iron or a MIG welder. The molten metal you apply fills the gap and solidifies, creating a strong bond. The amount of "melt" you apply (weld size) and the length of the seam you create are directly analogous to weld size and length in steel structures.

**Exam Tip:** For welded connections, clearly indicate the type of weld (fillet, groove), its size (throat thickness), and its extent (length, continuous/intermittent). Use standard weld symbols as per IS codes.

#### Detailing Specifics for Truss Connections:

When drawing the details of a connection, we need to be precise. This is where **CO2 (K2)** and the referencing of codes like **IS 962:1989** come into play.

*   **Clear Dimensioning:** All dimensions – bolt diameters, hole sizes, edge distances, pitch, gauge, weld sizes, lengths, and the dimensions of the gusset plate itself – must be clearly and accurately shown.
*   **Member Overlap:** Show precisely how members overlap or abut.
*   **Clearances:** Ensure sufficient clearance between bolt heads/nuts and adjacent members or structures.
*   **Material Specifications:** Indicate the grade of steel for members and fasteners.
*   **Standard Conventions:** Use standard drafting symbols for welds, finishes, and material indications.

### 5. Putting It All Together: Drawing the Complete Set

A complete set of drawings for a steel roof truss typically includes:

1.  **General Arrangement Drawing:** This is often an elevation showing the overall layout, member sizes, and panel points.
2.  **Connection Details:** Enlarged views of critical joints, showing the type of connection (bolted/welded), gusset plates, bolt/weld specifications, and all relevant dimensions. You might have several of these for different types of connections within the truss.
3.  **Shop Drawings:** These are detailed drawings prepared by the fabricator for manufacturing the individual members and components. They include precise cutting lengths, hole locations, and weld details for each piece.
4.  **Erection Drawings:** These show how the fabricated members are to be assembled on-site.

Our focus in the lab will be on producing the **General Arrangement (Elevation)** and clear **Connection Details**.

**Connecting to CO4 (K3):** Understanding these manual drafting principles is essential before moving to software. CAD software allows for precise geometric representation, but you first need to know *what* to represent and *how*. The skills you develop here will make your CAD drawings more accurate and efficient.

### 6. Important Considerations and Codes

*   **National Building Code of India (NBC):** Provides general guidelines for structural design and drafting practices.
*   **Indian Standards (IS Codes):**
    *   **IS 800:** For general construction in steel.
    *   **IS 875 (Parts 1-5):** For loads on buildings and structures.
    *   **IS 962:** For architectural and building drawings, specifying conventions for line types, dimensions, and symbols.
*   **Kerala Panchayat Building Rules / Kerala Municipality Building Rules:** May have specific requirements for building construction, including roof structures, depending on the jurisdiction.

Always refer to the latest versions of these codes for current regulations and practices.

### Summary and Key Takeaways

*   Steel roof trusses rely on the geometric stability of triangles.
*   Drawings must clearly show the **elevation** (external appearance) and **section** (internal arrangement).
*   **Connection details** are paramount for truss strength and must be meticulously drawn, showing types of fasteners, bolt/weld specifications, and precise dimensions.
*   Understanding **gusset plates**, **bolt pitch/gauge**, and **weld sizes/types** is crucial for connection detailing.
*   Accuracy and adherence to drafting standards (like IS 962) are vital for professional drawings.

Remember, a well-drawn set of plans ensures that the truss is fabricated correctly and erected safely, translating sound structural engineering into a tangible, reliable structure.

## Sample Questions with Answers

Here are a few questions to test your understanding, covering both concepts and common exam scenarios:

**Q1. Why are triangles the fundamental building blocks of a roof truss?**

**Answer:** Triangles are inherently rigid and stable geometric shapes. Unlike quadrilaterals, which can be deformed without changing the length of their sides (becoming a parallelogram), a triangle's angles must change if its side lengths change. This property ensures that a truss, made up of interconnected triangles, can effectively distribute loads without buckling or deforming significantly, as explained in our discussion on truss principles.

**Q2. In a bolted connection for a steel truss, what is a gusset plate, and what role does it play?**

**Answer:** A gusset plate is a thin steel plate used as a common connecting surface for multiple steel members within a truss. It acts as a rigid joining element, allowing various members (like top chords, bottom chords, and web members) to be fastened to a single point. Members are typically attached to the gusset plate using bolts, and the plate itself is designed to transfer the forces from the connected members. This centralizes the load transfer and allows for efficient assembly of complex joints.

**Q3. When drawing a detail of a bolted connection, what are the key dimensions that must be clearly indicated?**

**Answer:** Key dimensions for a bolted connection detail include:
    *   Diameter of bolts.
    *   Diameter of bolt holes.
    *   Edge distance (distance from the center of a bolt hole to the edge of the plate or member).
    *   Pitch (distance between the centers of adjacent bolt holes in a line).
    *   Gauge (distance between the centers of bolt holes in adjacent parallel lines).
    *   Dimensions of the gusset plate.
    *   Clearances between bolt heads/nuts and adjacent members.
    *   Material grades of steel and bolts.

Accurate indication of these ensures proper load transfer and prevents premature failure of the connection, as stressed in our discussion on detailing.

**Q4. Explain the significance of the "throat thickness" when detailing a welded connection in a steel roof truss.**

**Answer:** The throat thickness is the minimum thickness of a fillet weld, measured from the root of the weld to the face of the weld. It is the critical dimension that determines the strength of a fillet weld. The effective strength of the weld is directly proportional to its throat thickness and its length. When detailing a welded connection, specifying the correct throat thickness (along with weld type and length) is crucial for ensuring the weld can safely carry the intended loads transmitted between the connected members. This is a vital aspect of ensuring the integrity of the connection, as covered in our section on welded connections.

**Q5. If you are asked to draw a sectional elevation of a truss connection, what would be the primary purpose of this drawing?**

**Answer:** The primary purpose of a sectional elevation drawing of a truss connection is to clearly illustrate the **three-dimensional arrangement** of the members and the **nature of their interface**. It reveals:
    *   How members overlap or abut each other.
    *   The type of connection being used (e.g., whether members are directly bolted to each other or connected via a gusset plate).
    *   The exact placement and pattern of bolts or the extent and type of welds.
    *   Any necessary clearances or offsets between members.

This view is essential for understanding the spatial relationships and the precise construction of the joint, which is not always evident from a simple elevation or plan view alone. It fulfills **CO3 (K3)** by helping to translate a 3D concept into a 2D drawing.
