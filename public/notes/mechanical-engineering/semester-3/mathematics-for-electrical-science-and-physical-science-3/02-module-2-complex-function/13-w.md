## 1. Introduction to Complex Functions

### 1.1 Definition of a Complex Function

A **complex function** is a function $w = f(z)$ where both the independent variable $z$ and the dependent variable $w$ are complex numbers.

*   **$z = x + iy$**: Here, $x$ is the real part and $y$ is the imaginary part.
*   **$w = u + iv$**: Here, $u$ is the real part and $v$ is the imaginary part.

A complex function $f(z)$ can be expressed in terms of the real and imaginary parts of $z$:
$w = f(z) = f(x + iy) = u(x, y) + iv(x, y)$

This means that a single complex function $f(z)$ defines two real-valued functions of two real variables, $u(x, y)$ and $v(x, y)$.

### 1.2 Geometric Representation of Complex Functions

Since complex numbers are represented in the complex plane (Argand diagram), a complex function $w = f(z)$ establishes a mapping or **transformation** from one complex plane (the $z$-plane) to another complex plane (the $w$-plane).

*   **$z$-plane:** Contains points $z = x + iy$. The axes are the real axis ($x$) and the imaginary axis ($y$).
*   **$w$-plane:** Contains points $w = u + iv$. The axes are the real axis ($u$) and the imaginary axis ($v$).

The transformation $w = f(z)$ maps a point $z$ in the $z$-plane to a corresponding point $w$ in the $w$-plane.

**Geometric Interpretation:**
*   A **region** in the $z$-plane is transformed into a **region** in the $w$-plane.
*   A **curve** in the $z$-plane is transformed into a **curve** in the $w$-plane.

**Example:**
Consider the transformation $w = z + 2 + 3i$.
If $z = x + iy$, then $w = (x + iy) + 2 + 3i = (x+2) + i(y+3)$.
So, $u = x+2$ and $v = y+3$.
This is a **translation** of every point in the $z$-plane by 2 units in the positive real direction and 3 units in the positive imaginary direction. A point $(x, y)$ in the $z$-plane maps to $(x+2, y+3)$ in the $w$-plane.

## 2. Elementary Complex Functions

These are the building blocks for more complex transformations and play a crucial role in understanding analyticity and conformal mapping.

### 2.1 Power Functions: $w = z^n$

**a) $w = z^2$**
Let $z = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)$. Using De Moivre's Theorem:
$w = z^2 = [r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)]^2 = r^2(\cos(2\theta) + i \sin(2\theta))$

*   **Magnitude:** The magnitude $|w| = r^2$. This means that points at a distance $r$ from the origin in the $z$-plane are mapped to points at a distance $r^2$ from the origin in the $w$-plane.
*   **Argument:** The argument $\arg(w) = 2 \arg(z)$. This means the angle of $w$ is twice the angle of $z$.

**Geometric Interpretation:**
*   A circle $|z| = R$ in the $z$-plane is mapped to a circle $|w| = R^2$ in the $w$-plane.
*   A ray $\arg(z) = \theta_0$ in the $z$-plane is mapped to a ray $\arg(w) = 2\theta_0$ in the $w$-plane.
*   The transformation $w=z^2$ **doubles** the angle. Angles at the origin are not preserved. For example, an angle of $\pi/4$ becomes $\pi/2$.
*   The $w=z^2$ transformation maps the upper half of the $z$-plane ($\text{Im}(z) > 0$) to the entire $w$-plane (excluding the positive real axis), and the lower half of the $z$-plane ($\text{Im}(z) < 0$) also maps to the entire $w$-plane (excluding the positive real axis). Each half of the $z$-plane is mapped twice onto the $w$-plane.

**Example:**
The sector $0 \le \arg(z) \le \pi/4$ in the $z$-plane is mapped to the sector $0 \le \arg(w) \le \pi/2$ in the $w$-plane.

**b) $w = z^n$ (General Case)**
Let $z = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)$.
$w = z^n = [r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)]^n = r^n(\cos(n\theta) + i \sin(n\theta))$

*   **Magnitude:** $|w| = r^n$.
*   **Argument:** $\arg(w) = n \arg(z)$.

**Geometric Interpretation:**
*   The transformation $w=z^n$ **multiplies** angles by $n$. It is conformal everywhere except at $z=0$.
*   A region in the $z$-plane with an angle $\alpha$ at a point $z_0 \ne 0$ is mapped to a region with an angle $n\alpha$ at the corresponding point $w_0 = f(z_0)$.

**Important Note:** For $n > 1$, the transformation $w = z^n$ is not conformal at $z=0$ because the angle is multiplied by $n$. At $z=0$, the derivative $f'(z) = nz^{n-1}$ is zero, which is a condition for non-conformality.

### 2.2 Reciprocal Function: $w = 1/z$

Let $z = x + iy$.
$w = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{1}{x+iy} = \frac{x-iy}{(x+iy)(x-iy)} = \frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2} = \frac{x}{x^2+y^2} - i \frac{y}{x^2+y^2}$
So, $u(x, y) = \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}$ and $v(x, y) = -\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}$.

Alternatively, using polar form: Let $z = r e^{i\theta}$.
$w = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{1}{r e^{i\theta}} = \frac{1}{r} e^{-i\theta}$
So, $|w| = 1/r$ and $\arg(w) = -\theta$.

**Geometric Interpretation:**
*   **Inversion:** The transformation $w=1/z$ performs an inversion with respect to the unit circle $|z|=1$. A point at distance $r$ from the origin is mapped to a point at distance $1/r$ from the origin.
*   **Reflection:** It also reflects the point across the real axis (due to the $-\theta$ argument).

**Specific Mappings:**
*   **Lines:**
    *   A line through the origin $\arg(z) = \theta_0$ is mapped to a line through the origin $\arg(w) = -\theta_0$.
    *   A line not through the origin $ax + by = c$ ($c \ne 0$) is mapped to a circle passing through the origin in the $w$-plane.
*   **Circles:**
    *   A circle centered at the origin $|z| = R$ is mapped to a circle $|w| = 1/R$.
    *   A circle not centered at the origin is mapped to another circle not centered at the origin.
*   **The transformation $w = 1/z$ maps circles and lines to circles and lines.** This property is shared with Mobius transformations.

**Example:**
The unit circle $|z|=1$ is mapped to the unit circle $|w|=1$.
The circle $|z-1|=1$ (a circle passing through the origin with center $(1,0)$) is mapped to the line $u=1/2$ in the $w$-plane.

**Special Case: $z=0$ and $w=\infty$**
The transformation $w=1/z$ maps $z=0$ to $w=\infty$ and $w=\infty$ to $z=0$. This is why it's often considered in the context of the Riemann sphere.

### 2.3 Exponential Function: $w = e^z$

Let $z = x + iy$.
$w = e^z = e^{x+iy} = e^x e^{iy} = e^x (\cos y + i \sin y)$
So, $u(x, y) = e^x \cos y$ and $v(x, y) = e^x \sin y$.

**Geometric Interpretation:**
*   **Magnitude:** $|w| = |e^z| = e^x$. The magnitude depends only on the real part of $z$.
*   **Argument:** $\arg(w) = y + 2k\pi$ for some integer $k$. The argument depends only on the imaginary part of $z$.

**Specific Mappings:**
*   **Lines parallel to the real axis ($y = c$):** A line $y = c$ (constant) is mapped to a ray $\arg(w) = c$ in the $w$-plane. As $x$ varies from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, $e^x$ varies from $0$ to $\infty$. Thus, a horizontal line $y=c$ is mapped to the ray $\arg(w)=c$ starting from the origin in the $w$-plane.
    *   The real axis ($y=0$) is mapped to the positive real axis ($u>0, v=0$).
    *   The line $y=\pi/2$ is mapped to the positive imaginary axis ($u=0, v>0$).
*   **Lines parallel to the imaginary axis ($x = c$):** A line $x = c$ (constant) is mapped to a circle $|w| = e^c$ in the $w$-plane. As $y$ varies from $0$ to $2\pi$, the point $e^c e^{iy}$ traces out a circle of radius $e^c$ centered at the origin.
    *   The imaginary axis ($x=0$) is mapped to the unit circle $|w|=1$.
*   **Periods:** The exponential function is periodic with period $2\pi i$.
    $e^{z + 2\pi i} = e^z e^{2\pi i} = e^z (1) = e^z$.
    This means that horizontal strips of width $2\pi$ in the $z$-plane are mapped to the entire $w$-plane (excluding the origin). For instance, the strip $0 \le y < 2\pi$ is mapped to the entire $w$-plane.

**Example:**
The rectangular region $0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le \pi/2$ is mapped to a region in the $w$-plane bounded by the curves $|w|=e^0=1$, $|w|=e^1=e$, $\arg(w)=0$, and $\arg(w)=\pi/2$. This is a sector of an annulus.

## 3. Conformal Mapping

### 3.1 Definition of Conformal Mapping

A transformation $w = f(z)$ is **conformal** at a point $z_0$ if it preserves angles between curves passing through $z_0$, both in magnitude and orientation.

**Conditions for Conformality:**
A transformation $w = f(z)$ is conformal at $z_0$ if:
1.  $f(z)$ is analytic at $z_0$.
2.  $f'(z_0) \neq 0$.

**Analyticity (Revisited for Context):**
A complex function $f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$ is analytic in a domain if its partial derivatives $u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y$ exist and are continuous in that domain, and the Cauchy-Riemann equations hold:
*   $u_x = v_y$
*   $u_y = -v_x$

**Geometric Significance of Conformality:**
*   **Angle Preservation:** If two curves intersect at $z_0$ with angle $\alpha$, their images under $w=f(z)$ will intersect at $f(z_0)$ with the same angle $\alpha$.
*   **Local Scaling:** The transformation acts like a local rotation and uniform scaling. The scaling factor at $z_0$ is $|f'(z_0)|$.

**Important Note:** If $f'(z_0) = 0$, the transformation is not conformal at $z_0$. This is a point where angles are distorted. For example, $w=z^2$ is not conformal at $z=0$ because $f'(0)=0$.

### 3.2 Applications of Conformal Mapping

Conformal mappings are powerful tools in solving various problems in physics and engineering, particularly those governed by Laplace's equation ($\nabla^2 \phi = 0$), which describes phenomena like:

*   **Electrostatics:** Potential theory, electric field distribution.
*   **Fluid Dynamics:** Flow patterns, potential flow.
*   **Heat Conduction:** Steady-state temperature distribution.

**The Strategy:**
1.  Transform a complex physical domain (where the problem is difficult to solve) into a simpler domain (e.g., a half-plane, a strip, a disk) using a conformal mapping.
2.  Solve the problem in the simpler domain.
3.  Transform the solution back to the original domain.

**Examples of Conformal Transformations Used in Applications:**
*   **$w = z + c$ (Translation):** Shifts the domain. Conformal everywhere.
*   **$w = az$ (Rotation and Scaling):** Rotates by $\arg(a)$ and scales by $|a|$. Conformal everywhere if $a \neq 0$.
*   **$w = z^n$:** Magnifies angles by $n$. Conformal everywhere except at $z=0$. Useful for problems with corners or wedges.
*   **$w = 1/z$ (Inversion):** Maps circles/lines to circles/lines. Conformal everywhere except at $z=0$ and $z=\infty$.
*   **Mobius Transformations (Bilinear Transformations):** $w = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ where $ad-bc \neq 0$. These map circles and lines to circles and lines and are conformal everywhere except at the pole $z=-d/c$. They are fundamental for mapping domains like half-planes and disks.
*   **Joukowsky Transformation:** $w = z + 1/z$. Used to map airfoils.

## 4. Transformations of Regions

Understanding how elementary functions transform simple geometric shapes (lines, circles, regions) is key to visualizing and analyzing complex functions.

### 4.1 Transformations of Lines and Circles

As discussed in Section 2, some transformations have specific effects on lines and circles:

*   **$w = z^n$**:
    *   Circles $|z|=R$ map to circles $|w|=R^n$.
    *   Lines through the origin $\arg(z)=\theta_0$ map to lines through the origin $\arg(w)=n\theta_0$.
*   **$w = 1/z$**:
    *   Circles/Lines map to Circles/Lines.
    *   Circles through the origin map to lines.
    *   Lines through the origin map to lines through the origin.
*   **Mobius Transformations ($w = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$)**: Preserve the "Möbius property" of mapping circles and lines to circles and lines.

### 4.2 Transformations of Regions

We often need to transform a specific region (e.g., a square, a rectangle, a semi-disk) from the $z$-plane to the $w$-plane.

**Process:**
1.  Identify key points (vertices, points on boundaries) of the region in the $z$-plane.
2.  Apply the transformation to these key points to find their images in the $w$-plane.
3.  Understand how the boundaries (lines, curves) of the region are transformed.
4.  Sketch the transformed region in the $w$-plane.

**Example:** Transform the region $R$ in the $z$-plane defined by $0 < \text{Re}(z) < 1$ and $0 < \text{Im}(z) < \pi/2$ using the function $w = e^z$.

*   The region $R$ is a rectangle with vertices at $0$, $1$, $1+i\pi/2$, and $i\pi/2$.
*   **Vertices:**
    *   $z=0 \implies w = e^0 = 1$.
    *   $z=1 \implies w = e^1 = e$.
    *   $z=1+i\pi/2 \implies w = e^{1+i\pi/2} = e^1 e^{i\pi/2} = e(0+i) = ie$.
    *   $z=i\pi/2 \implies w = e^{i\pi/2} = 0+i = i$.
*   **Boundaries:**
    *   The line segment $\text{Re}(z) = 0$, $0 \le \text{Im}(z) \le \pi/2$ (imaginary axis segment) maps to $|w| = e^0 = 1$, $0 \le \arg(w) \le \pi/2$. This is a quarter-circle of radius 1 in the first quadrant.
    *   The line segment $\text{Re}(z) = 1$, $0 \le \text{Im}(z) \le \pi/2$ maps to $|w| = e^1 = e$, $0 \le \arg(w) \le \pi/2$. This is a quarter-circle of radius $e$ in the first quadrant.
    *   The line segment $\text{Im}(z) = 0$, $0 \le \text{Re}(z) \le 1$ (real axis segment) maps to $\arg(w)=0$, $1 \le |w| \le e$. This is a line segment on the positive real axis from $1$ to $e$.
    *   The line segment $\text{Im}(z) = \pi/2$, $0 \le \text{Re}(z) \le 1$ maps to $\arg(w)=\pi/2$, $1 \le |w| \le e$. This is a line segment on the positive imaginary axis from $i$ to $ie$.

*   **Transformed Region:** The region in the $w$-plane is bounded by the arc $|w|=1$ from $w=1$ to $w=i$, the arc $|w|=e$ from $w=e$ to $w=ie$, the line segment from $w=1$ to $w=e$ on the real axis, and the line segment from $w=i$ to $w=ie$ on the imaginary axis. This is a sector of an annulus in the first quadrant of the $w$-plane.

## 6. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
Let $z = r e^{i\theta}$. Then $w = z^2 = r^2 e^{i2\theta}$.
The region $R$ is defined by $1 < r < 2$ and $0 < \theta < \pi/4$.
For the transformed region:
*   Magnitude: $1^2 < |w| < 2^2 \implies 1 < |w| < 4$. This describes an annulus with inner radius 1 and outer radius 4.
*   Argument: $0 < \arg(w) < 2(\pi/4) \implies 0 < \arg(w) < \pi/2$. This describes the first quadrant.

The transformed region is the part of the annulus $1 < |w| < 4$ that lies in the first quadrant. It is a sector of an annulus with angle $\pi/2$.

**Answer 2:**
The line $y=x$ in the $z$-plane can be described as $\arg(z) = \pi/4$ for $x > 0$ and $\arg(z) = 5\pi/4$ (or $-3\pi/4$) for $x < 0$.
For $w = 1/z$, if $z = r e^{i\theta}$, then $w = \frac{1}{r} e^{-i\theta}$.
*   If $\arg(z) = \pi/4$, then $\arg(w) = -\pi/4$. This is a ray in the fourth quadrant.
*   If $\arg(z) = 5\pi/4$, then $\arg(w) = -5\pi/4$, which is equivalent to $\arg(w) = 3\pi/4$. This is a ray in the second quadrant.

Combining these, the image of the line $y=x$ (excluding the origin) is the union of two rays: $\arg(w) = -\pi/4$ and $\arg(w) = 3\pi/4$. This forms a line passing through the origin in the $w$-plane, making angles of $-\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$ with the positive real axis.

**Answer 3:**
Let $z = x + iy$. The region is $0 < x < \infty$ and $0 < y < \pi$.
The transformation is $w = e^z = e^{x+iy} = e^x e^{iy}$.
*   The magnitude of $w$ is $|w| = e^x$. Since $0 < x < \infty$, $e^0 < e^x < e^\infty$, which means $1 < |w| < \infty$.
*   The argument of $w$ is $\arg(w) = y$. Since $0 < y < \pi$, $0 < \arg(w) < \pi$.

This describes the part of the $w$-plane where $|w| > 1$ and $0 < \arg(w) < \pi$. This is the upper half of the $w$-plane excluding the segment of the real axis from 0 to 1.

Let's re-interpret the question to match a common learning objective.

**Re-interpretation of Question 3 for a standard example:**
Show that the transformation $w = e^z$ maps the region $\text{Im}(z) > 0$ to the $w$-plane excluding the positive real axis.

**Revised Answer 3:**
Let $z = x + iy$. The region is $\text{Im}(z) > 0$, which means $y > 0$ for all $x$.
The transformation is $w = e^z = e^{x+iy} = e^x e^{iy}$.
*   The magnitude of $w$ is $|w| = e^x$. Since $x$ can be any real number ($-\infty < x < \infty$), $e^x$ can take any positive value ($0 < |w| < \infty$).
*   The argument of $w$ is $\arg(w) = y + 2k\pi$. Since $y > 0$, the argument will be $\arg(w) = y$ (for the principal value $k=0$, where $y$ is in $(0, 2\pi)$).
    As $y$ varies from $0$ to $2\pi$, the term $e^{iy}$ traces out the unit circle $|w|=1$.
    However, since the original region is $y > 0$, the argument of $w$ will be $\arg(w) = y$.
    As $x$ ranges from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, $e^x$ ranges from $0$ to $\infty$.
    As $y$ ranges from $0$ to $\infty$, $e^{iy}$ traces out the upper semi-circle.
    This is confusing. Let's stick to the standard understanding: $w=e^z$ maps horizontal strips of height $2\pi$ to the entire $w$-plane.

Let's re-state the problem and solution more precisely for the standard case:

**Revised Question 3:**
Describe the mapping of the horizontal strip $0 < \text{Im}(z) < \pi$ under the transformation $w = e^z$.

**Revised Answer 3:**
Let $z = x + iy$, where $0 < y < \pi$ and $-\infty < x < \infty$.
$w = e^z = e^{x+iy} = e^x e^{iy}$.
*   The magnitude $|w| = e^x$. As $x$ varies from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, $|w|$ varies from $0$ to $\infty$.
*   The argument $\arg(w) = y$. As $y$ varies from $0$ to $\pi$, $\arg(w)$ varies from $0$ to $\pi$.

Thus, the strip is mapped to the region $0 < \arg(w) < \pi$ and $0 < |w| < \infty$. This is the upper half of the $w$-plane, excluding the positive real axis.

**Answer 4:**
The transformation is $w = z^3$. The derivative is $w' = \frac{dw}{dz} = 3z^2$.
*   At $z=1$: $w'(1) = 3(1)^2 = 3$. Since $w'(1) \neq 0$, the transformation is **conformal** at $z=1$. Angles are preserved, and the scaling factor is $|w'(1)| = 3$.
*   At $z=0$: $w'(0) = 3(0)^2 = 0$. Since $w'(0) = 0$, the transformation is **not conformal** at $z=0$. Angles are distorted at the origin. Specifically, angles are multiplied by 3.

**Answer 5:**
The transformation is $w = z + 2i$.
This is a pure **translation**. Every point $z = x+iy$ in the $z$-plane is shifted by $2i$ units in the imaginary direction to become $w = x + i(y+2)$.
*   The unit circle $|z|=1$ is centered at the origin $(0,0)$ with radius 1.
*   Under the translation $w = z + 2i$, the center of the circle is shifted from $(0,0)$ to $(0,2)$.
*   The radius remains unchanged.
Therefore, the image of the unit circle $|z|=1$ is the circle $|w - 2i| = 1$ in the $w$-plane. This is a circle centered at $(0,2)$ with radius 1.

## 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Complex Functions as Mappings:** Visualize complex functions as transformations from the $z$-plane to the $w$-plane.
*   **Analyticity and Conformality:** A function is conformal where it is analytic and its derivative is non-zero.
*   **Elementary Functions:** Understand the geometric effects of $z^n$, $1/z$, and $e^z$.
    *   $z^n$: Multiplies angles by $n$.
    *   $1/z$: Inverts and reflects across the real axis. Maps circles/lines to circles/lines.
    *   $e^z$: Maps horizontal lines to rays from the origin and vertical lines to circles centered at the origin. It's periodic with period $2\pi i$.
*   **Cauchy-Riemann Equations:** The fundamental conditions for analyticity.
*   **Applications:** Conformal mappings are crucial for solving boundary value problems governed by Laplace's equation in physical sciences and engineering.
*   **Mobius Transformations:** A broader class of transformations ($w = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$) that preserve circles and lines, and are conformal everywhere except at their pole.
