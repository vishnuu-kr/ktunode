# Fluid Mechanics and Machinery - Module 4: Centrifugal Pumps

## Introduction to Centrifugal Pumps

Centrifugal pumps are a type of dynamic pump that moves fluids by the conversion of rotational kinetic energy to the hydrodynamic energy of the fluid. They are widely used in various applications, from domestic water supply to industrial processes. The core principle behind their operation is the **centrifugal force** generated by a rotating impeller.

**Key Concept:** The pump adds energy to the fluid by increasing its velocity and pressure through the action of a rotating impeller.

**Relevance to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO2:** The flow of fluid through the pump is governed by conservation of mass and momentum, which are key principles.
*   **CO3:** While not directly about pipe flow losses, pumps are crucial components in piping networks, and understanding their performance is essential for system design.
*   **CO5:** This topic directly addresses the selection of a suitable turbomachine (centrifugal pump) for specific applications by understanding its operating parameters.

## 1. Principle and Working of Centrifugal Pumps

### 1.1 Principle of Operation

The working principle of a centrifugal pump is based on the conversion of mechanical energy (supplied by a prime mover like an electric motor) into fluid energy. This is achieved through the following steps:

1.  **Suction:** The fluid enters the pump casing at the center of the impeller (the "eye").
2.  **Impeller Action:** The impeller, rotating at high speed, imparts kinetic energy to the fluid. The vanes of the impeller push the fluid radially outwards.
3.  **Velocity to Pressure Conversion:** As the fluid moves from the eye towards the periphery of the impeller, its velocity increases. This high-velocity fluid then enters the volute casing or a diffuser. The volute casing is designed to gradually increase in cross-sectional area. This expansion causes the fluid velocity to decrease, and according to Bernoulli's principle, this decrease in kinetic energy is converted into an increase in pressure energy.
4.  **Discharge:** The high-pressure fluid is then directed to the discharge outlet of the pump.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Impeller:** The rotating component with vanes that imparts energy to the fluid.
*   **Volute Casing:** A spiral-shaped casing that surrounds the impeller. Its gradually expanding area converts kinetic energy into pressure energy.
*   **Diffuser:** A stationary set of vanes surrounding the impeller, used in some pumps to convert kinetic energy to pressure energy more efficiently than a volute casing.

### 1.2 Working of a Centrifugal Pump

A centrifugal pump consists of the following main components:

*   **Impeller:** The heart of the pump, it has vanes that rotate with the shaft.
*   **Casing (Volute or Diffuser):** Encloses the impeller and collects the fluid discharged from it.
*   **Shaft:** Transmits mechanical power from the prime mover to the impeller.
*   **Bearings:** Support the shaft.
*   **Seals:** Prevent leakage along the shaft.
*   **Suction Pipe:** Carries the fluid from the source to the pump eye.
*   **Delivery Pipe:** Carries the fluid from the pump discharge to the destination.

**Working Process:**
1.  The prime mover rotates the shaft, which in turn rotates the impeller.
2.  Fluid enters the pump through the suction pipe and reaches the eye of the impeller.
3.  As the impeller rotates, its vanes push the fluid outwards due to centrifugal force. The fluid gains both velocity and pressure.
4.  The high-velocity fluid leaves the impeller periphery and enters the volute casing.
5.  In the volute casing, the cross-sectional area increases gradually, causing the velocity of the fluid to decrease and its pressure to increase (kinetic energy to pressure energy conversion).
6.  The fluid is then discharged through the delivery pipe.

**Important Point to Remember:** Centrifugal pumps are essentially **dynamic pumps** that operate on the principle of **centrifugal force**.

## 2. Head Imparted by a Centrifugal Pump

The "head" imparted by a pump refers to the total energy per unit weight of the fluid that the pump adds to the system. It is typically expressed in meters of the fluid column.

### 2.1 Types of Head

*   **Manometric Head ($H_m$):** This is the head developed by the pump. It is the difference between the total energy at the delivery flange and the total energy at the suction flange.
    $H_m = \frac{P_{delivery}}{\rho g} + \frac{V_{delivery}^2}{2g} + Z_{delivery} - \left(\frac{P_{suction}}{\rho g} + \frac{V_{suction}^2}{2g} + Z_{suction}\right)$
    Where:
    *   $P$ is pressure
    *   $\rho$ is fluid density
    *   $g$ is acceleration due to gravity
    *   $V$ is fluid velocity
    *   $Z$ is elevation
    *   Subscripts 'delivery' and 'suction' refer to the respective flanges.

*   **Static Head ($H_{static}$):** The difference in elevation between the free surface of the liquid at the suction side and the free surface of the liquid at the delivery side.
    $H_{static} = H_{suction \, lift} + H_{delivery \, head}$
    Where $H_{suction \, lift}$ is the vertical distance from the pump center line to the free surface of the liquid in the sump, and $H_{delivery \, head}$ is the vertical distance from the pump center line to the free surface of the liquid in the delivery tank.

*   **Suction Head ($h_s$):** The vertical distance between the center line of the pump and the free surface of the liquid in the suction tank. This can be positive (liquid level above pump center line) or negative (suction lift, liquid level below pump center line).

*   **Delivery Head ($h_d$):** The vertical distance between the center line of the pump and the free surface of the liquid in the delivery tank.

*   **Friction Head:** Losses due to friction in the suction and delivery pipes.

*   **Velocity Head:** The kinetic energy per unit weight of the fluid, typically represented as $V^2/2g$.

**Important Note:** In many practical scenarios, especially in problems where velocity triangle calculations are *not* required, the **Manometric Head ($H_m$)** is often considered the "head developed by the pump" and is the primary parameter of interest. This head represents the total useful energy given to the fluid.

## 3. Work Required by a Centrifugal Pump

The work done by the pump on the fluid is the energy transferred to the fluid per unit mass or per unit volume.

### 3.1 Theoretical Work Done per Unit Weight of Fluid

The theoretical work done by the impeller on the fluid per unit weight of fluid is often related to the velocity of the fluid at the impeller outlet. If we consider the outlet velocity ($V_2$) and the corresponding tangential component of velocity ($V_{w2}$), and the inlet velocity ($V_1$) and its tangential component ($V_{w1}$), the theoretical head developed by the impeller is given by:

$H_{th} = \frac{V_{w2}u_2 - V_{w1}u_1}{g}$

Where:
*   $V_{w1}$ and $V_{w2}$ are the tangential components of the absolute velocity of the fluid at the inlet and outlet of the impeller, respectively.
*   $u_1$ and $u_2$ are the peripheral velocities of the impeller at the inlet and outlet, respectively ($u = \omega r$, where $\omega$ is angular velocity and $r$ is radius).

**However, since problems using velocity triangles are not required, we focus on the output of the pump.**

The actual energy imparted to the fluid per unit weight is the Manometric Head ($H_m$).

Therefore, the **Work Done per Unit Weight of Fluid** by the pump is equal to the **Manometric Head ($H_m$)**.

### 3.2 Theoretical Work Done per Unit Mass of Fluid

Theoretical Work Done per unit mass = $g \times H_m$ (Joules/kg)

### 3.3 Theoretical Power Required

Theoretical Power Required = (Weight flow rate) $\times$ $H_m$
Theoretical Power Required = $(\rho \times Q \times g) \times H_m$
Where:
*   $\rho$ is the density of the fluid (kg/m³)
*   $Q$ is the discharge or flow rate (m³/s)
*   $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity (m/s²)
*   $H_m$ is the Manometric Head (m)

**Important Point:** The theoretical power is the minimum power required to pump the fluid, assuming no losses.

## 4. Efficiencies of Centrifugal Pumps

No pump is 100% efficient. Some energy is lost due to various factors. Efficiencies are used to quantify how effectively the input power is converted into useful fluid power.

### 4.1 Types of Efficiencies

*   **Manometric Efficiency ($\eta_m$):** The ratio of the useful work done by the pump (Manometric Head) to the work supplied to the impeller.
    $\eta_m = \frac{\text{Work done on fluid per unit weight}}{\text{Work supplied to impeller per unit weight}} = \frac{g H_m}{E_t}$
    Where $E_t$ is the energy imparted to the fluid by the impeller per unit mass (this is related to the tangential velocity components, which we are avoiding in calculations).

    **More practically, in terms of power:**
    $\eta_m = \frac{\text{Hydraulic Power Output}}{\text{Impeller Power Input}} = \frac{\rho g Q H_m}{P_{impeller}}$

*   **Mechanical Efficiency ($\eta_{mech}$):** The ratio of the power delivered to the impeller to the power supplied by the prime mover to the pump shaft. This accounts for mechanical losses like friction in bearings and seals, and disk friction of the impeller.
    $\eta_{mech} = \frac{\text{Shaft Power}}{\text{Power delivered to impeller}} = \frac{P_{shaft}}{P_{impeller}}$

*   **Volumetric Efficiency ($\eta_v$):** The ratio of the actual discharge ($Q$) to the theoretical discharge that would be discharged by the impeller if there were no leakage losses. Leakage occurs from the high-pressure side to the low-pressure side through the clearance between the impeller and casing.
    $\eta_v = \frac{\text{Actual Discharge (Q)}}{\text{Theoretical Discharge (Q_th)}}$

*   **Overall Efficiency ($\eta_{overall}$ or $\eta_{pump}$):** The ratio of the useful hydraulic power output to the power input from the prime mover (shaft power). This is the most common efficiency considered for practical purposes.
    $\eta_{overall} = \frac{\text{Hydraulic Power Output}}{\text{Shaft Power Input}} = \frac{\rho g Q H_m}{P_{shaft}}$

**Relationship between Efficiencies:**
$\eta_{overall} = \eta_m \times \eta_{mech} \times \eta_v$

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   Efficiencies are always less than 1 (or 100%).
*   Overall efficiency is the product of the individual efficiencies.
*   Higher efficiency means less power is wasted, leading to lower operating costs.

**Textbook Integration:**
*   **Cengel & Cimbala (2013):** Will discuss pump efficiencies in relation to performance curves.
*   **Som & Bhattacharyya (2011):** Will provide detailed derivations and definitions of various efficiencies.
*   **Bansal (2005):** Covers efficiencies extensively, including their impact on pump selection.

## 5. Priming of Centrifugal Pumps

Priming is a process that is necessary for centrifugal pumps to start operating correctly. It involves filling the pump casing and the suction pipe with the fluid being pumped before starting the pump.

### 5.1 Why is Priming Necessary?

Centrifugal pumps cannot create a vacuum to draw fluid from a source if the casing is filled with air. Air is much less dense than the fluid, and the pressure difference generated by the impeller rotating in air is insufficient to lift the fluid. Priming ensures that the pump casing and suction pipe are completely filled with liquid, allowing the impeller to operate in the liquid and create the necessary pressure difference for suction.

### 5.2 Methods of Priming

Several methods can be used for priming:

1.  **Manual Priming:** Filling the pump casing and suction pipe with water from an external source (e.g., a bucket or a pipe connected to a water supply) until the casing is full.
2.  **Foot Valve and Air Vessel:** A foot valve is usually fitted at the end of the suction pipe submerged in the fluid source. It opens inwards to allow fluid to enter but closes when the pump stops, preventing the fluid from flowing back. An air vessel can be attached to the suction pipe to trap some air, which helps in expelling the air from the casing during the initial stages of operation.
3.  **Using a Vacuum Pump:** A vacuum pump can be connected to the pump casing to exhaust the air, thereby creating a vacuum that draws the fluid into the casing and up the suction pipe.
4.  **Using a Self-Priming Pump:** Some pumps are designed to be self-priming. They typically have a mechanism (like a small internal pump or a special casing design) that helps in trapping and recirculating some fluid to expel the air from the suction side, allowing the pump to prime itself.
5.  **Jacket Priming:** The pump casing is surrounded by a jacket through which water is circulated at high pressure. This creates a partial vacuum in the casing, drawing air out.

**Important Point to Remember:** Centrifugal pumps must be primed before starting if the pump casing and suction pipe are not already filled with the liquid.

## 6. Cavitation in Centrifugal Pumps

Cavitation is a phenomenon that occurs in pumps when the pressure of the fluid at certain points (usually at the suction side or the eye of the impeller) falls below its vapor pressure. This causes the formation of vapor bubbles within the liquid. When these bubbles move to regions of higher pressure, they collapse violently, creating shock waves.

### 6.1 Causes of Cavitation

*   **Low Suction Pressure:** If the pressure at the suction inlet is too low.
*   **High Fluid Temperature:** Higher temperatures mean lower vapor pressure.
*   **High Pumping Speed:** Higher speeds can lead to lower pressures at the impeller eye.
*   **Excessive Friction Losses in the Suction Pipe:** Long or narrow suction pipes can cause significant pressure drops.
*   **High Static Suction Lift:** Pumping fluid from a level significantly below the pump.

### 6.2 Effects of Cavitation

*   **Noise and Vibration:** The collapsing vapor bubbles create loud noises resembling the sound of gravel passing through the pump. This also leads to significant vibration.
*   **Damage to Impeller and Casing:** The sudden collapse of bubbles can erode and damage the impeller vanes and casing surfaces. This damage is often referred to as "pitting."
*   **Reduction in Pump Performance:** Cavitation reduces the flow rate and head delivered by the pump and decreases its efficiency.
*   **Catastrophic Failure:** In severe cases, the damage caused by cavitation can lead to the complete failure of the pump.

### 6.3 Prevention of Cavitation

*   **Ensure Adequate NPSH:** Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH) available at the pump suction must be greater than the NPSH required by the pump. NPSH is the absolute pressure head available at the suction port of the pump, expressed in terms of the fluid column.
    *   **NPSH Available ($NPSH_A$):** Calculated based on system parameters.
    *   **NPSH Required ($NPSH_R$):** Specified by the pump manufacturer, dependent on pump design and operating speed.
    *   Condition for no cavitation: $NPSH_A > NPSH_R$.

*   **Avoid Low Suction Pressures:** Ensure the suction tank pressure is sufficiently high.
*   **Reduce Suction Pipe Losses:** Use larger diameter suction pipes, minimize bends and fittings, and keep the suction pipe as short as possible.
*   **Lower Fluid Temperature:** If possible, reduce the temperature of the fluid being pumped.
*   **Reduce Pump Speed:** Lowering the operating speed can reduce the likelihood of cavitation.
*   **Use Pumps with Higher NPSH Characteristics:** Select pumps designed for low NPSH applications.
*   **Submerge Suction Intake:** Ensure the suction intake is sufficiently submerged to avoid vortex formation.

**Important Point to Remember:** Cavitation is a serious issue that can damage the pump and reduce its performance. It is crucial to operate the pump within its safe operating limits to prevent it.

## Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
Define Manometric Head. What are the factors affecting it?

**Answer 1:**
Manometric Head ($H_m$) is the total head developed by the pump. It is the difference between the total energy at the delivery flange and the total energy at the suction flange of the pump.
$H_m = \frac{P_{delivery}}{\rho g} + \frac{V_{delivery}^2}{2g} + Z_{delivery} - \left(\frac{P_{suction}}{\rho g} + \frac{V_{suction}^2}{2g} + Z_{suction}\right)$
Factors affecting it include:
*   Pressure at suction and delivery.
*   Velocity of fluid at suction and delivery.
*   Elevation difference between suction and delivery points.
*   System characteristics (e.g., friction losses in pipes, which are implicitly accounted for in the pressure terms).

**Question 2:**
What is priming, and why is it necessary for a centrifugal pump?

**Answer 2:**
Priming is the process of filling the pump casing and suction pipe with the fluid to be pumped before starting the pump. It is necessary because centrifugal pumps cannot displace air effectively. Air in the casing prevents the formation of the necessary pressure difference to draw liquid from the source.

**Question 3:**
Explain cavitation in centrifugal pumps. What are its detrimental effects?

**Answer 3:**
Cavitation is the formation and subsequent collapse of vapor bubbles within the liquid when the pressure drops below the vapor pressure of the liquid.
Detrimental effects include:
*   Noise and vibration.
*   Erosion and damage to impeller and casing.
*   Reduction in pump performance (head and flow rate).
*   Potential for catastrophic pump failure.

**Question 4:**
Calculate the theoretical power required to pump 1000 liters of water per minute against a head of 25 meters. (Assume density of water = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²)

**Answer 4:**
Given:
Flow rate, $Q = 1000$ liters/min
Head, $H_m = 25$ m
Density of water, $\rho = 1000$ kg/m³
Acceleration due to gravity, $g = 9.81$ m/s²

First, convert flow rate to m³/s:
$Q = 1000 \frac{\text{liters}}{\text{min}} \times \frac{1 \text{ m}^3}{1000 \text{ liters}} \times \frac{1 \text{ min}}{60 \text{ s}} = \frac{1}{60} \text{ m}^3/\text{s} \approx 0.01667 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$

Theoretical Power Required ($P_{th}$) = $\rho g Q H_m$
$P_{th} = 1000 \, \text{kg/m}^3 \times 9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2 \times 0.01667 \, \text{m}^3/\text{s} \times 25 \, \text{m}$
$P_{th} = 4070.875 \, \text{Watts}$
$P_{th} \approx 4.07 \, \text{kW}$

**Question 5:**
If a centrifugal pump delivers 5 m³/min of water at a manometric head of 20 m, and the overall efficiency is 75%, calculate the actual power input required. (Assume density of water = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²)

**Answer 5:**
Given:
Flow rate, $Q = 5$ m³/min
Manometric Head, $H_m = 20$ m
Overall efficiency, $\eta_{overall} = 75\% = 0.75$
Density of water, $\rho = 1000$ kg/m³
Acceleration due to gravity, $g = 9.81$ m/s²

First, convert flow rate to m³/s:
$Q = 5 \frac{\text{m}^3}{\text{min}} \times \frac{1 \text{ min}}{60 \text{ s}} = \frac{5}{60} \text{ m}^3/\text{s} = \frac{1}{12} \text{ m}^3/\text{s} \approx 0.08333 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$

Hydraulic Power Output ($P_h$) = $\rho g Q H_m$
$P_h = 1000 \, \text{kg/m}^3 \times 9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2 \times 0.08333 \, \text{m}^3/\text{s} \times 20 \, \text{m}$
$P_h = 16350 \, \text{Watts}$

Overall efficiency is defined as:
$\eta_{overall} = \frac{\text{Hydraulic Power Output}}{\text{Shaft Power Input}}$
Shaft Power Input ($P_{shaft}$) = $\frac{\text{Hydraulic Power Output}}{\eta_{overall}}$
$P_{shaft} = \frac{16350 \, \text{W}}{0.75}$
$P_{shaft} = 21800 \, \text{Watts}$
$P_{shaft} = 21.8 \, \text{kW}$
