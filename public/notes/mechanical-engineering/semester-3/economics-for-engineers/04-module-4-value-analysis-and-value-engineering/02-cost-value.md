# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 4: Value Analysis and Value Engineering

## Topic: Cost Value

### 1. Introduction

This module focuses on understanding and improving the value of products, processes, and services through **Value Analysis (VA)** and **Value Engineering (VE)**. At its core, these disciplines aim to achieve the required function at the lowest possible cost without compromising quality, reliability, or performance. The concept of **Cost Value** is fundamental to this process.

### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

#### 2.1. Value

In the context of VA/VE, **Value** is defined as the ratio of **Function** to **Cost**.

$$
\text{Value (V)} = \frac{\text{Function (F)}}{\text{Cost (C)}}
$$

*   **Function:** What a product, process, or service *does*. It's the purpose it serves or the need it fulfills. Functions can be primary (essential to the product's existence and operation) or secondary (enhance the product but are not critical to its core purpose).
    *   *Example (Pencil):* Primary function is to write. Secondary functions might include being aesthetically pleasing, having an eraser, or being easy to hold.
*   **Cost:** The total resources (money, time, labor, materials) expended to achieve a particular function. This includes all costs associated with design, manufacturing, marketing, sales, and support.

**Objective of VA/VE:** To increase value by either:
*   **Increasing Function while keeping Cost constant.**
*   **Decreasing Cost while keeping Function constant.**
*   **Increasing Function and decreasing Cost simultaneously.**
*   **Increasing Function more than Cost increases.**
*   **Decreasing Cost more than Function decreases.**

#### 2.2. Cost

Understanding different types of costs is crucial for cost analysis in VA/VE.

*   **Direct Costs:** Costs directly attributable to the production of a specific product or service.
    *   **Direct Material Costs:** Cost of raw materials and components used.
    *   **Direct Labor Costs:** Wages paid to workers directly involved in production.
*   **Indirect Costs (Overheads):** Costs not directly tied to a specific product but necessary for overall operations.
    *   **Manufacturing Overheads:** Factory rent, utilities, depreciation of machinery, indirect labor (supervisors, maintenance).
    *   **Selling & Administrative Overheads:** Marketing, sales salaries, office rent, executive salaries.
*   **Fixed Costs:** Costs that do not change with the level of output (e.g., rent, salaries of permanent staff).
*   **Variable Costs:** Costs that vary directly with the level of output (e.g., raw materials, direct labor per unit).
*   **Total Cost:** Fixed Costs + Variable Costs.
*   **Cost of Ownership:** Includes initial purchase price, operating costs, maintenance costs, and disposal costs. (Reference: Thuesen & Fabrycky)

#### 2.3. Cost Value Relationship

The cost value relationship is central to VA/VE. A high-value item performs its function efficiently at a low cost. A low-value item may have a high cost relative to its function.

*   **High Value:** Low Cost / High Function
*   **Low Value:** High Cost / Low Function

**Example:** Consider two types of screws.
*   **Screw A:** Costs $0.10 per unit, strong, durable, easy to install.
*   **Screw B:** Costs $0.05 per unit, weaker, prone to stripping, requires special tooling to install.

If Screw A performs the required fastening function adequately and reliably, and the time/cost saved in installation outweighs its higher unit cost, Screw A might offer higher value. Conversely, if Screw B's lower cost is significant and its functional deficiencies are minimal for the application, it might be the higher-value choice.

### 3. Value Analysis (VA) vs. Value Engineering (VE)

While both aim to improve value, they differ in their timing and application.

#### 3.1. Value Analysis (VA)

*   **Focus:** Applied to **existing products, services, or processes**.
*   **Timing:** Conducted *after* a product has been designed and is in production.
*   **Objective:** To reduce costs and improve the value of *current* offerings.
*   **Methodology:** Involves a systematic review of existing designs, materials, manufacturing methods, and supply chains.
*   **Reference:** Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015) often discuss VA in the context of improving the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of existing business operations.

#### 3.2. Value Engineering (VE)

*   **Focus:** Applied to **new products or services during the design phase**.
*   **Timing:** Conducted *before* a product is manufactured or a service is implemented.
*   **Objective:** To ensure the design incorporates maximum value from the outset, preventing unnecessary costs.
*   **Methodology:** A proactive approach that integrates value considerations into the design process, exploring alternative materials, designs, and manufacturing techniques.
*   **Reference:** Thuesen & Fabrycky (1966) and Paneerselvam (2012) emphasize VE as a design philosophy to optimize for performance and cost throughout the product lifecycle.

**Important Note:** While VA and VE are distinct, the principles and techniques are largely the same. The primary difference is *when* they are applied.

### 4. The Value Analysis/Engineering Job Plan (The FAST System)

Both VA and VE typically follow a structured job plan, often referred to as the "FAST" system (which stands for Function, Activity, System, Technique, or sometimes Function Analysis System Technique). This plan ensures a systematic and comprehensive approach.

#### 4.1. The Seven Phases of the VA/VE Job Plan

1.  **Information Phase:**
    *   **Objective:** Gather all relevant data about the product/service.
    *   **Activities:**
        *   Define the product/service and its purpose.
        *   Identify customers and their needs.
        *   Collect cost data (materials, labor, overheads).
        *   Understand performance requirements, specifications, and quality standards.
        *   Analyze the market and competitor products.
        *   Understand the manufacturing process and supply chain.
    *   **Key Question:** "What is it?"
    *   **Reference:** Blank & Tarquin (7th Ed.) highlight the importance of a thorough information gathering stage for any economic analysis.

2.  **Function Phase:**
    *   **Objective:** Define and understand the functions performed by the product/service.
    *   **Activities:**
        *   Identify **primary** and **secondary** functions.
        *   Use techniques like **Functional Analysis System Technique (FAST)** diagramming. FAST diagrams visually represent the relationships between functions and how they contribute to the overall purpose.
        *   Quantify functions where possible (e.g., load capacity, speed, durability).
    *   **Key Question:** "What does it do?"
    *   **Example (Door Lock):**
        *   Primary Function: Secure the door.
        *   Secondary Functions: Allow access, provide aesthetics, indicate lock status.
    *   **Reference:** Park (2001) emphasizes functional analysis as the cornerstone of VA/VE, moving beyond features to understand "what it does."

3.  **Creative Phase (Brainstorming):**
    *   **Objective:** Generate a wide range of alternative ideas to perform the identified functions.
    *   **Activities:**
        *   Brainstorming sessions involving a multidisciplinary team.
        *   Encourage "wild" ideas.
        *   Defer judgment and criticism.
        *   Quantity over quality initially.
    *   **Key Question:** "How else can it be done?"
    *   **Example (Door Lock - Alternative Mechanisms):** Electronic keypad, biometric scanner, smart lock integration, simpler latch.

4.  **Evaluation Phase:**
    *   **Objective:** Analyze, screen, and select the most promising ideas from the creative phase.
    *   **Activities:**
        *   Evaluate ideas based on feasibility, cost, potential for value improvement, risks, and alignment with objectives.
        *   Use criteria such as cost reduction potential, functional improvement, manufacturability, and market acceptance.
        *   Perform preliminary cost-benefit analysis for promising alternatives.
    *   **Key Question:** "Which of these ideas are the best?"

5.  **Development Phase:**
    *   **Objective:** Develop the selected ideas into concrete proposals.
    *   **Activities:**
        *   Refine chosen concepts.
        *   Perform detailed cost estimates for the proposed alternatives.
        *   Develop preliminary designs, material specifications, and manufacturing process plans.
        *   Assess technical feasibility and potential challenges.
    *   **Key Question:** "How can we make this idea work?"

6.  **Presentation Phase:**
    *   **Objective:** Present the proposed solution to decision-makers for approval.
    *   **Activities:**
        *   Prepare a clear and concise proposal outlining the problem, proposed solution, expected benefits (cost savings, performance improvements), implementation plan, and required resources.
        *   Use data and analysis to support the recommendation.
    *   **Key Question:** "What is the recommendation?"

7.  **Implementation Phase:**
    *   **Objective:** Execute the approved plan.
    *   **Activities:**
        *   Implement the changes (new design, materials, processes).
        *   Monitor the implementation process.
        *   Track costs and performance to verify expected benefits.
        *   Make adjustments as needed.
    *   **Key Question:** "Let's do it and track the results."
    *   **Reference:** Newman & Lavelle (2002) emphasize the importance of proper implementation and follow-up to realize the full benefits of economic analyses.

### 5. Tools and Techniques in VA/VE

#### 5.1. Function Analysis System Technique (FAST)

*   **Purpose:** A diagrammatic technique used in the Function Phase to identify and define functions and their logical relationships. It helps to visualize how functions are achieved and where opportunities for improvement exist.
*   **How it works:** Functions are arranged in a logical sequence, starting with the basic function and branching out to supporting functions, which are then answered by "how" and "when."
*   **Example (Ballpoint Pen):**
    *   Basic Function: **Apply Ink to Surface**
    *   How: **Through writing tip**
    *   When: **When held by user**
    *   How: **User holds pen**
    *   When: **To convey information**
    *   How: **By writing**

#### 5.2. Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)

*   **Purpose:** To compare the total expected benefits of a project or proposal against its total expected costs. Essential for evaluating the economic viability of proposed changes.
*   **Formula:**
    $$
    \text{Benefit-Cost Ratio (BCR)} = \frac{\text{Total Discounted Benefits}}{\text{Total Discounted Costs}}
    $$
    *   A BCR > 1 generally indicates that the benefits outweigh the costs.
*   **Reference:** CO4 explicitly mentions using cost-benefit analysis to solve business problems.

#### 5.3. Break-Even Analysis

*   **Purpose:** To determine the point at which total revenue equals total costs, meaning there is no profit or loss. Useful for understanding the cost implications of different production levels and pricing strategies for proposed changes.
*   **Formula:**
    $$
    \text{Break-Even Point (Units)} = \frac{\text{Total Fixed Costs}}{\text{Selling Price Per Unit - Variable Cost Per Unit}}
    $$
*   **Reference:** CO4 mentions using break-even analysis for business problems.

#### 5.4. Life Cycle Costing (LCC)

*   **Purpose:** To consider all costs associated with a product or system over its entire lifespan, from acquisition to disposal. This provides a more holistic view of value and cost than just initial purchase price.
*   **Components:** Design costs, manufacturing costs, operating costs, maintenance costs, disposal costs.
*   **Reference:** Thuesen & Fabrycky (1966) and Park (2001) emphasize the importance of LCC in engineering economics for long-term decision-making.

### 6. Cost Reduction and Value Improvement Strategies

#### 6.1. Material Substitution

*   Replacing a high-cost material with a lower-cost material that performs the same function adequately.
*   *Example:* Replacing a machined metal part with a molded plastic part if strength and temperature resistance requirements are met.

#### 6.2. Design Simplification

*   Reducing the number of parts, complexity of assembly, or manufacturing steps.
*   *Example:* Designing a product with fewer fasteners or integrating multiple components into a single part.

#### 6.3. Manufacturing Process Improvement

*   Adopting more efficient manufacturing techniques, automation, or process optimization.
*   *Example:* Switching from batch processing to continuous flow manufacturing.

#### 6.4. Standardization

*   Using standard parts and components rather than custom-designed ones.
*   *Example:* Using standard bolts and screws instead of custom-made ones.

#### 6.5. Supplier Negotiation and Sourcing

*   Finding alternative suppliers, negotiating better prices, or exploring different sourcing strategies.

#### 6.6. Packaging Optimization

*   Reducing packaging material, size, or weight without compromising product protection.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:** Define Value in the context of Value Analysis and Value Engineering. How can value be increased? (Knowledge Level: K2, Aligns with CO4)

**Answer:** Value is defined as the ratio of Function to Cost: $V = F/C$. Value can be increased by:
*   Increasing Function while keeping Cost constant.
*   Decreasing Cost while keeping Function constant.
*   Increasing Function and decreasing Cost simultaneously.
*   Increasing Function more than Cost increases.
*   Decreasing Cost more than Function decreases.

**Question 2:** Differentiate between Value Analysis and Value Engineering. (Knowledge Level: K2, Aligns with CO4)

**Answer:**
*   **Value Analysis (VA):** Applied to *existing* products/services/processes, typically after design and during production. Aims to reduce costs and improve value of current offerings.
*   **Value Engineering (VE):** Applied to *new* products/services *during the design phase*. Aims to incorporate maximum value from the outset and prevent future unnecessary costs.

**Question 3:** A company is considering a new design for a component. The current design has a manufacturing cost of $50 per unit and performs its function adequately. A proposed design change would reduce the manufacturing cost to $40 per unit but might slightly reduce its lifespan by 10%. The primary function is critical.
a) Calculate the initial value of the current design, assuming its functional performance is rated at 10 units.
b) Calculate the potential value of the proposed design change, assuming the functional performance is rated at 9 units (a 10% reduction).
c) Based on the value calculation, would you recommend the change? Justify your answer. (Knowledge Level: K3, Aligns with CO4)

**Answer:**
Let Function be represented by a score out of 10.
Initial Functional Score = 10.
Current Cost = $50.

a) **Value of current design:**
$V_{\text{current}} = \frac{\text{Function}}{\text{Cost}} = \frac{10}{50} = 0.2$

Proposed Functional Score = 9 (10% reduction).
Proposed Cost = $40.

b) **Value of proposed design:**
$V_{\text{proposed}} = \frac{\text{Function}}{\text{Cost}} = \frac{9}{40} = 0.225$

c) **Recommendation:** Yes, the proposed design change should be recommended. The value of the proposed design (0.225) is higher than the value of the current design (0.2). This indicates that the reduction in cost is proportionally greater than the reduction in function, leading to an overall improvement in value.

**Question 4:** Explain the importance of the Information Phase in the VA/VE Job Plan. Name at least three types of information that should be gathered. (Knowledge Level: K2, Aligns with CO4)

**Answer:** The Information Phase is crucial because it forms the foundation for all subsequent analysis. Without comprehensive and accurate data, the VA/VE effort is unlikely to identify meaningful improvements or make sound decisions. Key information to gather includes:
*   **Cost Data:** Material costs, labor costs, overheads, tooling costs.
*   **Performance Specifications:** Technical requirements, quality standards, reliability targets, operating conditions.
*   **Design Details:** Drawings, material specifications, manufacturing processes, assembly methods.
*   **Customer Requirements:** User needs, expectations, market trends.
*   **Production Volume:** Current and projected production quantities.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Value = Function / Cost:** This is the core equation driving VA/VE.
*   **Focus on Function:** Always understand *what* a product or process does before evaluating *how* it does it or *what* it costs.
*   **Teamwork:** VA/VE is most effective when conducted by a multidisciplinary team.
*   **Systematic Approach:** The structured job plan (Information, Function, Creative, Evaluation, Development, Presentation, Implementation) ensures a thorough analysis.
*   **Cost is not the only factor:** While cost reduction is a primary goal, it must be balanced with maintaining or improving function, quality, and reliability.
*   **VA for existing, VE for new:** Understand the timing difference.
*   **Quantify whenever possible:** Use data to support analysis and decision-making.
