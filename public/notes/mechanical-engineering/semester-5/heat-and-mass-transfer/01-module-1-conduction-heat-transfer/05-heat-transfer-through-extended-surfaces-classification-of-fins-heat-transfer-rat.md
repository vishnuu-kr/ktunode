# HEAT AND MASS TRANSFER - Module 1: Conduction Heat Transfer

## Topic: Heat Transfer Through Extended Surfaces (Fins)

### 1. Introduction to Extended Surfaces (Fins)

Extended surfaces, commonly known as fins, are one-dimensional heat transfer surfaces used to enhance the rate of heat transfer between a solid surface and a surrounding fluid. They are attached to a primary surface to increase its effective surface area for convection and/or radiation.

**Why use fins?**

*   **To increase heat transfer rate:** When the convection heat transfer coefficient ($h$) is low, or when the available surface area is limited, fins can significantly boost the amount of heat transferred. This is particularly useful in applications where compact and efficient heat transfer is desired.
*   **To reduce the temperature difference:** By increasing the surface area, fins allow for more effective heat dissipation, leading to a lower temperature of the primary surface.

**Examples of Fins in Engineering:**

*   **Engine Cylinder Fins:** To dissipate heat generated by the engine to the surrounding air.
*   **Heat Exchanger Fins:** To increase the heat transfer between fluids in heat exchangers (e.g., radiators, air conditioning coils).
*   **Electronics Cooling:** Fins on CPU heat sinks to dissipate heat from the processor.
*   **Boiler Tubes:** Fins on boiler tubes to enhance heat transfer from combustion gases to water.

**Key Concept:** The primary mechanism of heat transfer from a fin to the surrounding fluid is **convection**. However, heat is conducted along the fin from the base to its tip.

### 2. Classification of Fins

Fins can be classified based on various criteria:

**2.1. Based on Geometry:**

*   **Rectangular Fin:** A fin with a uniform rectangular cross-section.
    *   *Textbook Reference:* Sachdeva (6th ed., 2022), Nag (3rd ed., 2011), Cengel & Ghajar (6th ed., 2020)
*   **Pin Fin (Circular Cross-section):** A fin with a circular cross-section, often used for spot cooling.
    *   *Textbook Reference:* Sachdeva (6th ed., 2022), Nag (3rd ed., 2011), Cengel & Ghajar (6th ed., 2020)
*   **Spine Fin:** Similar to pin fins but typically longer and slender.
*   **Annular Fin:** A fin that is attached around a cylindrical surface, like fins on a pipe.
    *   *Textbook Reference:* Sachdeva (6th ed., 2022), Nag (3rd ed., 2011), Cengel & Ghajar (6th ed., 2020)
*   **Trapezoidal Fin:** Fins with a trapezoidal cross-section, which can offer improved efficiency compared to uniform fins.
*   **Parabolic Fin:** Fins with a parabolic profile, theoretically offering optimal performance.

**2.2. Based on Material:**

*   **Homogeneous Fins:** Made of a single material (e.g., aluminum, copper, cast iron).
*   **Composite Fins:** Made of multiple materials with different thermal conductivities.

**2.3. Based on Heat Flow Direction:**

*   **Unilateral Fins:** Fins attached to only one side of a primary surface.
*   **Bilateral Fins:** Fins attached to both sides of a primary surface.

**2.4. Based on Transverse Fin Area Variation:**

*   **Straight Fins:** Fins that extend perpendicularly from the base.
*   **Curved Fins:** Fins that have a curvature.
*   **Transverse Fins:** Fins that are perpendicular to the primary surface.

**2.5. Based on Heat Transfer Direction (Relative to Fin Base):**

*   **Fin Attachment:** Where the fin is attached to a primary surface.
*   **Fin Base:** The surface where the fin is attached.
*   **Fin Surface:** The exposed surfaces of the fin where convection occurs.
*   **Fin Tip:** The free end of the fin.

**Important Note:** The most common types of fins analyzed in introductory heat transfer are **straight fins of uniform cross-section** (rectangular and pin fins) and **annular fins**.

### 3. Heat Transfer Rate from a Fin

To determine the heat transfer rate from a fin, we need to analyze the temperature distribution along its length. This involves applying the principles of one-dimensional conduction along the fin and convection from its surface.

**3.1. Governing Differential Equation:**

Consider a small elemental volume of a fin with thickness $dx$. Assuming one-dimensional conduction along the length ($x$) and steady-state conditions:

*   **Heat conducted into the element at $x$:** $q_x = -k A_c \frac{dT}{dx}$
*   **Heat conducted out of the element at $x+dx$:** $q_{x+dx} = -k A_c \frac{dT}{dx} + \frac{d}{dx}(-k A_c \frac{dT}{dx}) dx$
*   **Net heat conducted into the element:** $q_{cond,net} = q_x - q_{x+dx} = k A_c \frac{d^2T}{dx^2} dx$
*   **Heat convected from the surface of the element:** $q_{conv} = h P dx (T - T_\infty)$
    *   $h$: Convection heat transfer coefficient.
    *   $P$: Fin perimeter.
    *   $dx$: Elemental length.
    *   $T$: Temperature of the fin at $x$.
    *   $T_\infty$: Temperature of the surrounding fluid.

For steady-state, the net heat conducted into the element must equal the heat convected from it:

$k A_c \frac{d^2T}{dx^2} dx = h P dx (T - T_\infty)$

$\frac{d^2T}{dx^2} - \frac{hP}{kA_c} (T - T_\infty) = 0$

Let $\theta = T - T_\infty$. Then $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{dT}{dx}$ and $\frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2} = \frac{d^2T}{dx^2}$.
The equation becomes:

$\frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2} - m^2 \theta = 0$

where $m^2 = \frac{hP}{kA_c}$. This is a second-order linear homogeneous differential equation with constant coefficients.

The general solution is: $\theta(x) = C_1 e^{mx} + C_2 e^{-mx}$
Or, in hyperbolic form: $\theta(x) = C_3 \cosh(mx) + C_4 \sinh(mx)$

**3.2. Heat Transfer Rate from a Rectangular Fin (Uniform Cross-section):**

Consider a rectangular fin of length $L$, base temperature $T_b$, and width $w$ and thickness $\delta$.
*   Cross-sectional area ($A_c$) = $w \delta$
*   Fin perimeter ($P$) = $2(w + \delta)$
*   For thin fins ($w \gg \delta$), $P \approx 2w$.
*   $m^2 = \frac{hP}{kA_c} = \frac{h(2w)}{k(w\delta)} = \frac{2h}{k\delta}$ (for thin fins)

**Boundary Conditions:**

The solution depends on the boundary conditions at the fin tip.

**Case 1: Infinitely Long Fin ($L \rightarrow \infty$)**

*   **Condition:** $T(L) = T_\infty$ or $\theta(L) = 0$ as $L \rightarrow \infty$.
*   **Solution:** For $\theta(x) = C_1 e^{mx} + C_2 e^{-mx}$, as $x \rightarrow \infty$, $\theta(x)$ must remain finite. This implies $C_1 = 0$. So, $\theta(x) = C_2 e^{-mx}$.
*   At the base ($x=0$), $\theta(0) = T_b - T_\infty = \theta_b$.
*   Therefore, $\theta_b = C_2 e^0 = C_2$.
*   So, $\theta(x) = \theta_b e^{-mx}$.
*   **Heat transfer rate ($q_{fin}$):**
    $q_{fin} = -k A_c \frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=0}$
    $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = -m \theta_b e^{-mx}$
    $q_{fin} = -k A_c (-m \theta_b e^0) = k A_c m \theta_b$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty)$

**Case 2: Fin with Adiabatic Tip (Negligible Heat Transfer from the Tip)**

*   **Condition:** $\frac{dT}{dx}|_{x=L} = 0$ or $\frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=L} = 0$.
*   **Solution:** Using $\theta(x) = C_3 \cosh(mx) + C_4 \sinh(mx)$.
    *   At the base ($x=0$), $\theta(0) = \theta_b$. So, $\theta_b = C_3 \cosh(0) + C_4 \sinh(0) = C_3$.
    *   Thus, $\theta(x) = \theta_b \cosh(mx) + C_4 \sinh(mx)$.
    *   Differentiating: $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = m \theta_b \sinh(mx) + m C_4 \cosh(mx)$.
    *   At the tip ($x=L$), $\frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=L} = 0$.
    *   $0 = m \theta_b \sinh(mL) + m C_4 \cosh(mL)$
    *   $C_4 = -\theta_b \frac{\sinh(mL)}{\cosh(mL)} = -\theta_b \tanh(mL)$.
    *   So, $\theta(x) = \theta_b \left(\cosh(mx) - \tanh(mL) \sinh(mx)\right)$.
*   **Heat transfer rate ($q_{fin}$):**
    $q_{fin} = -k A_c \frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=0}$
    $\frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=0} = m \theta_b \sinh(0) + m C_4 \cosh(0) = m C_4$
    $q_{fin} = -k A_c (m C_4) = -k A_c m (-\theta_b \tanh(mL))$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} \theta_b \tanh(mL)$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty) \tanh(mL)$

**Case 3: Fin with Convective Tip**

*   **Condition:** Heat conducted to the tip equals heat convected from the tip.
    $-k A_c \frac{dT}{dx}|_{x=L} = h A_{tip} (T(L) - T_\infty)$
    $-k A_c \frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=L} = h A_{tip} \theta(L)$
    where $A_{tip}$ is the cross-sectional area of the fin tip.
*   **Solution:** This leads to more complex solutions involving hyperbolic functions with modified arguments.
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} \theta_b \frac{\sinh(mL) + (h/mk) \cosh(mL)}{\cosh(mL) + (h/mk) \sinh(mL)}$
    This can be rewritten in terms of $m_{tan} = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}} \frac{h + mk}{mk + h}$
    or by defining an effective length $L_e = L + \frac{A_c}{P}$. This approximation simplifies the heat transfer rate to the form of an adiabatic tip with $L_e$ instead of $L$.

**3.3. Heat Transfer Rate from a Pin Fin (Circular Cross-section):**

For a pin fin of diameter $D$, radius $r_o = D/2$, and length $L$:
*   Cross-sectional area ($A_c$) = $\pi r_o^2 = \frac{\pi D^2}{4}$
*   Fin perimeter ($P$) = $2 \pi r_o = \pi D$
*   $m^2 = \frac{hP}{kA_c} = \frac{h(\pi D)}{k(\pi D^2/4)} = \frac{4h}{kD}$

The formulas for heat transfer rate are the same as for rectangular fins, with $m$ calculated using the pin fin parameters.

**Summary of Heat Transfer Rate formulas:**

| Fin Type / Tip Condition     | Heat Transfer Rate ($q_{fin}$)                                                                                             |
| :--------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Infinitely Long Fin          | $\sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty)$                                                                                         |
| Adiabatic Tip                | $\sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty) \tanh(mL)$                                                                               |
| Convective Tip (Approximation using $L_e$) | $\sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty) \tanh(mL_e)$, where $L_e = L + A_c/P$ (effective length)                     |
| Convective Tip (Exact)       | $\sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty) \frac{\sinh(mL) + (h/mk) \cosh(mL)}{\cosh(mL) + (h/mk) \sinh(mL)}$                               |

**Important Point:** The term $\sqrt{hPk A_c}$ represents the heat transfer rate from a fin of infinite length.

### 4. Boundary Conditions for Fins

The solution to the fin differential equation $\frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2} - m^2 \theta = 0$ depends critically on the conditions specified at the fin's boundaries (base and tip).

**4.1. Fin Base:**

*   **Prescribed Temperature:** The most common condition is that the fin base is at a uniform temperature $T_b$.
    *   $\theta(0) = T_b - T_\infty = \theta_b$.

**4.2. Fin Tip:**

*   **Adiabatic Tip (Insulated Tip):** Assumes no heat transfer from the fin tip. This is often a good approximation for short, thick fins where the tip area is small compared to the side surface area, or when the tip is covered by insulation.
    *   $\frac{dT}{dx}|_{x=L} = 0 \implies \frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=L} = 0$.
*   **Infinitely Long Fin:** Assumes the temperature at the tip is equal to the fluid temperature. This is useful for very long fins where the temperature approaches the ambient temperature well before the tip.
    *   $\theta(L) = 0$.
*   **Convective Tip:** Accounts for heat transfer by convection from the fin tip to the surrounding fluid. This is the most realistic condition but leads to more complex solutions.
    *   $-k A_c \frac{dT}{dx}|_{x=L} = h A_{tip} (T(L) - T_\infty)$
    *   $-k A_c \frac{d\theta}{dx}|_{x=L} = h A_{tip} \theta(L)$.

**4.3. Other Boundary Conditions (Less Common for Introductory Analysis):**

*   **Radiation from the Tip:** If radiation is significant from the fin tip.
*   **Heat Transfer to a Second Fluid at the Tip:** In certain heat exchanger configurations.

**Example:** Consider a rectangular fin with length $L=0.05$ m, width $w=0.1$ m, thickness $\delta=0.002$ m, $T_b=100^\circ\text{C}$, $T_\infty=25^\circ\text{C}$, $h=50 \text{ W/m}^2\text{K}$, and $k=200 \text{ W/mK}$. Calculate the heat transfer rate assuming an adiabatic tip.

*   $A_c = w \times \delta = 0.1 \times 0.002 = 0.0002 \text{ m}^2$
*   $P = 2(w + \delta) = 2(0.1 + 0.002) = 0.204 \text{ m}$
*   $m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}} = \sqrt{\frac{50 \times 0.204}{200 \times 0.0002}} = \sqrt{\frac{10.2}{0.04}} = \sqrt{255} \approx 15.97 \text{ m}^{-1}$
*   $mL = 15.97 \times 0.05 = 0.7985$
*   $\theta_b = T_b - T_\infty = 100 - 25 = 75^\circ\text{C}$
*   $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} \theta_b \tanh(mL)$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{50 \times 0.204 \times 200 \times 0.0002} \times 75 \times \tanh(0.7985)$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{4.08} \times 75 \times 0.6647$
    $q_{fin} = 2.02 \times 75 \times 0.6647 \approx 100.9 \text{ W}$

### 5. Fin Effectiveness and Fin Efficiency

These two parameters are crucial for evaluating the performance and economic viability of using fins.

**5.1. Fin Effectiveness ($\epsilon_{fin}$)**

Fin effectiveness is the ratio of the heat transfer rate from the finned surface to the heat transfer rate from the same surface if it were not extended (i.e., without fins).

$\epsilon_{fin} = \frac{\text{Heat transfer rate with fin}}{\text{Heat transfer rate without fin}}$

*   **Heat transfer rate without fin:** This is simply the convection from the base area ($A_b$) of the primary surface.
    $q_{no \, fin} = h A_b (T_b - T_\infty)$
*   **Heat transfer rate with fin:** This is the total heat transferred from the fin and the base.
    $q_{fin \, surface} = q_{fin} + q_{base \, convection}$
    The heat transferred from the base area not covered by fins ($A_{base, bare}$) is $h A_{base, bare} (T_b - T_\infty)$.
    However, often the primary concern is the *enhancement* provided by the fins relative to the heat transfer from the primary surface *as if it were bare*. In this context, we often consider the heat transfer from the fin base area $A_b$.

    If the fins are attached to a surface of area $A_b$, and the fins themselves have a surface area $A_{fin}$, the total heat transfer from the finned surface is $q_{total} = q_{fin} + h A_{base, bare}(T_b - T_\infty)$.
    A common definition for effectiveness compares the heat transfer *from the fin* to the heat transfer *from the base area occupied by the fin if it were bare*.

    $\epsilon_{fin} = \frac{q_{fin}}{h A_b (T_b - T_\infty)}$

    Where $A_b$ is the cross-sectional area at the base of the fin.
    *   For a rectangular fin attached to a flat surface: $A_b = w \delta$.
    *   For a pin fin attached to a flat surface: $A_b = \frac{\pi D^2}{4}$.
    *   For an annular fin attached to a pipe: $A_b$ is the area of the pipe section it's attached to.

*   **Interpretation:**
    *   $\epsilon_{fin} > 1$: The fins increase the heat transfer rate.
    *   $\epsilon_{fin} = 1$: The fins do not change the heat transfer rate.
    *   $\epsilon_{fin} < 1$: The fins decrease the heat transfer rate (undesirable).

*   **Factors affecting Fin Effectiveness:**
    *   **Fin Material Thermal Conductivity ($k$):** Higher $k$ leads to higher effectiveness.
    *   **Convection Heat Transfer Coefficient ($h$):** Higher $h$ leads to lower effectiveness.
    *   **Fin Geometry (Length, Area, Perimeter):** Longer fins or fins with larger surface area relative to their cross-sectional area generally have higher effectiveness.
    *   **Temperature Difference ($T_b - T_\infty$):** Effectiveness is generally independent of temperature difference.

*   **General observation:** Fins are most effective when $h$ is low and $k$ is high. For very high $h$ (e.g., in boiling or condensation), the primary surface itself is very effective, and adding fins might not be beneficial.

**5.2. Fin Efficiency ($\eta_{fin}$)**

Fin efficiency is the ratio of the actual heat transfer rate from a fin to the maximum possible heat transfer rate from the fin if the entire fin were at the base temperature.

$\eta_{fin} = \frac{\text{Actual heat transfer rate from fin}}{\text{Maximum possible heat transfer rate from fin}}$

$\eta_{fin} = \frac{q_{fin}}{h A_{fin} (T_b - T_\infty)}$

Where $A_{fin}$ is the total surface area of the fin available for convection.

*   **Maximum possible heat transfer rate:** This occurs if the entire fin surface is at the base temperature ($T_b$), meaning the temperature gradient along the fin is zero, leading to pure convection.
*   **Interpretation:**
    *   $\eta_{fin} = 1$: The entire fin is at the base temperature (ideal, but unrealistic).
    *   $\eta_{fin} < 1$: The fin temperature decreases along its length due to conduction resistance, reducing convective heat transfer.

*   **Calculation of $\eta_{fin}$ for Adiabatic Tip Fin:**
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty) \tanh(mL)$
    $A_{fin} = P L$ (for straight fins of uniform cross-section)
    $\eta_{fin} = \frac{\sqrt{hPk A_c} (T_b - T_\infty) \tanh(mL)}{h (PL) (T_b - T_\infty)}$
    $\eta_{fin} = \frac{\sqrt{hPk A_c}}{h P L} \tanh(mL)$
    $\eta_{fin} = \frac{1}{PL} \sqrt{\frac{k A_c}{h P}} \tanh(mL)$
    $\eta_{fin} = \frac{1}{mL} \tanh(mL)$

    Here, $m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}}$.

*   **Fin Efficiency for other boundary conditions:** Similar expressions can be derived for other boundary conditions, often involving modified arguments for the hyperbolic functions.

*   **Factors affecting Fin Efficiency:**
    *   **Fin Geometry:** Longer fins and fins with smaller $P/A_c$ ratios (e.g., thick fins) have lower efficiency.
    *   **Material Thermal Conductivity ($k$):** Higher $k$ leads to higher efficiency.
    *   **Convection Heat Transfer Coefficient ($h$):** Higher $h$ leads to lower efficiency.

**Relationship between Effectiveness and Efficiency:**

$q_{fin} = \eta_{fin} h A_{fin} (T_b - T_\infty)$
$\epsilon_{fin} = \frac{q_{fin}}{h A_b (T_b - T_\infty)} = \frac{\eta_{fin} h A_{fin} (T_b - T_\infty)}{h A_b (T_b - T_\infty)} = \eta_{fin} \frac{A_{fin}}{A_b}$

So, $\epsilon_{fin} = \eta_{fin} \frac{A_{fin}}{A_b}$. This shows that effectiveness depends on both efficiency and the ratio of fin surface area to base area.

**Important Point:** Fin efficiency typically decreases with increasing fin length ($L$) and fin parameter ($mL$). It is also affected by the fin's cross-sectional shape and aspect ratio.

**Graphical Representation:** Fin efficiency is often presented in charts as a function of $mL$ and fin geometry (e.g., aspect ratio $L/D$ for pin fins or $L/\sqrt{A_c}/P$ for rectangular fins). These charts are useful for design purposes.

*   *Textbook Reference:* Sachdeva (6th ed., 2022), Nag (3rd ed., 2011), Cengel & Ghajar (6th ed., 2020) provide these charts.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
Explain why fins are used to enhance heat transfer and under what conditions they are most effective.

**Answer:** Fins are used to increase the surface area available for convection, thereby increasing the heat transfer rate from a primary surface to a surrounding fluid. They are most effective when the convection heat transfer coefficient ($h$) is low and the thermal conductivity of the fin material ($k$) is high.

**Question 2 (Calculation - Rectangular Fin):**
A rectangular fin of thermal conductivity $k = 200 \text{ W/mK}$ has a length of $L = 10 \text{ cm}$, a width of $w = 5 \text{ cm}$, and a thickness of $\delta = 2 \text{ mm}$. The fin is attached to a surface at $T_b = 150^\circ\text{C}$ and is exposed to air at $T_\infty = 30^\circ\text{C}$ with a convection heat transfer coefficient of $h = 40 \text{ W/m}^2\text{K}$. Calculate the heat transfer rate from the fin, assuming an adiabatic tip.

**Solution:**
*   $L = 0.1 \text{ m}$, $w = 0.05 \text{ m}$, $\delta = 0.002 \text{ m}$
*   $A_c = w \times \delta = 0.05 \times 0.002 = 0.0001 \text{ m}^2$
*   $P = 2(w + \delta) = 2(0.05 + 0.002) = 0.104 \text{ m}$
*   $m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}} = \sqrt{\frac{40 \times 0.104}{200 \times 0.0001}} = \sqrt{\frac{4.16}{0.02}} = \sqrt{208} \approx 14.42 \text{ m}^{-1}$
*   $mL = 14.42 \times 0.1 = 1.442$
*   $\theta_b = T_b - T_\infty = 150 - 30 = 120^\circ\text{C}$
*   $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} \theta_b \tanh(mL)$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{40 \times 0.104 \times 200 \times 0.0001} \times 120 \times \tanh(1.442)$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{0.832} \times 120 \times 0.8946$
    $q_{fin} = 0.912 \times 120 \times 0.8946 \approx 97.7 \text{ W}$

**Question 3 (Calculation - Fin Efficiency):**
For the fin in Question 2, calculate the fin efficiency.

**Solution:**
*   Using the formula $\eta_{fin} = \frac{\tanh(mL)}{mL}$ for an adiabatic tip fin.
*   $mL = 1.442$
*   $\eta_{fin} = \frac{\tanh(1.442)}{1.442} = \frac{0.8946}{1.442} \approx 0.620$ or 62.0%

**Question 4 (Calculation - Fin Effectiveness):**
For the fin in Question 2, calculate the fin effectiveness.

**Solution:**
*   First, calculate the heat transfer rate from the base area if it were bare:
    $A_b = w \times \delta = 0.0001 \text{ m}^2$ (This is the area occupied by the fin at the base)
    $q_{no \, fin} = h A_b (T_b - T_\infty) = 40 \times 0.0001 \times 120 = 0.48 \text{ W}$
*   However, the definition of effectiveness commonly compares the heat transfer from the fin to the heat transfer from the base area *of the primary surface* that the fin is attached to. If the fin is attached to a larger surface, $A_b$ would be the area of that attachment. If the entire surface is finned, then $A_b$ is effectively the total surface area.
    Let's assume the fin is attached to a larger surface, and the "base area" referred to in the effectiveness definition is the area *of the primary surface* where the fin is attached. For a rectangular fin of width $w$ and thickness $\delta$ attached to a surface, the area occupied by the fin is $A_b = w \times \delta$. This seems small.
    A more common interpretation for a fin attached to a primary surface of area $A_{primary}$ where the fin is attached is:
    $\epsilon_{fin} = \frac{q_{fin}}{h A_{primary} (T_b - T_\infty)}$.
    If the fin is a sole protruding element, then $A_b$ refers to its base cross-sectional area.
    Let's use the definition $\epsilon_{fin} = \eta_{fin} \frac{A_{fin}}{A_b}$.
    $A_{fin} = P \times L = 0.104 \times 0.1 = 0.0104 \text{ m}^2$
    $A_b = A_c = 0.0001 \text{ m}^2$
    $\epsilon_{fin} = 0.620 \times \frac{0.0104}{0.0001} = 0.620 \times 104 = 64.48$

    Let's re-evaluate the definition of effectiveness for a fin attached to a primary surface. The heat transfer from the *finned surface* is $q_{finned} = q_{fin} + h A_{base, bare} (T_b - T_\infty)$.
    Effectiveness is the ratio of heat transfer from the finned surface to the heat transfer from the *same surface without fins*. If the entire surface were bare, the heat transfer would be $h A_{total} (T_b - T_\infty)$. If the surface is partially finned, and $A_b$ is the base area of the fin, then the total heat transfer from the finned surface is $q_{fin} + h (A_{total} - A_b)(T_b - T_\infty)$.
    The simplest definition for $\epsilon_{fin}$ is usually comparing the heat transfer from the fin itself to the heat transfer from the base area occupied by the fin if it were bare:
    $\epsilon_{fin} = \frac{q_{fin}}{h A_b (T_b - T_\infty)}$
    $q_{fin} = 97.7 \text{ W}$
    $h A_b (T_b - T_\infty) = 40 \times 0.0001 \times 120 = 0.48 \text{ W}$
    $\epsilon_{fin} = \frac{97.7}{0.48} \approx 203.5$

    This large value of effectiveness indicates that the fin transfers significantly more heat than the base area alone would. This is the intended use of fins. The definition $\epsilon_{fin} = \eta_{fin} \frac{A_{fin}}{A_b}$ is also valid and yields a large number because $A_{fin}$ is much larger than $A_b$.

**Question 5 (Calculation - Pin Fin):**
A $5 \text{ cm}$ long pin fin with a diameter of $5 \text{ mm}$ is attached to a surface at $200^\circ\text{C}$ and is exposed to air at $25^\circ\text{C}$. The thermal conductivity of the fin material is $k = 150 \text{ W/mK}$, and the convection coefficient is $h = 60 \text{ W/m}^2\text{K}$. Calculate the heat transfer rate from the pin fin, assuming an adiabatic tip.

**Solution:**
*   $L = 0.05 \text{ m}$, $D = 0.005 \text{ m}$
*   $A_c = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (0.005)^2}{4} = 1.963 \times 10^{-5} \text{ m}^2$
*   $P = \pi D = \pi (0.005) = 0.0157 \text{ m}$
*   $m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}} = \sqrt{\frac{60 \times 0.0157}{150 \times 1.963 \times 10^{-5}}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.942}{0.00294}} = \sqrt{320.4} \approx 17.9 \text{ m}^{-1}$
*   $mL = 17.9 \times 0.05 = 0.895$
*   $\theta_b = T_b - T_\infty = 200 - 25 = 175^\circ\text{C}$
*   $q_{fin} = \sqrt{hPk A_c} \theta_b \tanh(mL)$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{60 \times 0.0157 \times 150 \times 1.963 \times 10^{-5}} \times 175 \times \tanh(0.895)$
    $q_{fin} = \sqrt{0.279} \times 175 \times 0.714$
    $q_{fin} = 0.528 \times 175 \times 0.714 \approx 66.0 \text{ W}$

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Fins enhance heat transfer** by increasing the surface area for convection.
*   The **fundamental equation for fin analysis** is $\frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2} - m^2 \theta = 0$, where $m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}}$.
*   The **boundary conditions** at the fin tip (adiabatic, convective, infinite) significantly affect the heat transfer rate.
*   **Fin effectiveness ($\epsilon_{fin}$)** compares the heat transfer with fins to the heat transfer without fins from the same area. Fins are effective when $\epsilon_{fin} > 1$.
*   **Fin efficiency ($\eta_{fin}$)** represents how well the fin is performing compared to an ideal fin at base temperature. High efficiency means the fin temperature does not drop too rapidly.
*   Fins are most beneficial when the convection coefficient ($h$) is low and the fin material's thermal conductivity ($k$) is high.
*   For optimization, a balance must be struck between fin size, material, and density to achieve desired heat transfer rates without excessive cost or weight.
*   The charts for fin efficiency are valuable tools in practical fin design.
