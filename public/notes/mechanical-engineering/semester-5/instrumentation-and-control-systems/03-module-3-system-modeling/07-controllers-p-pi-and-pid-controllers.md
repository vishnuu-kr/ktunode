# Instrumentation and Control Systems - Module 3: System Modeling

## Topic: Controllers - P, PI, and PID Controllers

### 1. Introduction to Control Systems and Controllers

**Course Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO4 (K3):** To describe the role of various control blocks and components in feedback systems.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Control System:** A system that manages, commands, directs, or regulates the behavior of other devices or systems.
*   **Feedback Control System:** A system where the output is measured and compared to a desired setpoint, and the difference (error) is used to adjust the input to minimize the error.
*   **Controller:** The "brain" of a feedback control system. It receives the error signal and generates a control output to manipulate the plant (the system being controlled) to achieve the desired setpoint.
*   **Plant:** The physical system or process that is being controlled (e.g., a motor, a furnace, a tank).
*   **Setpoint (Reference Input):** The desired value for the controlled variable.
*   **Controlled Variable:** The variable that is being measured and controlled (e.g., temperature, speed, pressure).
*   **Error Signal ($e(t)$):** The difference between the setpoint and the measured controlled variable: $e(t) = \text{Setpoint} - \text{Measured Variable}$.
*   **Control Output ($u(t)$):** The signal generated by the controller that is sent to the actuator, which then affects the plant.

**Role of the Controller (CO4):**
The controller's primary role is to reduce the error between the desired setpoint and the actual measured output of the plant. It does this by intelligently processing the error signal and generating an appropriate control action. Different types of controllers (P, PI, PID) employ different algorithms to achieve this, each with its own strengths and weaknesses in terms of speed, accuracy, and stability.

**Reference:**
*   Doebelin, E. O. (1990). *Measurement Systems: Application and Design*. McGraw-Hill Publishing Company. (Provides foundational understanding of measurement and control principles).
*   Nise, N. S. (2004). *Control Systems Engineering*. Wiley Eastern. (Chapter 1 introduces feedback control and the role of controllers).

### 2. Proportional (P) Controller

**Course Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO1 (K2):** To get basic knowledge about industrial measurement system and different elements involved in it. (Controllers are key elements).
*   **CO4 (K3):** To describe the role of various control blocks and components in feedback systems. (Understanding P controller's role).

**Key Concepts & Definition:**
*   **Proportional Control:** The control output is directly proportional to the error signal.
*   **Proportional Gain ($K_p$):** A tuning parameter that determines the magnitude of the control output for a given error. A higher $K_p$ results in a larger control action for the same error.

**Mathematical Representation:**
$u(t) = K_p e(t)$

**Block Diagram:**
```
      +-----+      +-------+      +-----+
e(t) ---->| Sum |----->|  Kp   |----->| u(t) |-----> Plant ---> Output
      ^     +-----+      +-------+      +-----+
      |                                     ^
      |-------------------------------------| Measurement
```

**Advantages:**
*   **Simplicity:** Easy to understand and implement.
*   **Responsiveness:** Provides a control action as soon as an error occurs.

**Disadvantages:**
*   **Steady-State Error (Offset):** A major limitation. For a constant disturbance or a change in setpoint, the proportional controller will typically settle at an output that is not zero even when the error is small, leading to a persistent difference between the setpoint and the actual output. This is because a non-zero error is required to maintain a non-zero control output.
*   **Limited Performance:** May not achieve the desired setpoint accurately.
*   **Sensitivity to Noise:** High $K_p$ can amplify noise in the measurement signal, leading to erratic control action.

**Example:**
Consider a temperature control system for a room. If the setpoint is 22°C and the current temperature is 20°C (error = 2°C), a P controller with $K_p = 5$ would output $u(t) = 5 \times 2 = 10$ units of heating power. If the temperature rises to 21°C (error = 1°C), the output would be $u(t) = 5 \times 1 = 5$ units of heating power. To maintain exactly 22°C, the error needs to be zero, which would result in zero heating power. This might not be enough to counteract heat loss and keep the temperature at 22°C, leading to a steady-state error (e.g., the temperature might settle at 21.5°C).

**Tuning:**
*   Increasing $K_p$ generally reduces steady-state error but can lead to oscillations and instability.
*   Decreasing $K_p$ reduces oscillations but increases steady-state error.

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   P-only control is often insufficient for applications requiring precise setpoint tracking.
*   Steady-state error is an inherent characteristic of P-only control.

**Reference:**
*   Krishnaswamy, K. (2003). *Industrial Instrumentation*. New Age International Publishers. (Discusses basic controller types).
*   Ogata, K. (2002). *Modern Control Engineering*. Prentice Hall of India. (Provides detailed analysis of controller actions).

### 3. Proportional-Integral (PI) Controller

**Course Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO4 (K3):** To describe the role of various control blocks and components in feedback systems. (Understanding PI controller's role).
*   **CO6 (K4):** Analyse the stability of the given LTI system. (PI controllers can impact stability).

**Key Concepts & Definition:**
*   **Integral Control:** The control output is proportional to the integral of the error signal over time.
*   **Integral Gain ($K_i$) or Integral Time ($T_i$):** Tuning parameters.
    *   $K_i$ is directly proportional to the integral action.
    *   $T_i$ (Integral Time or Reset Time) is the time required for the integral term to match the proportional term for a constant error. A smaller $T_i$ means a faster integral action.
*   **Integral Term:** $\int_0^t e(\tau) d\tau$

**Mathematical Representation:**
$u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau) d\tau$

Alternatively, in terms of $K_p$ and $T_i$:
$u(t) = K_p e(t) + \frac{K_p}{T_i} \int_0^t e(\tau) d\tau$

**Block Diagram:**
```
      +-----+      +-------+      +---------+      +-----+
e(t) ---->| Sum |----->|  Kp   |----->|  + Ki*int |----->| u(t) |-----> Plant ---> Output
      ^     +-----+      +-------+      | integral |      +-----+
      |                                   +---------+         ^
      |-------------------------------------------------------| Measurement
```

**Advantages:**
*   **Eliminates Steady-State Error:** The integral term accumulates error over time. As long as there is an error, the integral term will continue to increase or decrease the control output, driving the error towards zero.
*   **Improves Accuracy:** Achieves better setpoint tracking compared to P-only control.

**Disadvantages:**
*   **Slower Response:** The integral action can slow down the system's response to changes.
*   **Increased Oscillations:** Can cause the system to oscillate more, especially if $K_i$ is too high or $T_i$ is too low.
*   **Windup:** If the controller output saturates (reaches its maximum or minimum limit), the integral term can continue to accumulate error, leading to excessive control action when the system eventually comes out of saturation. This needs to be addressed with anti-windup mechanisms.

**Example:**
Using the same temperature control example:
With a P controller, the temperature might settle at 21.5°C. With a PI controller ($K_p = 5, T_i = 10$ minutes), if the error is 0.5°C, the proportional term is $5 \times 0.5 = 2.5$. The integral term starts accumulating. If the error persists, the integral term will grow, increasing the heating power. Eventually, the integral action will provide enough power to counteract the heat loss, bringing the temperature to exactly 22°C (zero error).

**Tuning:**
*   Increasing $K_p$ improves responsiveness but can increase oscillations.
*   Decreasing $T_i$ (or increasing $K_i$) eliminates steady-state error faster but can cause overshoot and oscillations.
*   Tuning involves balancing the speed of response, steady-state error reduction, and stability.

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   PI controllers are very common in industrial applications because they eliminate steady-state error.
*   Tuning is crucial to avoid excessive oscillations and windup.

**Reference:**
*   Patranabis, D. (2017). *Principles of Industrial Instrumentation*. McGraw-Hill Education. (Provides practical insights into PI controller operation).
*   Kuo, B. C. (1991). *Automatic Control Systems*. Prentice Hall of India. (Detailed mathematical analysis of PI control).

### 4. Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) Controller

**Course Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO4 (K3):** To describe the role of various control blocks and components in feedback systems. (Understanding PID controller's role).
*   **CO5 (K4):** To analyse the time domain responses of the linear systems and apply Root locus technique to assess the performance. (PID controllers significantly alter system dynamics).
*   **CO6 (K4):** Analyse the stability of the given LTI system. (PID tuning is critical for stability).

**Key Concepts & Definition:**
*   **Derivative Control:** The control output is proportional to the rate of change of the error signal.
*   **Derivative Gain ($K_d$) or Derivative Time ($T_d$):** Tuning parameters.
    *   $K_d$ is directly proportional to the derivative action.
    *   $T_d$ (Derivative Time or Rate Time) is the time constant associated with the derivative term. A higher $T_d$ provides a stronger derivative action.
*   **Derivative Term:** $\frac{d e(t)}{d t}$

**Mathematical Representation:**
$u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau) d\tau + K_d \frac{d e(t)}{d t}$

Alternatively, in terms of $K_p$, $T_i$, and $T_d$:
$u(t) = K_p e(t) + \frac{K_p}{T_i} \int_0^t e(\tau) d\tau + K_p T_d \frac{d e(t)}{d t}$

**Block Diagram:**
```
      +-----+      +-------+      +---------+      +---------+      +-----+
e(t) ---->| Sum |----->|  Kp   |----->|  + Ki*int |----->| + Kd*deriv|----->| u(t) |-----> Plant ---> Output
      ^     +-----+      +-------+      | integral |      |  derivative |      +-----+
      |                                   +---------+      +---------+         ^
      |-----------------------------------------------------------------------| Measurement
```

**Advantages:**
*   **Fast Response:** The derivative term anticipates future error by looking at the rate of change. If the error is decreasing rapidly, the derivative action will reduce the control output, preventing overshoot.
*   **Improved Stability:** Can improve system stability by damping oscillations.
*   **Combines Benefits:** Offers the advantages of both P (responsiveness) and I (zero steady-state error) control, plus the added benefit of anticipating future error.

**Disadvantages:**
*   **Sensitivity to Noise:** The derivative term amplifies high-frequency noise in the error signal, leading to erratic control output. This is a significant drawback, and practical PID controllers often filter the derivative term.
*   **Does Not Improve Steady-State Error:** The derivative term is zero when the error is constant, so it does not help in eliminating steady-state error.
*   **Tuning Complexity:** Tuning three parameters ($K_p$, $K_i$, $K_d$) can be challenging.

**Practical Implementation of Derivative Term:**
To mitigate the noise sensitivity, the derivative term is often implemented as:
$u_d(t) = K_d \frac{d e_f(t)}{d t}$, where $e_f(t)$ is a filtered error signal. A common filter is a first-order low-pass filter.

**Example:**
Consider a motor speed control system.
*   **P-only:** Might reach the target speed but with a steady-state error.
*   **PI:** Will reach the target speed precisely, but the response might be a bit slow, and there might be some overshoot.
*   **PID:** With proper tuning, the PID controller can make the motor reach the target speed quickly, with minimal overshoot, and precisely (due to the I term). The D term helps to slow down the controller's output as the speed approaches the setpoint, preventing overshoot.

**Tuning:**
Tuning PID controllers is an iterative process and can be done using various methods:
*   **Manual Tuning:** Adjusting $K_p$, $K_i$, and $K_d$ based on observed system response.
*   **Ziegler-Nichols Methods:** Empirical tuning rules based on system step response.
*   **Internal Model Control (IMC) Tuning:** Model-based tuning.
*   **Auto-tuning features:** Many modern controllers have built-in auto-tuning functions.

**General Tuning Guidelines:**
1.  **Start with P-only:** Tune $K_p$ until the system oscillates with a sustained amplitude. Record this gain as $K_u$ and the oscillation period as $P_u$.
2.  **Tune PI:**
    *   $K_p = 0.45 K_u$
    *   $T_i = P_u / 1.2$
3.  **Tune PID:**
    *   $K_p = 0.6 K_u$
    *   $T_i = P_u / 2$
    *   $T_d = P_u / 8$

**(Note:** These are starting points, and further fine-tuning is usually required.)

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   PID controllers are the workhorses of industrial control due to their versatility.
*   The derivative term's sensitivity to noise necessitates filtering in practical applications.
*   Proper tuning is essential for optimal performance and stability.

**Reference:**
*   Nise, N. S. (2004). *Control Systems Engineering*. Wiley Eastern. (Chapter 8 covers PID controller design and tuning).
*   Gopal, M. (2002). *Control Systems Principles and Design*. Tata McGraw Hill. (Discusses PID controller applications).
*   Varmah, K. R. (2010). *Control Systems*. Tata McGrawHill. (Covers tuning methods).

### 5. Controller Action and Tuning Parameters

**Course Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO1 (K2):** Basic knowledge about system elements.
*   **CO4 (K3):** Role of control blocks.
*   **CO5 (K4):** Time domain analysis.

**Summary of Tuning Parameters:**

| Controller Type | Tuning Parameters      | Primary Effect                                                                                              |
| :-------------- | :--------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **P**           | $K_p$ (Proportional Gain) | Increases speed of response, reduces steady-state error but cannot eliminate it, can cause oscillations. |
| **PI**          | $K_p$, $T_i$ (Integral Time) | $K_p$: Same as P. $T_i$ (or $K_i$): Eliminates steady-state error, slows down response, can increase overshoot/oscillations. |
| **PID**         | $K_p$, $T_i$, $T_d$ (Derivative Time) | $K_p, T_i$: Same as PI. $T_d$ (or $K_d$): Improves stability, reduces overshoot and settling time by anticipating changes. Sensitive to noise. |

**Impact of Tuning Parameters on Performance Metrics:**

| Parameter Change | Rise Time $\uparrow$ | Overshoot $\uparrow$ | Settling Time $\uparrow$ | Steady-State Error $\downarrow$ | Stability $\downarrow$ |
| :--------------- | :------------------- | :------------------- | :----------------------- | :------------------------------ | :--------------------- |
| $K_p \uparrow$   | $\downarrow$         | $\uparrow$           | $\uparrow$               | $\downarrow$                    | $\downarrow$           |
| $T_i \downarrow$ ($K_i \uparrow$) | $\uparrow$           | $\uparrow$           | $\uparrow$               | $\downarrow$                    | $\downarrow$           |
| $T_d \uparrow$ ($K_d \uparrow$) | $\downarrow$         | $\downarrow$         | $\downarrow$             | (No direct effect)              | $\uparrow$             |

**Important Note:** The effects listed above are general and can interact. For instance, increasing $K_p$ to reduce steady-state error might also increase overshoot and decrease stability. Tuning is about finding a balance.

### 6. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1 (CO4, K3):**
A proportional controller has a gain $K_p = 10$. If the error signal is $e(t) = 5$ units, what is the controller output $u(t)$?
**Answer:** $u(t) = K_p \times e(t) = 10 \times 5 = 50$ units.

**Question 2 (CO4, K3):**
What is the primary advantage of using an integral term in a controller compared to a proportional-only controller?
**Answer:** The primary advantage is the elimination of steady-state error.

**Question 3 (CO5, K4):**
What is the main drawback of the derivative term in a PID controller, and how is it typically addressed in practice?
**Answer:** The main drawback is its sensitivity to noise in the error signal. It is typically addressed by filtering the derivative term.

**Question 4 (CO4, K3):**
For a step change in setpoint, describe how the proportional, integral, and derivative actions contribute to the controller's output.
**Answer:**
*   **Proportional:** Immediately generates an output proportional to the initial error.
*   **Integral:** Starts accumulating the error. If the error persists, this term will increase or decrease the control output over time to drive the error to zero.
*   **Derivative:** Generates an output proportional to the rate of change of the error. If the error is decreasing rapidly towards zero, the derivative term will oppose the change, reducing overshoot. If the error is increasing rapidly, it will increase the output to counteract it.

**Question 5 (CO5, K4):**
A system is found to oscillate with a period of 20 seconds when using only proportional control, with the gain at which oscillation begins being $K_u = 30$. Using the Ziegler-Nichols tuning rule for PID controllers, calculate the recommended initial values for $K_p$, $T_i$, and $T_d$.
**Answer:**
Given: $P_u = 20$ seconds, $K_u = 30$.
PID Tuning Rules:
*   $K_p = 0.6 K_u = 0.6 \times 30 = 18$
*   $T_i = P_u / 2 = 20 / 2 = 10$ seconds
*   $T_d = P_u / 8 = 20 / 8 = 2.5$ seconds

**Question 6 (CO1, K2; CO4, K3):**
In a feedback control loop, the controller is a critical component. Briefly explain the function of the controller in relation to the error signal.
**Answer:** The controller receives the error signal (difference between setpoint and measured output) and processes it to generate a control signal. This control signal is then used to manipulate the plant (through an actuator) in a way that aims to reduce the error and achieve the desired setpoint.

### 7. Self-Reflection and Further Study

*   **Understand the Trade-offs:** P, PI, and PID controllers offer different levels of performance. Understand the trade-offs between response speed, steady-state error, overshoot, and sensitivity to noise.
*   **Tuning is Key:** The performance of a PID controller is heavily dependent on its tuning. Explore different tuning methods and practice applying them (even in simulation).
*   **Applications:** Consider where each type of controller is typically used in industry (e.g., P for simple level control where precision isn't critical, PI for most process control, PID for faster dynamic systems like motor speed or robotic control).
*   **Advanced Controllers:** PID is a foundational controller. Research other types of controllers like fuzzy logic controllers, model predictive controllers (MPC), etc., to see how they build upon or differ from PID.

This concludes the study notes for P, PI, and PID controllers. Ensure you can explain their mathematical representations, their individual contributions to control, their advantages, disadvantages, and how they are tuned.
