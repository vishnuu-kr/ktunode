# DESIGN FOR MANUFACTURE AND ASSEMBLY

## Module 4: Sand Casting: Introduction to Sand Casting

### Topic: Welding Process: Different Types of Welding Processes

**Relevance to Module 4:** While Module 4 primarily focuses on sand casting, understanding different welding processes is crucial in the broader context of Design for Manufacture and Assembly (DFMA). Many cast components are subsequently joined to other parts using welding. Therefore, a designer must consider how casting features might affect subsequent welding operations and how welding choices influence the overall manufacturability and assembly of the product. This topic directly contributes to **Course Outcome 5: Design and improve parts for better welded joints.**

---

### 1. Introduction to Welding

Welding is a fabrication process that **joins materials, usually metals or thermoplastics, by causing fusion**. This is often accomplished by melting the workpieces and adding a filler material to form a pool of molten metal that cools to become a strong joint, with pressure sometimes applied so that the welding can occur either without melting the base metal or with filler metal.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Fusion:** The process of melting the base materials and filler material (if used) to create a homogeneous joint.
*   **Base Metal:** The parent material being joined.
*   **Filler Material:** An additional material added to the joint to improve its strength and integrity.
*   **Heat Source:** Energy applied to melt the base and filler materials (e.g., electric arc, gas flame, laser).
*   **Shielding:** Protecting the molten weld pool from atmospheric contamination (e.g., oxygen, nitrogen) which can degrade the weld quality. This is achieved through gases or flux.
*   **Weld Joint:** The specific configuration of the base materials being joined (e.g., butt, lap, corner, T-joint).

**Importance in DFMA:**

*   **Simplifies Assembly:** Welding can create strong, permanent joints, reducing the need for separate fasteners (screws, rivets) and simplifying assembly steps.
*   **Material Versatility:** Joins a wide range of materials, including dissimilar metals, which might not be possible with other joining methods.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** Can be more economical than mechanical fastening in many applications, especially for large-scale production.
*   **Design Flexibility:** Allows for complex shapes and integrated designs that might be difficult to achieve with machining or casting alone.
*   **Impact on Design:** Design considerations for weldability (e.g., accessibility for welding, joint preparation, material selection) are critical for efficient manufacturing. (Relates to CO5).

**Reference:**

*   Boothroyd, Dewhurst, Knight (2010) likely discusses joining methods as part of assembly considerations, emphasizing how design choices impact the ease and cost of assembly.
*   Dieter (2000) provides comprehensive information on material processing, including welding as a joining technology.

### 2. Classification of Welding Processes

Welding processes can be classified based on the heat source, the method of shielding, and the application of pressure. The most common classification is based on the heat source:

*   **Arc Welding:** Uses an electric arc as the heat source.
*   **Resistance Welding:** Uses electrical resistance to generate heat at the interface of the parts.
*   **Solid-State Welding:** Joins materials without melting, using pressure and/or friction.
*   **Other Heat Source Welding:** Utilizes non-electric sources like gas flames, lasers, or electron beams.

### 3. Major Types of Welding Processes

#### 3.1. Arc Welding Processes

Arc welding is the most widely used category of welding. The electric arc generates intense heat, melting the base metals and filler metal.

**Key Principles:**

*   An electric arc is struck between an electrode and the workpiece.
*   The arc's high temperature melts the base metal and filler metal (if used).
*   Shielding is crucial to protect the molten weld pool from atmospheric contamination.

**Common Arc Welding Processes:**

*   **Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) / Stick Welding:**
    *   **Description:** Uses a consumable electrode coated in flux. The flux melts to form a slag layer and shielding gas to protect the weld pool.
    *   **Advantages:** Simple equipment, portable, versatile for various joint types and positions, cost-effective for smaller jobs.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Slower deposition rates, requires frequent electrode changes, slag removal is necessary, difficult for thin materials.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Ensure good access for the electrode. Consider the need for slag removal. Can be used for joining cast parts.

*   **Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW) / MIG Welding (Metal Inert Gas):**
    *   **Description:** Uses a consumable wire electrode fed through a welding gun. An inert gas (e.g., Argon, Helium) or a mixture of gases shields the weld pool.
    *   **Advantages:** High deposition rates, continuous welding, easy to learn, good for various metals and thicknesses, less slag than SMAW.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires a gas supply, equipment can be more complex than SMAW, sensitive to drafts, less portable for outdoor use.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design for good accessibility for the gun. Consider the shielding gas requirements and potential for porosity if drafts are present. Excellent for joining sheet metal and thicker sections, including welded assemblies involving cast components.

*   **Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) / TIG Welding (Tungsten Inert Gas):**
    *   **Description:** Uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode. Filler material is added separately by hand or automatically. An inert gas shields the weld pool.
    *   **Advantages:** Produces high-quality, clean welds with excellent appearance, precise control, good for thin materials and exotic metals (aluminum, stainless steel), no slag.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Slower welding speed, requires higher skill level, more expensive equipment, sensitive to contamination.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design for good accessibility for both the electrode and filler rod. Suitable for critical joints where aesthetics and strength are paramount. Can be used for joining cast parts, especially for repair or modifying features.

*   **Flux-Cored Arc Welding (FCAW):**
    *   **Description:** Similar to GMAW but uses a tubular wire electrode filled with flux. Can be self-shielded or gas-shielded.
    *   **Advantages:** High deposition rates, deep penetration, good for outdoor use (self-shielded), suitable for various materials.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Produces slag, can generate more fumes than GMAW.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Similar to GMAW, ensure accessibility. Slag removal is necessary.

*   **Submerged Arc Welding (SAW):**
    *   **Description:** The arc is submerged under a blanket of granular flux. The flux melts to provide shielding and can also add alloying elements.
    *   **Advantages:** Very high deposition rates, deep penetration, excellent weld quality, minimal fumes and spatter, suitable for long, continuous welds.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited to flat or horizontal positions, requires specialized equipment, flux recovery system needed.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Primarily used for large fabrications like pressure vessels or beams. Design must allow for long, straight welds. Less common for joining small cast parts.

*   **Plasma Arc Welding (PAW):**
    *   **Description:** Similar to GTAW, but the arc is constricted through a nozzle, creating a more concentrated and hotter plasma stream.
    *   **Advantages:** Higher speed and penetration than GTAW, excellent arc stability, precise control.
    *   **Disadvantages:** More complex equipment than GTAW.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Can be used for high-precision joining.

**Reference:**

*   Chitale & Gupta (2011) would likely cover these common arc welding processes in detail within their manufacturing section.
*   Bralla (1998) in the Design for Manufacturability Handbook would offer insights into selecting welding processes for manufacturability and cost.

#### 3.2. Resistance Welding Processes

These processes use the electrical resistance of the parts to generate heat at the interface, combined with pressure.

**Key Principles:**

*   High electrical current is passed through the workpieces.
*   Heat is generated at the point of contact due to resistance ($H = I^2 R t$).
*   Pressure is applied to forge the joint as the materials soften.
*   No filler material is typically used.

**Common Resistance Welding Processes:**

*   **Spot Welding:**
    *   **Description:** Two or more pieces of metal are joined by applying pressure and current through pointed electrodes. The current flows through the workpieces, creating a localized molten "nugget" at the interface.
    *   **Advantages:** Fast, economical for mass production of sheet metal assemblies, no filler metal or flux required, easily automated.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited to lap joints, limited to thin materials, equipment maintenance is critical, spot strength can be a concern.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design parts with overlapping flanges for spot welding. Ensure access for the electrodes. Consider the number and spacing of spots required for adequate strength. Can be used to join sheet metal parts to cast flanges.

*   **Seam Welding:**
    *   **Description:** Similar to spot welding but uses rotating wheel electrodes to create a continuous, leak-tight weld seam.
    *   **Advantages:** Creates a continuous leak-proof joint, suitable for tanks and containers.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited to lap joints, more complex equipment than spot welding.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design for overlapping seams.

*   **Projection Welding:**
    *   **Description:** Raised projections are embossed onto one or both parts. Current is concentrated at these projections, which melt to form the weld.
    *   **Advantages:** Allows multiple welds to be made simultaneously, precise control over weld location, good for joining dissimilar thickness materials.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires pre-processing to create projections.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design for the inclusion of projections. This can be challenging on cast parts but might be feasible for specific features.

**Reference:**

*   Dieter (2000) would cover resistance welding as a fundamental manufacturing process.
*   Molloy, Tilley, Warman (1998) would likely discuss resistance welding in the context of joining methods relevant to product assembly.

#### 3.3. Solid-State Welding Processes

These processes join materials without melting, relying on pressure, friction, or diffusion.

**Key Principles:**

*   Materials are joined below their melting point.
*   Requires clean surfaces and significant pressure.

**Common Solid-State Welding Processes:**

*   **Friction Welding:**
    *   **Description:** One workpiece is rotated against a stationary workpiece under pressure. The friction generates heat, causing plastic deformation and forging of the joint.
    *   **Advantages:** High-strength joints, suitable for dissimilar materials, minimal distortion, fast process.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires specialized equipment, limited to joining cylindrical or symmetrical parts.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design parts with rotational symmetry. Can be used to join shafts or rods to cast components.

*   **Ultrasonic Welding:**
    *   **Description:** High-frequency ultrasonic vibrations are applied to workpieces clamped together under pressure. The vibrations cause localized heating and plastic deformation at the interface.
    *   **Advantages:** Fast, low temperature process, good for plastics and thin metals, no filler material or flux needed, good for delicate parts.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited to lap joints, workpiece size and geometry limitations, requires specific tooling.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design parts with appropriate joint geometries (e.g., shear steps) for ultrasonic welding. Suitable for joining thin metal parts or plastics to cast components.

*   **Explosion Welding:**
    *   **Description:** Uses the force of a controlled explosion to create a high-velocity impact between two workpieces, forming a bond.
    *   **Advantages:** Can join dissimilar materials with very different properties, large surface areas can be joined.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Specialized facility required, safety concerns, limited applications.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Typically used for cladding or joining large plates. Less relevant for typical component assembly involving castings.

**Reference:**

*   Dieter (2000) would provide detailed explanations of solid-state welding.
*   Whitney (2004) on Mechanical Assemblies might touch upon these joining methods for creating integrated structures.

#### 3.4. Other Heat Source Welding Processes

These processes use various energy sources for joining.

*   **Oxy-Fuel Welding (Gas Welding):**
    *   **Description:** Uses a flame produced by burning a fuel gas (e.g., acetylene) with oxygen. Filler metal is often used.
    *   **Advantages:** Portable, relatively inexpensive equipment, versatile for various thicknesses and joint types.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Slower than arc welding, can cause significant heat distortion, lower welding speeds.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Ensure good access for the torch. Consider heat input and potential for distortion. Can be used for repair welding of cast iron.

*   **Laser Beam Welding (LBW):**
    *   **Description:** Uses a highly concentrated laser beam as the heat source.
    *   **Advantages:** Very high precision, deep penetration with narrow welds, minimal heat affected zone, high welding speeds, suitable for automation.
    *   **Disadvantages:** High equipment cost, requires precise joint fit-up, sensitive to surface finish.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Design for tight tolerances and good joint alignment. Can be used for high-precision joining of cast parts or joining cast components to other manufactured parts.

*   **Electron Beam Welding (EBW):**
    *   **Description:** Uses a beam of high-velocity electrons as the heat source, typically in a vacuum.
    *   **Advantages:** Very deep penetration, narrow welds, minimal heat affected zone, can weld materials with high melting points.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires a vacuum chamber, high equipment cost, safety precautions for X-ray radiation.
    *   **Design Considerations (CO5):** Similar to LBW regarding precision and tolerances. Suitable for high-performance applications.

**Reference:**

*   Lesko (1999) in the Industrial Design, Materials and Manufacture Guide would provide an overview of various manufacturing processes including laser and electron beam welding.
*   Boothroyd (2005) on Assembly Automation would discuss processes suitable for automated assembly, where laser welding is often employed.

### 4. Design Considerations for Weldability (Relates to CO5)

When designing products that will be welded, especially when integrating cast components, the following factors are crucial:

*   **Joint Design:**
    *   **Accessibility:** Ensure the weld joint is easily accessible for the welding electrode, torch, or gun. Avoid tight corners or obstructions.
    *   **Joint Type:** Select appropriate joint types (butt, lap, fillet, corner) based on strength requirements, material thickness, and welding process. Lap joints are often preferred for resistance welding and can be easier to align.
    *   **Root Gap and Fit-up:** For processes like TIG and MIG, precise control of root gap and alignment is important. Casting tolerances may need to be considered here.
    *   **Weld Edge Preparation:** Some welding processes require edge preparation (e.g., beveling for butt welds). Design should accommodate this.

*   **Material Selection:**
    *   **Weldability:** Different materials have varying weldability. Consider the base metal's composition, carbon content, and presence of alloying elements. Cast irons, for example, can be challenging to weld due to their microstructure and susceptibility to cracking.
    *   **Filler Material Compatibility:** Select filler materials compatible with the base metals to ensure strong and ductile welds.
    *   **Dissimilar Material Joining:** If joining dissimilar materials (e.g., a cast steel part to a wrought aluminum part), consider the potential for galvanic corrosion or brittle intermetallic formation.

*   **Heat Affected Zone (HAZ):**
    *   **Distortion:** The heat input during welding can cause distortion, especially in thin-walled or complex assemblies. Design to minimize heat input or incorporate features to counteract distortion.
    *   **Metallurgical Changes:** The HAZ can experience changes in microstructure, affecting material properties (e.g., hardness, strength, ductility). Design to avoid critical areas being located in the HAZ if possible.

*   **Post-Weld Operations:**
    *   **Slag Removal:** If processes like SMAW or FCAW are used, ensure there is access for cleaning slag.
    *   **Inspection:** Design for inspectability. Welds may require visual inspection, non-destructive testing (NDT), or destructive testing.

*   **Cost and Productivity:**
    *   **Process Selection:** Choose the welding process that offers the best balance of speed, quality, and cost for the specific application.
    *   **Automation:** For high-volume production, consider automated welding processes like GMAW or robotic welding.

**Example:** When designing a bracket that is to be welded to a cast housing, you might design a flat flange on the bracket that overlaps with a machined surface on the housing. This overlap allows for easy spot welding or fillet welding, and the design ensures sufficient access for the welding equipment. If the cast housing has a complex geometry, you might need to ensure a smooth surface area is available for the weld, or even consider adding a welded-on boss to the casting to facilitate a cleaner weld joint.

**Reference:**

*   Boothroyd, Dewhurst, Knight (2010) and Boothroyd (2005) are excellent resources for designing for assembly, which includes considerations for joining methods like welding.
*   Bralla (1998) offers a wealth of practical advice on designing for manufacturability across various processes.
*   Chitale & Gupta (2011) and Dieter (2000) provide the fundamental engineering principles behind welding processes and material behavior.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Which welding process uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and requires a separate filler material to be added manually?
a) GMAW (MIG)
b) SMAW (Stick)
c) GTAW (TIG)
d) Resistance Spot Welding

**Answer:** c) GTAW (TIG)

**Question 2:**
For designing sheet metal parts intended for spot welding, what is a crucial design consideration?
a) The ability to perform fillet welds.
b) The need for significant edge preparation.
c) The design of overlapping flanges.
d) The requirement for a shielding gas.

**Answer:** c) The design of overlapping flanges.

**Question 3:**
Discuss how the choice of welding process can impact the assembly time and cost of a product. Provide an example.

**Answer:**
The choice of welding process significantly impacts assembly time and cost.
*   **High-speed processes** like GMAW or resistance spot welding are ideal for mass production, reducing labor time and cost per unit compared to slower processes like GTAW.
*   **Processes requiring less preparation or post-weld cleaning** (e.g., GTAW with no slag vs. SMAW with slag) can also reduce assembly steps and costs.
*   **Automation** with processes like robotic GMAW or laser welding can drastically reduce assembly time and labor costs in high-volume manufacturing.

**Example:** Assembling a car body involves thousands of spot welds. Using resistance spot welding is highly efficient and cost-effective for this high-volume application, allowing for rapid joining of sheet metal panels. In contrast, assembling a high-precision aerospace component might use GTAW or laser welding for critical joints, prioritizing weld quality and integrity over pure speed, which could increase assembly time and cost but is necessary for the application's demands.

**Question 4:**
Explain the term "Heat Affected Zone (HAZ)" in the context of welding and why it is important for product design. (Relates to CO5)

**Answer:**
The Heat Affected Zone (HAZ) is the region of the base material adjacent to the weld that has not been melted but whose microstructure and mechanical properties have been altered by the heat of the welding process. The temperature gradient across this zone leads to various metallurgical changes, such as grain growth, phase transformations, or precipitation hardening/softening.

It is important for product design because:
*   **Mechanical Properties:** Changes in the HAZ can affect the strength, hardness, ductility, and toughness of the joint area. Designers need to ensure that critical structural elements are not located within the HAZ if those changes would compromise performance.
*   **Distortion:** Differential heating and cooling in the HAZ can lead to residual stresses and distortion of the component or assembly. Design considerations might involve limiting heat input or designing features that can mitigate distortion.
*   **Corrosion Resistance:** Metallurgical changes in the HAZ can sometimes reduce corrosion resistance, especially in certain stainless steels or alloys.

**Question 5 (Design Exercise):**
Imagine you are designing a fixture that attaches to a cast iron engine block. You need to attach a steel bracket to the block. Briefly describe two different welding processes you might consider for this joint and discuss the design implications for each. (Relates to CO5)

**Possible Answer 1: Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) / Stick Welding**
*   **Process:** SMAW is robust and can be used for cast iron.
*   **Design Implications:**
    *   **Accessibility:** The bracket design must allow sufficient access for the welding electrode and the welder's hand.
    *   **Joint Type:** A fillet weld connecting a flange on the steel bracket to a machined surface on the cast iron block would be suitable.
    *   **Surface Preparation:** The cast iron surface may need cleaning and potentially preheating, depending on the specific cast iron alloy, to prevent cracking. The bracket might need a slight chamfer.
    *   **Post-Weld:** Slag will need to be removed.

**Possible Answer 2: Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) / TIG Welding**
*   **Process:** GTAW can produce high-quality welds and is often used for cast iron repair and joining.
*   **Design Implications:**
    *   **Accessibility:** Requires good access for the torch and filler rod.
    *   **Joint Type:** A precisely fitted joint (e.g., a groove weld or a fillet weld on a machined surface) would be beneficial.
    *   **Surface Preparation:** Extremely thorough cleaning of the cast iron surface is crucial to avoid contamination and porosity. Preheating might be necessary.
    *   **Precision:** This process offers more control but is slower and more costly than SMAW. The bracket design might incorporate features that facilitate precise alignment.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   Welding is a critical joining process that significantly impacts product manufacturability and assembly.
*   Each welding process has unique advantages, disadvantages, and design considerations.
*   Understanding the principles of arc welding, resistance welding, and solid-state welding is essential for effective DFMA.
*   Designing for weldability involves considering joint accessibility, joint type, material compatibility, and the impact of the heat-affected zone.
*   When welding cast components, the specific properties of the cast material (e.g., cast iron's susceptibility to cracking) must be carefully considered.
*   The choice of welding process should align with production volume, cost targets, and required weld quality.
*   **This topic directly supports CO5 by emphasizing the design aspects necessary for creating robust and efficiently manufactured welded joints.**
