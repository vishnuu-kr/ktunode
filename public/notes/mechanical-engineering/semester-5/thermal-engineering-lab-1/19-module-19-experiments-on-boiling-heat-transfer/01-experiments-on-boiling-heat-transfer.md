# THERMAL ENGINEERING LAB-1: Module 19 - Experiments on Boiling Heat Transfer

## Introduction to Boiling Heat Transfer

Boiling is a phase-transition heat transfer process that occurs when a liquid is heated to its boiling point and vaporizes. It is a highly efficient mode of heat transfer due to the latent heat of vaporization involved and the vigorous fluid motion generated by bubble formation and detachment. This module focuses on conducting experiments to understand and quantify the phenomena of boiling heat transfer.

**Key Concept:** Boiling is a convective heat transfer process characterized by the formation and departure of vapor bubbles from a heated surface immersed in a liquid.

## 19.2 Key Concepts and Definitions

### 19.2.1 Boiling Heat Transfer

Boiling is the vaporization of a liquid at a solid surface when the surface temperature is above the saturation temperature of the liquid. The heat transfer mechanism involves both convection and phase change.

*   **Saturation Temperature ($T_{sat}$):** The temperature at which a liquid boils at a given pressure.
*   **Surface Temperature ($T_s$):** The temperature of the heated surface.
*   **Superheat ($\Delta T_{sat}$):** The difference between the surface temperature and the saturation temperature of the liquid: $\Delta T_{sat} = T_s - T_{sat}$. This is a primary driving force for boiling heat transfer.

### 19.2.2 Boiling Regimes

As the surface superheat increases, the boiling process progresses through distinct regimes, each with its characteristic heat transfer mechanism and heat flux. Understanding these regimes is crucial for experimental analysis.

1.  **Natural Convection:**
    *   **Description:** At very low superheats, no bubbles form. Heat is transferred from the surface to the liquid primarily by natural convection.
    *   **Heat Flux:** Relatively low.
    *   **Visual Observation:** Fluid circulates due to density differences, but no bubble formation.

2.  **Nucleate Boiling:**
    *   **Description:** As superheat increases, vapor bubbles begin to form at specific sites on the heated surface (nucleation sites). These bubbles grow, detach, and rise to the surface, causing significant fluid motion and high heat transfer rates.
    *   **Sub-regimes of Nucleate Boiling:**
        *   **Isolated Bubble Regime:** Small, individual bubbles form and detach. Heat transfer is efficient.
        *   **Columnar Bubble Regime:** Bubbles begin to coalesce and form vapor columns. Heat transfer remains high.
    *   **Heat Flux:** High and increases rapidly with superheat.
    *   **Visual Observation:** Rapid formation and detachment of bubbles from specific points on the heated surface.
    *   **Critical Heat Flux (CHF):** The maximum heat flux achievable in nucleate boiling. Beyond this point, the heat transfer coefficient starts to decrease.

3.  **Transition Boiling (or Wavy Film Boiling):**
    *   **Description:** Occurs after the CHF point. Vapor bubbles coalesce into a continuous vapor film that partially covers the heated surface. The heat transfer is a combination of nucleate boiling (from unblanketed areas) and film boiling (from blanketed areas). Heat transfer decreases with increasing superheat.
    *   **Heat Flux:** Decreases with increasing superheat.
    *   **Visual Observation:** Intermittent vapor film formation and detachment, a chaotic boiling pattern.

4.  **Film Boiling:**
    *   **Description:** At high superheats, a stable vapor film completely covers the heated surface. Heat is transferred through this vapor film by conduction and radiation. This regime is less efficient than nucleate boiling.
    *   **Heat Flux:** Low and increases with increasing superheat due to radiative contribution.
    *   **Visual Observation:** A continuous vapor blanket surrounds the heated surface.

### 19.2.3 Boiling Curve

A boiling curve is a plot of heat flux ($q''$) as a function of surface superheat ($\Delta T_{sat}$). It visually represents the different boiling regimes.

*   **X-axis:** Surface Superheat ($\Delta T_{sat}$)
*   **Y-axis:** Heat Flux ($q''$)

**Reference:** Cengel, Chapter 12 (Boiling and Condensation) discusses boiling regimes and boiling curves in detail. Holman, Chapter 7 (Boiling and Condensation) also provides a thorough explanation.

### 19.2.4 Heat Transfer Coefficient in Boiling

The heat transfer coefficient ($h$) in boiling is defined as:

$h = \frac{q''}{\Delta T_{sat}}$

Where:
*   $q''$ is the heat flux (W/m²)
*   $\Delta T_{sat}$ is the surface superheat (°C or K)

In boiling, $h$ can be significantly higher than in forced convection or natural convection due to the phase change and bubble dynamics.

### 19.2.5 Critical Heat Flux (CHF)

CHF is a crucial parameter in boiling heat transfer, representing the maximum heat flux that can be sustained before the onset of film boiling. Exceeding CHF can lead to a rapid increase in surface temperature, potentially causing equipment failure or burnout.

**Factors Affecting CHF:**
*   Fluid properties (viscosity, surface tension, thermal conductivity, latent heat)
*   Surface properties (roughness, material, cleanliness)
*   Pressure
*   Flow conditions (flow rate, flow direction)
*   Geometry of the heated surface

**Reference:** Sachdeva, Chapter 7 (Convection Heat Transfer) and Incropera & DeWitt, Chapter 10 (Phase Change Heat Transfer: Boiling and Condensation) offer detailed explanations of boiling correlations and CHF.

### 19.2.6 Nucleate Boiling Correlations

Several empirical correlations have been developed to predict heat transfer coefficients in the nucleate boiling regime. A common and widely used correlation is the **Jens-Zuber Correlation**:

$h_{nucleate} = 0.0060 \times (P_{critical})^{0.69} \times \Delta T_{sat}^{0.69} \times (C_p \times (T_s - T_{sat}) / h_{fg})^{2.11}$  (This is a simplification and often represented as $h = C_1 \Delta T_{sat}^n$)

A more general form often cited is:

$q'' = \mu_l h_{fg} \left[ \frac{g(\rho_l - \rho_v)}{\sigma} \right]^{1/2} \left( \frac{C_{p,l} (T_s - T_{sat})}{C_{sf} h_{fg} Pr_l^n} \right)^3$

Where:
*   $\mu_l$: Dynamic viscosity of the liquid
*   $h_{fg}$: Latent heat of vaporization
*   $g$: Acceleration due to gravity
*   $\rho_l$: Density of the liquid
*   $\rho_v$: Density of the vapor
*   $\sigma$: Surface tension
*   $C_{p,l}$: Specific heat of the liquid
*   $T_s$: Surface temperature
*   $T_{sat}$: Saturation temperature
*   $C_{sf}$: Boiling heat transfer empirical constant (depends on heating surface-fluid combination)
*   $Pr_l$: Prandtl number of the liquid
*   $n$: Empirical exponent (typically ~1.7)

**Reference:** Cengel, Chapter 12 provides an overview of various correlations. Kothandaraman, Chapter 10 also discusses these correlations.

## 19.3 Experimental Setup and Procedure

The typical experimental setup for studying boiling heat transfer involves:

*   **Heated Surface:** A specimen (e.g., a metal rod or plate) that is heated electrically.
*   **Heating Element:** Usually a cartridge heater embedded within the specimen.
*   **Temperature Measurement:** Thermocouples or RTDs to measure the surface temperature of the specimen and the bulk liquid temperature.
*   **Power Supply:** A variable DC or AC power supply to control the heat input to the element.
*   **Voltmeter and Ammeter:** To measure the voltage across and current through the heating element, allowing calculation of heat input ($Q = VI$).
*   **Liquid Container:** A vessel to hold the liquid being boiled (e.g., water, ethanol).
*   **Condenser (Optional):** To condense the vapor and return it to the container, maintaining constant liquid level.
*   **Pressure Gauge:** To monitor the system pressure.

### 19.3.1 General Procedure

1.  **Setup:** Assemble the experimental apparatus, ensuring proper electrical connections and temperature sensor placements.
2.  **Fill with Liquid:** Fill the container with the desired liquid (e.g., distilled water).
3.  **Initial State:** Ensure the liquid is at room temperature or a controlled initial temperature.
4.  **Heating:** Gradually increase the power supplied to the heating element.
5.  **Data Acquisition:**
    *   Record the voltage ($V$) and current ($I$) supplied to the heater.
    *   Record the surface temperature ($T_s$) at various points on the heated surface.
    *   Record the bulk liquid temperature ($T_{bulk}$).
6.  **Observations:** Visually observe and record the boiling behavior (bubble formation, detachment, film formation) at different power levels. Note the onset of different boiling regimes.
7.  **Repeat:** Repeat steps 4-6 for a range of power inputs, covering different boiling regimes up to potentially CHF.
8.  **Cooling/Pressure Adjustment (if applicable):** If studying the effect of pressure, adjust the system pressure accordingly.

## 19.4 Data Analysis and Calculations

### 19.4.1 Heat Input

The electrical power supplied to the heater is dissipated as heat. Assuming all power is transferred to the liquid, the heat input ($Q$) is calculated as:

$Q = V \times I$ (Watts)

### 19.4.2 Heat Flux ($q''$)

The heat flux is the heat transfer rate per unit surface area. Assuming the heated surface is a cylinder of diameter $D$ and length $L$ (or submerged length), the heat flux is:

$q'' = \frac{Q}{A_{surface}}$

Where $A_{surface}$ is the surface area of the heated element (e.g., for a cylinder, $A_{surface} = \pi D L$).

### 19.4.3 Surface Superheat ($\Delta T_{sat}$)

This is the temperature difference between the heated surface and the saturation temperature of the liquid at the operating pressure.

$\Delta T_{sat} = T_s - T_{sat}$

The saturation temperature ($T_{sat}$) can be obtained from steam tables or online calculators for the given pressure. If boiling at atmospheric pressure, $T_{sat}$ for water is approximately 100°C.

### 19.4.4 Heat Transfer Coefficient ($h$)

The local or average heat transfer coefficient is calculated using:

$h = \frac{q''}{\Delta T_{sat}}$

### 19.4.5 Boiling Curve Plotting

Plot the calculated heat flux ($q''$) on the y-axis against the surface superheat ($\Delta T_{sat}$) on the x-axis. This plot will illustrate the different boiling regimes.

### 19.4.6 Comparison with Correlations

Compare the experimentally obtained heat transfer coefficients ($h_{exp}$) or heat fluxes ($q''_{exp}$) with those predicted by theoretical correlations (e.g., Jens-Zuber, Rohsenow correlation) for similar conditions.

**Rohsenow Correlation (Nucleate Boiling):**
$ \frac{C_{p,l} (T_s - T_{sat})}{h_{fg} Pr_l^1.7} = C_{sf} \left[ \frac{q''}{\mu_l h_{fg}} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{g(\rho_l - \rho_v)}} \right]^{0.333} $

Where:
*   $C_{sf}$: Experimental constant, typically 0.013 for water/agitated liquids on polished stainless steel.

**Reference:** Cengel, Chapter 12 provides detailed discussion on the application of these correlations.

## 19.5 Examples and Applications

*   **Boiling Water Reactors (Nuclear Power Plants):** Efficient heat removal from fuel rods relies on nucleate boiling. CHF is a critical design parameter.
*   **Refrigeration and Air Conditioning:** Evaporators in refrigeration cycles utilize boiling heat transfer to absorb heat from the surroundings.
*   **Electronics Cooling:** Boiling can be used for high-power electronic component cooling due to its high heat transfer capability.
*   **Industrial Processes:** Evaporators, reboilers, and distillation columns all rely on boiling phenomena.

## 19.6 Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Describe the four main boiling regimes and the typical trend of heat flux with surface superheat for each.

**Answer:**
1.  **Natural Convection:** No bubbles, heat flux increases slowly with superheat.
2.  **Nucleate Boiling:** Bubble formation and detachment, heat flux increases rapidly with superheat.
3.  **Transition Boiling:** Intermittent vapor film, heat flux decreases with increasing superheat.
4.  **Film Boiling:** Stable vapor film, heat flux increases slowly with superheat (due to radiation).

**Question 2:**
A heated horizontal copper rod of diameter 1 cm and length 5 cm is submerged in saturated water at atmospheric pressure. The rod is heated electrically, and at a particular power input, the surface temperature is measured to be 105°C. The voltage across the heater is 20 V, and the current is 1.5 A. Calculate the heat flux and the heat transfer coefficient for this condition.

**Given:**
*   Diameter ($D$) = 1 cm = 0.01 m
*   Length ($L$) = 5 cm = 0.05 m
*   Surface Temperature ($T_s$) = 105°C
*   Saturation Temperature of water at atmospheric pressure ($T_{sat}$) $\approx$ 100°C
*   Voltage ($V$) = 20 V
*   Current ($I$) = 1.5 A

**Solution:**

1.  **Calculate Heat Input ($Q$):**
    $Q = V \times I = 20 \, \text{V} \times 1.5 \, \text{A} = 30 \, \text{W}$

2.  **Calculate Surface Area ($A_{surface}$):**
    $A_{surface} = \pi D L = \pi \times 0.01 \, \text{m} \times 0.05 \, \text{m} = 0.00157 \, \text{m}^2$

3.  **Calculate Heat Flux ($q''$):**
    $q'' = \frac{Q}{A_{surface}} = \frac{30 \, \text{W}}{0.00157 \, \text{m}^2} \approx 19108 \, \text{W/m}^2$

4.  **Calculate Surface Superheat ($\Delta T_{sat}$):**
    $\Delta T_{sat} = T_s - T_{sat} = 105^\circ\text{C} - 100^\circ\text{C} = 5^\circ\text{C}$

5.  **Calculate Heat Transfer Coefficient ($h$):**
    $h = \frac{q''}{\Delta T_{sat}} = \frac{19108 \, \text{W/m}^2}{5^\circ\text{C}} \approx 3822 \, \text{W/m}^2\text{°C}$

**Answer:** The heat flux is approximately 19108 W/m², and the heat transfer coefficient is approximately 3822 W/m²°C.

**Question 3:**
What is Critical Heat Flux (CHF) and what are its implications in engineering applications?

**Answer:**
Critical Heat Flux (CHF) is the maximum heat flux that can be achieved in the nucleate boiling regime before a significant reduction in heat transfer capability occurs. It is the peak of the boiling curve. If CHF is exceeded, the heat transfer surface can become insulated by a vapor film, leading to a rapid rise in surface temperature (burnout). In engineering applications like nuclear reactors or power electronics cooling, operating below CHF is crucial to prevent component damage and ensure safe operation.

**Question 4:**
How does surface roughness generally affect nucleate boiling heat transfer?

**Answer:**
Generally, increased surface roughness tends to enhance nucleate boiling heat transfer. Rougher surfaces typically have more nucleation sites, which are preferential locations for bubble formation. A higher density of nucleation sites leads to more vigorous bubble generation and departure, resulting in a higher heat transfer coefficient in the nucleate boiling regime. However, very rough surfaces can also promote film boiling at lower superheats if not properly managed.

## 19.7 Important Points to Remember

*   **Superheat is the driving force:** The greater the $\Delta T_{sat}$, the higher the heat flux, up to CHF.
*   **Nucleate boiling is the most efficient regime:** Characterized by high heat transfer coefficients due to bubble dynamics.
*   **CHF is a critical limit:** Operating beyond CHF can lead to rapid surface temperature increases.
*   **Boiling curve visualizes regimes:** Plotting $q''$ vs. $\Delta T_{sat}$ is essential for understanding the boiling process.
*   **Surface properties matter:** Surface roughness, material, and cleanliness influence nucleation site density and boiling performance.
*   **Fluid properties are crucial:** Viscosity, surface tension, specific heat, thermal conductivity, and latent heat all play a role in boiling heat transfer.
*   **Pressure affects saturation temperature:** Changes in pressure will alter $T_{sat}$ and consequently $\Delta T_{sat}$.
