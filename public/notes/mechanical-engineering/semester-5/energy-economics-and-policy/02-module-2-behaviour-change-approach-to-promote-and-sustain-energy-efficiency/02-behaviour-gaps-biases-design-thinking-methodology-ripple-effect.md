# Energy Economics and Policy: Module 2 - Behaviour Change Approach to Promote and Sustain Energy Efficiency

## Topic: Behaviour Gaps, Biases, Design Thinking Methodology, Ripple Effect

This module delves into the crucial area of behaviour change in promoting and sustaining energy efficiency. Understanding why individuals and organizations don't always act in their perceived best energy-saving interests is key to designing effective policies and interventions. We will explore the psychological factors influencing decision-making, a structured approach to problem-solving, and the wider impacts of successful behaviour change.

---

### 1. Behaviour Gaps: The Disconnect Between Intention and Action

**1.1. Definition:**
A **behaviour gap** refers to the discrepancy between an individual's stated intentions or beliefs about energy conservation and their actual energy-saving behaviours. This highlights that simply knowing something is good for the environment or their wallet isn't enough to guarantee action.

**1.2. Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Intentions:** What people say they will do (e.g., "I intend to turn off lights when I leave a room").
*   **Actions:** What people actually do (e.g., leaving lights on).
*   **Attitudes:** A person's overall evaluation of something (e.g., "Energy conservation is important").
*   **Norms:** Socially shared expectations about how people should behave.
    *   **Descriptive Norms:** What people perceive others are doing (e.g., "My neighbours leave their porch lights on").
    *   **Injunctive Norms:** What people perceive others approve or disapprove of (e.g., "It's frowned upon to waste electricity").

**1.3. Causes of Behaviour Gaps:**

*   **Cognitive Biases (discussed in detail below):** Systematic patterns of deviation from norm or rationality in judgment.
*   **Information Overload and Complexity:** Too much information or complex instructions can overwhelm individuals, leading to inaction.
*   **Lack of Salience/Visibility:** Energy usage and savings are often abstract and not immediately apparent.
*   **High Upfront Costs:** Energy-efficient technologies may have higher initial purchase prices, deterring adoption even with long-term savings.
*   **Inconvenience/Effort:** Energy-saving actions might require more time, effort, or a change in habits, which people are often reluctant to undertake.
*   **Myopia/Short-Term Thinking:** People tend to prioritize immediate gratification over future benefits.
*   **Lack of Feedback:** Not knowing how much energy is being used or saved can hinder behavioural change.
*   **Social Influence:** The behaviour of others (especially peers) can significantly impact individual choices.

**1.4. Examples:**

*   A homeowner understands the benefits of insulating their attic but delays the project due to the perceived hassle and upfront cost.
*   Employees know that turning off their computers at the end of the day saves energy, but habit or a belief that "it doesn't make much difference" leads them to leave them on.
*   A person expresses concern about climate change and their carbon footprint but continues to drive a gas-guzzling car for convenience.

**1.5. Relevance to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO2 (Understanding energy efficiency and behaviour change approaches):** Directly addresses the core concepts of why energy efficiency measures are not always adopted despite understanding their benefits.

### 2. Cognitive Biases: The Mental Shortcuts Affecting Energy Decisions

**2.1. Definition:**
**Cognitive biases** are predictable, systematic errors in thinking that affect our judgments and decisions. They are often a result of our brains trying to simplify complex information processing, but they can lead to irrational choices, particularly in energy consumption.

**2.2. Key Biases Affecting Energy Behaviour:**

*   **Present Bias (Hyperbolic Discounting):** The tendency to overvalue immediate rewards and undervalue future rewards.
    *   *Example:* Choosing to buy a cheaper, less energy-efficient appliance because the upfront cost is lower, ignoring the higher energy bills over its lifetime.
*   **Status Quo Bias:** The preference for the current state of affairs. People tend to stick with what they know and resist change.
    *   *Example:* Continuing to use old, inefficient appliances simply because they are already owned and functional, rather than investing in newer, more efficient ones.
*   **Loss Aversion:** The tendency to prefer avoiding losses over acquiring equivalent gains. The pain of losing is psychologically about twice as powerful as the pleasure of gaining.
    *   *Example:* People may be more motivated by a penalty for high energy usage than by a rebate for low usage.
*   **Availability Heuristic:** Overestimating the likelihood of events that are easily recalled or vivid in memory.
    *   *Example:* If a news report highlights a power outage due to extreme weather, people might overestimate the likelihood of future outages and invest in backup generators, even if the probability is low.
*   **Confirmation Bias:** The tendency to search for, interpret, favour, and recall information in a way that confirms one's pre-existing beliefs or hypotheses.
    *   *Example:* Someone who believes that renewable energy is unreliable might focus on news stories about intermittency issues while ignoring successes.
*   **Anchoring Bias:** The tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information offered (the "anchor") when making decisions.
    *   *Example:* A utility company stating the "average household uses X kWh per month" can anchor people's perception of normal usage, making it harder to strive for significantly lower consumption.
*   **Overconfidence Bias:** The tendency to be more confident in one's own abilities or judgments than is objectively warranted.
    *   *Example:* Believing one is naturally good at conserving energy without actually monitoring usage, leading to complacency.

**2.3. Incorporating from Textbooks:**

*   **Sreekanth (2018)**, in its broader perspective on energy policy, would likely acknowledge that policy effectiveness is contingent on understanding human behaviour and the deviations from purely rational economic models.

### 3. Design Thinking Methodology: A Human-Centred Approach to Energy Solutions

**3.1. Definition:**
**Design Thinking** is an iterative, human-centred process for creative problem-solving. It focuses on understanding the users' needs, challenging assumptions, and redefining problems to identify alternative strategies and solutions that might not be immediately apparent with a traditional, linear approach.

**3.2. The Design Thinking Process (Iterative Stages):**

1.  **Empathize:**
    *   **Goal:** To gain a deep understanding of the people you are designing for.
    *   **Methods:** Interviews, observation, surveys, shadowing users, creating "empathy maps."
    *   *Energy Context:* Understanding the daily routines, motivations, barriers, and energy habits of households or businesses. Why do they use energy the way they do? What are their real concerns about energy bills or efficiency?

2.  **Define:**
    *   **Goal:** To synthesize the findings from the empathize phase into a clear, actionable problem statement. Frame the problem from the user's perspective.
    *   **Methods:** Affinity mapping, "How Might We" (HMW) questions.
    *   *Energy Context:* "Homeowners in suburban areas struggle to find the time and expertise to conduct home energy audits, leading to missed savings opportunities." (HMW: How might we make energy audits accessible and effortless for busy homeowners?)

3.  **Ideate:**
    *   **Goal:** To generate a wide range of potential solutions. Encourage wild ideas and defer judgment.
    *   **Methods:** Brainstorming, mind mapping, worst possible idea.
    *   *Energy Context:* Ideas for user-friendly energy monitoring apps, community energy workshops, simplified DIY energy-saving guides, gamified energy challenges, incentives for behavioural change.

4.  **Prototype:**
    *   **Goal:** To build low-fidelity versions of potential solutions to test.
    *   **Methods:** Storyboards, sketches, wireframes, simple models, role-playing.
    *   *Energy Context:* Creating mock-ups of an energy app interface, drafting a simple brochure for energy tips, or simulating a community energy competition.

5.  **Test:**
    *   **Goal:** To get feedback on prototypes from actual users. This phase is not about "debugging" but about learning and refining.
    *   **Methods:** User testing sessions, feedback surveys, A/B testing.
    *   *Energy Context:* Showing the app mock-up to homeowners and asking them to perform a task, or presenting the brochure and asking for clarity and usefulness.

**3.3. How it Addresses Behaviour Gaps and Biases:**

*   **Empathy:** Directly tackles behaviour gaps by understanding the "why" behind actions, uncovering hidden barriers and motivations that traditional economic models might miss.
*   **User-Centricity:** Designs solutions that are intuitive, convenient, and appealing, working *with* human psychology rather than against it. It can help mitigate biases by making energy-saving actions easier (reducing effort), providing clear and immediate feedback (countering myopia), or framing benefits in terms of avoiding loss.

**3.4. Incorporating from Textbooks:**

*   While not explicitly a "design thinking" textbook, **Bhattacharyya (2011)** might offer insights into market design and consumer behaviour that can inform the "Define" and "Ideate" stages of energy-related interventions.
*   **Sreekanth (2018)**, in discussing policy challenges, would implicitly support a human-centred approach by recognizing the limitations of purely technocratic or market-based solutions when faced with complex human behaviour.

*   **CO2 (Understand energy efficiency and behaviour change approaches):** Design Thinking is a prime example of a behaviour change approach.

### 4. Ripple Effect: The Wider Impacts of Behaviour Change

**4.1. Definition:**
The **ripple effect** refers to the widening and spreading influence of a particular action or event. In the context of energy efficiency, successful behaviour change in one area can lead to cascading positive effects across various domains.

**4.2. Types of Ripple Effects:**

*   **Direct Energy Savings:** The most immediate impact – reduced energy consumption and lower energy bills for the individual or organization.
*   **Increased Awareness and Knowledge:** Individuals who change their behaviour often become more informed about energy issues, leading them to adopt other energy-saving practices.
*   **Social Norm Diffusion:** When individuals see their peers adopting energy-saving behaviours, it can influence their own actions (norm diffusion). This can lead to widespread adoption.
*   **Technological Adoption:** Increased demand for energy-efficient products and services can drive innovation and lower the cost of these technologies, making them more accessible to others.
*   **Economic Benefits:**
    *   **Reduced Demand:** Lower overall energy demand can lead to increased energy security and potentially lower wholesale energy prices.
    *   **Job Creation:** Growth in the energy efficiency sector (manufacturing, installation, consulting) creates new jobs.
    *   **Innovation:** Drives investment in clean tech and efficient solutions.
*   **Environmental Benefits:**
    *   **Reduced Emissions:** Lower energy consumption, especially from fossil fuels, directly reduces greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution.
    *   **Resource Conservation:** Less strain on natural resources like coal, oil, and natural gas.
*   **Improved Well-being:**
    *   **Health:** Reduced air pollution can lead to better public health outcomes.
    *   **Financial Stability:** Lower energy bills can improve household financial security.
    *   **Community Engagement:** Collective efforts in energy saving can foster a sense of community and shared purpose.

**4.3. Examples:**

*   A community program that encourages home energy audits and provides subsidized insulation leads to:
    *   Lower energy bills for participating households.
    *   Increased local demand for insulation installers, creating jobs.
    *   Some residents, now more energy-conscious, start using public transport or buying electric vehicles.
    *   Local government sees a reduction in peak electricity demand, potentially delaying the need for expensive new power plants.
    *   Better indoor air quality in insulated homes.
*   A university campus initiative to reduce energy consumption by turning off lights and optimizing HVAC systems results in:
    *   Significant cost savings for the university, which can be redirected to educational programs.
    *   Students becoming more aware of energy use and adopting similar habits at home.
    *   A reduction in the university's carbon footprint, contributing to its sustainability goals.

**4.4. Incorporating from Textbooks:**

*   **Hotelling (1931)**, while focused on resource economics, provides a foundational understanding of scarcity and the economic implications of resource use, which underpins the environmental benefits of energy efficiency.
*   **Stevens (2000)** would offer broader economic perspectives on energy markets and policy, where the "ripple effect" of demand-side management and efficiency measures would be a key consideration for overall market stability and economic welfare.
*   **Sreekanth (2024)**, in its focus on energy sustainability and emission abatement, will undoubtedly discuss the multifaceted benefits of efficiency improvements, directly aligning with the concept of ripple effects.

*   **CO2 (Understand energy efficiency and behaviour change approaches):** Illustrates why promoting behaviour change is a powerful strategy for achieving broader energy and sustainability goals.

### Practice Questions & Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define the term "behaviour gap" and provide two common reasons why such gaps exist in the context of energy efficiency.

**Answer 1:**
A **behaviour gap** is the discrepancy between what people say they will do regarding energy conservation and their actual energy-saving actions. Two common reasons include:
1.  **Present Bias:** People prioritize immediate comfort or convenience over future energy savings.
2.  **High Upfront Costs/Inconvenience:** The perceived difficulty or cost of implementing energy-saving measures outweighs the abstract or delayed benefits.

**Question 2:**
Which cognitive bias best explains why a homeowner might choose to buy a slightly less energy-efficient appliance to save money upfront, even if it will cost more in energy bills over time?

**Answer 2:**
The **Present Bias** (or Hyperbolic Discounting) best explains this behaviour. The immediate gratification of saving money on the purchase price is valued more highly than the future, albeit larger, savings from lower energy bills.

**Question 3:**
Describe the five stages of the Design Thinking methodology. For each stage, briefly explain how it can be applied to develop a new energy-saving behaviour intervention for a university student population.

**Answer 3:**
1.  **Empathize:** Understand students' daily routines, energy usage habits (e.g., dorm room behaviour, laptop charging), motivations (e.g., saving money, social responsibility), and barriers (e.g., lack of awareness, busy schedules). Methods: surveys, focus groups, observation in dorms.
2.  **Define:** Synthesize findings into a problem statement. E.g., "Students leave lights and electronics on in vacant common areas due to forgetfulness and a lack of immediate feedback." HMW: How might we make students more mindful of turning off electronics when leaving a room?
3.  **Ideate:** Brainstorm solutions: smart power strips that auto-shutoff, fun reminders/gamification apps, peer-to-peer energy challenges, visual cues near switches.
4.  **Prototype:** Create low-fidelity versions. E.g., sketch a user interface for an energy-saving app, design a prototype smart plug, or create sample reminder stickers.
5.  **Test:** Get feedback from students. Show them the app mock-up, ask them to use a prototype smart plug for a week, or place stickers and observe if behaviour changes. Refine based on feedback.

**Question 4:**
Explain the concept of the "ripple effect" in energy efficiency. Provide one example of a direct benefit and one example of an indirect benefit.

**Answer 4:**
The **ripple effect** refers to the widening and spreading influence of a particular action or event. In energy efficiency, successful behaviour change can lead to cascading positive impacts.
*   **Direct Benefit:** Reduced energy consumption and lower energy bills for the individual or organization implementing the change.
*   **Indirect Benefit:** Increased social awareness and adoption of energy-saving behaviours among peers, leading to broader societal impacts like reduced greenhouse gas emissions or job creation in the efficiency sector.

**Question 5 (Application/CO1):**
Imagine you are advising a city council on promoting energy efficiency among its residents. Considering the behavioural concepts discussed, what are two key types of policy interventions you would recommend, and why?

**Answer 5:**
1.  **Feedback-Oriented Programs:** Implement smart meter programs that provide households with clear, real-time data on their energy consumption and cost. This directly addresses the lack of salience and provides immediate feedback, counteracting present bias and promoting awareness.
2.  **Social Norming Campaigns:** Launch campaigns that highlight the energy-saving behaviours of "average" or "leading" households in the city (e.g., "Your neighbours are saving X% on their energy bills"). This leverages descriptive norms to encourage adoption and can create a positive social contagion effect, tapping into the ripple effect for wider adoption.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Human behaviour is not always rational:** Traditional economic models often assume rationality, but cognitive biases and psychological factors significantly influence energy decisions.
*   **Knowledge is not enough:** Simply informing people about energy efficiency benefits is often insufficient to change behaviour.
*   **Empathy is key:** Understanding the user's context, motivations, and barriers is crucial for designing effective interventions.
*   **Design Thinking offers a structured, human-centred approach:** It helps overcome behaviour gaps by fostering user understanding and iterative solution development.
*   **Small changes can have large impacts:** The ripple effect highlights how individual and localized behaviour changes can lead to significant societal, economic, and environmental benefits.
*   **Policy must account for psychology:** Effective energy policies integrate insights from behavioural economics and psychology to promote adoption and sustainability.
