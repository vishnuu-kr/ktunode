# Module 2: 2D Aerofoils - Momentum and Circulation Theory of Aerofoils: Characteristics

## Introduction

This module delves into the fundamental theories that explain the aerodynamic forces generated by a 2D aerofoil. We will explore the momentum theory and the circulation theory, understanding their principles, characteristics, and how they contribute to lift and drag. This knowledge is crucial for understanding aircraft performance, as outlined in Course Outcome 1 (CO1).

**Key Concepts:**

*   **2D Aerofoil:** A cross-section of an aircraft wing, analyzed as an infinite span wing.
*   **Lift:** The aerodynamic force perpendicular to the direction of the relative airflow.
*   **Drag:** The aerodynamic force parallel to the direction of the relative airflow.
*   **Relative Airflow:** The airflow velocity as seen by the aerofoil.

---

## 1. Momentum Theory of Aerofoils

Momentum theory, attributed to Sir Isaac Newton, provides a simplified, yet insightful, approach to understanding lift generation. It focuses on the change in momentum of the air as it flows over and under the aerofoil.

### 1.1 Fundamental Principle

The theory posits that lift is generated by deflecting a mass of air downwards. According to Newton's second law of motion, a change in momentum of a fluid mass results in a force. In this context, the downward deflection of air by the aerofoil creates an upward reaction force, which is lift.

*   **Core Idea:** The aerofoil acts like a "pusher," imparting a downward velocity component to the air passing over and under it.
*   **Force Generation:** The reaction force to this downward momentum imparted to the air is the upward lift force.

### 1.2 Simplified Model

Consider a simplified model where the aerofoil acts as a flat plate inclined at an angle of attack. The air is deflected downwards.

*   **Assumptions:**
    *   The air is an incompressible fluid.
    *   The aerofoil is infinitely long (2D assumption).
    *   The flow is steady.
    *   The air mass affected is well-defined.

*   **Derivation (Conceptual):**
    *   Let $A$ be the frontal area of the aerofoil.
    *   Let $\rho$ be the air density.
    *   Let $V$ be the freestream velocity.
    *   Assume the aerofoil deflects a streamtube of air with velocity $V$ and mass flow rate $\dot{m} = \rho A V$.
    *   If this streamtube is deflected downwards by an angle $\delta$, the downward component of velocity is $V \sin \delta$.
    *   The rate of change of vertical momentum is $\dot{m} (V \sin \delta) = (\rho A V) (V \sin \delta) = \rho A V^2 \sin \delta$.
    *   By Newton's third law, the upward force (lift) $L$ is equal to this rate of change of momentum.
    *   For small angles of attack ($\alpha \approx \delta$), $L \approx \rho A V^2 \alpha$.

### 1.3 Limitations of Simple Momentum Theory

While intuitive, the simple momentum theory has significant limitations when applied to actual aerofoils.

*   **Doesn't Explain the Shape:** It doesn't inherently explain why specific aerofoil shapes generate more lift than others or why they can generate lift at zero angle of attack.
*   **No Lift at Zero Angle:** A flat plate at zero angle of attack should, by this theory, produce no lift, which contradicts experimental observations.
*   **Drag Prediction:** It primarily focuses on lift and doesn't directly address drag in a comprehensive manner.
*   **Idealized Flow:** It assumes a simplified deflection, not accounting for the complex flow patterns around curved surfaces.

**Relevance to Textbooks:**

*   **Kermode, Barnard, & Philpott (Mechanics Of Flight):** Likely covers the basic concept of momentum change and its relation to force.
*   **Anderson (Fundamentals Of Aerodynamics):** Provides a more detailed mathematical treatment and discusses the limitations of simplified momentum theory in the context of more advanced aerodynamic models.

## 2. Circulation Theory of Aerofoils

Circulation theory, built upon the work of researchers like Helmholtz, Kelvin, and Lanchester, provides a more accurate and complete explanation of lift generation by aerofoils, particularly for cambered aerofoils and lift at zero angle of attack.

### 2.1 What is Circulation?

Circulation ($\Gamma$, Gamma) is a concept in fluid dynamics representing the "spinning" or "swirling" motion of a fluid around a closed curve. It is defined as the line integral of the velocity vector along a closed contour:

$$ \Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$

*   **Units:** $\Gamma$ has units of velocity $\times$ length (e.g., m²/s).
*   **Physical Interpretation:** A non-zero circulation around an object implies a net swirling motion of the fluid around it.

### 2.2 The Kutta-Joukowski Theorem

This is the cornerstone of circulation theory for lift generation. It states that the lift per unit span ($L'$) on a 2D aerofoil is directly proportional to the freestream velocity ($V$), air density ($\rho$), and the circulation ($\Gamma$) around the aerofoil.

$$ L' = \rho V \Gamma $$

*   **Implication:** Lift is directly related to the amount of circulation generated by the aerofoil. More circulation means more lift.

### 2.3 How Circulation is Generated in Aerofoils

Circulation is not an inherent property of the aerofoil but is induced by the flow.

*   **Mechanism:** The shape of the aerofoil, especially its camber (curvature of the mean line), forces the air to travel at different speeds on the upper and lower surfaces.
*   **Bernoulli's Principle:** Higher velocity on the upper surface leads to lower pressure, and lower velocity on the lower surface leads to higher pressure. This pressure difference creates an upward force.
*   **Circulation as the Cause:** The differing velocities on the upper and lower surfaces are a manifestation of circulation. Imagine a vortex superimposed on a uniform flow. The combination creates higher velocity on one side and lower on the other.

### 2.4 The Role of the Kutta Condition

For a finite amount of circulation to be established and maintained around an aerofoil, the flow must leave the trailing edge smoothly without infinite velocity. This condition is known as the Kutta condition.

*   **The Problem:** If the flow were to wrap around the trailing edge, it would create infinite velocities, which is physically impossible.
*   **The Solution:** The flow must separate from the trailing edge in a way that allows the circulation to be finite. This naturally occurs at a non-zero angle of attack for cambered aerofoils and at the same angle of attack as the camber for a symmetric aerofoil.
*   **Consequence:** The Kutta condition dictates the magnitude of circulation that will form on an aerofoil for a given shape and angle of attack.

### 2.5 Combining Circulation and Freestream Flow

The total velocity ($v_{total}$) at any point around the aerofoil is the vector sum of the freestream velocity ($V_{\infty}$) and the velocity induced by the circulation ($v_{\text{circ}}$).

*   **Velocity Distribution:** The circulation creates a velocity field around the aerofoil. The sum of this induced velocity and the freestream velocity results in a non-uniform velocity distribution over the aerofoil surface.
*   **Lift Generation:** The pressure distribution over the aerofoil, determined by these velocities via Bernoulli's equation, leads to the net lift force.

### 2.6 Characteristics Explained by Circulation Theory

Circulation theory excels at explaining several key characteristics that simple momentum theory struggles with.

*   **Lift at Zero Angle of Attack (for Cambered Aerofoils):** A cambered aerofoil will have circulation even at zero angle of attack due to its shape and the Kutta condition. This leads to lift.
*   **Stall:** While circulation theory itself doesn't fully predict stall (which is a flow separation phenomenon), it explains that as angle of attack increases, circulation (and thus lift) increases until flow separation begins to disrupt the established circulation pattern.
*   **Aerofoil Shape Influence:** The curvature (camber) and thickness distribution of an aerofoil directly influence the circulation it can generate, explaining why different aerofoils have different lift characteristics.
*   **Angle of Attack vs. Lift:** The linear relationship between lift and angle of attack ($L' \propto \alpha$) for small angles is a direct consequence of how circulation changes with angle of attack.

**Relevance to Textbooks:**

*   **Anderson (Fundamentals Of Aerodynamics):** This is a primary source for detailed explanations of circulation, the Kutta-Joukowski theorem, and the Kutta condition. It will likely cover the superposition of flows and Bernoulli's equation application.
*   **Houghton, Carpenter, Collicott, & Valentine (Aerodynamics for Engineering Students):** This reference book will provide a rigorous mathematical treatment of circulation theory and its applications to aerofoils, including potential flow theory.
*   **Kermode, Barnard, & Philpott (Mechanics Of Flight):** May offer a conceptual introduction to circulation and its role in lift.

## 3. Aerofoil Characteristics Derived from Theory

Both momentum and circulation theories help us understand the fundamental characteristics of aerofoils.

### 3.1 Lift Curve

The lift curve plots lift coefficient ($C_L$) against angle of attack ($\alpha$).

*   **$C_L$:** A dimensionless parameter representing lift. $L = C_L \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 A$.
*   **Shape of the Lift Curve:**
    *   **Linear Region:** For small angles of attack, $C_L$ increases approximately linearly with $\alpha$. This is predicted by circulation theory, where circulation is proportional to $\alpha$.
        *   **Slope:** The slope of the lift curve ($dC_L/d\alpha$) for a 2D aerofoil is a key characteristic. For thin aerofoils, it's approximately $2\pi$ (radians).
    *   **Stall:** At a critical angle of attack ($\alpha_{stall}$), the flow separates from the upper surface, causing a rapid decrease in lift. This is a non-linear phenomenon beyond the scope of ideal circulation theory but is a critical characteristic.
    *   **Zero Lift Angle of Attack ($\alpha_{ZL}$):** The angle of attack at which zero lift is generated. For symmetric aerofoils, $\alpha_{ZL} = 0$. For cambered aerofoils, $\alpha_{ZL}$ is negative.

**Example:** A typical aerofoil might have $dC_L/d\alpha \approx 0.10$ to $0.11$ per degree.

### 3.2 Drag Curve

The drag curve plots drag coefficient ($C_D$) against angle of attack ($\alpha$).

*   **$C_D$:** A dimensionless parameter representing drag. $D = C_D \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 A$.
*   **Shape of the Drag Curve:**
    *   **Low Angles of Attack:** $C_D$ is relatively low and increases slowly. This drag is primarily **pressure drag** due to the shape and viscous effects (form drag) and some **skin friction drag**.
    *   **Stall Region:** As the angle of attack increases towards stall, flow separation increases, leading to a significant rise in pressure drag.
    *   **Beyond Stall:** After stall, $C_D$ often reaches a peak and may decrease slightly or remain high.

### 3.3 Moment Curve

The moment curve plots pitching moment coefficient ($C_m$) about a reference point against angle of attack ($\alpha$).

*   **$C_m$:** A dimensionless parameter representing pitching moment. $M = C_m \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 A c$, where $c$ is the chord length.
*   **Aerodynamic Center:** For thin aerofoils and at small angles of attack, the pitching moment is often nearly constant, indicating a point on the chord where the moment is independent of the angle of attack. This is the **aerodynamic center**, typically located at the quarter-chord point ($c/4$).
*   **Zero Moment Angle of Attack:** The angle of attack at which the pitching moment is zero.

### 3.4 Angle of Attack and Camber Effects

*   **Angle of Attack ($\alpha$):** The primary control for lift. Increasing $\alpha$ increases lift (up to stall) and usually increases drag.
*   **Camber:** The curvature of the aerofoil's mean line.
    *   **Positive Camber:** Increases lift at zero angle of attack, shifts the zero-lift angle of attack to negative values, and can increase stall angle.
    *   **Negative Camber:** Decreases lift at zero angle of attack, shifts the zero-lift angle of attack to positive values.

### 3.5 Pressure Distribution

*   **Bernoulli's Equation:** $P + \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 = \text{constant}$ (for inviscid flow).
*   **Aerofoil Surfaces:** The velocity on the upper surface is generally higher than the freestream velocity, and lower on the lower surface (in the region of circulation). This leads to lower pressure on the upper surface and higher pressure on the lower surface, resulting in lift.
*   **Flow Separation:** At high angles of attack, the adverse pressure gradient on the upper surface can cause the boundary layer to separate, disrupting the smooth flow and leading to stall.

**Relevance to Textbooks:**

*   **Anderson (Introduction to Flight):** Provides a good overview of the lift, drag, and moment curves and their relationship to angle of attack. It will explain how to interpret these curves for aircraft performance.
*   **Kermode, Barnard, & Philpott (Mechanics Of Flight):** Will cover the practical aspects of these curves and their significance in determining flight characteristics.
*   **Houghton, Carpenter, Collicott, & Valentine (Aerodynamics for Engineering Students):** Will offer detailed analysis of pressure distributions, boundary layer effects leading to stall, and how these relate to the observed lift and drag curves.

## 4. Connecting Theories and Characteristics

It's essential to understand how momentum and circulation theories work together (or as different perspectives) to explain aerofoil characteristics.

*   **Momentum Theory:** Good for a basic understanding of force generated by deflecting fluid mass. Useful for initial concepts and for high-lift devices (like flaps) where significant mass deflection is involved.
*   **Circulation Theory:** Provides the more accurate and complete explanation for lift generation, especially for typical aerofoil shapes, lift at zero angle, and the linear lift-curve slope. It is the foundation for most aerodynamic analysis of lift.
*   **Combined Effect:** The pressure distribution around the aerofoil, which causes lift, is a consequence of the combined flow field resulting from the freestream and the circulation induced by the aerofoil's shape and angle of attack. The downward deflection of air by the entire aerofoil (as seen by momentum theory) is ultimately caused by this circulation-induced velocity field.

## 6. Key Points to Remember

*   **Momentum Theory:** Lift is generated by deflecting air downwards (Newton's 2nd Law). It's a simplified model with limitations.
*   **Circulation Theory:** Lift is proportional to the product of air density, freestream velocity, and circulation ($\Gamma$) around the aerofoil (Kutta-Joukowski Theorem: $L' = \rho V \Gamma$).
*   **Kutta Condition:** Ensures finite velocity at the trailing edge, dictating the amount of circulation.
*   **Lift Curve:** Shows the linear relationship between lift coefficient ($C_L$) and angle of attack ($\alpha$) up to stall.
*   **Drag Curve:** Shows how drag coefficient ($C_D$) varies with $\alpha$, typically increasing significantly near stall.
*   **Moment Curve:** Shows pitching moment, often nearly constant at the aerodynamic center ($c/4$).
*   **Camber:** Affects the zero-lift angle of attack and the lift coefficient at zero angle of attack.
*   **Bernoulli's Principle:** Explains how velocity differences on the aerofoil surfaces lead to pressure differences and thus lift.

## 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Explain the fundamental difference between momentum theory and circulation theory in explaining lift generation by an aerofoil. Which theory is more comprehensive and why?

**Answer:**
Momentum theory explains lift as the reaction force to the downward momentum imparted to a mass of air. It's a simplified view based on Newton's laws. Circulation theory explains lift through the concept of circulation ($\Gamma$) around the aerofoil, where lift is proportional to $\rho V \Gamma$ (Kutta-Joukowski Theorem). Circulation theory is more comprehensive because it can explain lift at zero angle of attack for cambered aerofoils and provides a more accurate basis for understanding the lift curve and the effect of aerofoil shape, by incorporating the Kutta condition and Bernoulli's principle.

**Question 2:**
What is the Kutta condition, and why is it important for circulation theory?

**Answer:**
The Kutta condition states that the flow must leave the sharp trailing edge of an aerofoil smoothly, without infinite velocity. It is important because it determines the specific magnitude of circulation that will be established around the aerofoil for a given shape and angle of attack. Without it, the circulation would be indeterminate.

**Question 3:**
A 2D aerofoil has a lift curve slope of $0.11$ per degree. If the zero-lift angle of attack is $-3$ degrees, what is the lift coefficient at an angle of attack of $5$ degrees?

**Answer:**
*   Lift curve slope ($a$) = $0.11$ $C_L$/degree
*   Zero-lift angle of attack ($\alpha_{ZL}$) = $-3$ degrees
*   Target angle of attack ($\alpha$) = $5$ degrees

The relationship between $C_L$ and $\alpha$ can be approximated as:
$C_L = a (\alpha - \alpha_{ZL})$

$C_L = 0.11 \text{ } C_L/\text{degree} \times (5 \text{ degrees} - (-3 \text{ degrees}))$
$C_L = 0.11 \times (5 + 3)$
$C_L = 0.11 \times 8$
$C_L = 0.88$

**Question 4:**
Describe how Bernoulli's principle contributes to lift generation on an aerofoil, as explained by circulation theory.

**Answer:**
Circulation theory explains that the flow around an aerofoil results in higher velocities on the upper surface and lower velocities on the lower surface (relative to the freestream, averaged over the chord). According to Bernoulli's principle ($P + \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 = \text{constant}$), where velocity is higher, pressure is lower, and where velocity is lower, pressure is higher. Therefore, the higher velocity on the upper surface leads to lower pressure there, and the lower velocity on the lower surface leads to higher pressure there. This pressure difference creates an upward force, which is lift.

**Question 5:**
Consider a symmetric aerofoil and a cambered aerofoil, both at zero angle of attack. Which aerofoil would you expect to produce lift, and why, according to circulation theory?

**Answer:**
The cambered aerofoil would be expected to produce lift at zero angle of attack. According to circulation theory, cambered aerofoils, due to their curved mean line and the Kutta condition, will establish circulation even at zero angle of attack. This circulation leads to a velocity difference across the surfaces (higher on the upper, lower on the lower), resulting in a pressure difference and thus lift. A symmetric aerofoil at zero angle of attack, in the absence of circulation, would ideally have equal velocities and pressures on both surfaces, producing no net lift.
