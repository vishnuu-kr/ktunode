# Module 3: 3D or Finite Aerofoils - Effect of Releasing the Wingtips

## Topic: Skin Friction and Form Drag - Changes in Finite Wing Plan Shape

This topic explores how the shape of a finite wing, particularly its planform, influences skin friction and form drag, and how these changes relate to the overall aerodynamic performance of an aircraft. This directly addresses **Course Outcome 2 (CO2)** by examining the impact of finite wing plan shapes on aerodynamic performance.

### 1. Introduction to Finite Wings and Aerodynamic Drag

A finite wing, unlike an infinite wing, has wingtips. This has profound implications for airflow and the resulting forces. The presence of wingtips is the fundamental reason for **induced drag**, a phenomenon directly linked to the three-dimensional nature of flow over a wing. However, this topic focuses on how the plan shape of a finite wing influences the more "conventional" drag components: skin friction drag and form drag.

### 2. Understanding Drag Components

Before delving into plan shape effects, it's crucial to understand the fundamental types of drag acting on an aircraft:

*   **Skin Friction Drag:**
    *   **Definition:** Drag resulting from the friction between the air molecules and the surface of the aircraft. It is caused by the viscosity of the air.
    *   **Dependence:** Proportional to the wetted area (total surface area in contact with the air) and the square of the velocity. It is also dependent on the Reynolds number and the nature of the boundary layer (laminar or turbulent).
    *   **Kermode (Barnard & Philpott):** Discusses the fundamental nature of skin friction and its dependence on surface roughness and the state of the boundary layer.
    *   **Anderson (Fundamentals of Aerodynamics):** Provides a more detailed mathematical treatment of skin friction, including laminar and turbulent boundary layer profiles and their drag coefficients.

*   **Form Drag (Pressure Drag):**
    *   **Definition:** Drag resulting from the pressure distribution around the aircraft's surfaces. It is caused by the separation of airflow and the resulting low-pressure wake behind bluff bodies.
    *   **Dependence:** Primarily dependent on the shape (form) of the body and the angle of attack. For streamlined bodies like airfoils, form drag is generally much smaller than skin friction drag.
    *   **Kermode (Barnard & Philpott):** Introduces form drag as a consequence of flow separation and the resulting pressure differences.
    *   **Anderson (Fundamentals of Aerodynamics):** Explains how flow separation, particularly at higher angles of attack, leads to a significantly expanded wake and increased form drag. It also highlights the importance of streamlining to minimize form drag.

*   **Induced Drag:**
    *   **Definition:** Drag created as a by-product of lift on a finite wing. It arises from the spanwise variation of lift, leading to wingtip vortices.
    *   **Dependence:** Inversely proportional to the aspect ratio (span squared divided by wing area) and the square of the lift coefficient.
    *   **Kermode (Barnard & Philpott):** Introduces induced drag as a consequence of finite wings and explains its relationship with wingtip vortices.
    *   **Anderson (Fundamentals of Aerodynamics):** Provides a comprehensive treatment of induced drag using lifting-line theory, explaining its origin from horseshoe vortices and its impact on aircraft performance. This is crucial for **CO2**.

*   **Wave Drag (Supersonic/Transonic):**
    *   **Definition:** Drag that occurs at supersonic speeds due to the formation of shock waves.
    *   **Note:** While not the primary focus of this specific topic, it's important to be aware of this drag component at higher speeds.

### 3. The Impact of Finite Wing Plan Shape on Skin Friction and Form Drag

The **plan shape** of a wing refers to its outline as viewed from above. Common plan shapes include rectangular, tapered, swept, and delta wings. Changes in plan shape significantly influence the distribution of surface area and the nature of airflow, thereby affecting skin friction and form drag.

#### 3.1. Rectangular Wings

*   **Characteristics:** Constant chord length along the entire span.
*   **Skin Friction Drag:**
    *   **Surface Area:** For a given wing area, a rectangular wing has a larger span and a smaller chord compared to a tapered wing. This generally leads to a larger wetted area for the same wing area, potentially increasing skin friction drag.
    *   **Flow Characteristics:** The boundary layer development is relatively uniform along the span, except near the wingtips.
*   **Form Drag:**
    *   **Flow Separation:** Can be more susceptible to flow separation at the wingtips, especially at higher angles of attack, contributing to form drag.
    *   **Overall:** For a given lift, rectangular wings can experience higher induced drag compared to tapered wings due to a less favorable lift distribution. While not directly form drag, this interaction affects the overall drag profile.

#### 3.2. Tapered Wings

*   **Characteristics:** Chord length decreases from root to tip.
*   **Skin Friction Drag:**
    *   **Surface Area:** For a given wing area, a tapered wing typically has a smaller wetted area than a rectangular wing of the same area and span. This can lead to lower skin friction drag.
    *   **Flow Characteristics:** The taper can influence the boundary layer development along the span.
*   **Form Drag:**
    *   **Flow Separation:** The tapering can help to "guide" the flow towards the tips, potentially reducing the severity of flow separation compared to a rectangular wing, especially at the wingtips. This can lead to lower form drag.
    *   **Lift Distribution:** Tapered wings can achieve a more elliptical lift distribution (closer to the ideal for minimum induced drag) when designed appropriately. This reduces induced drag.

#### 3.3. Swept Wings

*   **Characteristics:** Wing leading edge is angled backward relative to the fuselage.
*   **Skin Friction Drag:**
    *   **Wetted Area:** The sweep angle influences the effective chord and span, which in turn affects the wetted area and thus skin friction drag. The relationship is complex and depends on the specific sweep angle and airfoil section.
    *   **Flow Characteristics:** Sweep introduces spanwise flow components, which can affect boundary layer development and potentially increase skin friction.
*   **Form Drag:**
    *   **Flow Separation:** Swept wings tend to have a delayed stall at the wingtips due to the spanwise flow component. This can reduce form drag at higher angles of attack.
    *   **Transonic/Supersonic Effects:** Sweep is crucial for delaying the onset of compressibility effects at transonic speeds, significantly reducing wave drag. This is a primary reason for swept wings.

#### 3.4. Delta Wings

*   **Characteristics:** Triangular plan shape.
*   **Skin Friction Drag:**
    *   **Surface Area:** Delta wings typically have a large leading edge sweep and a large aspect ratio for their planform. The large leading edge area can contribute to skin friction.
    *   **Flow Characteristics:** At higher angles of attack, delta wings generate strong leading-edge vortices, which can influence the boundary layer and the overall skin friction.
*   **Form Drag:**
    *   **Leading-Edge Vortices:** The defining feature of delta wings at moderate to high angles of attack is the formation of powerful leading-edge vortices. These vortices re-energize the upper surface flow, delaying stall and creating a "vortex lift" component. This complex flow can influence form drag in ways that are distinct from conventional airfoils.
    *   **Stall Characteristics:** Delta wings generally have excellent stall characteristics, with a gentle stall onset due to the vortex lift.

### 4. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Wetted Area:** The total surface area of the wing that is exposed to the airflow.
*   **Chord:** The distance from the leading edge to the trailing edge of an airfoil.
*   **Span:** The distance from wingtip to wingtip.
*   **Aspect Ratio (AR):** The ratio of the square of the wingspan to the wing area (AR = b²/S). A higher aspect ratio generally means a longer, narrower wing.
*   **Lift Distribution:** The variation of lift along the wingspan. An elliptical lift distribution is ideal for minimizing induced drag.
*   **Flow Separation:** The detachment of the airflow from the surface of the airfoil, leading to the formation of a wake and increased pressure drag (form drag).
*   **Boundary Layer:** A thin layer of air near the surface of the wing where viscous effects are significant.

### 5. Examples and Applications

*   **Gliders and Long-Range Aircraft:** Often utilize high aspect ratio, tapered wings to minimize induced drag for efficient cruising.
*   **Fighter Jets:** Employ swept wings or delta wings to achieve high speeds and delay compressibility effects, while also benefiting from the improved high-angle-of-attack handling characteristics of swept configurations.
*   **Transport Aircraft:** Typically have moderately swept, tapered wings to balance the need for good cruise efficiency with structural considerations and to manage compressibility effects at typical cruising speeds.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Explain how a change in wing plan shape from rectangular to tapered can affect skin friction drag.

**Answer 1:**
A tapered wing, for a given wing area and span, generally has a smaller wetted area compared to a rectangular wing. Since skin friction drag is proportional to the wetted area, a tapered wing would likely experience lower skin friction drag.

**Question 2:**
What is the primary advantage of swept wings in relation to drag, particularly at higher speeds?

**Answer 2:**
The primary advantage of swept wings is their ability to delay the onset of compressibility effects (e.g., shock waves) at transonic and supersonic speeds. This significantly reduces wave drag, which is a dominant drag component at these speeds. They also offer benefits in terms of stall characteristics by reducing tip stall.

**Question 3:**
Define aspect ratio and explain its relationship with induced drag.

**Answer 3:**
Aspect Ratio (AR) is defined as the square of the wingspan divided by the wing area (AR = b²/S). Induced drag is inversely proportional to the aspect ratio. Therefore, wings with higher aspect ratios (longer and narrower) experience lower induced drag for a given amount of lift.

**Question 4:**
How do leading-edge vortices on a delta wing influence the drag characteristics compared to a conventional airfoil?

**Answer 4:**
Leading-edge vortices on a delta wing, especially at higher angles of attack, re-energize the upper surface flow, delaying stall and creating "vortex lift." This complex flow can alter the pressure distribution and the nature of the boundary layer, influencing both skin friction and form drag in unique ways compared to conventional airfoils. While these vortices reduce the effective angle of attack at the trailing edge of the vortex core, the overall effect on drag is a trade-off between increased vortex formation drag and delayed stall.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Finite wings have wingtips, which are the root cause of induced drag.**
*   **Skin friction drag depends on wetted area, velocity, and boundary layer characteristics.**
*   **Form drag depends on the shape of the body and flow separation.**
*   **Wing plan shape significantly influences both skin friction and form drag by altering wetted area, flow patterns, and susceptibility to separation.**
*   **Tapered wings generally have lower skin friction drag and can be designed for more favorable lift distributions to reduce induced drag.**
*   **Swept wings are crucial for delaying compressibility effects and reducing wave drag at high speeds.**
*   **Delta wings utilize leading-edge vortices for enhanced high-angle-of-attack performance, which affects their drag characteristics.**
*   **Aspect ratio is a key parameter for minimizing induced drag.**
