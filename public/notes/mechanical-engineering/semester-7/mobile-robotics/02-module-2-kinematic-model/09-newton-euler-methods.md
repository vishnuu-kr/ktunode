# Mobile Robotics: Module 2: Kinematic Model - Newton-Euler Methods

## 1. Introduction to Newton-Euler Methods in Mobile Robotics

The Newton-Euler method is a fundamental approach to deriving the dynamic model of a robotic system. It's based on Newton's second law of motion ($\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$) and Euler's equation for rotational motion. For mobile robots, these methods are crucial for understanding how forces and torques applied to the robot translate into motion, and vice-versa. This understanding is essential for control, trajectory planning, and simulation.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Inertia:** A body's resistance to changes in its state of motion. For rigid bodies, this is characterized by mass and the distribution of mass (inertia tensor).
*   **Forces:** Interactions that tend to change the motion of an object.
*   **Torques:** Rotational forces that tend to cause an object to rotate.
*   **Linear Velocity:** The rate of change of linear position.
*   **Angular Velocity:** The rate of change of angular position.
*   **Linear Acceleration:** The rate of change of linear velocity.
*   **Angular Acceleration:** The rate of change of angular velocity.
*   **Centroidal Frame:** A reference frame attached to the center of mass of a rigid body.
*   **Non-Centroidal Frame:** A reference frame not attached to the center of mass.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Siegwart & Nourbakhsh (2011):** While this textbook primarily focuses on kinematics and locomotion, it provides the foundational understanding of how forces (like friction and actuation) affect mobile robot movement, which is the basis for dynamics. They discuss the "force-velocity" relationship in the context of locomotion.
*   **Corke (2011):** This book offers a detailed exposition of rigid-body dynamics, including the Newton-Euler formulation for manipulators, which can be readily adapted for mobile robots. It emphasizes the importance of coordinate transformations and the representation of forces and torques.
*   **Tzafestas (2000):** This text likely delves into the more mathematical aspects of robotic dynamics, providing a solid foundation for understanding the derivation of equations of motion.

## 2. Principles of Newton-Euler Methods

The Newton-Euler method decomposes the dynamic analysis of a rigid body into two parts:

### 2.1. Linear Motion Analysis (Newton's Second Law)

This part deals with the translation of the robot's center of mass.

**Equation:**

$$ \sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}_{c} $$

Where:
*   $\sum \mathbf{F}$ is the sum of all external forces acting on the rigid body.
*   $m$ is the mass of the rigid body.
*   $\mathbf{a}_{c}$ is the linear acceleration of the rigid body's center of mass.

**In a specific coordinate frame (e.g., inertial frame $I$):**

$$ \sum \mathbf{F}^I = m\mathbf{a}_{c}^I $$

**Important Considerations:**

*   **Forces:** These include forces from actuators (motors), contact forces (ground reaction forces, friction), gravity, and external disturbances.
*   **Center of Mass (CoM):** The point where the entire mass of the object can be considered concentrated.

### 2.2. Rotational Motion Analysis (Euler's Equation)

This part deals with the rotation of the rigid body about its center of mass.

**Equation:**

$$ \sum \boldsymbol{\tau}_{c} = \mathbf{I}_{c} \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I}_{c} \boldsymbol{\omega}) $$

Where:
*   $\sum \boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}$ is the sum of all external torques acting on the rigid body, *about the center of mass*.
*   $\mathbf{I}_{c}$ is the inertia tensor of the rigid body *about its center of mass*.
*   $\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ is the angular acceleration of the rigid body.
*   $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the angular velocity of the rigid body.
*   $\times$ denotes the cross product.

**Important Considerations:**

*   **Inertia Tensor ($\mathbf{I}_c$):** A 3x3 symmetric matrix that describes how mass is distributed relative to the axes of rotation. It is typically constant in the body-fixed frame.
    $$ \mathbf{I}_c = \begin{bmatrix} I_{xx} & I_{xy} & I_{xz} \\ I_{yx} & I_{yy} & I_{yz} \\ I_{zx} & I_{zy} & I_{zz} \end{bmatrix} $$
    In a principal axis frame, the off-diagonal elements are zero:
    $$ \mathbf{I}_c = \begin{bmatrix} I_x & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & I_z \end{bmatrix} $$
*   **Torques:** These include torques generated by actuators (e.g., steering motors, drive motors), torques due to forces not passing through the CoM, and gyroscopic effects.
*   **Coordinate Frames:** Euler's equation is most conveniently expressed in a frame rigidly attached to the body. If working in an inertial frame, transformations are needed.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Corke (2011):** Provides detailed derivations of Euler's equations for rigid bodies and the concept of the inertia tensor, which is directly applicable to mobile robots.
*   **Siegwart & Nourbakhsh (2011):** While not going into deep derivations, they discuss the effects of mass and inertia on robot behavior, particularly regarding turning radius and stability.

## 3. Applying Newton-Euler to Mobile Robots

Mobile robots, especially wheeled robots, have specific kinematic and dynamic characteristics. The Newton-Euler method is applied to each rigid body in the robot (e.g., the chassis, wheels).

### 3.1. Two-Wheeled Differential Drive Robot Example

Let's consider a simplified two-wheeled differential drive robot.

**Assumptions:**

*   The robot chassis is a rigid body.
*   The wheels are massless and frictionless (for simplification in this example, though in reality, tire dynamics are complex).
*   The robot moves on a flat horizontal surface.
*   The analysis is done in a 2D plane (for forward and turning motion), but the principles extend to 3D.

**Coordinate Frames:**

*   **Inertial Frame ($I$):** Fixed to the ground, typically $(x, y, z)$.
*   **Body Frame ($B$):** Attached to the robot's chassis, often at the origin of the wheels, with the $x$-axis pointing forward and the $y$-axis pointing to the left (or right).

**Robot State:**

The state of the robot can be described by its position $(x, y)$ and orientation $\theta$ in the inertial frame, and its linear and angular velocities $(\dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{\theta})$.

**Forces and Torques on the Chassis:**

1.  **Driving Forces ($F_{left}$, $F_{right}$):** Applied by the motors to the wheels, causing translational motion. These forces act at the wheel contact points.
2.  **Friction Forces ($F_{friction, left}$, $F_{friction, right}$):** Opposing motion.
3.  **Ground Reaction Forces:** Vertical forces supporting the robot's weight.
4.  **Motor Torques ($\tau_{left}$, $\tau_{right}$):** Applied by the motors to rotate the wheels.
5.  **Gravity:** Acts on the center of mass.
6.  **Centrifugal and Coriolis forces:** Arise due to the rotation of the body frame if not handled correctly in the formulation.

**Newton-Euler Derivation for the Chassis:**

Let the robot chassis have mass $m$ and inertia $I_z$ about the vertical axis through its center of mass. Let the center of mass be at a distance $d$ from the wheel axis.

**Linear Motion (in the inertial frame):**

*   **X-direction:** $m \ddot{x} = F_{friction, left} \cos \theta + F_{friction, right} \cos \theta - F_{gravity, x}$ (assuming gravity is vertical, its x-component is zero). The main forces contributing here are from the wheels, projected onto the x-axis. If we consider the forces from wheels acting at the contact points, these can be simplified to a total forward force and a turning force/torque. Let $F_{total, forward}$ be the sum of forces driving the robot forward.
    $$ m \ddot{x} = F_{total, forward} \cos \theta $$
*   **Y-direction:** $m \ddot{y} = F_{friction, left} \sin \theta + F_{friction, right} \sin \theta - F_{gravity, y}$. Similarly, if we consider the lateral forces from the wheels that contribute to turning:
    $$ m \ddot{y} = F_{total, lateral} \cos \theta $$

**Rotational Motion (about the vertical axis $z$ through the CoM):**

The net torque about the center of mass causes angular acceleration. The torques come from the driving forces acting at the wheel contact points.
Let $r$ be the wheel radius, and $b$ be the distance between the wheels (half-width $b/2$).
The driving forces from the wheels are $F_{left} = \tau_{left}/r$ and $F_{right} = \tau_{right}/r$.
These forces act at a distance $b/2$ from the robot's longitudinal axis.

The net torque about the center of mass (assuming CoM is on the longitudinal axis between wheels) is:
$$ \tau_{net, z} = (F_{right} \times \frac{b}{2}) - (F_{left} \times \frac{b}{2}) $$
Substituting $F = \tau/r$:
$$ \tau_{net, z} = (\frac{\tau_{right}}{r} \times \frac{b}{2}) - (\frac{\tau_{left}}{r} \times \frac{b}{2}) $$
$$ \tau_{net, z} = \frac{b}{2r} (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$

Using Euler's equation for rotation about the $z$-axis (which is aligned with the robot's heading $\theta$ for simplicity of this example):
$$ I_z \ddot{\theta} = \tau_{net, z} $$
$$ I_z \ddot{\theta} = \frac{b}{2r} (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$

**Relating Wheel Velocities to Robot Velocities (Kinematics connection):**

This is where kinematics and dynamics intertwine. The linear velocity of the robot's chassis in the forward direction $v$ is related to the wheel velocities.
For a differential drive robot, the velocity of the robot's center of rotation is $v = r \omega_{wheels}$ where $\omega_{wheels}$ is the average angular velocity of the wheels.
The forward velocity of the robot is $v = \frac{r}{2}(\omega_{left} + \omega_{right})$.
The angular velocity of the robot is $\dot{\theta} = \frac{r}{b}(\omega_{right} - \omega_{left})$.

If we relate motor torques to wheel angular accelerations ($\tau = I_{wheel} \dot{\omega}_{wheel}$), we can eventually derive accelerations.

**Simplified Dynamic Model:**

Combining the linear and rotational dynamics, we can express the robot's accelerations in terms of forces and torques.

*   **Forward Acceleration ($a_{forward}$):** If we consider the net forward force $F_{forward}$ and assume the robot's forward velocity is $v$:
    $$ m \dot{v} = F_{forward} $$
    Where $F_{forward}$ can be related to the net torque applied to the wheels and motor characteristics.
*   **Angular Acceleration ($\ddot{\theta}$):**
    $$ I_z \ddot{\theta} = \frac{b}{2r} (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$

**Important Note:** This is a simplified model. A complete dynamic model would consider:
*   The inertia of the wheels themselves.
*   The dynamics of the motors.
*   Friction at the wheel bearings.
*   Tire-slip dynamics.
*   Centrifugal and Coriolis forces when formulating in a non-inertial frame.
*   The location of the center of mass relative to the wheel axis.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Siegwart & Nourbakhsh (2011):** Chapter 7 discusses robot dynamics and provides simplified equations for wheeled robots, including the relationship between motor torques and angular velocities, and the forces at wheel contacts. They emphasize the role of mass and inertia in the robot's motion response.
*   **Corke (2011):** Provides the general framework for deriving dynamic equations using Newton-Euler, which can be specialized for mobile robots. He illustrates how to handle joint torques and forces.

## 4. Generalization and Other Mobile Robot Types

The Newton-Euler method is general and can be applied to any rigid body or articulated system.

### 4.1. Articulated Mobile Robots

For mobile robots with manipulators or complex suspension systems, the Newton-Euler method is applied to each link of the robot sequentially. This involves calculating the forces and torques acting on each link based on the motion of the preceding link and the applied joint torques.

### 4.2. Non-Holonomic Constraints

Mobile robots often have non-holonomic constraints, meaning their velocities are constrained in a way that cannot be integrated into path constraints on the configuration variables. For example, wheeled robots typically cannot move sideways directly. The Newton-Euler formulation must account for these constraints, often by introducing Lagrange multipliers or by reformulating the equations in a minimal set of generalized coordinates.

### 4.3. General Mobile Robots (e.g., Omnidirectional, Tracked)

The core principles remain the same: sum of forces = mass times acceleration, and sum of torques = inertia times angular acceleration. However, the specific forces, torques, and their application points will differ based on the locomotion mechanism.

*   **Omnidirectional Robots:** Use wheels that can move in multiple directions (e.g., mecanum wheels). The forces and torques are generated differently, but the dynamic equations still balance forces and torques.
*   **Tracked Robots:** Use continuous tracks. The dynamics involve the interaction of the track with the ground, the tension in the tracks, and the forces from the drive sprockets.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Siegwart & Nourbakhsh (2011):** Discusses different locomotion types and their kinematic and *qualitative* dynamic behaviors.
*   **Tzafestas (2000):** Likely provides a more in-depth treatment of formulating dynamics for systems with non-holonomic constraints.
*   **Corke (2011):** Covers the dynamics of general robotic arms, which provides the mathematical tools applicable to any articulated system, including those with mobile bases.

## 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A simplified mobile robot chassis can be modeled as a rectangular box with mass $m=50$ kg and inertia $I_z = 10$ kg m$^2$ about its vertical axis. The robot is driven by two wheels of radius $r=0.05$ m, separated by a distance $b=0.2$ m. The motors can provide a maximum torque of $\tau_{max} = 5$ Nm to each wheel. Assuming no friction and that the center of mass is on the axis midway between the wheels, derive the equation of motion for the robot's angular acceleration $\ddot{\theta}$ in terms of the applied motor torques.

**Answer 1:**
The net torque about the center of mass due to the wheel torques is:
$$ \tau_{net, z} = \frac{b}{2r} (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$
Using Euler's equation for rotation:
$$ I_z \ddot{\theta} = \tau_{net, z} $$
Substituting the given values:
$$ 10 \ddot{\theta} = \frac{0.2}{2 \times 0.05} (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$
$$ 10 \ddot{\theta} = \frac{0.2}{0.1} (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$
$$ 10 \ddot{\theta} = 2 (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$
$$ \ddot{\theta} = 0.2 (\tau_{right} - \tau_{left}) $$
The maximum angular acceleration occurs when $\tau_{right} = \tau_{max}$ and $\tau_{left} = -\tau_{max}$ (or vice-versa, for turning). For example, to turn left with maximum angular acceleration:
$$ \ddot{\theta}_{max} = 0.2 (5 - (-5)) = 0.2 \times 10 = 2 \text{ rad/s}^2 $$

**Question 2:**
Consider a mobile robot with mass $m$. If the robot is accelerating forward with linear acceleration $a$, and the only force contributing to this acceleration is the sum of the friction forces from the wheels acting parallel to the robot's forward direction, derive the relationship between the forward force and the linear acceleration. What is the effective forward force required to achieve this acceleration?

**Answer 2:**
According to Newton's second law for linear motion:
$$ \sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}_{c} $$
In the forward direction (let's call it the $x$-direction), the net force $F_{forward}$ is equal to mass times forward acceleration $\ddot{x}$ (or $a$):
$$ F_{forward} = m a $$
The effective forward force required is simply $ma$. This force is typically generated by the motors driving the wheels, which in turn exert forces on the ground, and through Newton's third law, the ground exerts reaction forces on the wheels, propelling the robot forward.

**Question 3 (Conceptual):**
Explain why the inertia tensor $\mathbf{I}_c$ is generally not diagonal in an arbitrary body-fixed frame, but can be diagonalized in a principal axis frame. How does the choice of coordinate frame affect the complexity of the dynamic equations derived using Newton-Euler methods?

**Answer 3:**
The inertia tensor describes how mass is distributed relative to the axes of rotation. In an arbitrary body-fixed frame, the axes might not align with the distribution of mass (e.g., if the frame is tilted relative to the robot's geometric axes). This misalignment leads to off-diagonal terms ($I_{xy}, I_{xz}, I_{yz}$) in the inertia tensor. These off-diagonal terms represent products of inertia, which couple rotations about different axes.

In a *principal axis frame*, the axes are chosen such that they align with the directions of maximum and minimum inertia. In this frame, the products of inertia are zero, and the inertia tensor becomes diagonal:
$$ \mathbf{I}_c^{\text{principal}} = \begin{bmatrix} I_x & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & I_z \end{bmatrix} $$
where $I_x, I_y, I_z$ are the principal moments of inertia.

The choice of coordinate frame significantly affects the complexity.
*   **Principal Axis Frame:** Euler's equation simplifies considerably:
    $$ \sum \boldsymbol{\tau}_{c} = \mathbf{I}_c^{\text{principal}} \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I}_c^{\text{principal}} \boldsymbol{\omega}) $$
    If the angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is expressed in this frame, the cross product term becomes simpler, and the equations for each component of angular acceleration are decoupled if $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is aligned with a principal axis.
*   **Arbitrary Body Frame:** The equations are more complex due to the presence of off-diagonal terms in $\mathbf{I}_c$, which introduce coupling between different rotational motions. This requires more careful calculation of the cross-product term.

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Newton's Second Law ($\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}$):** Governs the linear motion of the robot's center of mass.
*   **Euler's Equation ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{I}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\omega})$):** Governs the rotational motion of the robot about its center of mass.
*   **Inertia Tensor ($\mathbf{I}_c$):** Key to rotational dynamics; represents mass distribution. It's a property of the rigid body and can be expressed in different coordinate frames.
*   **Coordinate Frames:** The choice of an inertial frame and body-fixed frames is crucial. Transformations between frames are often necessary.
*   **Forces and Torques:** All forces and torques acting on the robot must be identified and correctly accounted for.
*   **Simplification:** For mobile robots, the dynamics are often simplified by assuming certain axes are aligned or by focusing on specific degrees of freedom (e.g., 2D motion).
*   **Coupling:** Dynamics can be coupled (e.g., linear motion affecting rotational motion through forces not acting at the CoM, or vice-versa).
