# Mobile Robotics: Module 3 - Sensors for Mobile Robot Navigation
## Topic: Wheel/Motor Sensors

This module focuses on sensors that directly measure the motion and state of a mobile robot's wheels and motors. These sensors are crucial for odometry, which is the process of estimating a robot's position and orientation based on its own motion.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Odometry:** The process of estimating a robot's pose (position and orientation) by integrating measurements of its own motion. Wheel encoders are a primary sensor for odometry.
*   **Pose:** A robot's state in the environment, typically described by its position (x, y) and orientation (theta).
*   **Encoder:** A sensor that converts the angular or linear displacement of a shaft into a sequence of electrical pulses.
*   **Counts per Revolution (CPR):** A measure of encoder resolution, indicating how many pulses are generated for one full rotation of the encoder's shaft. Higher CPR means higher resolution.
*   **Ticks:** The individual pulses generated by an encoder.
*   **Dead Reckoning:** A method of navigation that relies on estimating current position from a known past position by using a model of motion and its controlling parameters (e.g., wheel speeds). Odometry is a form of dead reckoning.
*   **Drift:** The accumulation of errors in odometry over time, leading to a divergence between the estimated pose and the true pose.
*   **Slip:** The phenomenon where a wheel rotates but does not produce corresponding linear motion of the robot due to lack of traction.

### 1. Rotary Encoders

Rotary encoders are the most common type of wheel/motor sensors used for odometry in mobile robots. They measure the rotational speed and displacement of the motor or wheel shaft.

**1.1 Types of Rotary Encoders:**

*   **Incremental Encoders:**
    *   **Principle:** Generate a series of pulses as the shaft rotates. The number of pulses is proportional to the amount of rotation, and the frequency of pulses is proportional to the speed.
    *   **Output:** Typically have two output channels, A and B, which are phase-shifted by 90 degrees. This phase shift allows for:
        *   **Direction Detection:** By observing the order in which channels A and B go high or low, the direction of rotation can be determined.
        *   **Quadrature Encoding:** This provides 4 times the resolution of a single channel. If channel A leads channel B, it's one direction; if B leads A, it's the other.
    *   **Index Pulse (Z or I Channel):** Many incremental encoders also have an index pulse that occurs once per revolution. This is useful for homing or absolute positioning reference.
    *   **Advantages:** Cost-effective, high resolution, good speed and position sensing.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Do not provide absolute position; require initialization or a home position. A power interruption will cause loss of position information.
    *   **Reference:** *Siegwart & Nourbakhsh (2011)* extensively discuss the use of incremental encoders for odometry in Chapter 4, "Locomotion and Actuation." They highlight how the quadrature output is used to track both distance and direction.

    *   **Example:** A robot with a wheel diameter of 0.1 meters and an encoder with 1000 CPR.
        *   Circumference = $\pi \times \text{diameter} = \pi \times 0.1 \, \text{m} \approx 0.314 \, \text{m}$
        *   For one revolution of the wheel, the encoder generates 1000 ticks.
        *   Distance per tick = Circumference / CPR = $0.314 \, \text{m} / 1000 \, \text{ticks} \approx 0.000314 \, \text{m/tick}$

*   **Absolute Encoders:**
    *   **Principle:** Provide a unique digital code for each shaft position. They can directly report the absolute angle of the shaft, even after power cycles.
    *   **Output:** Digital output representing the shaft's angle.
    *   **Advantages:** Retain position information even after power loss, no need for homing.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Generally more expensive and have lower resolution than incremental encoders for the same price point.
    *   **Application:** Less common for wheel odometry on typical mobile robots due to cost, but useful for joints or applications requiring continuous absolute position.

**1.2 Using Rotary Encoders for Odometry:**

*   **Measuring Wheel Rotation:** By counting the ticks from the incremental encoder attached to a motor or wheel, the amount of rotation ($\Delta \theta_{wheel}$) can be calculated.
*   **Calculating Linear Distance:** The linear distance traveled by the wheel along the ground can be calculated:
    $d = \Delta \theta_{wheel} \times R$, where $R$ is the wheel radius.
*   **Estimating Robot Displacement:** For a differential drive robot with two independently driven wheels, the robot's linear and angular velocity can be estimated from the wheel velocities.
    *   Let $v_L$ and $v_R$ be the linear velocities of the left and right wheels, respectively.
    *   $v_L = \omega_L \times R$ and $v_R = \omega_R \times R$, where $\omega_L$ and $\omega_R$ are the angular velocities of the left and right wheels.
    *   The robot's linear velocity ($v_x$) and angular velocity ($\omega$) can be approximated using these wheel velocities and the robot's track width ($W$).
        *   $v_x \approx \frac{v_L + v_R}{2}$
        *   $\omega \approx \frac{v_R - v_L}{W}$
    *   These velocities can then be integrated over time to update the robot's pose.
*   **Reference:** *Corke (2011)* in Chapter 3, "Kinematics," explains how wheel velocities are related to the robot's pose for various robot configurations, including differential drive, which is fundamental to using wheel encoder data. *Siegwart & Nourbakhsh (2011)* also provide detailed derivations for differential drive kinematics in their Chapter 4.

**1.3 Motor Encoders vs. Wheel Encoders:**

*   **Motor Encoders:** Mounted directly on the motor shaft. Measure the motor's rotation, which may be affected by gear reduction ratios.
*   **Wheel Encoders:** Mounted directly on the wheel shaft (or an extension). Directly measure the wheel's rotation.
*   **Considerations:**
    *   If using motor encoders, the gear ratio of the motor's gearbox must be known to translate motor rotation to wheel rotation.
    *   Wheel encoders are generally preferred for more accurate odometry as they directly measure the interaction with the ground.

### 2. Motor Current/Torque Sensors

While not directly measuring displacement, motor current sensors can provide indirect information about wheel behavior.

*   **Principle:** Motors draw more current when encountering higher loads. Increased current can indicate:
    *   **Obstacles:** A wheel encountering an obstacle will experience increased resistance, leading to higher motor current.
    *   **Slippage:** When a wheel slips, it rotates faster than expected for the power applied. While this might initially seem to reduce load, a consistent slip can cause the motor to work harder to maintain a certain rotation rate against the loss of traction.
    *   **Acceleration/Deceleration:** Higher currents are needed for rapid changes in speed.
*   **Applications:**
    *   **Slippage Detection:** Monitoring current can help detect when wheels are slipping, allowing for correction or adaptation of motion.
    *   **Obstacle Detection (Limited):** A sudden, significant spike in current might indicate an impediment.
    *   **Estimating Force:** In some advanced systems, motor current can be used to estimate the torque applied to the wheels and, by extension, the forces the robot is exerting or experiencing.
*   **Limitations:**
    *   **Indirect Measurement:** Not a direct measure of distance or angle.
    *   **Variability:** Motor efficiency and load characteristics can vary, making precise calibration difficult.
    *   **Reference:** While *Siegwart & Nourbakhsh*, *Corke*, and *Tzafestas* focus more on direct kinematic sensors, the concept of motor load and its relation to robot behavior is a fundamental aspect of mobile robot dynamics discussed in the context of control. *Thrun, Burgard, & Fox (2005)* in "Probabilistic Robotics" might touch upon using such signals as sensor modalities for state estimation, albeit often fused with more direct measurements.

### 3. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) - While not *strictly* wheel/motor sensors, they are often used in conjunction for odometry correction.

*   **Principle:** IMUs contain accelerometers and gyroscopes to measure linear acceleration and angular velocity.
*   **Role in Odometry:**
    *   **Gyroscope:** Measures the rate of rotation. This can be integrated to estimate the change in orientation, providing a complementary measurement to the angular velocity derived from wheel encoders (especially for non-holonomic robots where wheel speed differences determine turning).
    *   **Accelerometer:** Measures linear acceleration. Can be integrated twice to estimate changes in position. However, accelerometers are very susceptible to noise and gravity, making direct position estimation challenging without sophisticated filtering.
*   **Fusion:** By fusing IMU data with wheel encoder data, more robust and accurate odometry can be achieved. The IMU can help correct for wheel slippage and improve the estimation of angular motion.
*   **Reference:** *Probabilistic Robotics (Thrun, Burgard, & Fox, 2005)* is a seminal work that extensively details sensor fusion techniques, including combining wheel odometry with IMU data using Kalman Filters or particle filters for improved state estimation.

### 4. Sources of Error in Wheel/Motor Sensors and Odometry:

Even with sophisticated sensors, odometry is prone to errors that accumulate over time, leading to "drift."

*   **Encoder Resolution:** Finite CPR means that very small rotations might not be detected or accurately counted.
*   **Encoder Accuracy/Calibration:** Inaccuracies in the specified CPR or the relationship between encoder ticks and actual distance.
*   **Wheel Slippage:**
    *   **Definition:** When a wheel rotates but does not translate the robot along the intended path due to lack of traction. This can happen on slippery surfaces, during rapid acceleration/deceleration, or when turning.
    *   **Impact:** Causes odometry to overestimate the distance traveled or the turning angle.
    *   **Example:** If a wheel slips during a turn, the robot's turning angle derived from wheel speed difference will be incorrect.
*   **Wheel Diameter Variations:** Changes in wheel diameter due to tire wear, temperature, or deformation under load can lead to systematic errors.
*   **Asymmetric Wheel Behavior:** Imperfections in wheels or their mounting can cause them to roll slightly differently.
*   **Motor Control Inaccuracies:** Inconsistent motor performance or control loops can lead to deviations from intended wheel speeds.
*   **Gearbox Backlash/Slop:** Play in the gearbox can lead to a delay in response or imprecise movement detection.
*   **Vibrations and Shocks:** External disturbances can cause spurious encoder counts or affect sensor readings.
*   **Sensor Noise:** Electrical noise can affect the signal integrity of encoder outputs.

*   **Reference:** *Siegwart & Nourbakhsh (2011)* discuss odometry error sources and modeling in detail in their sections on odometry and localization. *La Valle (2009)* also covers error propagation in motion models as part of his discussion on motion planning and localization.

---

### 5. Improving Odometry Accuracy:

*   **High-Resolution Encoders:** Using encoders with higher CPR reduces resolution-based errors.
*   **Accurate Calibration:** Carefully calibrate the relationship between encoder ticks, wheel diameter, and robot dimensions (e.g., track width).
*   **Sensor Fusion:**
    *   Combine wheel encoder odometry with IMU data (gyroscopes and accelerometers) using filtering techniques (e.g., Extended Kalman Filters, Unscented Kalman Filters, Particle Filters). This is a key topic in *Probabilistic Robotics (Thrun, Burgard, & Fox, 2005)*.
    *   Using a gyroscope for angular velocity can provide a more direct measurement of turning rate than inferring it from wheel speed differences, which is particularly useful when slippage is suspected.
*   **Slip Detection and Compensation:**
    *   Develop algorithms that detect slip (e.g., by comparing wheel velocities to estimates from other sensors like IMU or vision).
    *   When slip is detected, adjust the odometry estimate to compensate.
*   **Regular Re-localization:** Periodically re-localize the robot using external sensors (e.g., cameras, LiDAR, GPS) to correct accumulated drift.
*   **Well-Designed Drive System:** Use high-quality motors, gearboxes, and wheels with good traction.

### Practice Questions:

1.  **Question:** A mobile robot uses differential drive with wheels of radius 0.05 meters. Each wheel is equipped with an encoder that generates 500 pulses per revolution (CPR). If the left wheel encoder registers 2500 ticks and the right wheel encoder registers 2400 ticks in a small time interval, what is the estimated linear distance traveled by the robot and the estimated change in its orientation?
    *   **Answer:**
        *   Distance per tick for one wheel = (2 * $\pi$ * 0.05 m) / 500 ticks = 0.000628 m/tick
        *   Left wheel rotation = 2500 ticks * 0.000628 m/tick = 1.57 m
        *   Right wheel rotation = 2400 ticks * 0.000628 m/tick = 1.5072 m
        *   Assuming differential drive, linear distance traveled by the robot is the average of wheel distances: (1.57 m + 1.5072 m) / 2 = 1.5386 m.
        *   *Note: To calculate the turning angle, the track width (W) would be required. If we assume W = 0.2 m:*
        *   Left wheel velocity $v_L = 1.57 \, \text{m} / \Delta t$
        *   Right wheel velocity $v_R = 1.5072 \, \text{m} / \Delta t$
        *   Angular velocity $\omega \approx (v_R - v_L) / W = (1.5072 - 1.57) / 0.2 = -0.0314 \, \text{rad/s}$
        *   Change in orientation $\Delta \theta = \omega \times \Delta t$. If we assume $\Delta t = 1$ second, $\Delta \theta \approx -0.0314$ radians (a slight right turn).

2.  **Question:** What is the primary advantage of using an absolute encoder over an incremental encoder for a mobile robot's wheel position sensing, and why are incremental encoders still more commonly used for this purpose?
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Advantage of Absolute Encoder:** An absolute encoder retains its position information even after a power interruption. It can directly report the shaft's angle at any time.
        *   **Why Incremental Encoders are More Common:** Incremental encoders are typically significantly less expensive and offer higher resolution for a given cost compared to absolute encoders. For most mobile robot applications, a known starting position (homing) is sufficient, and the cost-effectiveness and resolution of incremental encoders make them the preferred choice.

3.  **Question:** Explain the concept of "drift" in odometry and list at least three common causes for it.
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Drift:** Drift refers to the gradual accumulation of errors in the estimated position and orientation of a mobile robot over time, as derived from its motion sensors (like wheel encoders). This leads to a divergence between the robot's estimated pose and its true pose in the environment.
        *   **Common Causes:**
            1.  **Wheel Slippage:** Wheels rotating without corresponding linear movement due to loss of traction.
            2.  **Encoder Resolution Limits:** Finite counts per revolution mean small movements may not be accurately registered.
            3.  **Wheel Diameter Variations:** Changes in wheel size due to wear, temperature, or pressure.
            4.  (Other valid answers include: sensor noise, inaccuracies in calibration, gearbox backlash).

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Wheel encoders are the workhorses of mobile robot odometry.** They directly measure wheel rotation.
*   **Incremental encoders are most common** due to their cost-effectiveness and high resolution.
*   **Odometry is a dead reckoning technique** and inherently prone to **drift** due to various error sources, primarily **wheel slippage**.
*   **Sensor fusion** (e.g., with IMUs) and **external re-localization** are crucial for mitigating odometry errors and achieving robust navigation.
*   Understanding the kinematics of the robot is essential to translate wheel motion into robot pose changes.
