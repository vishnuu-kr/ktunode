# Module 4: Jet Propulsion – Cycles and Analysis – Gas Turbine Engines

## Topic: Turbojet

This module delves into the fundamental principles of jet propulsion, focusing on gas turbine engines. We will specifically examine the turbojet engine, a foundational type of jet engine.

---

### 1. Introduction to Turbojet Engines

The turbojet is the simplest form of gas turbine engine used for jet propulsion. It operates on the **Brayton cycle** and produces thrust by expelling a high-velocity jet of exhaust gases.

*   **Core Principle:** The turbojet works by drawing in air, compressing it, mixing it with fuel and igniting it, and then expanding the hot gases through a turbine and nozzle to produce thrust.

*   **Key Components:**
    *   **Inlet (Diffuser):** Slows down the incoming air and increases its static pressure before it enters the compressor.
    *   **Compressor:** Increases the pressure and temperature of the air. This is typically an axial-flow or centrifugal compressor.
    *   **Combustor (Combustion Chamber):** Fuel is injected and mixed with the compressed air and ignited, leading to a rapid increase in temperature and volume of the gas.
    *   **Turbine:** Extracts energy from the high-temperature, high-pressure gas to drive the compressor and accessories.
    *   **Nozzle (Exhaust Nozzle):** Accelerates the hot gases to a high velocity, producing thrust. The shape of the nozzle is crucial for optimizing thrust.

---

### 2. The Brayton Cycle for a Turbojet Engine

The ideal turbojet engine operates on the **ideal Brayton cycle**, which consists of four thermodynamic processes.

*   **Process 1-2: Isentropic Compression (Compressor)**
    *   Air enters the compressor at state 1 and is compressed isentropically to state 2.
    *   Pressure and temperature increase.
    *   $P_2 > P_1$, $T_2 > T_1$.

*   **Process 2-3: Constant Pressure Heat Addition (Combustor)**
    *   Fuel is added and burned at constant pressure, increasing the temperature of the working fluid to state 3.
    *   $T_3 > T_2$.
    *   **Important Note:** In real engines, there is a slight pressure drop in the combustor.

*   **Process 3-4: Isentropic Expansion (Turbine and Nozzle)**
    *   The hot gases expand isentropically through the turbine and then through the nozzle to produce thrust.
    *   Pressure and temperature decrease.
    *   $P_4 < P_3$, $T_4 < T_3$.

*   **Process 4-1: Constant Pressure Heat Rejection (Exhaust)**
    *   In a closed cycle, heat is rejected at constant pressure. In an open-cycle turbojet, this corresponds to the exhaust gases being expelled into the atmosphere.

*   **Visual Representation:** The ideal Brayton cycle is typically represented on a Temperature-Entropy (T-s) diagram and a Pressure-Volume (P-v) diagram.

    *   **T-s Diagram:**
        *   1-2: Isentropic compression (upward sloping curve)
        *   2-3: Constant pressure heat addition (horizontal line at higher temperature)
        *   3-4: Isentropic expansion (downward sloping curve)
        *   4-1: Constant pressure heat rejection (horizontal line at lower temperature)

    *   **P-v Diagram:**
        *   1-2: Isentropic compression (curved line)
        *   2-3: Constant pressure heat addition (horizontal line)
        *   3-4: Isentropic expansion (curved line)
        *   4-1: Constant pressure heat rejection (horizontal line)

*   **Key Ratios:**
    *   **Pressure Ratio ($r_p$):** $P_2/P_1$ (This is a critical parameter affecting engine performance).
    *   **Temperature Ratio ($T_3/T_2$):** Related to the heat added.

**Reference:** Refer to Chapter 11 in *Gas Turbines* by V. Ganesan for a detailed explanation of the Brayton cycle.

### 3. Thrust Generation in a Turbojet Engine

Thrust is the force that propels the aircraft forward. It is generated by the momentum change of the air passing through the engine.

*   **Newton's Second Law of Motion:** The rate of change of momentum of a fluid is equal to the net force acting on it.
    *   Thrust is essentially the reaction force to the expulsion of mass at high velocity.

*   **Thrust Equation:**
    *   **Gross Thrust ($F_g$):** The force produced by the expulsion of exhaust gases.
        $F_g = \dot{m}_a (V_e - V_0) + (P_e - P_0) A_e$
        where:
            *   $\dot{m}_a$: Mass flow rate of air
            *   $V_e$: Velocity of exhaust gases at the nozzle exit
            *   $V_0$: Velocity of the aircraft (forward speed)
            *   $P_e$: Pressure of exhaust gases at the nozzle exit
            *   $P_0$: Ambient static pressure
            *   $A_e$: Area of the nozzle exit

    *   **Net Thrust ($F_{net}$):** The gross thrust minus the ram drag (force opposing the motion due to air entering the engine).
        $F_{net} = F_g - \dot{m}_a V_0$
        For a nozzle operating at ambient pressure ($P_e = P_0$), the thrust equation simplifies to:
        $F_{net} = \dot{m}_a (V_e - V_0)$

*   **Factors Affecting Thrust:**
    *   **Mass flow rate of air ($\dot{m}_a$):** Higher mass flow leads to higher thrust.
    *   **Exhaust velocity ($V_e$):** Higher exhaust velocity leads to higher thrust.
    *   **Aircraft speed ($V_0$):** Thrust decreases as aircraft speed increases.
    *   **Nozzle Pressure Ratio ($P_e/P_0$):** If $P_e > P_0$, there is an additional pressure thrust component.

**Example:** A turbojet engine flying at 200 m/s with an exhaust velocity of 500 m/s and an air mass flow rate of 50 kg/s will produce a net thrust of:
$F_{net} = 50 \, \text{kg/s} \times (500 \, \text{m/s} - 200 \, \text{m/s}) = 50 \times 300 \, \text{N} = 15000 \, \text{N}$.

**Reference:** Chapter 4 in *Gas Turbine & Jet Rocket Propulsion* by Mathur M L covers thrust calculation.

### 4. Performance Analysis of a Turbojet Engine

Analyzing the performance of a turbojet involves understanding its efficiency and specific thrust.

*   **Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$):** The ratio of the useful work done (or kinetic energy imparted to the exhaust jet) to the heat supplied.
    $\eta_{th} = \frac{\text{Kinetic Energy of Jet}}{\text{Heat Supplied}}$
    For an ideal turbojet, it is related to the pressure ratio:
    $\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}$
    where $\gamma$ is the ratio of specific heats.

*   **Propulsive Efficiency ($\eta_p$):** The ratio of the propulsive power (thrust power) to the kinetic energy of the jet.
    $\eta_p = \frac{F_{net} \times V_0}{\dot{m}_a (V_e^2 - V_0^2)/2}$
    $\eta_p = \frac{2 V_0}{V_e + V_0}$

*   **Overall Efficiency ($\eta_o$):** The product of thermal efficiency and propulsive efficiency.
    $\eta_o = \eta_{th} \times \eta_p$

*   **Specific Thrust ($F_{sp}$):** The net thrust per unit mass flow rate of air.
    $F_{sp} = \frac{F_{net}}{\dot{m}_a} = V_e - V_0$

*   **Specific Impulse ($I_{sp}$):** A measure of the efficiency of a rocket or jet engine. It is the thrust produced per unit weight flow rate of propellant. For jet engines, it can be approximated as:
    $I_{sp} = \frac{\text{Thrust}}{\text{Weight Flow Rate of Fuel}} \approx \frac{F_{net}}{\dot{m}_f \times g}$
    Alternatively, it is related to the engine's thrust and fuel consumption rate:
    $I_{sp} = \frac{V_e}{g_0} - \frac{V_0}{g_0}$ (where $g_0$ is standard gravity)

*   **Maximum Thrust Conditions:** Thrust is generally maximized at low flight speeds and high exhaust velocities.

**Important Point:** Propulsive efficiency is high when the exhaust velocity ($V_e$) is close to the aircraft speed ($V_0$). However, for maximum thrust, a large difference between $V_e$ and $V_0$ is desired. This is a trade-off.

**Reference:** Chapter 6 in *Gas Turbine Theory* by H. Cohen provides a comprehensive analysis of turbojet performance.

### 5. Variations and Limitations of Turbojets

While simple, turbojets have certain limitations that led to the development of other jet engine types.

*   **Variations:**
    *   **Afterburning Turbojet:** An afterburner section is added after the turbine to inject more fuel and increase exhaust temperature and velocity, significantly boosting thrust. This improves thrust but at the cost of greatly increased fuel consumption and reduced thermal efficiency.

*   **Limitations:**
    *   **Low Propulsive Efficiency at Low Speeds:** When the aircraft speed ($V_0$) is much lower than the exhaust velocity ($V_e$), the propulsive efficiency is poor. This makes turbojets less suitable for take-off and low-speed flight.
    *   **High Fuel Consumption:** Compared to turbofan engines, turbojets are generally less fuel-efficient, especially at subsonic speeds.
    *   **Noise:** Turbojets tend to be very noisy due to the high velocity of the exhaust jet.

**Reference:** Chapter 12 in *The Jet Engine* by Rolls Royce discusses the evolution and characteristics of various jet engines, including turbojets.

---

### 6. Turbojet Applications

*   **Early Jet Aircraft:** Turbojets were used in the early development of jet-powered aircraft for military and some civilian applications.
*   **Supersonic Flight:** They are still used in some high-speed military aircraft and missiles where their performance at high Mach numbers is advantageous.

### 7. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
An ideal turbojet engine has a pressure ratio of 10. Calculate its ideal thermal efficiency.
(Given: $\gamma = 1.4$)

**Answer 1:**
The ideal thermal efficiency of a turbojet is given by:
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}$
Given $r_p = 10$ and $\gamma = 1.4$:
$(\gamma-1)/\gamma = (1.4-1)/1.4 = 0.4/1.4 \approx 0.2857$
$r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 10^{0.2857} \approx 1.9306$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{1.9306} \approx 1 - 0.5179 \approx 0.4821$
So, the ideal thermal efficiency is approximately **48.21%**.

**Question 2:**
A turbojet engine produces a net thrust of 20,000 N when flying at 250 m/s. If the mass flow rate of air through the engine is 60 kg/s, calculate the exhaust velocity of the gases.

**Answer 2:**
The net thrust equation is $F_{net} = \dot{m}_a (V_e - V_0)$.
Given $F_{net} = 20,000 \, \text{N}$, $\dot{m}_a = 60 \, \text{kg/s}$, and $V_0 = 250 \, \text{m/s}$.
$20,000 = 60 \times (V_e - 250)$
$\frac{20,000}{60} = V_e - 250$
$333.33 \approx V_e - 250$
$V_e \approx 333.33 + 250 = 583.33 \, \text{m/s}$
The exhaust velocity is approximately **583.33 m/s**.

**Question 3:**
Define specific impulse for a jet engine and list two factors that influence it.

**Answer 3:**
**Specific Impulse ($I_{sp}$)** is a measure of the efficiency of a jet engine, defined as the thrust produced per unit weight flow rate of fuel. It indicates how much thrust an engine can generate for a given amount of fuel consumed over time. A higher specific impulse means better fuel efficiency.

**Factors influencing Specific Impulse:**
1.  **Exhaust Velocity ($V_e$):** Higher exhaust velocity generally leads to higher specific impulse.
2.  **Aircraft Speed ($V_0$):** Higher aircraft speed (relative to exhaust velocity) can reduce specific impulse.
3.  **Fuel Consumption Rate:** Higher fuel efficiency (lower fuel consumption for a given thrust) leads to higher specific impulse.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   The turbojet is the simplest form of gas turbine engine for jet propulsion.
*   It operates on the **Brayton cycle**.
*   Thrust is generated by the momentum change of the exhaust gases: $F_{net} = \dot{m}_a (V_e - V_0)$.
*   **Thermal efficiency** is related to the pressure ratio and how effectively heat is converted to kinetic energy.
*   **Propulsive efficiency** is high when the exhaust velocity is close to the aircraft speed.
*   Turbojets are less fuel-efficient and have lower propulsive efficiency at low speeds compared to turbofans.
*   Afterburning significantly increases thrust but at the expense of fuel consumption.
