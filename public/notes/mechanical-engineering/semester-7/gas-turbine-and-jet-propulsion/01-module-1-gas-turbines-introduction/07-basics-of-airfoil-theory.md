### 1. Introduction to Airfoils in Gas Turbines

**Objective:** To understand the fundamental role of airfoils in gas turbine operation.

**Key Concept:** Airfoils are specially shaped surfaces designed to generate lift when moving through a fluid (like air). In gas turbines, these are the blades of the compressor and turbine.

*   **Compressor Blades:** Accelerate air and increase its pressure.
*   **Turbine Blades:** Extract energy from hot gases to drive the compressor and other machinery.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO3 (K4):** Understanding the characteristics of various components (blades) is crucial for analyzing gas turbine system performance.
*   **CO2 (K4):** The performance of axial flow compressors and turbines is directly dependent on the aerodynamic behavior of their blades.

**Textbook References:**
*   **Ganesan, V. (2017):** Introduces the basic geometry and aerodynamic principles of compressor and turbine blades.
*   **Yahya, S. M. (2011):** Provides detailed coverage of airfoil shapes and their application in turbomachinery.
*   **Mathur, M. L. (2010):** Explains the fundamental forces acting on airfoil sections within turbomachinery.

### 2. Airfoil Geometry and Terminology

**Objective:** To define and understand the standard geometric terms used to describe an airfoil.

**Key Concepts:** The shape of an airfoil is critical to its aerodynamic performance.

*   **Chord Line:** A straight line connecting the leading edge to the trailing edge.
    *   **Chord Length (c):** The length of the chord line.
*   **Leading Edge:** The forward-most point of the airfoil.
*   **Trailing Edge:** The rear-most point of the airfoil.
*   **Camber:** The curvature of the mean line of the airfoil from the chord line.
    *   **Mean Camber Line:** A line midway between the upper and lower surfaces of the airfoil.
    *   **Maximum Camber:** The maximum distance between the mean camber line and the chord line.
    *   **Camber Location:** The position along the chord where the maximum camber occurs.
*   **Thickness:** The maximum distance between the upper and lower surfaces, measured perpendicular to the chord line.
    *   **Maximum Thickness:** The largest thickness value.
    *   **Thickness Distribution:** How the thickness varies along the chord.
*   **Leading Edge Radius:** The radius of curvature at the leading edge.
*   **Trailing Edge Shape:** Can be sharp or rounded.

**Example:**
Consider a symmetrical airfoil (zero camber). The mean camber line is the same as the chord line. The shape is defined purely by the thickness distribution. A typical compressor blade might have a significant camber to direct the flow and increase pressure.

**Important Point to Remember:** The specific combination of camber and thickness distribution defines the airfoil's aerodynamic characteristics.

### 3. Aerodynamic Forces on an Airfoil

**Objective:** To understand the forces generated by an airfoil moving through a fluid.

**Key Concepts:** When an airfoil moves relative to a fluid, it experiences forces. These forces are typically resolved into lift and drag.

*   **Lift (L):** The component of the aerodynamic force perpendicular to the direction of the relative airflow.
    *   **Purpose in Compressor:** To turn the flow and increase static pressure.
    *   **Purpose in Turbine:** To produce torque by acting on the rotating blades.
*   **Drag (D):** The component of the aerodynamic force parallel to the direction of the relative airflow.
    *   Represents energy loss and reduces the efficiency of the turbomachine.
*   **Resultant Force (R):** The vector sum of lift and drag.

**Angle of Attack ($\alpha$):** The angle between the chord line of the airfoil and the direction of the relative airflow.

*   **Zero Angle of Attack:** Chord line is parallel to the airflow.
*   **Positive Angle of Attack:** Leading edge is raised relative to the airflow.
*   **Negative Angle of Attack:** Leading edge is lowered relative to the airflow.

**Reference Book Integration:**
*   **Cohen, H. (2019):** Thoroughly explains the decomposition of aerodynamic forces into lift and drag and their dependence on the angle of attack.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO1 (K3):** Understanding these forces is fundamental to the fluid dynamics principles governing gas turbine cycles.
*   **CO2 (K4):** Lift and drag are directly used to analyze compressor and turbine performance.

### 4. Lift Generation on an Airfoil

**Objective:** To explain the physical mechanisms responsible for generating lift.

**Key Concepts:** Lift is primarily generated due to the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces of the airfoil.

*   **Bernoulli's Principle:** States that for an inviscid flow, an increase in the speed of the fluid occurs simultaneously with a decrease in pressure or a decrease in the fluid's potential energy.
    *   **Application:** Air flowing over the curved upper surface travels a longer distance than air flowing under the flatter lower surface in the same amount of time. This results in higher velocity and lower pressure on the upper surface compared to the lower surface. The pressure difference creates an upward force (lift).
*   **Newton's Third Law (Action-Reaction):** The airfoil deflects the airflow downwards. By Newton's third law, the air exerts an equal and opposite upward force on the airfoil, contributing to lift.
    *   **Application:** The angle of attack and the airfoil's shape cause the air to be accelerated downwards (downwash). This downward momentum change of the air implies an upward force on the airfoil.
*   **Circulation Theory:** A more advanced concept that uses the idea of circulation around the airfoil to explain lift.

**Example:**
Imagine a symmetrical airfoil at a small positive angle of attack. The air is forced to accelerate around the upper surface, creating a lower pressure region. The air is also deflected downwards. Both effects contribute to lift.

**Textbook References:**
*   **Ganesan, V. (2017):** Explains Bernoulli's principle and its application to lift generation.
*   **Yahya, S. M. (2011):** Provides a more in-depth look at the combined effects of pressure difference and momentum change.

**Important Point to Remember:** Lift is a result of the *combination* of pressure distribution and the deflection of airflow.

### 5. Lift Coefficient ($C_L$) and Drag Coefficient ($C_D$)

**Objective:** To define and understand the dimensionless coefficients used to quantify lift and drag.

**Key Concepts:** Coefficients are used to normalize the forces and make them independent of flow velocity, air density, and airfoil size.

*   **Lift Coefficient ($C_L$):**
    $$C_L = \frac{L}{\frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 A}$$
    Where:
    *   $L$ = Lift force
    *   $\rho$ = Air density
    *   $V$ = Relative airflow velocity
    *   $A$ = Reference area (usually the planform area, which is chord length times span)

*   **Drag Coefficient ($C_D$):**
    $$C_D = \frac{D}{\frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 A}$$
    Where:
    *   $D$ = Drag force

*   **Lift-to-Drag Ratio ($L/D$ or $C_L/C_D$):**
    *   A crucial parameter indicating the aerodynamic efficiency of the airfoil. A higher ratio means more lift is generated for a given amount of drag.
    *   **Importance:** Maximizing the $L/D$ ratio is essential for efficient operation of compressor and turbine blades.

**Example:**
A specific airfoil at a given angle of attack might have a $C_L$ of 1.2 and a $C_D$ of 0.08. This gives an $L/D$ ratio of 15.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO1 (K3):** These coefficients are direct outputs of fluid dynamic analysis.
*   **CO2 (K4):** Used extensively in calculating compressor and turbine stage performance.

**Practice Question 1:**
An airfoil has a chord length of 0.5 m and a span of 1 m. If the relative airflow velocity is 100 m/s, the air density is 1.2 kg/m³, and the lift force is 6000 N, calculate the lift coefficient ($C_L$).

**Answer:**
Reference Area, $A = \text{chord length} \times \text{span} = 0.5 \text{ m} \times 1 \text{ m} = 0.5 \text{ m}^2$
$C_L = \frac{L}{\frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 A} = \frac{6000 \text{ N}}{\frac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \text{ kg/m}^3 \times (100 \text{ m/s})^2 \times 0.5 \text{ m}^2}$
$C_L = \frac{6000}{0.6 \times 10000 \times 0.5} = \frac{6000}{3000} = 2.0$

### 6. Airfoil Performance Characteristics (C_L vs. $\alpha$ and $C_D$ vs. $\alpha$)

**Objective:** To understand how lift and drag change with the angle of attack.

**Key Concepts:** The relationship between angle of attack and the coefficients is non-linear and crucial for design.

*   **$C_L$ vs. $\alpha$ Curve:**
    *   **Stall:** At a critical angle of attack (st<bos>all angle), the airflow separates from the upper surface, causing a drastic drop in lift and a sharp increase in drag.
    *   **Linear Region:** For small to moderate angles of attack, $C_L$ increases approximately linearly with $\alpha$.
    *   **Maximum $C_L$ ($C_{L,max}$):** The peak lift coefficient before stall.
    *   **Zero Lift Angle of Attack ($\alpha_0$):** The angle of attack at which $C_L = 0$. This is negative for airfoils with camber.

*   **$C_D$ vs. $\alpha$ Curve:**
    *   **Minimum Drag:** Occurs at a small positive or negative angle of attack (often near $\alpha_0$).
    *   **Stall Region:** Drag increases very rapidly beyond the stall angle.
    *   **Induced Drag:** A component of drag associated with lift, particularly significant in 2D airfoils when considering finite span effects. For simpler analysis, we often consider profile drag which includes skin friction and pressure drag.

*   **$C_L/C_D$ vs. $\alpha$ Curve:**
    *   **Maximum $L/D$:** Occurs at a specific angle of attack, which is generally lower than the angle for $C_{L,max}$. This is the point of maximum aerodynamic efficiency.

**Example:**
A typical NACA 4-digit airfoil might have a linear lift curve up to about 12-15 degrees, after which it stalls. The drag coefficient is relatively low and constant for small angles of attack but rises steeply after stall.

**Textbook References:**
*   **Ganesan, V. (2017):** Illustrates typical airfoil performance curves.
*   **Yahya, S. M. (2011):** Provides detailed discussions on stall phenomena and drag components.
*   **Cohen, H. (2019):** Focuses on the practical implications of these curves for turbomachinery design.

**Important Point to Remember:** Operating the airfoil at the angle of attack that maximizes the $L/D$ ratio is crucial for high efficiency. Compressor and turbine blades are designed to operate within a specific angle of attack range.

### 7. Flow Behavior Around an Airfoil

**Objective:** To understand the nature of airflow over the airfoil surface.

**Key Concepts:** The flow behavior is governed by viscosity and compressibility.

*   **Laminar Flow:** Smooth, orderly flow in layers. Low skin friction drag.
*   **Turbulent Flow:** Chaotic, irregular flow with eddies. Higher skin friction drag but more resistant to separation.
    *   **Boundary Layer:** A thin layer of fluid near the airfoil surface where viscous effects are dominant. It can be laminar or turbulent.
    *   **Flow Separation:** When the boundary layer detaches from the airfoil surface, typically occurring at higher angles of attack or adverse pressure gradients. This leads to a significant increase in drag and loss of lift (stall).
*   **Reynolds Number (Re):** A dimensionless number that characterizes the flow regime (laminar vs. turbulent).
    $$Re = \frac{\rho V c}{\mu}$$
    Where:
    *   $\mu$ = Dynamic viscosity of the fluid.
    *   For gas turbines, the Re is typically high, meaning the boundary layer is often turbulent.
*   **Mach Number (M):** The ratio of the flow velocity to the speed of sound.
    $$M = \frac{V}{a}$$
    Where:
    *   $a$ = Speed of sound.
    *   Compressibility effects become significant at high Mach numbers (typically M > 0.3). Compressor blades operate at high speeds, so compressibility is important.

**Example:**
On the upper surface of an airfoil at a moderate angle of attack, the flow accelerates, causing a decrease in pressure. This is an "adverse pressure gradient" (pressure increasing in the direction of flow), which can cause the boundary layer to separate if the airfoil shape is not optimized or the angle of attack is too high.

**Textbook References:**
*   **Ganesan, V. (2017):** Discusses boundary layers and flow separation.
*   **Yahya, S. M. (2011):** Covers Reynolds number and Mach number effects on airfoil performance.
*   **Cohen, H. (2019):** Details the impact of compressibility on airfoil characteristics, particularly relevant for modern high-speed compressors.

**Important Point to Remember:** Preventing flow separation is key to maintaining lift and minimizing drag. The design of the airfoil profile and the operating conditions (angle of attack) play critical roles.

### 9. Airfoil Sections in Turbomachinery

**Objective:** To understand the types of airfoil profiles used in gas turbines and their specific design considerations.

**Key Concepts:** Airfoil shapes in gas turbines are optimized for specific functions (compression or expansion) and operating conditions.

*   **Compressor Blades:**
    *   **Function:** Increase air pressure by accelerating it and diffusing it (reducing velocity).
    *   **Geometry:** Often have significant camber to achieve flow turning and pressure rise. Leading edges are generally sharp. Thickness distribution is optimized to prevent stall and manage the adverse pressure gradient.
    *   **Types:** Can be aerofoil sections or simpler profiles depending on the type of compressor (axial, centrifugal).
*   **Turbine Blades:**
    *   **Function:** Extract energy from high-velocity, high-temperature gases, causing them to expand and slow down.
    *   **Geometry:** Often designed with less camber than compressor blades but with careful contouring to maximize energy extraction and minimize aerodynamic losses. Trailing edges are often thin. The "profiles" might be more akin to nozzles.
*   **Casings and Stators:**
    *   **Stators (Guide Vanes):** Stationary airfoils that guide the flow into the rotating blades. They are also aerodynamically designed to impart the correct velocity and direction for optimal blade entry.

**Reference Book Integration:**
*   **Rolls Royce, The Jet Engine (2015):** Provides excellent real-world examples and illustrations of compressor and turbine blade designs in jet engines.
*   **Yahya, S. M. (2011):** Details the aerodynamic design principles for compressor and turbine blade sections.

**Learning Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO2 (K4):** Directly relates to understanding the performance characteristics of axial flow compressor and reaction turbine blades.
*   **CO3 (K4):** Understanding these airfoil characteristics is essential for analyzing the performance of gas turbine components.

**Important Point to Remember:** Airfoil design for turbomachinery is a compromise between achieving high lift (or work extraction), minimizing drag, and preventing stall or flow separation under varying operating conditions.

### 10. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define the terms chord line, leading edge, and trailing edge for an airfoil.

**Answer:**
*   **Chord Line:** A straight line connecting the leading edge to the trailing edge of the airfoil.
*   **Leading Edge:** The front-most point of the airfoil that meets the fluid flow.
*   **Trailing Edge:** The rear-most point of the airfoil where the airflow separates or rejoins.

**Question 2:**
What is the primary purpose of lift generated by compressor blades and turbine blades?

**Answer:**
*   **Compressor Blades:** To turn the airflow and increase its static pressure.
*   **Turbine Blades:** To extract kinetic and thermal energy from the hot gases, producing torque on the rotating shaft.

**Question 3:**
Explain the two primary physical principles that contribute to the generation of lift on an airfoil.

**Answer:**
1.  **Bernoulli's Principle:** The higher velocity of air over the curved upper surface leads to lower pressure compared to the lower surface, creating an upward force.
2.  **Newton's Third Law (Action-Reaction):** The airfoil deflects the airflow downwards, and in reaction, the air exerts an equal and opposite upward force on the airfoil.

**Question 4:**
Which parameter is maximized to ensure aerodynamic efficiency of an airfoil?
a) Lift Coefficient ($C_L$)
b) Drag Coefficient ($C_D$)
c) Lift-to-Drag Ratio ($C_L/C_D$)
d) Angle of Attack ($\alpha$)

**Answer:**
c) Lift-to-Drag Ratio ($C_L/C_D$)

**Question 5:**
What is flow separation, and what are its consequences for airfoil performance?

**Answer:**
Flow separation occurs when the boundary layer detaches from the airfoil surface. Its consequences include:
*   A drastic reduction in lift.
*   A sharp increase in drag.
*   Loss of smooth airflow control, leading to stall.

### Summary of Important Points to Remember:

*   **Airfoils are fundamental components** of gas turbine compressors and turbines, responsible for generating lift and extracting energy.
*   **Airfoil geometry** (camber, thickness, chord) dictates its aerodynamic performance.
*   **Lift** is generated due to pressure differences (Bernoulli) and flow deflection (Newton's Third Law).
*   **Drag** is a resistive force that reduces efficiency.
*   **Lift and Drag Coefficients ($C_L$, $C_D$)** are dimensionless parameters used to quantify performance.
*   **The Lift-to-Drag Ratio ($C_L/C_D$)** is the key indicator of aerodynamic efficiency.
*   **Angle of Attack ($\alpha$)** significantly influences $C_L$ and $C_D$.
*   **Stall** occurs at critical angles of attack, leading to a loss of lift and increased drag due to flow separation.
*   **Reynolds and Mach numbers** are critical dimensionless parameters affecting flow behavior (laminar/turbulent, compressible/incompressible).
*   **Compressor and turbine blades** have airfoil profiles optimized for their specific functions and operating conditions.
