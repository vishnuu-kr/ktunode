# Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) - Module 3: Ultrasonic Testing & Industrial Radiography

## Topic: Inspection Techniques in Industrial Radiography

This section focuses on the practical aspects of industrial radiography, including various inspection techniques, film selection and usage, quality assessment, processing, and interpretation of results. It builds upon the foundational understanding of ultrasonic testing principles from the earlier part of Module 3.

---

### 1. Radiographic Inspection Techniques

Industrial radiography utilizes penetrating radiation (X-rays or gamma rays) to detect internal flaws in materials. Different techniques are employed depending on the geometry of the object and the type of flaws being sought.

*   **Single Wall Single Image (SWSI):**
    *   **Description:** Radiation source and detector (film) are on opposite sides of the object. The image shows flaws that lie on the plane closest to the film.
    *   **Application:** Suitable for inspecting welds or components where the flaw of interest is likely near the surface facing the detector.
    *   **Advantage:** Simpler setup.
    *   **Disadvantage:** Can miss flaws located in the wall furthest from the film.

*   **Double Wall Single Image (DWSI):**
    *   **Description:** Radiation source is on one side, and the film is placed on the opposite side, capturing images of both walls and internal flaws. The radiation passes through both walls.
    *   **Application:** Commonly used for inspecting pipe welds where access to both sides is limited.
    *   **Advantage:** Allows inspection of both walls and the volume between them with a single exposure.
    *   **Disadvantage:** Image distortion and potential for overlapping flaws can make interpretation challenging. The sensitivity can be lower compared to SWSI.

*   **Double Wall Double Image (DWDI):**
    *   **Description:** Radiation source and film are placed on opposite sides, but the object is positioned to create two distinct images of the same flaw, one from each wall. This is often achieved by angling the source and film.
    *   **Application:** Used for inspecting larger diameter pipes or components where distinct images of flaws in both walls are required.
    *   **Advantage:** Provides two separate views of the same flaw, aiding in localization and characterization.
    *   **Disadvantage:** Requires precise positioning and may need multiple exposures for comprehensive coverage.

*   **Panoramic Exposure:**
    *   **Description:** The radiation source is placed in the center of a circular object (e.g., a pipe), and the film is wrapped around the outer circumference. The source emits radiation in all directions.
    *   **Application:** Ideal for inspecting 360-degree welds in pipes and pipelines.
    *   **Advantage:** Efficient for full circumferential inspection with a single exposure.
    *   **Disadvantage:** Requires specialized setup and can be sensitive to the positioning of the film.

*   **Real-Time Radiography (RTR):**
    *   **Description:** Instead of film, an image intensifier or digital detector captures the radiation, and the image is displayed on a monitor in real-time.
    *   **Application:** Allows for immediate viewing and manipulation of the image, enabling rapid inspection and adjustment.
    *   **Advantage:** Speed, no film processing required, ability to manipulate image contrast and brightness, potential for automation.
    *   **Disadvantage:** Typically lower resolution and sensitivity compared to film-based radiography for certain flaw types, initial equipment cost can be high.
    *   **Reference:** Hull & John (2012) discusses advancements in digital imaging techniques, including RTR.

### 2. Films Used in Industrial Radiography

The choice of film is crucial for achieving the desired image quality and detecting specific flaw types.

*   **Types of Film:**
    *   **Single Emulsion Film:** Emulsion coating on one side only. Often used with a single intensifying screen. Offers higher resolution.
    *   **Double Emulsion Film:** Emulsion coating on both sides. Used with two intensifying screens. Provides higher speed (less exposure time) and higher sensitivity. This is the most common type in industrial radiography.
    *   **Reference:** Baldev Raj (2008) provides detailed classifications of radiographic films and their characteristics.

*   **Speed of Films:**
    *   **Definition:** Film speed refers to the amount of radiation required to produce a specific optical density on the film.
    *   **Fast Films:** Require less radiation, leading to shorter exposure times. However, they generally have lower contrast and resolution, making them less suitable for detecting fine details.
    *   **Slow Films:** Require more radiation and longer exposure times but offer higher contrast and resolution, making them ideal for detecting small, low-contrast flaws.
    *   **Classification:** Films are often classified into categories like "Class I" (high definition, slow) to "Class IV" (high speed, low definition) based on ASTM standards.

### 3. Qualities of Film Screens Used in Radiography

Intensifying screens are used in conjunction with radiographic film to improve image quality and reduce exposure times.

*   **Types of Screens:**
    *   **Fluoroscopic Screens:** Used in fluoroscopy to convert radiation into visible light, allowing direct viewing.
    *   **Intensifying Screens:** Placed in direct contact with the film. They contain phosphors that fluoresce (emit light) when struck by radiation. This emitted light exposes the film, thereby intensifying the effect of the radiation.
        *   **Calcium Tungstate (CaWO₄):** Traditional phosphor, emits blue light.
        *   **Rare Earth Phosphors (e.g., Lanthanum Oxybromide):** Emit green light. These are more efficient and allow for faster film speeds and reduced exposure times with comparable image quality.
    *   **Reference:** Ravi Prakash (2009) details the function and types of intensifying screens in radiography.

*   **Qualities of Film Screens:**
    *   **Screen Sensitivity/Speed:** How efficiently the screen converts radiation to light. Higher sensitivity means less exposure needed.
    *   **Resolution:** The ability of the screen to record fine detail. Fine-grain phosphors generally provide better resolution.
    *   **Graininess:** Unwanted random variations in density on the film, which can mask fine flaws.
    *   **Matching:** The spectral sensitivity of the film must match the emission spectrum of the phosphor in the screen (e.g., blue-sensitive film with blue-emitting screens).

### 4. Quality of a Good Radiograph

A good radiograph effectively displays internal discontinuities with sufficient clarity for accurate interpretation.

*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   **Sharpness of Image:** Flaws should have well-defined edges.
    *   **Contrast:** Sufficient difference in density between the flaw and the surrounding material to make the flaw visible.
    *   **Resolution/Detail Visibility:** The ability to discern fine discontinuities or penetrameters.
    *   **Low Graininess:** Minimal random noise that could obscure details.
    *   **Absence of Artifacts:** No unwanted markings or distortions that could be mistaken for flaws.
    *   **Proper Density:** The overall lightness or darkness of the radiograph, which should be sufficient to see details but not so dark or light that it obscures them.
    *   **Geometric Unsharpness:** Minimized by using small focal spot sources and keeping the source-to-object distance as large as practical.
    *   **Reference:** Baldev Raj (2008) extensively covers the factors influencing radiograph quality and acceptance criteria.

### 5. Film Processing

The process of developing the latent image on the film into a visible, permanent image.

*   **Steps:**
    1.  **Developer:** Converts exposed silver halide crystals into metallic silver, forming the visible image.
    2.  **Stop Bath:** Neutralizes the developer, stopping the development process.
    3.  **Fixer:** Removes unexposed silver halide crystals, making the image permanent and insensitive to further light.
    4.  **Washing:** Removes residual processing chemicals from the film.
    5.  **Drying:** Removes moisture to prevent sticking or damage.
*   **Importance:** Proper processing is critical for achieving the full potential image quality and ensuring the longevity of the radiograph.
*   **Reference:** Hull & John (2012) discuss both manual and automatic film processing techniques and their implications.

### 6. Interpretation and Evaluation of Test Results

This is the crucial step where the radiograph is examined to identify and assess flaws.

*   **Interpretation:**
    *   **Process:** A trained interpreter carefully examines the radiograph, comparing it against established standards, codes, and specifications.
    *   **Tools:** Magnifying viewers, densitometers (to measure film density), and penetrameters (IQIs - Image Quality Indicators) are used.
    *   **Flaw Identification:** Recognition of indications that differ in density from the surrounding material. These can be pores, inclusions, cracks, voids, incomplete fusion, etc.
    *   **Reference:** Baldev Raj (2008) and Ravi Prakash (2009) provide guidance on interpreting various types of discontinuities found in welds and castings.

*   **Evaluation:**
    *   **Assessment:** Determining whether the identified flaws are acceptable based on acceptance criteria defined by codes (e.g., ASME, AWS) or customer specifications.
    *   **Flaw Characterization:**
        *   **Type:** What kind of discontinuity is it?
        *   **Size:** Length, width, depth (where possible).
        *   **Location:** Position within the material.
        *   **Orientation:** How the flaw is oriented relative to the radiation beam.
    *   **Acceptance/Rejection:** A flaw is deemed acceptable if it meets the specified criteria; otherwise, the component may need repair or rejection.
    *   **Course Outcome Alignment:** This directly relates to CO1 (carrying out inspections) and CO3 (image processing and evaluation of radiographic images).

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Penetrameters (IQIs):** Essential for verifying the sensitivity and quality of a radiograph. They represent the smallest flaw size that can be detected.
*   **Source-to-Film Distance (SFD) and Object-to-Film Distance (OFD):** Crucial for controlling geometric unsharpness. A larger SFD and smaller OFD generally result in sharper images.
*   **Radiation Type:** X-rays are typically used for thinner materials and lower energy applications, while gamma rays (from isotopes like Iridium-192 or Cobalt-60) are used for thicker materials and where portability is needed.
*   **Radiation Safety:** Industrial radiography involves ionizing radiation and requires strict adherence to safety protocols and regulations.
*   **Digital Radiography (DR) and Computed Radiography (CR):** Modern alternatives to film, offering advantages in image acquisition and manipulation but requiring different interpretation skills. RTR is a form of DR.

### Practice Questions & Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary advantage of Double Wall Single Image (DWSI) radiography?
**Answer:** DWSI allows for the inspection of both walls of a component, such as a pipe, with a single exposure, which is particularly useful when access is limited to only one side.

**Question 2:** Which type of film would you generally choose if you need to detect very fine, low-contrast flaws?
**Answer:** A slow film (e.g., Class I) with fine grain and used with fine-grain intensifying screens would be preferred for detecting fine, low-contrast flaws, as it offers higher contrast and resolution.

**Question 3:** What is the role of an intensifying screen in industrial radiography?
**Answer:** An intensifying screen converts incident radiation into light, which then exposes the radiographic film. This process increases the film's sensitivity to radiation, allowing for shorter exposure times or lower radiation levels while maintaining image quality.

**Question 4:** Briefly explain the difference between interpretation and evaluation in radiographic testing.
**Answer:** **Interpretation** is the process of examining the radiograph to identify any indications or potential flaws. **Evaluation** is the process of assessing these identified flaws against defined acceptance criteria to determine if the component is acceptable or requires further action.

**Question 5:** What is the purpose of a penetrameter (IQI)?
**Answer:** A penetrameter (Image Quality Indicator) is a small piece of metal with specific dimensions (e.g., wires or blocks of varying thicknesses) placed on the object being radiographed. It is used to demonstrate that the radiograph has sufficient sensitivity to detect flaws of a certain size, thereby verifying the quality of the radiograph.
