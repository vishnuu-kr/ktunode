# DESIGN OF MACHINE ELEMENTS

## Module 4: Design of Gears
### Topic: Helical Gears

This module focuses on the design of gear drives, which are crucial for transmitting power and motion between shafts. While spur gears transmit power between parallel shafts, helical gears offer a smoother and quieter operation and can transmit power between parallel and non-parallel shafts. This topic specifically delves into helical gears, building upon the foundational knowledge of spur gears.

---

### 1. Introduction to Helical Gears

Helical gears are similar to spur gears but have teeth that are cut at an angle to the axis of rotation. This helix angle allows for gradual engagement and disengagement of teeth, leading to:

*   **Smoother operation:** Reduced vibration and noise compared to spur gears.
*   **Higher load-carrying capacity:** More teeth are in contact simultaneously, distributing the load more evenly.
*   **Increased efficiency:** Reduced impact stresses.

However, helical gears introduce an axial thrust component, which needs to be considered in the design.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Helix Angle (ψ):** The angle between the tooth helix and the gear axis.
*   **Normal Module (m<sub>n</sub>):** The module measured in the normal plane to the tooth.
*   **Transverse Module (m<sub>t</sub>):** The module measured in the plane of rotation (similar to spur gears).
*   **Axial Module (m<sub>a</sub>):** The module measured in the plane of the axis of rotation.
*   **Normal Pressure Angle (α<sub>n</sub>):** The pressure angle measured in the normal plane.
*   **Transverse Pressure Angle (α<sub>t</sub>):** The pressure angle measured in the plane of rotation.

**Relationship between modules:**

*   $m_t = m_n / \cos(\psi)$
*   $m_a = m_n / \sin(\psi)$

**Relationship between pressure angles:**

*   $\tan(\alpha_t) = \tan(\alpha_n) / \cos(\psi)$

**Types of Helical Gears:**

*   **Single Helical Gears:** Teeth are cut at an angle on one side only. They generate an axial thrust component.
*   **Double Helical (or Herringbone) Gears:** Teeth are cut at an angle on both sides, forming a V-shape. The axial thrusts from the two halves cancel each other out, eliminating the need for thrust bearings.

### 2. Nomenclature of Helical Gears

The nomenclature for helical gears extends from spur gears with the addition of helix angle related terms.

**Key Terms:**

*   **Pitch Diameter (D):** The diameter of the pitch cylinder.
    *   $D = m_t \times Z$ (where Z is the number of teeth)
*   **Addendum (a):** The radial distance from the pitch surface to the top of the tooth. $a = m_n$
*   **Dedendum (b):** The radial distance from the pitch surface to the bottom of the tooth. $b = 1.25 m_n$
*   **Tooth Height (h):** The radial distance from the root to the tip of the tooth. $h = a + b = 2.25 m_n$
*   **Circular Pitch (p<sub>t</sub>):** The distance along the pitch circle from one point on a tooth to the corresponding point on the next tooth. $p_t = \pi \times m_t$
*   **Normal Pitch (p<sub>n</sub>):** The distance along the normal to the helix from one point on a tooth to the corresponding point on the next tooth. $p_n = \pi \times m_n$
*   **Face Width (F):** The width of the gear face along the axis of rotation.
*   **Helix Angle (ψ):** The angle between the tooth helix and the gear axis.
*   **Normal Pressure Angle (α<sub>n</sub>):** The pressure angle in the normal plane.
*   **Transverse Pressure Angle (α<sub>t</sub>):** The pressure angle in the plane of rotation.

**Important Relationships:**

*   **Number of Teeth:** $Z_t = Z_n / \cos^3(\psi)$ (This is a simplified approximation; more accurate methods exist for calculating the virtual number of teeth in the normal plane). The effective number of teeth in the normal plane that determines the bending strength is $Z_n = Z_t \cos(\psi)$.
*   **Axial Thrust:** The axial thrust force ($F_a$) generated by a helical gear is given by:
    $F_a = F_t \tan(\psi)$
    where $F_t$ is the tangential force acting at the pitch radius. This force acts along the axis of the gear.

**Example:**

A helical gear has a module of 5 mm and a helix angle of 20 degrees. If the number of teeth is 40, calculate:
a) Normal module
b) Transverse module
c) Diameter of the pitch circle

**Solution:**

Given:
$m_n = 5$ mm
$\psi = 20^\circ$
$Z_t = 40$

a) Normal module is given as $m_n = 5$ mm.

b) Transverse module $m_t = m_n / \cos(\psi) = 5 / \cos(20^\circ) = 5 / 0.9397 \approx 5.32$ mm.

c) Diameter of the pitch circle $D = m_t \times Z_t = 5.32 \times 40 \approx 212.8$ mm.

### 3. Design Procedure for Helical Gears (CO5 & CO6 Alignment)

The design of helical gears involves several steps to ensure their reliability and performance. The procedure is similar to spur gears but includes considerations for the helix angle and axial thrust.

**Steps for Helical Gear Design:**

1.  **Determine Input Parameters:**
    *   Power to be transmitted ($P$)
    *   Speed of the driving gear ($N_1$)
    *   Speed of the driven gear ($N_2$)
    *   Center distance between shafts ($a$)
    *   Type of operation (e.g., continuous, intermittent)
    *   Service factor ($K_v$ or $C_s$)

2.  **Select Material and Heat Treatment:**
    *   Choose appropriate materials (e.g., steel alloys like EN 8, EN 24) based on strength, wear resistance, and operating conditions.
    *   Specify heat treatment (e.g., hardening and tempering, case hardening) to achieve desired surface hardness and core strength.

3.  **Determine Gear Ratio and Speed:**
    *   Gear ratio ($i$) = $N_1 / N_2$ = $Z_2 / Z_1$
    *   Calculate tangential speed at the pitch circle ($v$).

4.  **Initial Selection of Modules and Face Width:**
    *   Select a preliminary helix angle ($\psi$). Common values range from 15 to 45 degrees. A higher helix angle generally leads to smoother operation but increases axial thrust.
    *   Determine the normal module ($m_n$). This can be based on experience or preliminary calculations related to power and speed.
    *   Calculate the transverse module ($m_t$) and the pitch diameter ($D$).
    *   Estimate the face width ($F$). A common starting point is $F \approx (10 \text{ to } 15) \times m_t$.

5.  **Calculate Forces Acting on the Gear Tooth:**
    *   **Tangential Force ($F_t$):** $F_t = (2 \times T) / D$, where $T$ is the torque in N-mm. Torque $T = (P \times 1000 \times 60) / (2 \pi N)$ (for power in kW and speed in rpm).
    *   **Radial Force ($F_r$):** $F_r = F_t \tan(\alpha_t)$, where $\alpha_t$ is the transverse pressure angle.
    *   **Axial Thrust ($F_a$):** $F_a = F_t \tan(\psi)$. This force acts along the axis of the gear.

6.  **Bending Strength Calculation:**
    *   The bending strength calculation is based on the Lewis formula or the AGMA (American Gear Manufacturers Association) bending strength formula. The key is to use the *normal module* and the *effective number of teeth in the normal plane*.
    *   **Lewis form factor ($Y$):** This factor depends on the tooth form, pressure angle, and number of teeth. It's typically obtained from tables or charts. For helical gears, the $Y$ factor is usually based on the number of teeth in the normal plane ($Z_n = Z_t \cos(\psi)$).
    *   **Bending Stress ($\sigma_b$):** $\sigma_b = (F_t \times K_s \times K_o \times K_m) / (m_n \times F \times Y)$
        *   $K_s$: Dynamic factor (accounts for speed variations)
        *   $K_o$: Overload factor
        *   $K_m$: Load distribution factor
    *   The calculated bending stress should be less than the allowable bending stress of the material.

7.  **Surface Durability (Wear) Calculation:**
    *   This calculation is based on the Hertzian contact stress. The AGMA surface durability formula is commonly used.
    *   **Surface Stress ($\sigma_c$):** $\sigma_c = C_p \sqrt{(F_t \times K_s \times K_o \times K_m \times K_v) / (F \times D \times I)}$
        *   $C_p$: Elastic coefficient (depends on the materials of the mating gears)
        *   $I$: Geometry factor (depends on the tooth form, pressure angles, and helix angle)
    *   The calculated surface stress should be less than the allowable surface stress of the material.

8.  **Axial Thrust Consideration:**
    *   The axial thrust force ($F_a$) needs to be managed.
    *   **For single helical gears:** A thrust bearing is required to counteract this force. The bearing should be selected based on the magnitude of $F_a$ and the operating speed.
    *   **For double helical gears:** The axial thrusts from the two halves cancel out, eliminating the need for a separate thrust bearing. However, the bearing surfaces of the gear teeth must be strong enough to withstand this thrust.

9.  **Checking for Deflection and Misalignment:**
    *   Consider the deflection of the gear teeth under load, especially for wider faces.
    *   Ensure proper mounting to minimize misalignment, which can lead to uneven load distribution.

10. **Finalization of Dimensions:**
    *   Adjust modules, face width, and helix angle based on the strength and durability checks.
    *   Ensure that all components (pinion, gear, shaft, bearings) are designed appropriately.

**References:**

*   **V.B. Bhandari, Design of Machine Elements:** Provides detailed explanations of gear design principles, including bending and wear strength calculations. Chapter on gears.
*   **R.L. Norton, Machine Design – An Integrated Approach:** Offers a practical approach to gear design, emphasizing design process and considerations for dynamic loading.
*   **AGMA Standards:** While not explicitly listed as textbooks, AGMA standards are the industry benchmark for gear design and should be consulted for precise calculations and factors.

---

### 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **Helix Angle Impact:** The helix angle is the defining characteristic of helical gears. It improves smoothness and load capacity but introduces axial thrust.
*   **Normal vs. Transverse Parameters:** Always distinguish between parameters measured in the normal plane (for bending strength) and the transverse plane (for pitch diameter and tangential forces).
*   **Axial Thrust Management:** For single helical gears, the axial thrust is a critical design factor that necessitates the use of thrust bearings. Double helical gears eliminate this issue by their design.
*   **Material Selection:** The choice of material and heat treatment significantly influences the strength and wear resistance of helical gears.
*   **AGMA Standards:** For accurate and reliable gear design, adherence to AGMA standards is highly recommended.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

A pair of helical gears are designed to transmit 5 kW of power at 1500 rpm of the driving pinion. The gear ratio is 3:1. The helix angle is 25 degrees, and the normal module is 4 mm. The pinion has 20 teeth. The normal pressure angle is 20 degrees. The allowable bending stress for the pinion material is 180 N/mm². The dynamic factor $K_v = (20 + \sqrt{v}) / 20$ where $v$ is the pitch line velocity in m/s. Assume $K_o = 1.5$, $K_m = 1.2$, and the Lewis form factor $Y$ for the pinion for a 20-tooth gear with a 20-degree pressure angle is 0.12. The face width can be assumed as 10 times the transverse module.

Determine:
a) The transverse module.
b) The pitch diameter of the pinion.
c) The tangential force on the tooth.
d) The axial thrust on the pinion.
e) The bending stress in the pinion tooth.
f) Check if the pinion is safe from bending stress.

**Solution:**

Given:
Power, $P = 5$ kW = $5000$ W
Speed of pinion, $N_1 = 1500$ rpm
Gear ratio, $i = 3:1$ (Pinion is the smaller gear)
Number of teeth on pinion, $Z_1 = 20$
Helix angle, $\psi = 25^\circ$
Normal module, $m_n = 4$ mm
Normal pressure angle, $\alpha_n = 20^\circ$
Allowable bending stress, $\sigma_{all} = 180$ N/mm²
$K_v = (20 + \sqrt{v}) / 20$
$K_o = 1.5$
$K_m = 1.2$
Lewis form factor for pinion, $Y = 0.12$
Face width, $F = 10 \times m_t$

a) **Transverse module ($m_t$):**
$m_t = m_n / \cos(\psi) = 4 / \cos(25^\circ) = 4 / 0.9063 \approx 4.41$ mm

b) **Pitch diameter of the pinion ($D_1$):**
Number of teeth on pinion, $Z_1 = 20$
$D_1 = m_t \times Z_1 = 4.41 \times 20 = 88.2$ mm

c) **Tangential force ($F_{t1}$):**
Torque on pinion, $T_1 = (P \times 1000 \times 60) / (2 \pi N_1) = (5000 \times 1000 \times 60) / (2 \pi \times 1500) = 318309.8$ N-mm
$F_{t1} = (2 \times T_1) / D_1 = (2 \times 318309.8) / 88.2 \approx 7220.6$ N

d) **Axial thrust on the pinion ($F_{a1}$):**
$F_{a1} = F_{t1} \tan(\psi) = 7220.6 \times \tan(25^\circ) = 7220.6 \times 0.4663 \approx 3367.0$ N

e) **Bending stress in the pinion tooth ($\sigma_b$):**
First, calculate the pitch line velocity ($v$):
$v = (\pi \times D_1 \times N_1) / (60 \times 1000) = (\pi \times 88.2 \times 1500) / (60 \times 1000) = 6.91$ m/s
Now, calculate the dynamic factor ($K_v$):
$K_v = (20 + \sqrt{6.91}) / 20 = (20 + 2.63) / 20 = 22.63 / 20 \approx 1.13$

Face width, $F = 10 \times m_t = 10 \times 4.41 = 44.1$ mm

Bending stress, $\sigma_b = (F_{t1} \times K_v \times K_o \times K_m) / (m_n \times F \times Y)$
$\sigma_b = (7220.6 \times 1.13 \times 1.5 \times 1.2) / (4 \times 44.1 \times 0.12)$
$\sigma_b = (13846.0) / (21.168) \approx 654.1$ N/mm²

f) **Check if the pinion is safe from bending stress:**
Calculated bending stress $\sigma_b = 654.1$ N/mm².
Allowable bending stress $\sigma_{all} = 180$ N/mm².

Since $\sigma_b > \sigma_{all}$, the pinion is NOT safe from bending stress. The design needs to be revised.

**Possible Revisions:**
*   Increase the normal module ($m_n$).
*   Increase the face width ($F$).
*   Use a material with a higher allowable bending stress.
*   Reduce the helix angle (which would increase $m_t$ and $D$, potentially reducing stress, but it's a complex trade-off).

**Question 2 (Conceptual):**

Explain why double helical gears are preferred over single helical gears in applications where high speeds and heavy loads are involved, especially when axial thrust is a concern.

**Answer:**
Double helical gears, also known as herringbone gears, have two sets of teeth cut in opposite directions with a helix angle. This arrangement causes the axial thrust forces generated by each set of teeth to be equal and opposite, thus canceling each other out. This eliminates the need for thrust bearings, which simplifies the bearing arrangement and reduces friction losses. In high-speed and heavy-load applications, the axial thrust from a single helical gear can be substantial, requiring large and robust thrust bearings that can be prone to failure. By eliminating this thrust, double helical gears provide a more reliable and efficient transmission system. They also offer smoother operation and higher load-carrying capacity due to more teeth in contact.
