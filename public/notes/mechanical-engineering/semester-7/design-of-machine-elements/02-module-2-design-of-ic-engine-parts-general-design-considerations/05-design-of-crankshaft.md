# DESIGN OF MACHINE ELEMENTS: Module 2 - Design of IC Engine parts: General Design Considerations

## Topic: Design of Crankshaft

This module focuses on the design considerations for various components within Internal Combustion (IC) Engines. This specific topic delves into the design of the crankshaft, a critical component responsible for converting the linear reciprocating motion of the piston into the rotational motion of the engine's output shaft.

---

### **1. Introduction to the Crankshaft**

The crankshaft is the backbone of the IC engine's mechanical system. It translates the forces generated by combustion into useful rotational power. Its design is paramount for the engine's efficiency, durability, and smooth operation.

*   **Function:**
    *   Converts the reciprocating motion of pistons into rotary motion.
    *   Transmits the generated torque to the flywheel and subsequently to the drivetrain.
    *   Supports the connecting rods and absorbs the forces acting upon them.

*   **Key Components of a Crankshaft:**
    *   **Crank Pins (Journals):** Where the connecting rods are attached. They are offset from the main journals.
    *   **Main Journals:** Support the crankshaft in the engine block.
    *   **Crank Webs (Cheeks):** Connect the crank pins to the main journals, forming the crank arms. They are often counterweighted.
    *   **Crankshaft Flange:** Connects the crankshaft to the flywheel.
    *   **Shaft Extension:** Can accommodate accessories like the oil pump, water pump, or timing gears.

---

### **2. Forces Acting on the Crankshaft**

Understanding the forces acting on the crankshaft is crucial for its proper design. These forces are primarily generated by the combustion process and the inertia of moving parts.

*   **Gas Forces (Pressure Forces):**
    *   Result from the high pressure developed during combustion within the cylinder.
    *   Act axially on the piston and are transmitted to the crank pin.
    *   Vary significantly throughout the engine cycle.
    *   **Calculation:** $P_{max} \times A_p$, where $P_{max}$ is the maximum cylinder pressure and $A_p$ is the piston area.

*   **Inertia Forces of Reciprocating Parts:**
    *   Caused by the acceleration and deceleration of the piston and the small end of the connecting rod.
    *   Tend to oppose the motion of the piston.
    *   **Calculation:** Often approximated using the formula $m_r \omega^2 r (1 + \frac{r}{l} \cos \theta)$, where $m_r$ is the equivalent reciprocating mass, $\omega$ is the angular velocity, $r$ is the crank radius, $l$ is the connecting rod length, and $\theta$ is the crank angle. (Refer to Bhandari, Chapter 12 for detailed analysis of forces).

*   **Inertia Forces of Rotating Parts:**
    *   Primarily due to the rotating mass of the connecting rod (big end) and the crankshaft itself.
    *   Act radially outward from the center of rotation.
    *   **Calculation:** $m_o \omega^2 r$, where $m_o$ is the equivalent rotating mass.

*   **Centrifugal Forces:**
    *   Arise from the rotating masses, especially the counterweights.
    *   Contribute to bending moments on the crankshaft.

*   **Torsional Forces:**
    *   The torque generated by the gas forces on the crank pin, which twists the crankshaft.
    *   Causes torsional shear stresses.

**Important Note:** The resultant force on the crank pin is the vector sum of the gas force and the inertia forces of reciprocating parts. This resultant force is then resolved into components:
*   **Tangential Force:** Acts along the crank radial line, producing torque.
*   **Crushed Force (Radial Force):** Acts perpendicular to the crank radial line, causing bending.

### **3. Design Considerations for Crankshafts**

The design of a crankshaft involves a comprehensive analysis of material selection, stress distribution, fatigue life, and manufacturing aspects.

*   **Material Selection:**
    *   Materials must possess high tensile and compressive strength, good fatigue strength, wear resistance, and toughness.
    *   Commonly used materials:
        *   **Carbon Steels:** Such as AISI 1040, 1045, 1050 (for less demanding applications). (Refer to Bhandari, Chapter 5 for material properties).
        *   **Alloy Steels:** Such as Chromium-Molybdenum steels (e.g., 4140, 4340), Nickel-Chromium steels (e.g., 3140). These offer superior strength, toughness, and fatigue resistance.
        *   **Nodular Cast Iron (Ductile Iron):** Used for some heavy-duty diesel engines, offering good damping characteristics and castability. (Refer to Norton, Chapter 11 on material selection).
        *   **Forged Steel:** Preferred for high-performance engines due to its superior strength and fatigue properties obtained through grain flow orientation during forging.

*   **Stress Analysis:**
    *   **Bending Stress:** Primarily occurs due to the radial component of the forces acting on the crank pin and the weight of the components. The web and journal sections are critical.
    *   **Torsional Stress:** Occurs due to the tangential component of the forces acting on the crank pin, causing twisting of the shaft.
    *   **Shear Stress:** Occurs due to the direct shear force and also combined with bending stresses.
    *   **Combined Stresses:** The actual stresses are a combination of bending, torsional, and sometimes axial stresses. Stress concentration occurs at fillets and changes in cross-section.

*   **Fatigue Strength:**
    *   Crankshafts are subjected to millions of stress cycles, making fatigue a critical design consideration.
    *   **S-N Curves (Stress-Number of Cycles):** Used to predict fatigue life. (Refer to Bhandari, Chapter 7 on fatigue).
    *   **Stress Concentration Factors:** Fillets at the junction of journals and webs are critical areas where stress concentration is high. Proper fillet radii are essential.
    *   **Surface Treatment:** Methods like nitriding, induction hardening, or shot peening can improve fatigue life by introducing compressive stresses on the surface. (Refer to Norton, Chapter 14 on surface integrity).

*   **Bearing Design:**
    *   Main journals and crank pins operate as journal bearings (typically sleeve bearings).
    *   Lubrication is critical to prevent seizure and wear.
    *   The length-to-diameter ratio of the bearings influences the bearing pressure and performance.

*   **Balancing:**
    *   To minimize vibrations and uneven loads, crankshafts are balanced.
    *   **Primary and Secondary Balancing:** For reciprocating masses.
    *   **Counterweights:** Fitted on the crank webs opposite to the connecting rod big end and sometimes on the crank pin itself to offset the inertia forces of the reciprocating and rotating masses. (Refer to Shigley, Chapter 8 on balancing).
    *   **Dynamic Balancing:** Ensures the crankshaft rotates smoothly at high speeds.

*   **Stiffness:**
    *   The crankshaft must be stiff enough to avoid excessive deflection under load, which can lead to misalignment of bearings and reduced oil film thickness.

*   **Manufacturing and Machining:**
    *   Crankshafts are typically forged and then machined.
    *   Surface finish and dimensional accuracy are critical for bearing performance and fatigue life.
    *   Methods like grinding and polishing are used for journals.

### **4. Design Procedure for Crankshaft**

While a detailed design involves complex calculations and simulations, a general procedure can be outlined:

1.  **Determine Engine Specifications:** Bore, stroke, engine speed, firing order, type of engine (petrol/diesel), number of cylinders. (CO2)
2.  **Calculate Forces:** Determine the maximum gas pressure, inertia forces (reciprocating and rotating), and hence the resultant forces on the crank pin at critical crank angles. (CO2)
3.  **Determine Crankshaft Geometry:** Based on engine layout and stroke, determine crank radius, journal diameters, web dimensions, and fillet radii.
4.  **Analyze Stresses:** Calculate bending moments and torsional moments acting on the crankshaft. Use stress concentration factors for critical sections (fillets).
5.  **Material Selection:** Choose an appropriate material based on strength, fatigue resistance, and cost requirements.
6.  **Determine Bearing Loads and Dimensions:** Calculate bearing reactions and select suitable journal diameters and bearing lengths.
7.  **Check for Strength and Stiffness:**
    *   **Bending Strength:** Ensure the shaft can withstand bending stresses. Use the bending stress formula: $\sigma_b = \frac{M}{Z}$, where $M$ is the bending moment and $Z$ is the section modulus.
    *   **Torsional Strength:** Ensure the shaft can withstand torsional stresses. Use the torsional stress formula: $\tau = \frac{T}{J/r}$, where $T$ is the torque and $J/r$ is the polar section modulus.
    *   **Fatigue Analysis:** Apply suitable fatigue factors of safety based on the type of loading and material properties. (CO2)
8.  **Design Counterweights:** Calculate the required mass and location of counterweights for proper balancing.
9.  **Consider Manufacturing Aspects:** Ensure the design is manufacturable and cost-effective.
10. **Final Verification:** Review all calculations and ensure compliance with relevant standards and safety factors.

### **5. Examples and Applications**

*   **Automobile Engines:** Typically use forged alloy steel crankshafts for their durability and performance. Multi-cylinder engines require careful balancing.
*   **Diesel Engines:** Experience higher pressures, requiring robust crankshafts often made of higher-strength alloy steels or sometimes nodular cast iron for specific applications.
*   **Marine Engines:** Large marine engines might use fabricated crankshafts or cast iron crankshafts for cost-effectiveness in lower-speed applications.

### **6. Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Crank Radius (r):** Half of the piston stroke.
*   **Stroke:** The distance traveled by the piston from Top Dead Center (TDC) to Bottom Dead Center (BDC).
*   **Bending Moment:** The algebraic sum of moments about a section due to all external forces acting on one side of that section.
*   **Torsional Moment (Torque):** The turning moment about the axis of the shaft.
*   **Section Modulus (Z):** A geometric property of a cross-section used in stress calculations ($\frac{I}{y_{max}}$).
*   **Polar Section Modulus (J/r):** A geometric property for torsional stress calculations.
*   **Stress Concentration:** The localized increase in stress at discontinuities or geometric changes.
*   **Fatigue:** The phenomenon of material failure under repeated or cyclic loading, even when the applied stress is below the ultimate tensile strength.
*   **Factor of Safety:** The ratio of the ultimate strength or yield strength to the allowable working stress.

### **7. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** What are the primary types of forces that act on a crankshaft?
**Answer:** Gas forces (pressure forces) and inertia forces (reciprocating and rotating) are the primary forces. Centrifugal forces also play a role, especially due to counterweights.

**Question 2:** Why are counterweights used in crankshafts?
**Answer:** Counterweights are used to balance the inertia forces of the reciprocating and rotating parts, thereby reducing vibrations and ensuring smoother operation.

**Question 3:** What are the critical areas in a crankshaft design from a stress perspective?
**Answer:** The fillets at the junction of crank pins and webs, and at the junction of webs and main journals are critical areas due to stress concentration.

**Question 4:** List common materials used for crankshafts and their advantages.
**Answer:**
*   **Forged Alloy Steels (e.g., Cr-Mo, Ni-Cr steels):** High strength, toughness, and excellent fatigue resistance. Preferred for high-performance engines.
*   **Carbon Steels (e.g., AISI 1045):** Lower cost, suitable for less demanding applications.
*   **Nodular Cast Iron:** Good damping characteristics, castable, used in some heavy-duty diesel engines.

**Question 5 (Conceptual):** A single-cylinder engine crankshaft experiences both bending and torsional moments. Explain how these moments arise.
**Answer:**
*   **Bending Moment:** The radial component of the resultant force on the crank pin causes bending. This force acts perpendicular to the crank radius and induces bending stresses in the journals and webs.
*   **Torsional Moment:** The tangential component of the resultant force on the crank pin acts along the crank radius and produces the torque that twists the crankshaft.

### **8. Important Points to Remember**

*   Crankshaft design is a balance between strength, fatigue life, weight, and cost.
*   Stress concentration at fillets is a major concern for fatigue failure.
*   Proper balancing is essential for smooth engine operation.
*   Material properties, especially fatigue strength, are crucial.
*   Lubrication of journals is critical for bearing life.
*   Always refer to design data handbooks (like Bhandari, PSG, Mahadevan & Reddy) for material properties, stress concentration factors, and design procedures.
