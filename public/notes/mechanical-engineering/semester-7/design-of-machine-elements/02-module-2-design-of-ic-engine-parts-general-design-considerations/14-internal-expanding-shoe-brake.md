### 1. Introduction to Internal Expanding Shoe Brakes

#### 1.1 What is a Brake?

A brake is a mechanical device that inhibits motion by absorbing kinetic energy from a moving system. This absorption is typically achieved through the friction generated by two surfaces moving against each other.

#### 1.2 Classification of Brakes:

Brakes can be broadly classified based on their operating mechanism and application. Internal expanding shoe brakes fall under the category of **friction brakes**. Other common types include:

*   **Band Brakes:** External or internal bands that wrap around a rotating drum.
*   **Disc Brakes:** Pads that press against a rotating disc.
*   **Block Brakes:** Blocks that press against a rotating wheel or drum.

#### 1.3 Internal Expanding Shoe Brake: Principle of Operation

*   **Mechanism:** Consists of a drum that rotates with the wheel and one or more curved **brake shoes** mounted on a fixed pivot or fulcrum.
*   **Actuation:** When the brake is applied, an actuating mechanism (e.g., hydraulic cylinder, cam, or lever) forces the ends of the brake shoes outwards, pressing them against the inner surface of the rotating drum.
*   **Friction:** Friction is generated between the brake shoe lining and the drum, creating a braking torque that opposes the rotation of the drum and thus the vehicle or machinery.
*   **Self-Energizing Effect (for leading shoes):** In some configurations, the friction force on the leading shoe can contribute to pressing the shoe more firmly against the drum, increasing the braking force without additional input. This is known as the self-energizing effect.

#### 1.4 Key Components:

*   **Brake Drum:** The rotating cylindrical component that comes into contact with the brake shoes. Typically made of cast iron or steel for good wear resistance and heat conductivity.
*   **Brake Shoes:** The curved components that house the friction lining. Usually made of metal (e.g., steel or aluminum alloy) for structural integrity.
*   **Brake Lining:** The friction material attached to the brake shoes. This is a critical component responsible for generating friction and must withstand high temperatures and wear. Examples include asbestos (historically), semi-metallic, ceramic, and organic composites.
*   **Pivot/Fulcrum:** The point around which the brake shoe pivots when actuated.
*   **Actuating Mechanism:** The system that applies force to the brake shoes (e.g., cam, wedge, hydraulic cylinder).
*   **Return Springs:** Springs that pull the brake shoes away from the drum when the actuation force is removed, allowing free rotation.
*   **Adjuster Mechanism:** To compensate for wear of the brake lining and maintain optimal braking performance.

### 2. Design Considerations for Internal Expanding Shoe Brakes

#### 2.1 Braking Torque Calculation:

The primary objective of brake design is to generate a specific braking torque to decelerate a moving system.

**Assumptions for simplified analysis:**

*   The friction lining has uniform pressure distribution.
*   The shoe pivots at a fixed point.

**Definitions:**

*   $T$ = Braking Torque (N-m)
*   $P_a$ = Actuating Force (N) (acting on the shoe)
*   $F_f$ = Frictional Force (N)
*   $F_n$ = Normal Force (N) (perpendicular to the shoe surface)
*   $C$ = Coefficient of Friction ($\mu$)
*   $R$ = Radius of the brake drum (m)
*   $r$ = Radius of the pivot to the point of application of the normal force (m)
*   $w$ = Width of the brake lining (m)
*   $\theta$ = Angle subtended by the brake lining at the center of the drum (radians)
*   $p$ = Intensity of pressure on the lining (N/m²)

**Basic Torque Equation:**

The frictional force on a small element of the lining is $dF_f = \mu dF_n$.
The braking torque due to this element is $dT = dF_f \times R = \mu dF_n \times R$.

**Case 1: Uniform Pressure Distribution**

In this case, the normal force intensity ($p$) is constant over the lining area.
$dF_n = p \times dA$, where $dA$ is the area of the small element.
$dA = r d\phi \times w$ (assuming lining width $w$ and angle $d\phi$ in radians).
$dF_n = p \times r w d\phi$.

The frictional force on the element: $dF_f = \mu p r w d\phi$.
The braking torque due to this element: $dT = (\mu p r w d\phi) \times R$.

To find the total torque, we integrate over the angle subtended by the lining ($\theta$):
$T = \int_{-\theta/2}^{\theta/2} \mu p r w R d\phi = \mu p r w R \int_{-\theta/2}^{\theta/2} d\phi$
$T = \mu p r w R [\phi]_{-\theta/2}^{\theta/2} = \mu p r w R (\theta/2 - (-\theta/2)) = \mu p r w R \theta$.

**Note:** This formula assumes $r \approx R$.

**Case 2: Uniform Rate of Wear**

The rate of wear is proportional to the normal pressure intensity and the sliding velocity. Since the sliding velocity is proportional to the radius, for uniform wear, $p \times r = \text{constant}$ ($p \cdot r = p_0 \cdot R$, where $p_0$ is the maximum pressure at radius $R$).
$p = \frac{p_0 R}{r}$.

$dF_n = p \times r w d\phi = \frac{p_0 R}{r} \times r w d\phi = p_0 R w d\phi$.
$dF_f = \mu dF_n = \mu p_0 R w d\phi$.
$dT = dF_f \times R = (\mu p_0 R w d\phi) \times R = \mu p_0 R^2 w d\phi$.

Integrating over the angle:
$T = \int_{-\theta/2}^{\theta/2} \mu p_0 R^2 w d\phi = \mu p_0 R^2 w [\phi]_{-\theta/2}^{\theta/2}$
$T = \mu p_0 R^2 w \theta$.

**Relationship between Forces and Torque:**

The actuating mechanism applies forces to the shoes. These forces must overcome the frictional forces to create braking torque. For a two-shoe brake actuated by a single cam or hydraulic cylinder, the design needs to consider how forces are transmitted.

**Self-Energizing Effect (Leading Shoe):**

*   When a shoe is placed such that the direction of friction force helps to pivot the shoe against the drum, it's a leading shoe.
*   The frictional force ($F_f$) on the lining generates an additional moment that pushes the shoe further against the drum.

**Analysis of Self-Energizing Shoe:**

Consider a shoe pivoted at point P. Let the actuating force be applied at point A.
The forces acting on the shoe are:
*   Actuating force ($P_a$)
*   Normal force ($F_n$) acting radially outwards
*   Frictional force ($F_f$) tangential to the drum, acting opposite to the direction of rotation.

For equilibrium, we take moments about the pivot P:

$P_a \times d_a - F_n \times d_n - F_f \times d_f = 0$

Where:
*   $d_a$ = Perpendicular distance from pivot P to the line of action of $P_a$.
*   $d_n$ = Perpendicular distance from pivot P to the line of action of $F_n$.
*   $d_f$ = Perpendicular distance from pivot P to the line of action of $F_f$.

Since $F_f = \mu F_n$:
$P_a \times d_a = F_n \times d_n + (\mu F_n) \times d_f = F_n (d_n + \mu d_f)$.
$F_n = \frac{P_a \times d_a}{d_n + \mu d_f}$.

The braking torque $T = F_f \times R = (\mu F_n) \times R = \mu \left( \frac{P_a \times d_a}{d_n + \mu d_f} \right) R$.

The **braking ratio** or **self-energizing ratio** is defined as the ratio of the torque produced by friction to the torque required to produce that friction. It can also be expressed as the ratio of the braking torque to the applied actuating torque.

A **trailing shoe** experiences an opposite effect, where friction tends to pull it away from the drum, reducing the braking force.

#### 2.2 Material Selection:

*   **Brake Drum:**
    *   **Cast Iron (e.g., Gray Iron, Meehanite):** Good wear resistance, good thermal conductivity, relatively low cost. Commonly used.
    *   **Steel:** Higher strength, can withstand higher temperatures, but generally lower thermal conductivity than cast iron.
    *   **Aluminum Alloys (with cast iron liners):** Lightweight, good thermal conductivity, but requires wear-resistant liners.
    *   **Considerations:** High tensile strength, good thermal conductivity, resistance to wear and scoring, resistance to thermal expansion and distortion.
*   **Brake Shoes:**
    *   **Steel:** Provides structural support and a base for the lining.
    *   **Aluminum Alloys:** Lighter weight, can help dissipate heat.
    *   **Considerations:** High rigidity, resistance to fatigue, good thermal conductivity (to transfer heat away from the lining).
*   **Brake Lining:**
    *   **Asbestos (historically):** Excellent thermal stability and friction characteristics, but health hazards led to its phase-out.
    *   **Semi-metallic:** Contains metal fibers (e.g., steel, copper), offering good durability and high-temperature performance. Can be noisy and abrasive to the drum.
    *   **Organic:** Made from composite materials (e.g., fiberglass, aramid fibers, resins). Good initial bite, quieter operation, but may have lower temperature limits.
    *   **Ceramic:** Excellent high-temperature performance, quiet operation, low wear. Can be more expensive.
    *   **Considerations:** High coefficient of friction (stable over a range of temperatures), high wear resistance, good thermal stability, low noise generation, resistance to fading (loss of friction at high temperatures).

#### 2.3 Wear and Life of Brake Linings:

*   **Wear Rate:** The rate at which the brake lining material wears down is crucial for determining the service life of the brake.
    *   Wear is generally proportional to the product of pressure and velocity.
    *   Wear rate $\propto p \times v$.
*   **Service Life:** The total amount of braking work (energy absorbed) before the lining wears down to its minimum thickness.
    *   $L \propto \frac{\text{Volume of lining}}{\text{Wear rate per unit area} \times \text{Area of lining}}$.
*   **Wear Calculation (Simplified):**
    *   Volume of lining = $w \times L_{arc} \times t$, where $L_{arc}$ is the arc length of the lining and $t$ is the initial thickness.
    *   Wear rate per unit area (for uniform wear) = $k \times p$, where $k$ is a wear factor.
    *   Total work done = $\int dW = \int T d\omega$, where $d\omega$ is the angular displacement.
*   **Design for Longevity:** Select materials with high wear resistance, ensure adequate lining area, and manage heat dissipation to prevent premature wear or fading.

#### 2.4 Heat Dissipation and Thermal Considerations:

*   **Energy Dissipation:** Braking converts kinetic energy into heat through friction.
    *   Kinetic Energy = $\frac{1}{2} m v^2$ or $\frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$.
    *   Braking Work = $T \times \Delta\theta$, where $\Delta\theta$ is the total angle of rotation during braking.
*   **Temperature Rise:** Excessive heat can lead to:
    *   **Brake Fade:** Reduced coefficient of friction due to high temperatures, leading to decreased braking effectiveness.
    *   **Lining Degradation:** Thermal decomposition of the lining material.
    *   **Drum Distortion:** Thermal stresses can cause the drum to warp, affecting braking performance and potentially leading to drum failure.
*   **Heat Dissipation Mechanisms:**
    *   **Conduction:** Heat flows from the lining to the brake shoe and then to the drum. The drum dissipates heat to the surroundings through convection and radiation.
    *   **Convection:** Heat transfer from the drum surface to the surrounding air.
    *   **Radiation:** Heat transfer from the drum surface as electromagnetic waves.
*   **Design Strategies:**
    *   **Larger Drum Diameter and Width:** Increase surface area for heat dissipation.
    *   **Material Selection:** Drums made of materials with high thermal conductivity (e.g., cast iron).
    *   **Ventilated Drums:** Drums with fins or holes to increase surface area and airflow.
    *   **Cooling Ducts:** Forced air cooling in some heavy-duty applications.
    *   **Lining Material:** Choosing linings with good thermal stability.
    *   **Intermittent Braking:** Allowing time for cooling between braking applications.

#### 2.5 Actuation Mechanisms:

*   **Cam Actuation:** A rotating cam pivots the brake shoes outwards. Common in simpler designs and drum brakes.
*   **Wedge Actuation:** A wedge is driven between the shoes, forcing them apart.
*   **Hydraulic Actuation:** A master cylinder generates hydraulic pressure, which acts on a slave cylinder or directly on the brake shoes via pistons. This is the most common mechanism in modern vehicles.
*   **Mechanical Actuation:** Linkages and cables for manual operation.

#### 2.6 Design of the Brake Shoe:

*   **Structural Integrity:** The shoe must withstand the forces from the actuating mechanism and the friction forces without deforming or fracturing.
*   **Rigidity:** A rigid shoe ensures uniform contact with the drum.
*   **Contact Area:** The curved surface of the shoe must match the inner profile of the drum.
*   **Pivot Design:** The pivot point must be strong and accurately located to ensure proper shoe movement.
*   **Adjustment Mechanism Integration:** Provision for adjusting the shoe-to-drum clearance.

#### 2.7 General Design Procedure (Summary):

1.  **Determine Braking Requirements:** Calculate the required braking torque based on the vehicle/machinery mass, speed, and deceleration requirements.
2.  **Select Drum and Lining Materials:** Based on operating conditions, temperature, wear, and cost.
3.  **Determine Drum Dimensions:** Diameter and width based on torque, heat dissipation, and space constraints.
4.  **Calculate Lining Area:** Based on allowable pressure intensity and desired wear life.
5.  **Analyze Shoe Forces:** Determine the actuating forces needed and consider the self-energizing effect if applicable.
6.  **Design the Shoe Structure:** Ensure sufficient strength and rigidity to withstand the applied forces.
7.  **Select Actuation Mechanism:** Choose an appropriate mechanism (cam, hydraulic, etc.).
8.  **Consider Wear and Heat Dissipation:** Incorporate design features to manage wear and heat.
9.  **Select Springs and Adjusters:** For proper return and maintenance of clearance.

### 4. Key Concepts and Definitions to Remember:

*   **Braking Torque:** The rotational force opposing motion.
*   **Coefficient of Friction ($\mu$):** Ratio of frictional force to normal force.
*   **Pressure Intensity ($p$):** Force per unit area on the lining.
*   **Uniform Pressure vs. Uniform Wear:** Two common assumptions for brake analysis.
*   **Self-Energizing Effect:** Amplification of braking force due to friction on the leading shoe.
*   **Brake Fade:** Loss of braking performance at high temperatures.
*   **Wear Rate:** Speed at which lining material is lost.
*   **Thermal Conductivity:** Ability of a material to conduct heat.
*   **Actuation Force:** The force applied to move the brake shoes.
*   **Pivot Point:** The point around which the brake shoe rotates.

---

### 5. Important Points to Remember:

*   **Material selection is critical** for balancing wear resistance, thermal performance, and cost.
*   **Heat dissipation is a major design challenge.** Inadequate heat management leads to fade and premature wear.
*   The **self-energizing effect** can significantly amplify braking torque but also makes the brake more sensitive to variations in friction coefficient.
*   **Wear compensation** through an adjustment mechanism is essential for maintaining effective braking.
*   **Safety factors** should be applied to calculations to account for uncertainties in material properties and operating conditions.
*   Consult **design data handbooks (e.g., Bhandari, PSG Tech, Mahadevan & Reddy)** for typical values of pressure, coefficient of friction, wear rates, and material properties.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:**
A single dry shoe brake is to be designed to generate a braking torque of 500 N-m. The drum radius is 150 mm. The shoe subtends an angle of 120 degrees. The coefficient of friction is 0.4. If the lining is subjected to uniform pressure, and the maximum permissible pressure is 0.8 N/mm², calculate:
a) The actuating force required if the shoe is pivoted such that it is a leading shoe and the pivot is located at the trailing end of the shoe. The lining width is 40 mm. (Assume $r=R$ for simplification in this part).
b) The braking torque generated by the self-energizing effect.

**Solution Approach for Question 1:**

*   **Part (a):**
    *   Calculate the required lining area.
    *   Determine the total normal force ($F_n$) required.
    *   Calculate the braking torque from $F_n$: $T = \mu F_n R$.
    *   To determine the actuating force, you'll need to analyze the geometry of the shoe and the moments about the pivot. This will involve calculating the distances $d_a$, $d_n$, and $d_f$ based on the shoe geometry and pivot location. This part requires more detailed geometric analysis than a simple formula.
    *   **Simplified approach if geometry is not provided:** If the problem implies a simplified analysis or focuses on the concept of self-energizing, you might use the ratio of forces. However, a full moment analysis is typically required. Let's assume a simplified moment analysis for demonstration.

    *   **Calculation for Uniform Pressure:**
        *   Lining Area = Width $\times$ Radius $\times$ Angle (in radians)
        *   Angle $\theta = 120^\circ = \frac{120}{180} \pi = \frac{2\pi}{3}$ radians.
        *   Lining Area = $40 \text{ mm} \times 150 \text{ mm} \times \frac{2\pi}{3}$ (This is incorrect, area is width x arc length).
        *   Arc Length = $R \theta = 150 \text{ mm} \times \frac{2\pi}{3} = 100\pi \text{ mm}$.
        *   Lining Area = $w \times (R\theta) = 40 \text{ mm} \times 100\pi \text{ mm} = 4000\pi \text{ mm}^2 = 0.004\pi \text{ m}^2$.
        *   Total Normal Force $F_n = p \times \text{Area} = (0.8 \text{ N/mm}^2) \times (4000\pi \text{ mm}^2) = 3200\pi \text{ N}$.
        *   Braking Torque $T = \mu F_n R = 0.4 \times (3200\pi \text{ N}) \times (0.150 \text{ m}) = 608.7 \text{ N-m}$.
        *   *Self-Correction:* The required torque is 500 N-m. The calculated torque is 608.7 N-m, implying that a maximum pressure of 0.8 N/mm² is perhaps too high or the assumption of uniform pressure might be too simple. Let's re-evaluate based on the target torque.

    *   **Revised Part (a) - Focusing on achieving 500 N-m:**
        *   Let the required total normal force be $F_n$.
        *   $T = \mu F_n R \implies 500 = 0.4 \times F_n \times 0.150$
        *   $F_n = \frac{500}{0.4 \times 0.150} = \frac{500}{0.06} = 8333.33 \text{ N}$.
        *   Now, we need to find the actuating force ($P_a$) to generate this $F_n$, considering the leading shoe geometry.
        *   **Assuming a typical leading shoe geometry (as depicted in many textbooks like Bhandari):**
            *   Let the pivot be at the origin (0,0).
            *   The drum is a circle of radius R.
            *   The shoe is a segment of an annulus.
            *   Let the actuating force $P_a$ be applied at a distance $d_a$ from the pivot.
            *   The normal force $F_n$ acts radially outwards at a distance $d_n$ from the pivot.
            *   The frictional force $F_f = \mu F_n$ acts tangentially at a distance $d_f$ from the pivot.
            *   **Typical geometry for a leading shoe pivoted at the trailing end:**
                *   $d_a$ might be related to the shoe's construction (e.g., where the cam contacts).
                *   $d_n$ is typically the radial distance from the pivot to the center of the lining surface, which is approximately $R$.
                *   $d_f$ is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the friction force. This depends on the angle subtended by the shoe and the pivot location.

        *   **For a leading shoe pivoted at the trailing end, and assuming the actuating force is applied at the leading end:**
            *   Let's consider the moment equation about the pivot P.
            *   $P_a \cdot d_a = F_n \cdot d_n + F_f \cdot d_f$
            *   $P_a \cdot d_a = F_n \cdot d_n + \mu F_n \cdot d_f = F_n (d_n + \mu d_f)$
            *   $P_a = F_n \frac{d_n + \mu d_f}{d_a}$

        *   **To proceed, we need specific geometric dimensions or typical values.** Let's assume for illustration (refer to Bhandari, Chapter 11, for detailed analysis of Shoe Brakes):
            *   Let the angle subtended by the shoe be $2\alpha = 120^\circ$, so $\alpha = 60^\circ$.
            *   Let the pivot P be at $(0,0)$.
            *   The center of the drum is at $(0, R)$.
            *   The shoe is roughly a sector of an annulus.
            *   For a leading shoe pivoted at the trailing edge, the line of action of $F_n$ passes through the pivot. This simplifies $d_n = 0$. This is a common simplification for the primary moment analysis.
            *   $d_f = R \sin(\alpha)$ if the friction force is considered at the midpoint of the lining arc.

        *   **With $d_n=0$ and $d_f = R \sin(\alpha)$:**
            *   $P_a = F_n \frac{\mu R \sin(\alpha)}{d_a}$.
            *   Let's assume the actuating force $P_a$ is applied at the leading edge, at a distance $d_a$ from the pivot. For a symmetric shoe, this could be related to $R \sin(\alpha)$.
            *   If $d_a \approx R \sin(\alpha)$: $P_a = F_n \mu$. This would mean no self-energizing effect, which is incorrect.

        *   **Correcting the moment analysis for a leading shoe pivoted at the trailing end:**
            *   Let pivot P be at $(0,0)$. Drum center is at $(0, R)$.
            *   Line of action of $F_n$ is radial, passing through the drum center $(0, R)$. The perpendicular distance from P to this line is $d_n = R$.
            *   Line of action of $F_f$ is tangential, at radius $R$. The perpendicular distance from P to this line is $d_f$.
            *   The angle subtended by the shoe is $2\theta$. Let's consider the force at the center of the arc. The tangent line to the arc at angle $\phi$ makes an angle $\phi$ with the horizontal.
            *   If the shoe is pivoted at its trailing end, and the lining extends from $-\theta$ to $+\theta$ relative to the line of symmetry.
            *   Let's assume the shoe is a sector of angle $2\alpha$.
            *   **Standard analysis in Bhandari (Chapter 11) for a leading shoe:**
                *   Moment about pivot P: $P_a \cdot d_a - F_n \cdot R - F_f \cdot R \sin \alpha = 0$. (Here $2\alpha$ is the subtended angle, and $R$ is the drum radius).
                *   $P_a \cdot d_a = F_n (R + \mu R \sin \alpha)$.
                *   $F_n = \frac{P_a \cdot d_a}{R(1 + \mu \sin \alpha)}$.
                *   Braking Torque $T = \mu F_n R = \mu \left( \frac{P_a \cdot d_a}{R(1 + \mu \sin \alpha)} \right) R = \frac{\mu P_a d_a}{1 + \mu \sin \alpha}$.
                *   From this, we can express the self-energizing ratio $k_s = \frac{T_{friction}}{T_{applied}} = \frac{\mu}{1 + \mu \sin \alpha}$ or the overall ratio $\frac{T}{P_a d_a} = \frac{\mu}{1 + \mu \sin \alpha}$.
                *   The total torque is $T_{total} = T_{applied} + T_{self-energizing} = P_a d_a + \mu F_n R$.

            *   Let's use the formula $T = \mu p r w R \theta$ derived earlier, with $r=R$.
            *   We have $T = 500 \text{ N-m}$, $\mu = 0.4$, $R = 0.15 \text{ m}$, $2\theta = 120^\circ$, $w = 0.04 \text{ m}$.
            *   $500 = 0.4 \times p \times 0.15 \times 0.04 \times 0.15 \times (2\pi/3)$.
            *   $500 = p \times (0.4 \times 0.15 \times 0.04 \times 0.15 \times 2.094)$.
            *   $500 = p \times 0.001256$.
            *   $p = \frac{500}{0.001256} = 398086 \text{ N/m}^2 \approx 0.398 \text{ N/mm}^2$.
            *   This pressure is lower than the assumed maximum of 0.8 N/mm². So, uniform pressure is feasible.

            *   **Now, let's find $P_a$ using the moment equation:**
                *   $F_n$ for 500 N-m torque with $\mu=0.4$ and $R=0.15$: $F_n = \frac{T}{\mu R} = \frac{500}{0.4 \times 0.15} = 8333.33 \text{ N}$.
                *   For a leading shoe pivoted at the trailing end, the moment equation from Bhandari is often simplified or presented with specific geometric assumptions. Let's use a common simplified form: $P_a \cdot d_a = F_n \cdot R + F_f \cdot R \sin \alpha$.
                *   Here $2\alpha = 120^\circ$, so $\alpha = 60^\circ$. $\sin \alpha = \sin 60^\circ = 0.866$.
                *   We need $d_a$. Let's assume $d_a$ is the distance from the pivot to the point of application of actuating force. A common assumption might be that the actuating force is applied at the center of the shoe's arc, which is at an angle $\alpha$ from the symmetry axis. If the pivot is at the trailing end, the distance from the pivot to the drum center is $R$.
                *   Let's consider the geometry where the pivot is at P, drum center at O. The shoe is a segment of the circle of radius R.
                *   For a leading shoe, friction force $F_f$ assists the actuation.
                *   $F_n$ acts radially outward. $F_f$ acts tangentially.
                *   Moment about P: $P_a \cdot d_a - F_n \cdot R - F_f \cdot (R \sin \alpha) = 0$. (This assumes $d_n=R$ and $d_f=R \sin \alpha$).
                *   $P_a \cdot d_a = F_n (R + \mu R \sin \alpha) = 8333.33 \times (0.15 + 0.4 \times 0.15 \times 0.866)$
                *   $P_a \cdot d_a = 8333.33 \times (0.15 + 0.05196) = 8333.33 \times 0.20196 = 16830 \text{ N-m}$.
                *   To find $P_a$, we need $d_a$. If we assume $d_a$ is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of $P_a$. If $P_a$ is applied at the leading edge, $d_a$ could be $2R \sin \alpha$.
                *   Let's assume $d_a$ is the lever arm for the actuating force.

*   **Uniform Wear:** $p \cdot r = \text{constant} = p_{max} \cdot R$.
    *   $p = \frac{p_{max} R}{r} = \frac{1.0 \text{ N/mm}^2 \times 150 \text{ mm}}{r} = \frac{150}{r} \text{ N/mm}^2$.
*   **Braking Torque Formula for Uniform Wear:** $T = \mu p_{max} R^2 \theta$.
    *   Drum Diameter = 300 mm $\implies$ Radius $R = 150 \text{ mm} = 0.15 \text{ m}$.
    *   Subtended Angle $2\theta = 100^\circ$. So, $\theta = 50^\circ = \frac{50}{180} \pi = \frac{5\pi}{18}$ radians.
    *   $\mu = 0.35$.
    *   $p_{max} = 1.0 \text{ N/mm}^2$.
    *   $T = 0.35 \times (1.0 \text{ N/mm}^2) \times (150 \text{ mm})^2 \times (\frac{5\pi}{18})$.
    *   $T = 0.35 \times 1.0 \times 22500 \times \frac{5\pi}{18} \text{ N-mm}$.
    *   $T = 0.35 \times 22500 \times 0.87266 \text{ N-mm}$.
    *   $T = 6886.9 \text{ N-mm} = 6.887 \text{ N-m}$.

*   **Correction needed:** The formula is $T = \mu p_{max} R^2 (2\theta)$ when $2\theta$ is the total angle.
    *   Let's re-derive for clarity. $dT = \mu (prw) R$. For uniform wear, $pr = p_{max}R$.
    *   $dT = \mu (p_{max}R) w R = \mu p_{max} R^2 w d\phi$.
    *   Integrating from $-\theta$ to $\theta$: $T = \int_{-\theta}^{\theta} \mu p_{max} R^2 w d\phi = \mu p_{max} R^2 w [2\theta]$.
    *   Here, the width $w$ is missing. Let's assume a width or assume the formula is for torque per unit width.

    *   **If the formula is $T = \mu p_{max} R^2 \theta$, this usually implies $\theta$ is half the angle.**
    *   $T = 0.35 \times 1.0 \times (0.15)^2 \times (\frac{5\pi}{18}) = 0.35 \times 0.0225 \times 0.87266 = 0.0068869 \text{ N-m}$. This is too small.

    *   **Assume the question implies torque per unit width, or a width is implicitly assumed.**
    *   If we use the formula $T = \mu p_{max} R^2 \theta$ where $\theta$ is half the angle, and $w$ is assumed to be 1:
        *   $T = 0.35 \times 1.0 \times (150)^2 \times (5\pi/18)$ = 6886.9 N-mm = 6.887 N-m. Still seems low for a brake.

**Answer to Question 2:**
Assuming the question asks for torque per unit width:
Torque per unit width ($T/w$) = $\mu p_{max} R^2 (2\theta)$
$R = 0.15 \text{ m}$
