# ENGINEERING MATERIALS - MODULE 2: INTRODUCTION TO METALS

## Topic: Types of Metals: Ferrous and Non-Ferrous, Crystal Structures and Properties

### **1. Introduction to Metals**

**Definition:** Metals are a class of materials that are characterized by their lustrous appearance, high electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability, ductility, and strength. They are typically formed by metallic bonding, where valence electrons are delocalized and form a "sea" around positively charged metal ions.

**Importance in Engineering:** Metals and their alloys are the backbone of modern engineering due to their excellent combination of mechanical properties, workability, and conductivity. They find applications in virtually every sector, from construction and automotive to aerospace and electronics.

**Key Concept:** Metallic Bonding (Valence electrons form a sea, holding positive ions together. This explains conductivity, malleability, and ductility.)

**Reference:** Callister & Rethwisch, Chapter 2 (Atomic Structure and Interatomic Bonding) - Focus on metallic bonding section.

### **2. Types of Metals: Ferrous and Non-Ferrous**

This classification is based on the primary metallic element present.

#### **2.1. Ferrous Metals**

**Definition:** Ferrous metals are alloys where iron is the primary constituent. They are generally characterized by high strength, hardness, and magnetic properties, but can be susceptible to corrosion.

**Key Components:** Iron (Fe) as the base element.

**Common Ferrous Alloys:**

*   **Iron:** Pure iron is relatively soft and not widely used in its pure form.
*   **Steel:** An alloy of iron and carbon, typically containing less than 2.14 wt% carbon.
    *   **Carbon Steel:** Primarily iron and carbon, with small amounts of manganese and silicon.
        *   **Low Carbon Steel (Mild Steel):** < 0.25% C. Ductile, weldable, good formability. Applications: Structural components, car bodies, pipes.
        *   **Medium Carbon Steel:** 0.25% - 0.60% C. Higher strength and hardness than low carbon steel. Applications: Gears, shafts, rails.
        *   **High Carbon Steel:** 0.60% - 1.40% C. Very hard and strong, but brittle. Applications: Cutting tools, springs, dies.
    *   **Alloy Steel:** Carbon steel with added alloying elements (e.g., chromium, nickel, molybdenum, vanadium) to enhance specific properties like strength, hardness, corrosion resistance, or wear resistance.
        *   **Stainless Steel:** Contains at least 10.5% chromium, which forms a passive oxide layer providing excellent corrosion resistance. Applications: Kitchenware, surgical instruments, chemical processing equipment.
        *   **Tool Steel:** High carbon alloy steels designed for hardness, wear resistance, and toughness for tooling applications.
*   **Cast Iron:** An alloy of iron and carbon, typically containing more than 2.14 wt% carbon, often with silicon.
    *   **Gray Cast Iron:** Carbon exists as graphite flakes. Good compressive strength, damping capacity, machinability. Applications: Engine blocks, machine bases.
    *   **Ductile (Nodular) Cast Iron:** Graphite in spherical nodules. Better ductility and toughness than gray cast iron. Applications: Pipes, crankshafts.
    *   **White Cast Iron:** Carbon exists as iron carbide (cementite). Very hard and brittle. Applications: Wear-resistant parts.
    *   **Malleable Cast Iron:** Produced by heat treating white cast iron. Graphite nodules formed from cementite decomposition. Good ductility. Applications: Pipe fittings, agricultural equipment.

**Advantages of Ferrous Metals:**
*   High strength and hardness
*   Good magnetic properties (for some types)
*   Relatively low cost
*   Abundant in nature

**Disadvantages of Ferrous Metals:**
*   Susceptibility to corrosion (rusting)
*   Can be heavy
*   Limited ductility and toughness in some high-carbon steels

**Reference:** Budinski & Budinski, Chapter 5 (Ferrous Metals); Callister & Rethwisch, Chapter 11 (Iron-Carbon Alloys).

#### **2.2. Non-Ferrous Metals**

**Definition:** Non-ferrous metals are alloys that do not have iron as their primary constituent. They generally exhibit good corrosion resistance, lower density, and a wider range of properties compared to ferrous metals.

**Key Components:** Aluminum, Copper, Nickel, Zinc, Titanium, Magnesium, Lead, Tin, precious metals (Gold, Silver, Platinum), etc.

**Common Non-Ferrous Metals and Alloys:**

*   **Aluminum (Al) and its Alloys:**
    *   **Properties:** Lightweight, excellent corrosion resistance (due to protective oxide layer), good electrical and thermal conductivity, moderate strength (can be enhanced by alloying and heat treatment).
    *   **Applications:** Aircraft frames, automotive parts, beverage cans, window frames, electrical conductors.
    *   **Key Alloys:** Duralumin (Al-Cu-Mg), Al-Si alloys for casting.
*   **Copper (Cu) and its Alloys:**
    *   **Properties:** Excellent electrical and thermal conductivity, good ductility, corrosion resistance, antimicrobial properties.
    *   **Applications:** Electrical wiring, plumbing, heat exchangers, cookware, coins.
    *   **Key Alloys:**
        *   **Brass:** Copper-Zinc alloy. Good strength, machinability, corrosion resistance. Applications: Plumbing fittings, decorative items.
        *   **Bronze:** Copper-Tin alloy (historically). Now often refers to copper alloys with other elements like aluminum, manganese, or silicon. High strength, wear resistance. Applications: Bearings, sculptures, marine hardware.
        *   **Cupronickel:** Copper-Nickel alloy. Excellent corrosion resistance, especially in seawater. Applications: Marine components, coinage.
*   **Nickel (Ni) and its Alloys:**
    *   **Properties:** Excellent corrosion resistance, high-temperature strength, magnetic properties.
    *   **Applications:** Stainless steels (as an alloying element), superalloys for jet engines, batteries, plating.
    *   **Key Alloys:** Inconel, Monel.
*   **Zinc (Zn) and its Alloys:**
    *   **Properties:** Good corrosion resistance (used for galvanizing steel), low melting point.
    *   **Applications:** Galvanizing, die casting alloys, brass production.
*   **Titanium (Ti) and its Alloys:**
    *   **Properties:** High strength-to-weight ratio, excellent corrosion resistance, biocompatibility, high melting point.
    *   **Applications:** Aerospace components, medical implants, chemical processing equipment, sporting goods.
*   **Magnesium (Mg) and its Alloys:**
    *   **Properties:** Very lightweight, good strength-to-weight ratio, but lower corrosion resistance and flammability concerns.
    *   **Applications:** Aircraft components, automotive parts (engine blocks), portable electronics.
*   **Lead (Pb), Tin (Sn), Precious Metals (Au, Ag, Pt):**
    *   **Lead:** Soft, dense, corrosion resistant, low melting point. Applications: Batteries, solders, radiation shielding.
    *   **Tin:** Soft, corrosion resistant, low melting point. Applications: Solders, plating (tin cans).
    *   **Gold, Silver, Platinum:** Excellent corrosion resistance, electrical conductivity, high cost. Applications: Electronics, jewelry, catalysis.

**Advantages of Non-Ferrous Metals:**
*   Excellent corrosion resistance
*   Lightweight (especially Aluminum and Magnesium)
*   Good thermal and electrical conductivity (Copper, Aluminum)
*   Non-magnetic (most)
*   Biocompatibility (Titanium)

**Disadvantages of Non-Ferrous Metals:**
*   Generally higher cost than steel
*   Lower strength and hardness compared to many steels (can be overcome with alloying and processing)
*   Flammability (Magnesium)
*   Difficult to work with at high temperatures (Titanium)

**Reference:** Budinski & Budinski, Chapters 6-10 (Non-Ferrous Metals); Callister & Rethwisch, Chapter 12 (Alloy Systems).

### **3. Crystal Structures of Metals**

Metals solidify from a molten state into crystalline structures. The arrangement of atoms in a regular, repeating three-dimensional pattern is called a crystal structure.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Crystal Lattice:** A 3D array of points representing the positions of atoms or molecules.
*   **Unit Cell:** The smallest repeating unit of the crystal lattice that, when translated in three dimensions, describes the entire crystal structure.
*   **Crystallographic Directions and Planes:** Specific orientations within the crystal lattice, important for understanding material properties.

**Common Crystal Structures in Metals:**

1.  **Body-Centered Cubic (BCC):**
    *   **Description:** Atoms are located at each corner of a cube and one atom is at the center of the cube.
    *   **Atoms per Unit Cell:** 2 (1 at center + 8 x 1/8 at corners)
    *   **Coordination Number:** 8 (each atom is surrounded by 8 nearest neighbors).
    *   **Atomic Packing Factor (APF):** 0.68 (relatively loosely packed).
    *   **Examples of Metals:** Iron (at room temperature), Chromium (Cr), Tungsten (W), Vanadium (V), Molybdenum (Mo).
    *   **Properties often associated with BCC:** Good strength and hardness at room temperature, ductile at higher temperatures, but can be brittle at low temperatures.

2.  **Face-Centered Cubic (FCC):**
    *   **Description:** Atoms are located at each corner of a cube and one atom is at the center of each face.
    *   **Atoms per Unit Cell:** 4 (1 at center of 6 faces + 8 x 1/8 at corners)
    *   **Coordination Number:** 12 (highly packed).
    *   **Atomic Packing Factor (APF):** 0.74 (most densely packed common metallic structure).
    *   **Examples of Metals:** Aluminum (Al), Copper (Cu), Nickel (Ni), Gold (Au), Silver (Ag), Platinum (Pt), Lead (Pb).
    *   **Properties often associated with FCC:** High ductility and malleability, good formability, generally less strong than BCC metals at room temperature.

3.  **Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP):**
    *   **Description:** A more complex structure than BCC and FCC. It involves 3 atoms in the center plane, 6 atoms in the outer planes (corners), and 1 atom at the center of the prism. The packing is very efficient.
    *   **Atoms per Unit Cell:** 6 (2 full atoms in the unit cell, but calculated as 12 corner atoms + 2 center atoms + 3 inner atoms = 6).
    *   **Coordination Number:** 12 (highly packed, similar to FCC).
    *   **Atomic Packing Factor (APF):** 0.74 (most densely packed common metallic structure, same as FCC).
    *   **Examples of Metals:** Magnesium (Mg), Zinc (Zn), Titanium (Ti), Cadmium (Cd), Cobalt (Co), Zirconium (Zr).
    *   **Properties often associated with HCP:** Can be less ductile than FCC metals due to fewer available slip systems. Properties can be anisotropic (direction-dependent).

**Important Point:** The crystal structure significantly influences a metal's mechanical properties, such as ductility, strength, and toughness. FCC metals are generally more ductile because they have more slip systems (planes and directions along which atoms can slide past each other).

**Reference:** Callister & Rethwisch, Chapter 3 (Crystal Structure and Coordination); Askeland, Chapter 3 (Crystallinity and Crystalline Defects).

### **4. Properties of Metals**

Properties are crucial for material selection. They can be broadly categorized as mechanical, physical, and chemical.

#### **4.1. Mechanical Properties**

These relate to a material's response to applied forces.

*   **Strength:** The ability to resist deformation or fracture under stress.
    *   **Tensile Strength:** Maximum stress a material can withstand while being stretched or pulled before breaking.
    *   **Yield Strength:** Stress at which a material begins to deform plastically (permanently).
    *   **Compressive Strength:** Ability to resist crushing forces.
*   **Hardness:** Resistance to indentation, scratching, or abrasion.
*   **Ductility:** Ability to deform plastically under tensile stress without fracturing (can be drawn into wires).
*   **Malleability:** Ability to deform plastically under compressive stress without fracturing (can be hammered or rolled into thin sheets).
*   **Toughness:** Ability to absorb energy and deform plastically before fracturing. It's a combination of strength and ductility.
*   **Stiffness (Elastic Modulus):** Resistance to elastic deformation under stress. A measure of rigidity.
*   **Fatigue Strength:** Ability to withstand repeated cyclic loading without failure.
*   **Creep Resistance:** Ability to resist slow deformation under sustained stress at elevated temperatures.

**How Crystal Structure Affects Mechanical Properties:**

*   **Ductility:** FCC metals (e.g., Al, Cu) are generally more ductile than BCC (e.g., Fe at RT) and HCP (e.g., Mg, Ti) metals due to the presence of more slip systems.
*   **Strength:** BCC metals can be stronger than FCC metals at room temperature, but FCC metals maintain ductility at higher temperatures. HCP metals can exhibit anisotropic behavior and lower ductility if slip systems are limited.

**Reference:** Callister & Rethwisch, Chapter 6 (Mechanical Properties of Metals); Budinski & Budinski, Chapter 3 (Mechanical Properties).

#### **4.2. Physical Properties**

These are intrinsic properties that do not depend on external forces.

*   **Density:** Mass per unit volume. Important for weight-sensitive applications (e.g., aerospace). Aluminum and Magnesium have low densities.
*   **Melting Point:** Temperature at which a solid transitions to a liquid. Tungsten has a very high melting point, useful for filaments.
*   **Thermal Conductivity:** Ability to transfer heat. Copper and Aluminum are excellent thermal conductors.
*   **Electrical Conductivity:** Ability to conduct electric current. Copper and Aluminum are excellent electrical conductors.
*   **Coefficient of Thermal Expansion:** Tendency of matter to change its volume in response to temperature changes.
*   **Magnetic Properties:** Ferrous metals (iron, nickel, cobalt, and their alloys) are ferromagnetic, meaning they can be strongly magnetized. Many non-ferrous metals are paramagnetic or diamagnetic.

**Reference:** Callister & Rethwisch, Chapter 18 (Physical Properties of Metals); Budinski & Budinski, Chapter 4 (Physical Properties).

#### **4.3. Chemical Properties**

These relate to a material's reactivity with its environment.

*   **Corrosion Resistance:** Ability to withstand degradation due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with its environment. Stainless steel and titanium have excellent corrosion resistance. Aluminum forms a passive oxide layer.
*   **Oxidation:** Reaction with oxygen. Iron readily oxidizes to form rust.

**Reference:** Budinski & Budinski, Chapter 17 (Corrosion and Wear); Callister & Rethwisch, Chapter 17 (Corrosion).

### **5. Applications (Connecting Properties to Use)**

Understanding the properties of metals allows engineers to select the most appropriate material for a given application. This aligns with Course Outcomes CO2 and CO4.

*   **Aerospace:** Lightweight, high strength-to-weight ratio, high-temperature resistance. **Aluminum alloys, Titanium alloys, Nickel superalloys.**
*   **Automotive:** Strength, toughness, formability, corrosion resistance, cost. **Steel (carbon and alloy), Aluminum alloys, Cast iron, Magnesium alloys.**
*   **Electrical Wiring:** High electrical conductivity, ductility. **Copper, Aluminum.**
*   **Construction:** High strength, stiffness, cost-effectiveness. **Steel (structural beams), Cast iron (pipes).**
*   **Medical Implants:** Biocompatibility, corrosion resistance, strength. **Titanium alloys, Stainless steel, Cobalt-Chromium alloys.**
*   **Cutting Tools:** Hardness, wear resistance, toughness. **High-carbon steels, Tool steels.**

**Reference:** Budinski & Budinski, Various Chapters on specific material applications; Shackelford, Chapters on material selection.

### **6. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** Differentiate between ferrous and non-ferrous metals, providing at least two examples of each and their common applications.
**Answer:**
*   **Ferrous metals:** Contain iron as the primary element.
    *   **Example 1:** Steel (alloy of iron and carbon). Applications: Structural beams in buildings, car bodies.
    *   **Example 2:** Cast iron. Applications: Engine blocks, machine bases.
*   **Non-ferrous metals:** Do not contain iron as the primary element.
    *   **Example 1:** Aluminum. Applications: Aircraft frames, beverage cans.
    *   **Example 2:** Copper. Applications: Electrical wiring, plumbing.

**Question 2:** Describe the three common crystal structures found in metals (BCC, FCC, HCP). Which structure is associated with the highest ductility and why?
**Answer:**
*   **BCC (Body-Centered Cubic):** Atoms at corners and center of the cube. Coordination number 8, APF 0.68. Example: Iron (RT).
*   **FCC (Face-Centered Cubic):** Atoms at corners and center of each face. Coordination number 12, APF 0.74. Example: Aluminum, Copper.
*   **HCP (Hexagonal Close-Packed):** A hexagonal arrangement. Coordination number 12, APF 0.74. Example: Magnesium, Titanium.

The FCC structure is associated with the highest ductility because it has more slip systems (planes and directions along which atoms can slide) compared to BCC and HCP, allowing for easier plastic deformation.

**Question 3:** A component in an aircraft needs to be lightweight, strong, and resistant to corrosion. Which type of metal or alloy would be most suitable and why?
**Answer:** An **Aluminum alloy** or a **Titanium alloy** would be most suitable.
*   **Aluminum alloys** are lightweight, have good strength-to-weight ratios (when alloyed and treated), and excellent corrosion resistance.
*   **Titanium alloys** offer an even higher strength-to-weight ratio and exceptional corrosion resistance, making them ideal for critical aerospace applications despite their higher cost.

**Question 4:** What property makes copper an excellent choice for electrical wiring?
**Answer:** Copper's excellent **electrical conductivity** and good **ductility** make it ideal for electrical wiring. Its ductility allows it to be drawn into thin wires, and its conductivity minimizes energy loss during electricity transmission.

**Question 5:** How does the carbon content affect the properties of steel?
**Answer:**
*   Increasing carbon content in steel generally increases **strength** and **hardness**.
*   However, it also tends to decrease **ductility** and **toughness**, making the steel more brittle.
*   Low carbon steel (<0.25% C) is ductile and formable (mild steel).
*   High carbon steel (>0.60% C) is hard and strong but brittle, suitable for cutting tools and springs.

### **7. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Metallic Bonding** is the fundamental bonding mechanism in metals, responsible for their characteristic properties.
*   **Ferrous metals** are iron-based, strong but prone to corrosion. **Non-ferrous metals** are diverse, offering better corrosion resistance and lower density.
*   **Crystal structure (BCC, FCC, HCP)** dictates many mechanical properties, especially ductility. FCC is generally the most ductile.
*   **Properties** (mechanical, physical, chemical) are the basis for selecting metals for specific engineering applications.
*   **Alloying** is a key method to tailor the properties of metals for enhanced performance.
*   Understanding the **trade-offs** between properties and cost is crucial for effective material selection.
