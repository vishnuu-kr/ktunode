# Product Design and Innovation: Module 4 - Product Costing

This module delves into the critical aspect of product costing, enabling us to understand the financial implications of our design decisions. By mastering product costing, we can make informed choices that lead to profitable and successful products.

**Learning Outcomes:**

*   Understand the various components and methods of product costing.
*   Analyze the impact of design decisions on product costs.
*   Apply costing principles to estimate and control product expenses throughout the development lifecycle.
*   Recognize the relationship between product costing and value engineering.
*   Appreciate how costing considerations influence design for manufacturing and assembly (DFMA).

---

## 1. Introduction to Product Costing

**Key Concept:** Product costing is the process of determining the cost of producing a product. This involves identifying and quantifying all expenses associated with raw materials, labor, manufacturing overhead, and other related costs.

**Importance:**

*   **Pricing Decisions:** Accurate cost information is essential for setting competitive and profitable prices.
*   **Profitability Analysis:** Understanding costs allows for the calculation of profit margins and the identification of areas for cost reduction.
*   **Make-or-Buy Decisions:** Costing helps in evaluating whether to produce components in-house or outsource them.
*   **Product Portfolio Management:** It aids in deciding which products are most profitable and deserve further investment.
*   **Cost Control and Reduction:** By identifying cost drivers, designers and managers can implement strategies to reduce expenses.

**Reference:** Ulrich & Eppinger, in their discussion of "Target Costing" (Chapter 7), emphasize that understanding costs early in the development process is crucial for achieving profitability. They highlight that design decisions have a significant impact on the final cost of a product.

## 2. Components of Product Cost

Product costs can be broadly categorized into direct and indirect costs.

### 2.1 Direct Costs

**Definition:** Costs that can be directly traced to a specific product.

*   **Direct Materials:** The cost of raw materials that become an integral part of the finished product.
    *   **Example:** The cost of plastic for a smartphone casing, the cost of wood for a chair.
    *   **From Ulrich & Eppinger:** Chapter 7 discusses materials selection and their cost implications. Choosing different materials can drastically alter the direct material cost.
*   **Direct Labor:** The wages paid to workers directly involved in the manufacturing or assembly of the product.
    *   **Example:** The wages of an assembly line worker, a machine operator.
    *   **Note:** This includes wages, benefits, and payroll taxes.

### 2.2 Indirect Costs (Overhead)

**Definition:** Costs that are not directly traceable to a specific product but are necessary for the overall production process. These are often allocated to products.

*   **Manufacturing Overhead:**
    *   **Indirect Materials:** Materials used in the production process but not directly incorporated into the final product (e.g., lubricants for machinery, cleaning supplies).
    *   **Indirect Labor:** Wages of workers not directly involved in production (e.g., factory supervisors, maintenance staff, quality control personnel).
    *   **Factory Utilities:** Electricity, water, and gas consumed by the factory.
    *   **Depreciation of Factory Equipment:** The cost of using machinery and equipment over its useful life.
    *   **Factory Rent/Mortgage:** Cost of the production facility.
    *   **Insurance on Factory Property:** Protection against damages.

*   **Non-Manufacturing Costs (Operating Expenses):**
    *   **Selling Expenses:** Costs incurred to market and sell the product (e.g., advertising, sales commissions, shipping).
    *   **Administrative Expenses:** Costs associated with the general management of the company (e.g., executive salaries, accounting, legal fees).

**Important Point to Remember:** The accurate allocation of indirect costs is crucial for determining the true cost of a product, especially when dealing with multiple product lines.

## 3. Methods of Product Costing

Different costing methods are used depending on the nature of the product and the business.

### 3.1 Job Costing

**Definition:** A costing system used when products are unique or produced in distinct batches. Costs are accumulated for each individual job or project.

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Each job is given a unique identifier.
    *   Direct materials and direct labor are tracked for each job.
    *   Overhead is allocated to jobs based on a predetermined overhead rate.
*   **Examples:** Custom furniture manufacturing, construction projects, consulting services, printing jobs.
*   **Reference:** Otto & Wood (2000) might discuss cost estimation for custom or one-off designs within their context of reverse engineering and new product development.

### 3.2 Process Costing

**Definition:** A costing system used when a company produces large volumes of identical or very similar products through a continuous production process. Costs are accumulated by department or process.

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Costs are averaged over all units produced within a period.
    *   Focuses on the cost per unit for each production stage.
*   **Examples:** Food processing, chemical manufacturing, oil refining, mass production of consumer goods (e.g., soda bottles).

### 3.3 Activity-Based Costing (ABC)

**Definition:** A costing method that identifies activities in an organization and assigns the cost of each activity to all products and services that consume the activity. It aims to provide a more accurate allocation of overhead costs.

*   **How it works:**
    1.  **Identify Activities:** Break down production into specific activities (e.g., machine setup, quality inspection, order processing).
    2.  **Assign Costs to Activities:** Pool costs related to each activity.
    3.  **Identify Cost Drivers:** Determine the factors that cause the cost of an activity to increase (e.g., number of setups, number of inspections).
    4.  **Allocate Costs to Products:** Assign activity costs to products based on their consumption of cost drivers.
*   **Benefits:** Provides more accurate product costs, especially in companies with diverse product lines and complex overhead structures. Helps identify non-value-adding activities.
*   **Example:** A company manufacturing both simple and complex electronic devices. ABC would allocate higher overhead costs to the complex devices that require more machine setups and quality inspections.
*   **Reference:** Ulrich & Eppinger might indirectly touch upon ABC principles when discussing cost management and how design choices can influence the complexity of production processes and thus overhead.

## 4. Cost Estimation and Product Development Lifecycle

**Key Concept:** Costs are incurred throughout the entire product development lifecycle, from concept to end-of-life. Early cost estimation is critical for making informed decisions and managing the overall project budget.

### 4.1 Concept Development Phase

*   **Focus:** Estimating the feasibility and potential profitability of different product concepts.
*   **Methods:** Rough order-of-magnitude estimates, analogy-based estimates, parametric estimates.
*   **Impact:** Crucial for go/no-go decisions on product development.

### 4.2 System-Level Design Phase

*   **Focus:** Estimating the costs of major subsystems and their integration.
*   **Methods:** Detailed cost breakdowns of subsystems, early vendor quotes.
*   **Impact:** Helps refine the overall product architecture and identify potential cost-saving opportunities in design.

### 4.3 Detail Design Phase

*   **Focus:** Estimating the precise costs of individual components, materials, manufacturing processes, and assembly.
*   **Methods:** Bottom-up costing, vendor quotes for specific parts, detailed labor estimates, simulation-based cost analysis.
*   **Impact:** This is where most detailed cost information is generated, allowing for precise cost control.

### 4.4 Testing and Refinement Phase

*   **Focus:** Estimating the costs associated with prototyping, testing, and design iterations.
*   **Impact:** Identifying design flaws that might lead to increased costs if not addressed early.

### 4.5 Production Ramp-Up Phase

*   **Focus:** Tracking actual production costs and comparing them to estimates.
*   **Impact:** Fine-tuning production processes and identifying inefficiencies.

**Important Point to Remember:** The earlier cost estimates are made, the greater the impact design decisions can have on the final cost. Costs are "locked in" progressively as the product moves through development.

## 5. Target Costing

**Definition:** A cost management strategy where the target cost is determined first, based on a product's selling price and desired profit margin, and then the design and production processes are engineered to meet that target cost.

*   **Formula:**
    `Target Cost = Target Selling Price - Target Profit Margin`

*   **Process:**
    1.  **Determine Target Selling Price:** Based on market research, competitor pricing, and customer perception of value.
    2.  **Determine Target Profit Margin:** Based on company goals and strategic objectives.
    3.  **Calculate Target Cost:** The allowable cost to produce the product.
    4.  **Cost Estimation:** Estimate the current or expected cost of the product.
    5.  **Cost Gap Analysis:** Compare the estimated cost with the target cost.
    6.  **Cost Reduction Efforts:** Implement design changes, process improvements, and sourcing strategies to close the cost gap.

*   **Example:** A company wants to launch a new smartphone at a retail price of $500 and aims for a 20% profit margin.
    *   Target Profit = $500 * 0.20 = $100
    *   Target Cost = $500 - $100 = $400
    The design and engineering teams must then ensure the product can be manufactured and sold for $400 or less.

*   **Reference:** Ulrich & Eppinger (Chapter 7) dedicate significant attention to target costing, presenting it as a proactive approach to cost management that integrates cost considerations directly into the design process. They emphasize the importance of cross-functional teams in achieving target costs.

## 6. Cost Implications of Design Decisions

**Key Concept:** Nearly every design decision has a cost implication. Understanding these implications allows designers to make trade-offs that optimize both product performance and profitability.

### 6.1 Material Selection

*   **Impact:** Different materials have varying costs per unit, processing costs, and waste factors.
*   **Example:** Using a premium alloy instead of standard steel will increase material costs but might offer better strength or corrosion resistance.

### 6.2 Manufacturing Process Selection

*   **Impact:** The choice of manufacturing processes (e.g., injection molding, machining, stamping) influences tooling costs, cycle times, labor requirements, and material waste.
*   **Example:** Injection molding requires significant upfront tooling costs but can be very cost-effective for high-volume production with consistent part quality. Machining offers flexibility for low volumes but can be slower and more expensive per unit.

### 6.3 Design Complexity and Features

*   **Impact:** More complex designs with more features generally require more materials, more intricate manufacturing processes, and longer assembly times, all contributing to higher costs.
*   **Example:** A product with many intricate moving parts will likely be more expensive to manufacture and assemble than a simpler product with fewer components.

### 6.4 Design for Manufacturing and Assembly (DFMA)

*   **Impact:** Applying DFMA principles aims to reduce manufacturing and assembly costs by simplifying designs.
*   **Key Principles:**
    *   **Reduce Part Count:** Fewer parts mean less material, fewer assembly steps, and reduced inventory.
    *   **Standardize Components:** Using standard, off-the-shelf parts reduces design, tooling, and purchasing costs.
    *   **Design for Ease of Assembly:** Designing parts to be self-aligning, symmetrical, and easy to handle reduces assembly time and errors.
*   **Reference:** **CO3: Introduce the concept of Design for Manufacturing and Assembly in product design (Knowledge Level: K2)**. This module directly supports this CO by showing how costing is intrinsically linked to DFMA. Ulrich & Eppinger likely cover DFMA and its cost benefits. Otto & Wood's "Techniques in Reverse Engineering" might implicitly highlight DFMA through the analysis of existing products' manufacturability.

## 7. Value Engineering and Costing

**Definition:** Value engineering is a systematic method to improve the "value" of goods or products and services by examining function. Value, in this context, is defined as the ratio of function to cost.

*   **Formula:**
    `Value = Function / Cost`

*   **How Costing is Integrated:**
    *   **Function Analysis:** Identifying the essential functions of a product.
    *   **Cost Analysis:** Determining the cost associated with each function.
    *   **Value Analysis:** Evaluating the cost-effectiveness of each function and identifying areas where functionality can be achieved at a lower cost.
*   **Example:** A chair's primary function is to support a person. Value engineering might explore using lighter, less expensive materials or simplifying the construction while maintaining the core function of support.
*   **Reference:** **CO4: Use value engineering in the development of product (Knowledge Level: K2)**. This module directly connects costing to the principles of value engineering.

## 8. Costing and Robust Design

**Key Concept:** Robust design aims to create products that are insensitive to variations in manufacturing, the environment, and usage. While not directly a costing method, it significantly impacts long-term costs.

*   **Impact on Cost:**
    *   **Reduced Warranty Costs:** Products that perform consistently under various conditions are less likely to fail prematurely, leading to fewer warranty claims.
    *   **Reduced Rework and Scrap:** Robust designs minimize manufacturing defects, reducing scrap and rework costs.
    *   **Improved Customer Satisfaction:** Leading to brand loyalty and repeat business, which indirectly contributes to profitability.
*   **Reference:** **CO2: Develop knowledge of robust design and conceptual design (Knowledge Level: K2)**. Understanding the cost implications of robust design is a key takeaway for this CO.

## 9. Costing and Ergonomics

**Key Concept:** Ergonomics focuses on designing products that are safe, comfortable, and efficient for human use.

*   **Impact on Cost:**
    *   **Reduced Development Costs:** By incorporating ergonomic principles early, costly redesigns to address user discomfort or safety issues can be avoided.
    *   **Reduced Manufacturing/Assembly Costs:** Well-designed interfaces and controls can lead to faster and more accurate assembly.
    *   **Reduced Post-Production Costs:** Comfortable and safe products can lead to fewer user injuries and associated liability costs.
*   **Reference:** **CO5: Incorporate ergonomics and rapid prototyping in product development (Knowledge Level: K2)**. This module highlights how ergonomic considerations have cost implications.

## 10. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** A company is designing a new electronic gadget. The target selling price is $50. The company desires a profit margin of 25%. What is the target cost for this product?

**Answer 1:**
Target Profit Margin = 25% of $50 = $0.25 * $50 = $12.50
Target Cost = Target Selling Price - Target Profit Margin
Target Cost = $50 - $12.50 = **$37.50**

**Question 2:** Identify three types of indirect manufacturing costs.

**Answer 2:**
1.  Indirect materials (e.g., lubricants for machines)
2.  Indirect labor (e.g., factory supervisor's salary)
3.  Depreciation of factory equipment

**Question 3:** Explain the primary difference between Job Costing and Process Costing.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Job Costing** is used for unique or custom products produced in batches, where costs are tracked for each individual job.
*   **Process Costing** is used for mass-produced, identical products through a continuous process, where costs are averaged over all units produced in a department or process.

**Question 4:** How can applying Design for Manufacturing and Assembly (DFMA) principles help in reducing product costs?

**Answer 4:**
DFMA helps reduce product costs by:
*   **Reducing part count:** Fewer parts mean less material, fewer assembly steps, and lower inventory costs.
*   **Standardizing components:** Using off-the-shelf parts reduces design, tooling, and purchasing expenses.
*   **Designing for ease of assembly:** Simpler assembly reduces labor time, errors, and rework, lowering manufacturing costs.

**Question 5:** Imagine you are tasked with redesigning a product to improve its "value" where Value = Function / Cost. What actions would you take from a costing perspective?

**Answer 5:**
To improve value, I would focus on:
*   **Increasing Function:** Exploring ways to enhance the product's performance or add beneficial features without a proportional increase in cost.
*   **Decreasing Cost:**
    *   **Material Substitution:** Investigating less expensive but equally functional materials.
    *   **Process Optimization:** Streamlining manufacturing or assembly processes to reduce labor, energy, or waste.
    *   **Simplifying Design:** Reducing part count or complexity.
    *   **Sourcing:** Finding more cost-effective suppliers for components.

## Important Points to Remember:

*   **Cost is a Design Parameter:** Cost should be considered as seriously as performance, aesthetics, and usability throughout the design process.
*   **Early Cost Estimation is Crucial:** The majority of a product's cost is determined in the early stages of development.
*   **Target Costing:** A proactive approach to cost management, setting cost goals before design is finalized.
*   **DFMA and Value Engineering:** These methodologies are heavily influenced by and contribute to cost reduction efforts.
*   **Overhead Allocation:** Accurate allocation of indirect costs is vital for understanding the true cost of a product, especially with complex product lines.
*   **Continuous Improvement:** Cost management is an ongoing process, even after a product is launched.
