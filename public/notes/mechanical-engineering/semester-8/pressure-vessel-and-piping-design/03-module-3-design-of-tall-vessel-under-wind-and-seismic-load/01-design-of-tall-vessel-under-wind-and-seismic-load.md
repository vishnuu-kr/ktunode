### 1. Introduction to Tall Vessels and External Loads

Tall vessels are a common sight in many process industries, such as refineries, chemical plants, and power generation facilities. These structures are designed to store or process large volumes of fluids at various temperatures and pressures. Unlike shorter vessels, tall vessels are significantly influenced by external environmental loads, particularly wind and seismic (earthquake) forces. These loads can impose substantial stresses on the vessel's shell, support structures, and associated piping, potentially leading to failure if not adequately addressed during the design phase.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Tall Vessel:** Typically, a vertical cylindrical vessel where the height-to-diameter ratio is significant, making it susceptible to overturning and buckling due to lateral loads.
*   **External Loads:** Forces acting on the vessel from its environment, including wind, seismic, snow, ice, and thermal expansion/contraction.
*   **Wind Load:** The force exerted by moving air on the vessel's surface.
*   **Seismic Load:** The inertial forces generated within the vessel and its contents due to ground acceleration during an earthquake.
*   **Overturning Moment:** A moment that tends to tip the vessel over, generated by lateral loads.
*   **Buckling:** A phenomenon where a slender structure under compressive stress suddenly deforms sideways.

### 2. Wind Load Design for Tall Vessels

Wind can exert significant lateral forces on tall vessels. The design must account for the pressure and suction effects of wind on the vessel's surface and any attached appurtenances.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Wind Pressure ($P_w$):** The pressure exerted by wind, typically calculated using the formula:
    $P_w = 0.5 \times \rho \times V^2 \times C_f$
    where:
    *   $\rho$ is the air density.
    *   $V$ is the design wind velocity.
    *   $C_f$ is the drag coefficient, which depends on the shape of the object and the Reynolds number.
*   **Design Wind Velocity ($V$):** This is a crucial parameter determined based on the geographical location and historical meteorological data. It's usually a peak gust velocity associated with a specific return period. Standards like ASCE 7 (American Society of Civil Engineers) provide guidelines for determining design wind speeds.
*   **Wind Load Distribution:** Wind pressure is not uniform. It's higher on the windward side and creates suction on the leeward side and the top. For tall cylindrical vessels, wind can cause:
    *   **Uniform Drag Force:** Acting horizontally across the vessel's projected area.
    *   **Pressure Distribution:** Higher pressure on the windward side, lower pressure on the leeward side, and suction on the top.
    *   **Vortex Shedding:** Can cause oscillatory forces, leading to vibration.
*   **Drag Coefficient ($C_f$):** For a smooth cylinder, the drag coefficient varies with the Reynolds number. For rough surfaces or structures with appendages, it can be significantly higher.
*   **Wind Moment:** The overturning moment generated by the wind pressure acting at various heights on the vessel.

**Design Considerations:**

*   **Local Wind Pressure:** Wind pressure can be amplified at corners or edges.
*   **Appurtenances:** Ladders, platforms, nozzles, and insulation can significantly increase the wind's effective surface area and alter the drag coefficient.
*   **Wind Direction:** The design should consider the most critical wind direction.
*   **Shielding:** If multiple vessels are closely spaced, they can shield each other, reducing the effective wind load.

**Calculation Example (Simplified):**

Consider a cylindrical vessel with a diameter of 10 m and a height of 50 m.
Assume:
*   Design wind velocity, $V$ = 40 m/s
*   Air density, $\rho$ = 1.225 kg/m³ (at sea level)
*   Drag coefficient for a cylinder, $C_f$ = 0.7 (can vary)
*   Factor for gust effect and shape, $C_g C_p$ = 1.4 (from standards)

Wind pressure, $P_w = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times (40)^2 \times 1.4 = 1372$ Pa = 1.372 kPa

The total drag force would be approximately:
$F_D = P_w \times (Vessel \ Diameter \times Height) = 1.372 \times (10 \times 50) = 686$ kN

This force acts horizontally. The overturning moment at the base would depend on the effective height of application of this force.

### 3. Seismic Load Design for Tall Vessels

Seismic loads are inertial forces generated by the acceleration of the ground during an earthquake. These forces are proportional to the mass of the vessel and its contents and the seismic acceleration.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Seismic Load:** Inertial force = mass × acceleration.
*   **Base Shear:** The total horizontal force that acts at the base of the structure due to seismic activity.
*   **Seismic Zone:** Geographical areas classified based on their seismic hazard level.
*   **Ground Acceleration:** The acceleration experienced by the ground during an earthquake.
*   **Response Spectrum:** A plot that shows the maximum response (e.g., acceleration, velocity, displacement) of a single-degree-of-freedom system with a given damping ratio to earthquake ground motion.
*   **Modal Analysis:** A technique used to determine the natural frequencies and mode shapes of a structure. This is crucial for understanding how the structure will respond to dynamic loads like earthquakes.
*   **Mass Distribution:** The distribution of mass within the vessel (shell, internals, liquid content) significantly affects the seismic response.
*   **Hydrodynamic Effects (Sloshing):** For vessels containing liquids, the liquid surface can slosh, creating additional dynamic forces and moments.
*   **Foundation Design:** The foundation must be able to transmit seismic forces to the ground without failure.

**Design Considerations:**

*   **Dynamic Analysis:** For tall vessels, a static analysis might not be sufficient. Dynamic analysis, often using response spectrum methods or time-history analysis, is required.
*   **Natural Frequency:** The vessel's natural frequency should ideally be different from the dominant frequencies of the expected earthquake motion to avoid resonance.
*   **Damping:** The structure's ability to dissipate energy.
*   **Mass of Contents:** A full vessel will experience much larger seismic forces than an empty one. The design must consider the worst-case scenario.
*   **Support Stiffness:** The flexibility of the support structure influences the seismic response.
*   **Anchorage:** The connections between the vessel and its foundation must be robust enough to withstand seismic forces.
*   **Code Requirements:** Seismic design is heavily governed by building codes and standards (e.g., ASCE 7, UBC, IBC) which specify seismic design parameters and methodologies.

**Calculation Approach (Simplified Response Spectrum Method):**

1.  **Determine Seismic Parameters:** Based on the seismic zone, soil type, and occupancy category, obtain design spectral acceleration values from relevant codes.
2.  **Model the Vessel:** Create a structural model of the vessel, including its mass distribution and stiffness.
3.  **Determine Natural Frequencies and Mode Shapes:** Perform a modal analysis.
4.  **Calculate Modal Responses:** For each mode, determine the seismic force and displacement based on the response spectrum.
5.  **Combine Modal Responses:** Use methods like the Square Root of the Sum of Squares (SRSS) or the Complete Quadratic Combination (CQC) to combine the responses from different modes to get the overall design forces and displacements.
6.  **Design for Forces and Displacements:** Design the vessel shell, supports, and anchorage to withstand the combined seismic forces and movements.

### 4. Combined Load Effects and Load Combinations

In practice, vessels are subjected to multiple loads simultaneously. The design must consider the combined effect of these loads to ensure safety.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Load Combinations:** Different sets of simultaneous loads that a structure might experience. Codes specify specific load combinations and their respective load factors.
*   **Load Factors:** Multipliers applied to individual loads to account for uncertainties in load estimation and structural analysis.
*   **Stress Combination:** The resultant stresses from various loads acting on the vessel.

**Common Load Combinations for Tall Vessels:**

*   **Dead Load (D) + Wind Load (W):** Considering the vessel's weight and wind forces.
*   **Dead Load (D) + Seismic Load (E):** Considering the vessel's weight and seismic forces.
*   **Dead Load (D) + Live Load (L) + Wind Load (W):** If there's an operational live load (e.g., maintenance personnel).
*   **Dead Load (D) + Live Load (L) + Seismic Load (E):** Similar to above, but with seismic instead of wind.
*   **Operating Condition:** Dead Load + Pressure Load + Live Load (if applicable) + Temperature Effects.
*   **Extreme Condition:** Dead Load + Operating Pressure + Wind/Seismic Load + Temperature Effects.

**Design Philosophy:**

The vessel and its supports must be designed to withstand the most critical combination of loads without exceeding the allowable stress limits or causing instability.

**Example Load Combination (from ASCE 7):**

A common load combination might be:
$1.2D + 0.5L + 1.6W$ (Dead Load + 50% Live Load + 1.6 Wind Load)
or
$1.2D + 0.5L + 1.0E$ (Dead Load + 50% Live Load + 1.0 Seismic Load)

The specific factors and combinations are dictated by the governing design codes.

### 5. Buckling Analysis of Tall Vessels Under Lateral Loads

Tall vessels are susceptible to buckling due to compressive stresses induced by wind and seismic loads.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Buckling:** The sudden lateral instability of a structural member subjected to compressive axial stress.
*   **Axial Compressive Stress:** Stress that tends to shorten the material along its axis. In tall vessels, wind and seismic loads can induce compressive stresses in the shell, especially near the base due to the overturning moment.
*   **Shell Buckling:** The phenomenon of instability in thin shells under compression.
*   **Critical Buckling Load/Stress:** The minimum compressive load or stress at which buckling occurs.
*   **Eigenvalue Buckling Analysis:** A common method to determine the theoretical buckling load, assuming linear elastic behavior and perfect geometry.
*   **Non-linear Buckling Analysis:** Accounts for material non-linearity, geometric imperfections, and large deformations, providing a more realistic buckling assessment.
*   **Shell Stability:** The ability of the cylindrical shell to resist buckling under compressive stresses.

**Causes of Compressive Stress in Tall Vessels:**

*   **Wind Overturning Moment:** The wind pressure acting at height creates an overturning moment, resulting in compression on the windward side and tension on the leeward side of the shell.
*   **Seismic Overturning Moment:** Similar to wind, seismic forces create an overturning moment leading to compressive stresses.
*   **External Pressure (Vacuum):** If the vessel operates under a vacuum, the external pressure can cause compressive stress in the shell.
*   **Axial Loads:** Dead weight of the vessel and contents acting downwards, but the key concern for buckling is lateral load-induced compression.

**Buckling Modes of Cylindrical Shells:**

*   **Axisymmetric Buckling:** The shell buckles uniformly around its circumference.
*   **Asymmetric Buckling:** The shell buckles in a more complex pattern with circumferential waves.

**Design Considerations for Buckling:**

*   **Buckling Strength:** The vessel shell must have sufficient buckling strength to resist the applied compressive stresses.
*   **Design Codes:** Codes like ASME BPVC Section VIII, Division 1 & 2, and AISC (for structural aspects) provide methods and allowable stresses for buckling design.
*   **Imperfections:** Real vessels have geometric imperfections and residual stresses from manufacturing, which can significantly reduce their buckling strength. Design codes often incorporate factors to account for these.
*   **Stiffeners:** Rings or longitudinal stiffeners can be added to the shell to increase its resistance to buckling.
*   **Support Stiffness:** The design of the supports and their connection to the vessel is critical. A stiff support can help prevent localized buckling.

**Calculation Example (Conceptual - based on Harvey):**

For a cylinder under uniform axial compression, the classical buckling stress is given by:
$\sigma_{cr} = \frac{Et}{\sqrt{3(1-\nu^2)}} \times \frac{1}{R}$ (for thin shells)
where:
*   $E$ is the Young's modulus of the material.
*   $t$ is the shell thickness.
*   $\nu$ is the Poisson's ratio.
*   $R$ is the vessel radius.

For wind/seismic induced compression, the stresses are not uniform. The compressive stress ($\sigma_c$) at any point on the circumference due to the overturning moment must be checked against the allowable buckling stress, which will be a fraction of the theoretical buckling stress to account for imperfections and safety factors.

### 6. Design of Supports for Tall Vessels Under Wind and Seismic Load

The supports are critical for the stability of tall vessels, especially under lateral loads.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Support Types:**
    *   **Leg Supports:** Legs attached to the vessel shell, transferring loads to the foundation.
    *   **Skirt Supports:** A cylindrical skirt attached to the vessel, extending to the foundation. These are very common for tall vessels.
    *   **Drums/Pads:** Used for shorter vessels, but not typical for tall ones.
    *   **Elevated Structures:** Vessels supported on complex structural steel frameworks.
*   **Foundation Design:** The foundation must be designed to resist the overturning moments and shear forces transmitted from the vessel and supports.
*   **Anchor Bolts:** Connect the support structure (e.g., skirt base ring, legs) to the concrete foundation.
*   **Allowable Bearing Pressure:** The maximum pressure the foundation soil can withstand.
*   **Uplift:** The tendency of the vessel and its foundation to lift off the ground due to overturning moments.

**Design Considerations for Skirt Supports:**

*   **Skirt Thickness and Stiffness:** The skirt must be designed to resist the bending stresses induced by the overturning moment at the base of the vessel. It must also be stiff enough to prevent buckling of the skirt itself.
*   **Skirt-to-Vessel Connection:** The attachment of the skirt to the vessel shell (usually via a base ring) must be robust.
*   **Skirt-to-Foundation Connection (Base Ring):** The base ring of the skirt is anchored to the foundation. The anchor bolts and the base ring itself must be designed to handle the tension, compression, and shear forces.
*   **Shell-to-Skirt Junction:** This is a critical area where stresses are concentrated. Stress analysis is crucial here.
*   **Skirt Buckling:** The skirt itself can buckle under compressive loads or due to overall vessel instability.

**Design Considerations for Leg Supports:**

*   **Leg Stiffness and Strength:** Legs must be designed to carry the axial and bending loads transferred from the vessel.
*   **Leg Spacing and Arrangement:** The number and arrangement of legs influence the load distribution and overturning resistance.
*   **Leg-to-Vessel Connection:** The attachment points on the vessel shell need reinforcement.
*   **Leg-to-Foundation Connection:** Similar to skirts, legs are anchored to foundations.

**Foundation Design:**

*   **Soil Investigation:** Essential to determine the soil's bearing capacity and settlement characteristics.
*   **Foundation Type:** Mat foundations, spread footings, or piles may be used depending on soil conditions and loads.
*   **Moment Resistance:** The foundation must resist the overturning moment generated by wind and seismic loads. This often involves designing a large, heavy footing or using deep foundations.
*   **Uplift Resistance:** If significant uplift is predicted, the foundation design must incorporate mechanisms to resist it (e.g., weight of the footing, anchor bolt tension).

**Textbook References:**

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A tall cylindrical vessel with a diameter of 15m and a height of 60m is located in a region with a design wind speed of 50 m/s. Calculate the total drag force acting on the vessel assuming a uniform wind pressure distribution and a drag coefficient of 0.8. If the overturning moment due to this force acts at an effective height of 30m, calculate the maximum compressive stress induced in the shell at the base due to wind. (Assume air density = 1.225 kg/m³, $C_g C_p$ = 1.5)

**Answer 1:**
*   **Wind Pressure ($P_w$):**
    $P_w = 0.5 \times \rho \times V^2 \times C_f \times C_g C_p$
    $P_w = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times (50)^2 \times 0.8 \times 1.5 = 2296.875$ Pa = 2.297 kPa
*   **Total Drag Force ($F_D$):**
    The projected area of the cylinder is Diameter × Height = 15m × 60m = 900 m².
    $F_D = P_w \times Projected \ Area$
    $F_D = 2296.875 \text{ Pa} \times 900 \text{ m}^2 = 2,067,187.5$ N = 2067.19 kN
*   **Maximum Compressive Stress ($\sigma_c$):**
    The overturning moment ($M_{wind}$) = $F_D \times Effective \ Height$
    $M_{wind} = 2067.19 \text{ kN} \times 30 \text{ m} = 62,015.7$ kNm
    The maximum compressive stress at the base due to bending is given by $\sigma_c = M_{wind} / Z$, where $Z$ is the section modulus of the vessel. For a thin-walled cylinder, $Z \approx \pi R^2 t$. Let's assume a shell thickness $t = 20$ mm = 0.02 m and radius $R = 15/2 = 7.5$ m.
    $Z = \pi \times (7.5)^2 \times 0.02 = 3.534$ m³
    $\sigma_c = 62,015.7 \text{ kNm} / 3.534 \text{ m}^3 = 17,549$ kN/m² = 17.55 MPa
    *Note: This is a simplified calculation. Real design involves pressure stresses, wind pressure distribution, and complex analysis.*

**Question 2:**
Explain the concept of response spectrum analysis in seismic design for tall vessels. Why is it preferred over equivalent static analysis for such structures?

**Answer 2:**
*   **Response Spectrum Analysis:** This dynamic analysis method uses a response spectrum to represent the maximum response of a structure to a specific earthquake. It involves determining the structure's natural frequencies and mode shapes, then using the response spectrum to find the peak response (acceleration, velocity, displacement) for each mode. These modal responses are then combined (e.g., SRSS, CQC) to estimate the overall design forces and displacements.
*   **Preference over Equivalent Static Analysis:**
    *   **Dynamic Nature of Earthquakes:** Earthquakes are inherently dynamic events, causing time-varying ground motion. Response spectrum analysis captures this dynamic behavior.
    *   **Multiple Frequencies:** Earthquakes contain a range of frequencies. Tall, flexible structures like tall vessels tend to have lower natural frequencies. Response spectrum analysis accounts for how the vessel will respond to different frequency components of the earthquake.
    *   **Accuracy:** For tall and slender structures, the dynamic amplification of forces and displacements can be significant. Response spectrum analysis provides a more accurate prediction of these effects compared to equivalent static analysis, which assumes a simplified static force distribution.
    *   **Resonance Avoidance:** By understanding the natural frequencies, engineers can assess the risk of resonance and design accordingly.

**Question 3:**
Describe the key design considerations for a skirt support of a tall vessel under wind and seismic loads, including its connection to the vessel and foundation.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Skirt Thickness and Stiffness:** The skirt must be designed to withstand bending stresses induced by the overturning moment at the vessel base. Its stiffness is crucial to prevent local buckling and to contribute to the overall stability of the vessel.
*   **Skirt-to-Vessel Connection:** Typically uses a base ring. This connection must be strong enough to transfer the loads and moments from the skirt to the vessel shell. Reinforcing pads might be required on the vessel shell.
*   **Skirt-to-Foundation Connection:** The base ring of the skirt is anchored to the foundation using anchor bolts. These bolts, along with the base ring itself, must be designed to resist tensile, compressive, and shear forces generated by wind and seismic loads, as well as prevent uplift.
*   **Shell-to-Skirt Junction:** This is a highly stressed area. Detailed stress analysis is required at this junction to ensure no overstressing or fatigue.
*   **Skirt Buckling:** The skirt needs to be checked for various buckling modes, including local buckling of the skirt panels between stiffeners (if any) and overall buckling of the skirt structure under compression.
*   **Foundation:** The foundation must be designed to withstand the overturning moment and shear forces, ensuring adequate bearing capacity and resistance to uplift.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Tall vessels are susceptible to overturning and buckling** due to lateral loads like wind and seismic forces.
*   **Wind load** is calculated based on wind velocity, air density, and the vessel's geometry and surface characteristics.
*   **Seismic load** is an inertial force proportional to the vessel's mass and seismic acceleration. Dynamic analysis (response spectrum) is often necessary.
*   **Load combinations** must be considered to account for simultaneous application of various loads with appropriate load factors.
*   **Buckling analysis** is critical to prevent catastrophic failure of the shell under compressive stresses induced by lateral loads.
*   **Skirt supports** are common for tall vessels and require careful design of the skirt, base ring, anchor bolts, and foundation to resist overturning and shear.
*   **Foundation design** is integral to the stability of the entire structure, especially under seismic conditions.
*   **Codes and standards (e.g., ASME, ASCE 7)** provide essential guidelines, formulas, and safety factors for the design of tall vessels under external loads.
