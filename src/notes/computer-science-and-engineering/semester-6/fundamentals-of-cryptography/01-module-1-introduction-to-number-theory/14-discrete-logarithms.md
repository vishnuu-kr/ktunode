# Fundamentals of Cryptography: Module 1 - Introduction to Number Theory: Discrete Logarithms

## Learning Outcomes:

*   Understand the definition of a discrete logarithm.
*   Compute discrete logarithms for small instances.
*   Explain the difficulty of computing discrete logarithms in large groups.
*   Describe the properties of discrete logarithms.
*   Explain how discrete logarithms are used in cryptographic algorithms.
*   Understand the relationship between the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) and other mathematical problems.

## 1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Discrete Logarithm (Dlog):**  Let *G* be a finite cyclic group of order *n*, and let *g* be a generator of *G*.  For any element *h* in *G*, the discrete logarithm of *h* to the base *g* is an integer *x* such that:

    ```
    g^x ≡ h  (mod n)
    ```

    We write this as:

    ```
    x = log_g(h)  (mod n)
    ```

    *   *G* is a finite cyclic group. A **cyclic group** is a group that can be generated by a single element (the generator).
    *   *g* is a **generator** of *G*. This means that every element in *G* can be expressed as a power of *g*.
    *   *h* is an element of *G*.
    *   *x* is the discrete logarithm we're trying to find.  It's an integer between 0 and *n*-1.
    *   The congruence is modulo *n*, the order of the group.

*   **Order of a Group (n):** The number of elements in the group *G*.

*   **Generator:** An element *g* in *G* such that the set {*g*<sup>0</sup>, *g*<sup>1</sup>, *g*<sup>2</sup>, ..., *g*<sup>n-1</sup>} contains all elements of *G*.  In other words, by repeatedly applying the group operation to *g*, you can generate every element in the group.

*   **Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP):** Given a finite cyclic group *G*, a generator *g* of *G*, and an element *h* in *G*, find the integer *x* such that *g*<sup>x</sup> = *h*.

*   **Hardness Assumption:** The Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) is computationally hard in certain groups, meaning there is no known efficient algorithm to solve it for large enough group sizes. This is a fundamental assumption upon which many cryptographic schemes are built.

## 2. Computing Discrete Logarithms for Small Instances

The simplest method for computing discrete logarithms is by exhaustive search (also known as brute-force).  However, this only works for small groups.

**Example 1:**

Let *G* = Z<sub>11</sub><sup>*</sup> (the multiplicative group of integers modulo 11), *g* = 2, and *h* = 8. Find *x* such that 2<sup>x</sup> ≡ 8 (mod 11).

1.  Calculate powers of 2 modulo 11:

    *   2<sup>1</sup> ≡ 2 (mod 11)
    *   2<sup>2</sup> ≡ 4 (mod 11)
    *   2<sup>3</sup> ≡ 8 (mod 11)

2.  We found that 2<sup>3</sup> ≡ 8 (mod 11).

3.  Therefore, *x* = log<sub>2</sub>(8) ≡ 3 (mod 10) because the order of Z<sub>11</sub><sup>*</sup> is 10 (all the numbers from 1 to 10 except 0 modulo 11).

**Example 2:**

Let *G* = Z<sub>7</sub><sup>*</sup>, *g* = 3, and *h* = 5. Find *x* such that 3<sup>x</sup> ≡ 5 (mod 7).

1.  Calculate powers of 3 modulo 7:

    *   3<sup>1</sup> ≡ 3 (mod 7)
    *   3<sup>2</sup> ≡ 2 (mod 7)
    *   3<sup>3</sup> ≡ 6 (mod 7)
    *   3<sup>4</sup> ≡ 4 (mod 7)
    *   3<sup>5</sup> ≡ 5 (mod 7)
    *   3<sup>6</sup> ≡ 1 (mod 7) (Confirms that 3 is a generator of Z<sub>7</sub><sup>*</sup> since 3<sup>6</sup> is 1 modulo 7)

2.  We found that 3<sup>5</sup> ≡ 5 (mod 7).

3.  Therefore, *x* = log<sub>3</sub>(5) ≡ 5 (mod 6) because the order of Z<sub>7</sub><sup>*</sup> is 6.

## 3. Difficulty of Computing Discrete Logarithms in Large Groups

As the size of the group (*n*) increases, the exhaustive search method becomes computationally infeasible.  The best-known algorithms for solving the DLP in general groups have a complexity that is sub-exponential but still too large for practical computation with sufficiently large numbers.

*   **Brute-Force Search:** Time complexity is O(*n*), where *n* is the order of the group.

*   **Baby-Step Giant-Step (BSGS):**  Time and space complexity are both O(√*n*).  Significantly better than brute-force, but still exponential.

*   **Pollard's Rho Algorithm:** Time complexity is approximately O(√*n*), with negligible space requirements.

*   **Index Calculus Algorithm:**  Most efficient for groups of the form Z<sub>p</sub><sup>*</sup> where *p* is a prime number. Has sub-exponential complexity, but the constants involved make it more practical for large values.

The security of cryptographic schemes based on the DLP relies on the choice of a group where the DLP is believed to be hard. This often means using large prime numbers (e.g., 2048 bits or larger) and carefully selecting the group structure.

## 4. Properties of Discrete Logarithms

Discrete logarithms share some properties with regular logarithms, making them useful in cryptographic protocols.

*   **log<sub>g</sub>(1) ≡ 0 (mod n)**

*   **log<sub>g</sub>(g) ≡ 1 (mod n)**

*   **log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>1</sub> * h<sub>2</sub>) ≡ log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>1</sub>) + log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>2</sub>) (mod n)**  This property is crucial for many cryptographic protocols.  It allows us to perform multiplication in the group by addition in the exponent.

*   **log<sub>g</sub>(h<sup>k</sup>) ≡ k * log<sub>g</sub>(h) (mod n)**  This is a direct consequence of the previous property.

*   **Change of Base:**  If *a* and *b* are both generators of *G*, then:

    ```
    log_a(h) ≡ log_b(h) * log_a(b) (mod n)
    ```

## 5. Applications in Cryptographic Algorithms

The hardness of the DLP is the basis for several important cryptographic algorithms:

*   **Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange:** Allows two parties to establish a shared secret key over an insecure channel. The security depends on the DLP being hard.

*   **ElGamal Encryption:** An asymmetric encryption algorithm based on the DLP.

*   **Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA):** A digital signature scheme based on the DLP.  Ensures authenticity and non-repudiation.

*   **Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC):** Uses elliptic curves over finite fields.  The Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP) is believed to be even harder than the regular DLP for comparable key sizes, leading to smaller key sizes and faster computations.

**Example: Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange**

1.  Alice and Bob agree on a large prime *p* and a generator *g* of Z<sub>p</sub><sup>*</sup>.

2.  Alice chooses a secret integer *a* and computes *A* = *g*<sup>a</sup> mod *p*. She sends *A* to Bob.

3.  Bob chooses a secret integer *b* and computes *B* = *g*<sup>b</sup> mod *p*. He sends *B* to Alice.

4.  Alice computes the shared secret key *K* = *B*<sup>a</sup> mod *p*.

5.  Bob computes the shared secret key *K* = *A*<sup>b</sup> mod *p*.

Since *A*<sup>b</sup> = (*g*<sup>a</sup>)<sup>b</sup> = *g*<sup>ab</sup> = (*g*<sup>b</sup>)<sup>a</sup> = *B*<sup>a</sup>, Alice and Bob arrive at the same shared secret key *K*.

An eavesdropper Eve sees *p*, *g*, *A*, and *B*.  To compute the key *K*, Eve needs to compute either *a* = log<sub>g</sub>(A) or *b* = log<sub>g</sub>(B). This is the Discrete Logarithm Problem.

## 6. Relationship between the DLP and Other Mathematical Problems

*   **Factoring:**  While DLP and factoring are distinct problems, their computational difficulty is often linked.  For example, the Index Calculus algorithm for the DLP shares some similarities with factoring algorithms.

*   **Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP):** A variation of the DLP defined on elliptic curves.  It's widely believed that the ECDLP is harder than the DLP for comparable key sizes.

*   **Hidden Subgroup Problem (HSP):** The DLP can be reduced to a special case of the Hidden Subgroup Problem, which has implications for quantum computing.  Shor's algorithm can efficiently solve the HSP (and thus the DLP) on a quantum computer, posing a threat to classical DLP-based cryptography.

## 7. Important Points to Remember

*   The security of many cryptographic systems relies on the hardness of the Discrete Logarithm Problem.
*   The choice of the group *G* is critical for security. Some groups are more vulnerable to attacks than others.
*   Key sizes must be large enough to prevent attacks using the best-known algorithms for solving the DLP.
*   Quantum computers pose a significant threat to classical DLP-based cryptography due to Shor's algorithm.  Post-quantum cryptography is actively being developed to address this threat.
*   Understanding the properties of discrete logarithms is essential for analyzing and designing cryptographic protocols.

## 8. Practice Questions/Exercises

**Question 1:**

In Z<sub>13</sub><sup>*</sup>, find log<sub>2</sub>(5). Show your work.

**Answer 1:**

*   Order of Z<sub>13</sub><sup>*</sup> is 12.
*   Calculate powers of 2 modulo 13:
    *   2<sup>1</sup> ≡ 2 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>2</sup> ≡ 4 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>3</sup> ≡ 8 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>4</sup> ≡ 3 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>5</sup> ≡ 6 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>6</sup> ≡ 12 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>7</sup> ≡ 11 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>8</sup> ≡ 9 (mod 13)
    *   2<sup>9</sup> ≡ 5 (mod 13)

*   Therefore, log<sub>2</sub>(5) ≡ 9 (mod 12).

**Question 2:**

Prove that log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>1</sub> * h<sub>2</sub>) ≡ log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>1</sub>) + log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>2</sub>) (mod n), where *g* is a generator of group *G* of order *n*, and *h<sub>1</sub>* and *h<sub>2</sub>* belong to *G*.

**Answer 2:**

Let *x* = log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>1</sub>) and *y* = log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>2</sub>). This means *g<sup>x</sup>* ≡ *h<sub>1</sub>* (mod n) and *g<sup>y</sup>* ≡ *h<sub>2</sub>* (mod n).

Then, *h<sub>1</sub>* * h<sub>2</sub> ≡ *g<sup>x</sup>* * *g<sup>y</sup>* ≡ *g<sup>x+y</sup>* (mod n).

Therefore, log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>1</sub> * h<sub>2</sub>) ≡ x + y ≡ log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>1</sub>) + log<sub>g</sub>(h<sub>2</sub>) (mod n).

**Question 3:**

Why is the Discrete Logarithm Problem important in cryptography?

**Answer 3:**

The hardness of the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) is the foundation for several important cryptographic algorithms and protocols, including Diffie-Hellman key exchange, ElGamal encryption, and DSA digital signatures. If the DLP could be solved efficiently, these systems would be vulnerable to attack and compromise.

**Question 4:**

Explain the main idea behind the Diffie-Hellman key exchange protocol. What security assumption is it based on?

**Answer 4:**

The Diffie-Hellman key exchange allows two parties to establish a shared secret key over an insecure channel without ever directly exchanging the key itself. The parties agree on a public prime *p* and a generator *g* of Z<sub>p</sub><sup>*</sup>. Each party chooses a secret number, raises *g* to the power of that secret number modulo *p*, and exchanges the result with the other party. Finally, each party raises the received value to the power of their own secret number modulo *p*.  This results in the same shared secret key, due to the properties of modular exponentiation.

The security of Diffie-Hellman relies on the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP). An eavesdropper who knows *p*, *g*, and the exchanged values must solve the DLP to compute the secret exponents and then calculate the shared secret key.

**Question 5:**

What is the main difference between the DLP and the ECDLP?

**Answer 5:**

Both the DLP and ECDLP are problems involving finding discrete logarithms in groups. However, the DLP is typically defined over multiplicative groups of integers modulo a prime number (Z<sub>p</sub><sup>*</sup>), while the ECDLP is defined over groups formed by points on an elliptic curve over a finite field.  It is generally believed that the ECDLP is harder than the DLP for similar key sizes, which leads to more efficient implementations (smaller key sizes and faster computations) for the same level of security.
