# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - MODULE 2: COST CONCEPTS - SHORT RUN COST CURVES

## Learning Outcomes:

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Define and differentiate between various short-run cost concepts (Fixed Cost, Variable Cost, Total Cost, Average Fixed Cost, Average Variable Cost, Average Total Cost, and Marginal Cost).
*   Illustrate short-run cost curves graphically and understand their shapes.
*   Explain the relationship between marginal cost and average cost curves.
*   Analyze the impact of changes in production on short-run costs.
*   Apply cost concepts to decision-making in engineering projects.

## 1. Key Concepts and Definitions:

In the **short run**, at least one factor of production (typically capital) is fixed, while others (typically labor and materials) are variable.

*   **Fixed Costs (FC):** Costs that *do not* change with the level of output.  These costs are incurred even if no output is produced. Examples: Rent, insurance, depreciation of equipment, salaries of permanent staff.

    *   **Example:**  A factory has a monthly rent of $5,000.  This rent remains constant regardless of how many units are produced that month.

*   **Variable Costs (VC):** Costs that *do* change directly with the level of output. Examples: Raw materials, direct labor costs, electricity used in production.

    *   **Example:** The cost of raw materials to produce one unit of a product is $10.  If 100 units are produced, the total raw material cost is $1000.

*   **Total Cost (TC):** The sum of fixed costs and variable costs.
    *   **Formula:**  TC = FC + VC

    *   **Example:**  If fixed costs are $5,000 and variable costs are $1,000, the total cost is $6,000.

*   **Average Fixed Cost (AFC):** Fixed cost per unit of output.
    *   **Formula:** AFC = FC / Q (where Q is the quantity of output)

    *   **Example:** If fixed costs are $5,000 and 100 units are produced, AFC is $5,000 / 100 = $50 per unit.

*   **Average Variable Cost (AVC):** Variable cost per unit of output.
    *   **Formula:** AVC = VC / Q (where Q is the quantity of output)

    *   **Example:** If variable costs are $1,000 and 100 units are produced, AVC is $1,000 / 100 = $10 per unit.

*   **Average Total Cost (ATC):** Total cost per unit of output.  Also equal to the sum of AFC and AVC.
    *   **Formula:** ATC = TC / Q  or  ATC = AFC + AVC

    *   **Example:** If total costs are $6,000 and 100 units are produced, ATC is $6,000 / 100 = $60 per unit.  Or, using the previous AFC and AVC examples, ATC = $50 + $10 = $60 per unit.

*   **Marginal Cost (MC):** The change in total cost resulting from producing one more unit of output.
    *   **Formula:** MC = ΔTC / ΔQ  (where ΔTC is the change in total cost and ΔQ is the change in quantity)

    *   **Example:**  Producing 100 units costs $6,000. Producing 101 units costs $6,040.  The marginal cost of the 101st unit is $6,040 - $6,000 = $40.

## 2. Short-Run Cost Curves and Their Shapes:

*   **Fixed Cost Curve (FC):**  A horizontal line, as fixed costs remain constant regardless of output.

*   **Variable Cost Curve (VC):** Typically increases as output increases.  It often exhibits an "S" shape, reflecting the law of diminishing returns. Initially, VC increases at a decreasing rate due to specialization and efficiency.  However, as more variable inputs are added to the fixed input, diminishing returns set in, and VC increases at an increasing rate.

*   **Total Cost Curve (TC):** Has the same shape as the VC curve but is shifted upwards by the amount of the fixed cost.  TC starts at the level of fixed costs when output is zero.

*   **Average Fixed Cost Curve (AFC):**  A downward sloping curve.  As output increases, the fixed cost is spread over more units, resulting in a lower AFC. It approaches zero as output increases indefinitely. This is known as "spreading the overhead."

*   **Average Variable Cost Curve (AVC):**  A U-shaped curve.  Initially, as output increases, AVC decreases due to increasing efficiency.  However, as diminishing returns set in, AVC eventually begins to increase.

*   **Average Total Cost Curve (ATC):**  Also a U-shaped curve.  ATC is the vertical sum of AFC and AVC.  The minimum point of the ATC curve represents the most efficient level of production.  ATC is generally higher than AVC, with the difference being AFC. As output increases, the gap between ATC and AVC narrows because AFC decreases.

*   **Marginal Cost Curve (MC):**  Also a U-shaped curve.  MC initially decreases due to specialization and efficiency, but eventually increases due to diminishing returns.

    *   **Important Point:** The MC curve intersects both the AVC and ATC curves at their minimum points.

## 3. Relationship Between Marginal Cost and Average Cost Curves:

*   **Key Relationship:**  If MC < AVC or ATC, then AVC or ATC is decreasing. If MC > AVC or ATC, then AVC or ATC is increasing.  Therefore, MC *must* intersect AVC and ATC at their minimum points.

    *   **Analogy:** Think of your GPA. If your grade in the current semester (marginal grade) is lower than your overall GPA (average grade), your GPA will fall. If your grade in the current semester is higher than your overall GPA, your GPA will rise.

*   **Why MC intersects at the minimum:** When MC is below AVC (or ATC), it pulls the average down. When MC is above AVC (or ATC), it pulls the average up. The only point where MC doesn't either pull the average up or down is where it's equal to the average - at the minimum.

## 4. Impact of Changes in Production on Short-Run Costs:

*   **Increasing Production:** Initially, increasing production can lead to lower average costs (especially AFC and potentially AVC) due to economies of scale within the constraints of the fixed factor. However, beyond a certain point, increasing production leads to diminishing returns and higher average and marginal costs.

*   **Decreasing Production:** Decreasing production reduces variable costs, but fixed costs remain.  AFC will increase, and AVC and ATC may initially decrease but then likely increase as economies of scale are lost.

*   **Important Note:** The optimal level of production in the short run is often considered to be at or near the minimum point of the ATC curve.  This minimizes the cost per unit.  However, *profit maximization* depends on both costs and revenue.

## 5. Application to Decision-Making in Engineering Projects:

*   **Cost Estimation:**  Engineers use cost concepts to estimate the cost of projects, including materials, labor, and overhead (fixed costs). Understanding the short-run cost structure helps in predicting how costs will change as the project progresses and the scale of production changes.

*   **Capacity Planning:**  Short-run cost curves are essential for determining the optimal capacity utilization of existing facilities. Engineers analyze these curves to identify the point where average costs are minimized and to avoid over-utilization that leads to diminishing returns.

*   **Make-or-Buy Decisions:**  Engineers use cost analysis to determine whether it is more cost-effective to manufacture a component internally (make) or purchase it from an external supplier (buy).  This involves comparing the variable costs of internal production with the purchase price.

*   **Process Selection:**  Different production processes have different cost structures.  Engineers analyze the short-run cost curves of alternative processes to select the process that minimizes costs for a given level of output.

*   **Shutdown Decisions:** If a firm's revenue is consistently below its average variable cost, it may be more profitable to temporarily shut down production in the short run. Fixed costs must be paid regardless, but suspending production can avoid further variable cost losses.

## 6. Practice Questions/Exercises with Answers:

**Question 1:** A company produces widgets. Its fixed costs are $10,000 per month. The variable cost of producing one widget is $5.

*   a) Calculate the total cost of producing 1000 widgets.
*   b) Calculate the average fixed cost of producing 1000 widgets.
*   c) Calculate the average variable cost of producing 1000 widgets.
*   d) Calculate the average total cost of producing 1000 widgets.
*   e) If the company produces 1001 widgets, and the total cost increases to $15,008, what is the marginal cost of the 1001st widget?

**Answer 1:**

*   a) TC = FC + VC = $10,000 + ($5 * 1000) = $15,000
*   b) AFC = FC / Q = $10,000 / 1000 = $10
*   c) AVC = VC / Q = ($5 * 1000) / 1000 = $5
*   d) ATC = TC / Q = $15,000 / 1000 = $15  (or ATC = AFC + AVC = $10 + $5 = $15)
*   e) MC = ΔTC / ΔQ = ($15,008 - $15,000) / (1001 - 1000) = $8

**Question 2:** Explain why the marginal cost curve intersects the average total cost curve at the average total cost curve's minimum point.

**Answer 2:** If marginal cost is below average total cost, it pulls the average total cost down. If marginal cost is above average total cost, it pulls the average total cost up. The only point where the marginal cost doesn't either pull the average total cost up or down is where it's equal to the average total cost - at its minimum.

**Question 3:** A manufacturing plant has a fixed cost of $50,000 per year for rent and insurance. The variable costs are $20 per unit for materials and labor.  The plant produces 10,000 units per year.  What is the average total cost (ATC)?

**Answer 3:**

*   Fixed Costs (FC) = $50,000
*   Variable Costs (VC) = $20/unit * 10,000 units = $200,000
*   Total Costs (TC) = FC + VC = $50,000 + $200,000 = $250,000
*   Average Total Cost (ATC) = TC / Q = $250,000 / 10,000 units = $25/unit

**Question 4:** Describe the shape of the Average Fixed Cost (AFC) curve and explain why it has that shape.

**Answer 4:** The AFC curve is downward sloping.  It slopes downward because fixed costs are constant regardless of the level of output.  Therefore, as output increases, the fixed cost is spread over more units, resulting in a lower AFC.  It approaches zero as output increases indefinitely.

## 7. Important Points to Remember:

*   Short-run analysis focuses on a time period where at least one input is fixed.
*   Understanding the relationship between marginal cost and average costs is crucial.
*   The U-shape of the AVC, ATC, and MC curves is primarily due to the law of diminishing returns.
*   Cost concepts are fundamental to making informed decisions in engineering projects, including cost estimation, capacity planning, and process selection.
*   The optimal production level isn't solely based on minimizing ATC but also considers revenue and profit maximization.
