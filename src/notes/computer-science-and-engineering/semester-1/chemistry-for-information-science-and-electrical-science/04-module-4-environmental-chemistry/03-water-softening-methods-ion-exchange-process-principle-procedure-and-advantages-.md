### 1. Water Softening Methods

Let's start with a common problem: hard water. What is hard water? Simply put, it's water that contains a high concentration of dissolved mineral ions, primarily **calcium (Ca²⁺)** and **magnesium (Mg²⁺)** ions. Now, why is this a problem?

*   **For your appliances:** Hard water forms scale, a deposit of calcium carbonate and magnesium hydroxide, inside pipes, kettles, water heaters, and washing machines. This scale reduces efficiency, clogs pipes, and can eventually damage equipment. Think of your kettle getting that white crusty layer – that's scale!
*   **For your laundry:** It makes soap less effective. Soap molecules react with calcium and magnesium ions to form insoluble precipitates (soap scum), meaning you need more soap to get things clean, and it leaves a residue.
*   **For you:** While not generally harmful to drink, it can cause problems in industrial processes where pure water is needed, and some people find it less pleasant for bathing.

So, how do we get rid of these "hard" ions? We soften the water. There are several methods, but we'll focus on two key ones today: **Ion Exchange** and **Reverse Osmosis**.

#### 1.1 Ion Exchange Process

This is one of the most common and effective methods for water softening, especially for domestic and industrial use.

**Principle:**

The core principle behind ion exchange is a **reversible chemical reaction** where unwanted ions in the water are exchanged for less objectionable ions held on a solid material called an **ion exchange resin**.

Imagine you have tiny beads, typically made of a polymer, that are like little sponges. These beads are initially "loaded" with sodium ions (Na⁺). When hard water flows through a bed of these beads, the calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺) ions, which have a stronger attraction to the resin, "stick" to the beads and "kick out" the sodium ions into the water.

The key here is the **selectivity of the resin**. The resin is designed to have a greater affinity for the divalent cations (like Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺) than for the monovalent cations (like Na⁺). It’s like having a magnet that’s much better at picking up bigger metal objects than smaller ones.

The chemical reaction looks something like this:

$ \text{Resin-Na⁺ + Ca²⁺} \rightarrow \text{Resin-Ca²⁺ + 2Na⁺} $

And for magnesium:

$ \text{Resin-Na⁺ + Mg²⁺} \rightarrow \text{Resin-Mg²⁺ + 2Na⁺} $

Notice that for every Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ion removed from the water, *two* Na⁺ ions are released. This is important to remember!

**Procedure:**

1.  **Softening Cycle:** Hard water is passed through a column packed with the ion exchange resin (often in the form of small beads). As the water flows, the Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions are adsorbed onto the resin, displacing Na⁺ ions. The softened water, now containing Na⁺ ions instead of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, exits the column.
2.  **Exhaustion:** Eventually, the resin beads become saturated with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions and can no longer effectively remove them. The resin is then "exhausted."
3.  **Regeneration Cycle:** To restore the resin's capacity, it needs to be regenerated. This is done by flushing the resin bed with a concentrated solution of sodium chloride (brine). The high concentration of Na⁺ ions in the brine forces the Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions off the resin, replacing them with Na⁺ ions again. The displaced Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions, along with the excess brine, are then washed away.

$ \text{Resin-Ca²⁺ + 2Na⁺ (from brine)} \rightarrow \text{Resin-2Na⁺ + Ca²⁺} $

The regenerated resin is now ready for another softening cycle.

**Advantages:**

*   **High Quality Soft Water:** It can produce very soft water with almost complete removal of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions. This is crucial for many industrial processes, like boiler feed water, where even small amounts of hardness can cause severe problems.
*   **Effective Removal:** It's highly effective in removing both calcium and magnesium ions.
*   **Relatively Simple Operation:** Once set up, the process is largely automated.
*   **Can be combined with other ion exchange resins:** You can also use resins that remove other undesirable ions like iron or manganese, or even nitrate and sulfate, by exchanging them for chloride or hydroxide ions.

**Considerations:**

*   **Sodium Addition:** The process adds sodium to the water. For individuals on very strict low-sodium diets, this might be a consideration.
*   **Brine Disposal:** The regeneration process produces a brine waste that needs proper disposal.
*   **Cost:** The initial setup and the ongoing cost of salt for regeneration can be a factor.

This method, as described in textbooks like Tembe, Kamaluddin, and Krishnan's "Engineering Chemistry," is a cornerstone of water treatment for its reliability and effectiveness.

#### 1.2 Reverse Osmosis (RO)

Reverse osmosis is another powerful water purification technique, and it's a bit different from ion exchange because it tackles a much broader range of impurities.

**Principle:**

Osmosis is a natural phenomenon where a solvent (like water) moves from an area of **lower solute concentration** to an area of **higher solute concentration** across a **semipermeable membrane**. This movement occurs to equalize the concentration on both sides of the membrane.

Think of a raisin in water. The raisin is concentrated with sugars. Water moves into the raisin, making it swell. That's osmosis.

Now, reverse osmosis does the opposite. We apply **external pressure** to the side with the **higher solute concentration** (the impure water). This pressure overcomes the natural osmotic pressure, forcing the solvent (water) to move from the **higher concentration side to the lower concentration side**, leaving the solutes (salts, minerals, bacteria, viruses, etc.) behind.

The **semipermeable membrane** is key here. It's a very fine filter that allows water molecules to pass through but is too small for most dissolved salts, ions, and larger molecules to get through.

So, to break it down:
*   **Natural Osmosis:** Low concentration → High concentration (driven by concentration difference)
*   **Reverse Osmosis:** High concentration → Low concentration (driven by applied pressure, overcoming natural osmotic pressure)

The pressure required is typically quite high, often hundreds of psi, to overcome the natural tendency of water to flow the other way.

**Process:**

1.  **Pre-treatment:** Raw water is often pre-treated to remove larger particles and chlorine, which can damage the RO membrane. This might involve sediment filters and activated carbon filters.
2.  **Pressurization:** The pre-treated water is pumped at high pressure into an RO module containing the semipermeable membrane.
3.  **Separation:** As water is forced through the membrane:
    *   **Permeate:** Pure water passes through the membrane and is collected. This is your purified water.
    *   **Concentrate/Reject:** The dissolved salts, minerals, and other impurities that cannot pass through the membrane are left behind and form a concentrated waste stream, which is flushed away.
4.  **Post-treatment:** The purified water might be re-mineralized or disinfected further, depending on its intended use.

**Advantages:**

*   **Broad Spectrum Purification:** RO is highly effective at removing a wide range of contaminants, including dissolved salts, heavy metals, bacteria, viruses, and even some organic molecules. This makes it excellent for desalination (removing salt from seawater) and producing ultrapure water for industries like semiconductor manufacturing and pharmaceuticals.
*   **No Chemicals Required:** Unlike some other methods, RO is a physical separation process and doesn't typically require the addition of chemicals (except for pre-treatment).
*   **High Purity:** It can achieve very high levels of water purity, far beyond what ion exchange alone can typically offer.

**Considerations:**

*   **High Energy Consumption:** The high pressures required mean RO systems can be energy-intensive.
*   **Wastewater Generation:** A significant portion of the incoming water is rejected as a concentrated brine stream. For desalination, this brine needs careful management to avoid environmental impact.
*   **Membrane Fouling:** The membranes can become fouled or clogged by suspended solids or biological growth, requiring cleaning or replacement.
*   **Removal of Beneficial Minerals:** RO removes virtually all dissolved solids, including beneficial minerals like calcium and magnesium. For drinking water, this can make the water taste flat and potentially reduce its nutritional value, necessitating post-treatment remineralization.

For information science and electrical science applications, where ultra-pure water is often a requirement for manufacturing processes (like rinsing silicon wafers), RO is indispensable. Atkins' "Physical Chemistry" often delves into the thermodynamic principles underlying osmosis and membrane transport, which is the theoretical basis for RO.

### 2. Water Disinfection Methods

Now that we've softened our water, or at least understood how to get rid of hardness, we need to tackle the biological contaminants. Water can harbor a host of harmful microorganisms – bacteria, viruses, protozoa – that can cause serious diseases. Disinfection is the process of killing or inactivating these pathogens.

We'll look at three common methods: **Chlorination**, **Ozone**, and **UV Irradiation**.

#### 2.1 Chlorination

Chlorination has been the workhorse of water disinfection for over a century, and it's still widely used due to its effectiveness and cost-efficiency.

**Principle:**

Chlorine, in its various forms (like chlorine gas Cl₂, hypochlorite ions OCl⁻, or hypochlorous acid HOCl), acts as a strong oxidizing agent. It disrupts essential cellular functions of microorganisms by:

*   **Oxidizing enzymes:** Chlorine reacts with and inactivates vital enzymes within the microbial cell, halting metabolic processes.
*   **Damaging cell walls and membranes:** It can disrupt the structural integrity of the cell.
*   **Interfering with DNA and RNA:** It can damage the genetic material, preventing reproduction.

The most potent disinfecting species in water is **hypochlorous acid (HOCl)**. The balance between HOCl and OCl⁻ depends on the pH of the water. At lower pH (acidic), HOCl is favored, making disinfection more efficient. At higher pH (alkaline), OCl⁻ predominates, which is a weaker disinfectant. This is why controlling pH is important during chlorination.

**Break-Point Chlorination:**

This is a crucial concept in achieving effective and safe chlorination. Imagine adding chlorine to water that contains reducing agents and organic matter (which are common contaminants). Initially, the chlorine added will react with these substances, and you won't see much free chlorine residual in the water.

*   **Phase 1: Chlorine Demand:** The chlorine added is consumed by reacting with impurities like ammonia, iron, manganese, and organic matter. The residual chlorine level in the water remains low or zero.
*   **Phase 2: Formation of Chloramines:** Once the easily oxidizable impurities are consumed, the chlorine starts to react with residual ammonia in the water to form **chloramines** (e.g., monochloramine NH₂Cl, dichloramine NHCl₂, trichloramine NCl₃). Chloramines are also disinfectants, but they are weaker and slower-acting than HOCl.
*   **Phase 3: Break-Point:** As more chlorine is added, it eventually oxidizes the chloramines, breaking them down into nitrogen gas (N₂) and water, and releasing free available chlorine (primarily HOCl). At this point, any further addition of chlorine results in a proportional increase in free chlorine residual. This point is called the **break-point**.

**Significance of Break-Point Chlorination:**

*   **Complete Oxidation:** Reaching the break-point ensures that most of the reducing agents and ammonia have been oxidized, meaning the chlorine added is now primarily available for disinfection.
*   **Effective Disinfection:** It ensures a sufficient residual of free chlorine (HOCl) to kill bacteria and viruses rapidly and provide a residual disinfectant effect that protects the water during distribution.
*   **Removal of Odor and Taste:** The breakdown of chloramines and ammonia at the break-point also helps to remove unpleasant odors and tastes associated with them.

**Procedure:**

Chlorine (usually as chlorine gas or sodium hypochlorite solution) is added to the water in a controlled manner, and the free chlorine residual is monitored. Sufficient chlorine is added to reach and slightly exceed the break-point.

**Advantages:**

*   **Effective Disinfectant:** Kills a wide range of bacteria and viruses.
*   **Residual Effect:** Provides a disinfectant residual that protects water quality in the distribution system. This means that if the water is re-contaminated after treatment, the residual chlorine can still kill the pathogens.
*   **Cost-Effective:** Relatively inexpensive to implement and maintain.
*   **Well-Established Technology:** Its use and monitoring are well-understood.

**Disadvantages:**

*   **Formation of Disinfection Byproducts (DBPs):** Chlorine can react with natural organic matter in the water to form potentially harmful DBPs, such as trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids (HAAs). This is a major concern and has led to regulations on chlorine use and residual levels.
*   **Taste and Odor:** Can impart a taste and odor to the water, especially at higher concentrations or if DBPs are formed.
*   **Ineffective Against Some Pathogens:** Less effective against certain protozoa like *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia* which are resistant to chlorine.
*   **Corrosivity:** Can be corrosive to certain materials.

#### 2.2 Ozone (O₃)

Ozone is a much more powerful oxidizing agent than chlorine and is gaining popularity as an alternative or supplement to chlorination.

**Principle:**

Ozone (O₃) is a molecule consisting of three oxygen atoms. It's unstable and readily decomposes to form diatomic oxygen (O₂), releasing a highly reactive oxygen atom in the process. This nascent oxygen atom is a very strong oxidizing agent.

O₃ → O₂ + [O] (nascent oxygen)

The nascent oxygen atom rapidly attacks and oxidizes cellular components of microorganisms, including cell membranes, enzymes, and nucleic acids, leading to their rapid inactivation or death. Ozone can also react directly with microbial DNA and RNA.

**Process:**

Ozone is generated on-site, typically by passing dry air or oxygen through a high-voltage electrical discharge (an "ozonator"). The ozone gas is then bubbled through the water in a contactor, allowing it to dissolve and react with the pathogens.

**Advantages:**

*   **Very Powerful Disinfectant:** Ozone is significantly more potent than chlorine and inactivates a wider range of pathogens, including chlorine-resistant protozoa like *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia*.
*   **Rapid Action:** It kills microorganisms very quickly.
*   **No Harmful DBPs (Generally):** Unlike chlorine, ozone generally does not form the same problematic chlorinated DBPs. It can, however, form other oxidation byproducts, depending on the water composition.
*   **Improves Taste and Odor:** It can oxidize compounds that cause unpleasant tastes and odors, improving water palatability.
*   **Oxidizes Other Contaminants:** Can also help remove iron, manganese, and organic matter from the water.

**Disadvantages:**

*   **No Residual Effect:** Ozone is unstable and decomposes quickly. This means it doesn't provide a lasting disinfectant residual in the water distribution system. Therefore, a secondary disinfectant (often chlorine) is usually added after ozonation.
*   **High Capital Cost:** Ozonation equipment is generally more expensive than chlorination systems.
*   **On-site Generation Required:** Ozone cannot be stored and must be generated on-site, requiring electricity and specialized equipment.
*   **Can be Corrosive:** Ozone is a strong oxidant and can be corrosive to materials.

#### 2.3 UV Irradiation

UV irradiation offers a chemical-free disinfection method that is also very effective.

**Principle:**

Ultraviolet (UV) light, specifically in the UV-C range (wavelengths between 200-280 nm), is used to disinfect water. UV light damages the genetic material (DNA and RNA) of microorganisms.

When UV photons of the correct wavelength are absorbed by the DNA or RNA within a bacterium, virus, or protozoan, they cause the formation of **pyrimidine dimers** (specifically, thymine dimers in DNA and uracil dimers in RNA). These dimers distort the structure of the nucleic acid, preventing it from replicating properly. If the cell cannot replicate its DNA, it cannot reproduce and is effectively inactivated or killed.

**Process:**

Water is passed through a chamber where it is exposed to UV lamps. The lamps emit UV-C radiation, and the intensity and contact time are controlled to ensure sufficient UV dose is delivered to the water. Typically, UV systems use low-pressure mercury vapor lamps that emit UV light primarily at 254 nm, which is highly effective against microorganisms.

**Advantages:**

*   **Chemical-Free:** No chemicals are added to the water, avoiding the formation of DBPs and taste/odor issues associated with chemical disinfectants.
*   **Effective Against a Broad Range:** Kills bacteria, viruses, and protozoa.
*   **No Residual Effect:** Similar to ozone, UV does not provide a disinfectant residual. A secondary disinfectant is often needed.
*   **No Change in Water Chemistry:** Does not alter the taste, odor, or chemical composition of the water.

**Disadvantages:**

*   **No Residual Effect:** As mentioned, this is a significant limitation for maintaining water safety in the distribution system.
*   **Requires Clear Water:** The effectiveness of UV light is significantly reduced by turbidity (cloudiness) or color in the water, as suspended particles can shield microorganisms from the UV light. Therefore, pre-treatment to remove turbidity is essential.
*   **Lamp Maintenance:** UV lamps have a limited lifespan and need regular cleaning and replacement.
*   **Higher Capital and Operational Costs (compared to chlorination):** While chemical-free, the initial cost of UV systems and the electricity consumption can be higher than chlorination.
*   **Susceptibility to Power Fluctuations:** Consistent power supply is crucial for effective operation.

### 3. Dissolved Oxygen (DO), BOD, and COD

Now that we've discussed how to treat water, let's talk about how we *measure* the quality of water, especially with respect to organic pollution. The health of an aquatic ecosystem and the suitability of water for various uses are often judged by the amount of dissolved oxygen and the levels of organic matter.

#### 3.1 Dissolved Oxygen (DO)

**Definition:**

**Dissolved Oxygen (DO)** is the amount of gaseous oxygen (O₂) dissolved in a body of water. It is a critical parameter for the survival of aquatic life.

**Significance:**

*   **Aquatic Life Support:** Fish, invertebrates, and most aquatic organisms require dissolved oxygen for respiration. A healthy aquatic ecosystem typically has DO levels between 5-10 mg/L or higher.
*   **Indicator of Water Quality:** Low DO levels are a strong indicator of pollution, particularly organic pollution. When organic matter enters a water body, bacteria and other microorganisms decompose it. This decomposition process consumes dissolved oxygen.
*   **Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Conditions:**
    *   **High DO:** Indicates that the water is well-aerated and can support aerobic life.
    *   **Low DO:** Suggests that the oxygen is being consumed, possibly by decomposition. If DO drops very low (near 0 mg/L), the conditions become **anaerobic**, meaning oxygen is absent. Anaerobic conditions are harmful to most aquatic life and can lead to the production of foul-smelling gases like hydrogen sulfide (H₂S).

**Factors Affecting DO:**

*   **Temperature:** Colder water holds more dissolved oxygen than warmer water. This is why DO levels often decrease in summer.
*   **Atmospheric Pressure:** Higher atmospheric pressure allows more oxygen to dissolve in water.
*   **Photosynthesis:** Aquatic plants and algae produce oxygen during photosynthesis, increasing DO levels.
*   **Respiration and Decomposition:** Aquatic organisms and decomposing organic matter consume oxygen, decreasing DO levels.
*   **Turbulence and Aeration:** Waterfalls, rapids, and wind mixing increase the surface area for oxygen exchange, leading to higher DO.

#### 3.2 Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)

**Definition:**

**Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)** is the amount of dissolved oxygen required by aerobic biological organisms to break down organic material present in a given water sample at a certain temperature over a specific time period. It is usually measured over **5 days** at **20°C**, hence commonly referred to as **BOD₅**.

**Significance:**

*   **Measure of Organic Pollution:** BOD is a direct measure of the amount of biodegradable organic matter in the water. Higher BOD values indicate a greater amount of organic pollution.
*   **Impact on DO:** As bacteria decompose organic matter, they consume dissolved oxygen. A high BOD means a high rate of oxygen consumption, leading to depleted DO levels. This can be devastating for aquatic life.
*   **Wastewater Treatment:** BOD is a key parameter in assessing the effectiveness of wastewater treatment plants. The goal is to reduce the BOD of wastewater before discharging it into rivers or lakes.

**How it's Measured (Simplified Concept):**

A water sample is collected and diluted with a known amount of clean, oxygen-rich water. The initial DO of the diluted sample is measured. The sample is then incubated in a sealed container at 20°C for 5 days. During incubation, microorganisms in the sample consume oxygen to decompose the organic matter. After 5 days, the final DO is measured.

$ \text{BOD₅} = \frac{(\text{Initial DO} - \text{Final DO}) \times \text{Dilution Factor}}{\text{Volume of sample in mL}} $

**Example:** If a sample is diluted 1:10, and the DO drops from 8 mg/L to 3 mg/L over 5 days, the BOD₅ would be (8 - 3) * 10 = 50 mg/L.

**Interpretation:**
*   BOD < 1 mg/L: Very clean water.
*   1-5 mg/L: Moderately clean water.
*   5-10 mg/L: Potentially polluted water.
*   > 10 mg/L: Heavily polluted water, likely with significant organic loading.

#### 3.3 Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)

**Definition:**

**Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)** is the amount of oxygen required to chemically oxidize the organic and inorganic matter present in a water sample. Unlike BOD, which relies on biological action, COD uses a strong chemical oxidizing agent (typically potassium dichromate, K₂Cr₂O₇) under acidic and heated conditions to oxidize all oxidizable material, both biodegradable and non-biodegradable.

**Significance:**

*   **Total Oxidizable Matter:** COD represents the total amount of oxygen required to oxidize all organic compounds in the water, regardless of whether they are biodegradable or not. This includes complex organic molecules that might not be easily decomposed by bacteria.
*   **Indicator of Pollution Strength:** Similar to BOD, a high COD indicates a high concentration of oxidizable pollutants.
*   **Faster Measurement:** COD tests are much faster than BOD tests, typically completed in a few hours, making them useful for rapid assessment or routine monitoring.
*   **Relationship with BOD:** In polluted waters, COD is generally higher than BOD because it includes both biodegradable and non-biodegradable organic matter. The ratio of BOD/COD can give an indication of the biodegradability of the organic matter present.
    *   A high BOD/COD ratio (e.g., > 0.6) suggests that most of the organic matter is biodegradable.
    *   A low BOD/COD ratio (e.g., < 0.2) suggests that a significant portion of the organic matter is non-biodegradable.

**How it's Measured (Simplified Concept):**

A water sample is mixed with a known excess of potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) in the presence of a strong acid (sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄) and a catalyst (silver sulfate, Ag₂SO₄). The mixture is heated under reflux for a specific time (usually 2 hours) to allow the oxidation to occur. The amount of dichromate that remains unreacted is then determined by titration with ferrous ammonium sulfate. The difference between the initial amount of dichromate added and the amount remaining is the COD.

$ \text{COD} = \frac{(\text{Volume of titrant} \times \text{Molarity of titrant} \times \text{Equivalent weight of O₂})}{\text{Volume of sample in mL}} $

**Interpretation:**
COD values are generally higher than BOD values for the same sample. For example, a river might have a BOD of 20 mg/L and a COD of 50 mg/L. This means that while 20 mg/L of oxygen are needed by microorganisms, a total of 50 mg/L of oxygen would be needed to chemically oxidize all the organic matter.

**Relevance to Information Science and Electrical Science:**

Understanding water quality parameters like DO, BOD, and COD is important even in fields that might seem distant from environmental science.
*   **Industrial Cooling Water:** Many electrical and computing systems generate significant heat and require cooling water. The quality of this cooling water can affect efficiency and lifespan. High organic loads (high BOD/COD) can lead to biofouling in cooling towers and heat exchangers, reducing their effectiveness.
*   **Water Used in Manufacturing:** In the production of semiconductors and other electronic components, water purity is paramount. While we focus on removing ions and particles, understanding how organic matter in source water could interfere with processes or affect waste streams is also relevant.
*   **Environmental Impact Assessment:** Any industrial facility, including those in IT or electronics manufacturing, needs to assess its environmental footprint. This includes managing its water intake and wastewater discharge, where parameters like BOD and COD are crucial for compliance and ecological responsibility.

**Summary of Key Takeaways:**

*   **Water Softening:** Essential to prevent scale formation and improve soap efficiency.
    *   **Ion Exchange:** Swaps Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ for Na⁺ using resin, regenerated with brine. Produces high-quality soft water.
    *   **Reverse Osmosis:** Uses pressure and a semipermeable membrane to separate a wide range of contaminants, producing very pure water but requiring high pressure and generating waste.
*   **Water Disinfection:** Kills harmful microorganisms.
    *   **Chlorination:** Effective, residual effect, cost-efficient, but can form DBPs. Break-point chlorination ensures sufficient residual.
    *   **Ozonation:** Powerful, rapid, broad-spectrum, no problematic DBPs, but no residual effect and higher cost.
    *   **UV Irradiation:** Chemical-free, effective, but no residual effect and requires clear water.
*   **Water Quality Parameters:**
    *   **DO:** Crucial for aquatic life; low DO indicates organic pollution.
    *   **BOD:** Measures biodegradable organic matter; high BOD depletes DO.
    *   **COD:** Measures total oxidizable matter (biodegradable + non-biodegradable); faster than BOD.

### Sample Questions and Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):** Explain the difference between osmosis and reverse osmosis, and state which process is used for desalination.

**Answer:**
Osmosis is the natural movement of a solvent (like water) from a region of lower solute concentration to a region of higher solute concentration across a semipermeable membrane, driven by the concentration gradient. Reverse osmosis is the process where external pressure is applied to the more concentrated solution, forcing the solvent to move from the high concentration side to the low concentration side, against the natural osmotic flow. Reverse osmosis is used for desalination because it effectively separates salt (solutes) from water.

**Question 2 (Application/Exam-Oriented):** A water treatment plant uses chlorination. They measure the free chlorine residual after adding chlorine gas. Initially, the residual is zero despite adding chlorine. As more chlorine is added, the residual remains low. Then, with further chlorine addition, the residual starts to increase significantly and linearly. What is this point called, and why is it important?

**Answer:**
This point where the free chlorine residual starts to increase significantly and linearly after initial additions have been consumed by impurities is called the **break-point**. It is important because reaching the break-point signifies that most of the reducing agents and ammonia in the water have been oxidized. The free chlorine residual present after the break-point is primarily available for effective and rapid disinfection of remaining pathogens and provides a residual to protect the water during distribution.

**Question 3 (Comparison/Understanding):** Compare and contrast Ozone and UV irradiation as water disinfection methods, focusing on their advantages and disadvantages.

**Answer:**

**Ozone (O₃):**
*   **Advantages:** Very powerful disinfectant, rapid action, effective against a broad range of pathogens (including protozoa), generally does not form harmful DBPs, can improve taste and odor.
*   **Disadvantages:** No residual effect (requires secondary disinfectant), high capital cost, on-site generation required, can be corrosive.

**UV Irradiation:**
*   **Advantages:** Chemical-free disinfection, effective against a broad range of pathogens, no change in water chemistry.
*   **Disadvantages:** No residual effect (requires secondary disinfectant), effectiveness reduced by turbidity (requires clear water), lamp maintenance required, susceptible to power fluctuations.

**Question 4 (Definition & Significance):** Define BOD and explain its significance in assessing water pollution.

**Answer:**
**BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand)** is the amount of dissolved oxygen required by aerobic biological organisms to break down organic material present in a given water sample at a certain temperature over a specific time period (typically 5 days at 20°C). Its significance lies in it being a direct measure of biodegradable organic pollution in water. A high BOD indicates a large amount of organic matter, which will consume dissolved oxygen in the water body during decomposition. This can lead to critically low DO levels, harming or killing aquatic life, and creating anaerobic conditions. Therefore, BOD is a key indicator of the organic load and the potential impact on aquatic ecosystems and is used to monitor the efficiency of wastewater treatment.
