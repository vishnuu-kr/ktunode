### Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR): Bringing Life Back

Let's start with CPR. This is arguably the most critical first aid skill we’ll cover. Imagine someone collapses, and their heart has stopped beating. Without immediate intervention, their brain and vital organs are deprived of oxygen. CPR is that immediate intervention. It’s about **buying time** until professional medical help arrives.

**What is CPR?**
Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation is a life-saving technique used when the heart and lungs have stopped working. It involves chest compressions to manually pump blood through the body and rescue breaths to provide oxygen.

**The ABCs and CPR**
You'll recall our primary survey: Airway, Breathing, Circulation. CPR directly addresses the **Circulation** (by pumping blood) and **Breathing** (with rescue breaths). If you’ve checked the scene for safety and the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, you’ve already gone through your primary survey and determined the need for CPR.

**The "How-To" of CPR (Focus on Adults for this session):**
*   **Call for Help First!** This is non-negotiable. Before you even touch the person, shout for someone nearby to call emergency services (like 911 or your local equivalent) and get an Automated External Defibrillator (AED) if one is available. If you’re alone, you perform CPR for about two minutes *before* calling yourself if you don’t have a phone readily available.
*   **Chest Compressions:**
    *   **Positioning:** Make sure the person is lying on a firm, flat surface. Kneel beside them.
    *   **Hand Placement:** Place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone (sternum). Place your other hand on top of the first hand and interlock your fingers. Ensure your fingers are off the chest.
    *   **Body Mechanics:** Position yourself so your shoulders are directly over your hands. Keep your arms straight, and use your body weight to push down.
    *   **Depth and Rate:** For adults, compress the chest about 2 inches (5 cm) deep. The rate should be between 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Think of the beat of the song "Stayin' Alive" by the Bee Gees – it’s a good rhythm!
    *   **Recoil:** Allow the chest to fully recoil between compressions. This lets the heart refill with blood.
*   **Airway and Breathing:**
    *   **Open the Airway:** After 30 compressions, tilt the head back slightly and lift the chin. This lifts the tongue away from the back of the throat, opening the airway.
    *   **Give Rescue Breaths:** Pinch the person's nostrils shut. Take a normal breath, seal your mouth over theirs, and give two breaths, each lasting about one second. Watch for the chest to rise with each breath.
    *   **Continue Cycles:** If you’re trained and willing, continue cycles of 30 compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths. If you’re not trained in rescue breathing, or unwilling, compression-only CPR is still incredibly effective. The American Heart Association and other major bodies emphasize that hands-on chest compressions are the most critical part.

**Why this is important (Exam Hint):** CPR is tested on its *steps* and *ratios* (30 compressions: 2 breaths). Knowing the *why* behind each step – like opening the airway to allow air to reach the lungs – is key to understanding and performing it correctly.

**Relatable Example:** Imagine you're at a park, and someone suddenly collapses. Their breathing stops, their pulse is gone. You remember your CPR training. You quickly call for help, get on your knees, position your hands on their chest, and start pushing, fast and hard. You’re not trying to restart their heart on your own; you’re keeping oxygenated blood moving to their brain and vital organs until paramedics arrive with advanced equipment. That’s the power of CPR.

### The Heimlich Maneuver: Clearing the Airway

Now, let’s talk about another critical airway management technique: the **Heimlich Maneuver**, also known as abdominal thrusts. This is for choking. When someone is choking, their airway is blocked by food or another object, preventing them from breathing.

**What is the Heimlich Maneuver?**
It's a first aid procedure that uses abdominal thrusts to dislodge an object from the airway of someone who is choking.

**When to Use It:**
You perform the Heimlich Maneuver if someone is:
*   **Conscious and Choking:** They can't cough, speak, or breathe effectively. They might be clutching their throat (the universal sign for choking).
*   **Not Conscious:** If they become unconscious, you would then proceed with CPR, checking the mouth for the obstruction before giving breaths.

**The "How-To" of the Heimlich Maneuver (for Conscious Adults):**
*   **Assess the Situation:** Ask, "Are you choking?" If they can cough or speak forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing. Only intervene if they cannot breathe, cough, or speak.
*   **Position Yourself:** Stand behind the person. If they are standing, wrap your arms around their waist. If they are sitting, stand behind their chair and wrap your arms around their waist.
*   **Hand Placement:** Make a fist with one hand. Place the thumb side of your fist against the person's abdomen, slightly above the navel and well below the rib cage.
*   **The Thrust:** Grasp your fist with your other hand. Deliver quick, upward thrusts into the abdomen. It’s like giving a strong hug from behind and pushing upwards.
*   **Continue:** Repeat these thrusts until the object is expelled, or the person can breathe or cough forcefully.

**What if the person is pregnant or very obese?**
In these cases, or if you cannot get your arms around them, perform **chest thrusts** instead. Place your hands in the center of the breastbone (sternum), similar to CPR hand placement, and deliver inward thrusts.

**What if the person becomes unconscious?**
If the person becomes unconscious, carefully lower them to the ground. Begin CPR, starting with chest compressions. **Crucially, before giving rescue breaths during CPR for a choking victim, look in their mouth. If you see the object and can easily remove it with your fingers, do so.** But never perform a blind finger sweep, as you could push the object further down.

**Why this is important (Exam Hint):** Again, focus on the steps, hand placement, and the direction of the thrusts. The key is to create a forceful expulsion of air from the lungs to push out the obstruction.

**Relatable Example:** You're at a restaurant, and someone at the next table suddenly starts gasping, their face turns red, and they're frantically pointing to their throat. You recognize the signs of choking. You quickly get up, go to them, ask if they're choking, and if they confirm with nods and no sound, you immediately move behind them and perform abdominal thrusts. You feel a slight movement, and then they cough, expelling a piece of food. They can now breathe! You’ve just saved a life with the Heimlich maneuver.

### Applying a Sling: Supporting Injured Limbs

Finally, let's talk about applying a sling. This is crucial for supporting injured arms or shoulders, whether it's a fracture, dislocation, or severe sprain. Its main purpose is to **immobilize** the injured part, reduce pain, and prevent further damage.

**What is a Sling?**
A sling is a triangular piece of cloth or a ready-made strap that is used to support an injured arm or hand. It works by holding the arm in a fixed position against the body.

**When to Use It:**
*   Fractures of the arm or collarbone (clavicle).
*   Severe sprains or strains of the shoulder or arm.
*   Dislocations of the shoulder or elbow.
*   Any injury where immobilizing the arm is necessary for comfort and healing.

**Types of Slings:**
While we often think of a triangular bandage (a "crepe bandage" is often used as a makeshift sling), many first aid kits include ready-made slings. For our purposes, understanding how to create one with a triangular bandage is valuable.

**The "How-To" of Applying a Basic Triangular Sling:**
1.  **Positioning the Arm:** Gently support the injured arm. If it’s a forearm injury, it’s usually best to support it at a right angle (90 degrees) at the elbow.
2.  **Placing the Bandage:**
    *   Take your triangular bandage (or a length of cloth folded into a wide strip).
    *   Place the center of the bandage under the injured arm, extending from the shoulder to the elbow.
    *   Bring the two ends of the bandage up and over the uninjured shoulder.
3.  **Tying the Sling:**
    *   Cross the ends of the bandage at the back of the neck.
    *   Tie them securely, but not too tightly, ensuring the knot is comfortable and off the bony prominence of the shoulder.
4.  **Securing the Sling (The "Tail"):**
    *   The tip of the bandage that hangs in front of the elbow needs to be secured.
    *   Bring this tip up towards the shoulder and tuck it into the knot at the back of the neck or tie it off securely to prevent the arm from swinging. You can also use safety pins for extra security.
5.  **Check for Comfort and Circulation:**
    *   Ensure the arm is comfortably supported and the sling is snug but not constricting.
    *   Check the fingers of the injured hand for color (they should be pink, not pale or blue) and warmth, and ask the person if they have any tingling or numbness. This checks if circulation is being cut off. If so, loosen the sling slightly.
6.  **Additional Support (Optional but Recommended):** You might use a broad bandage (like a roller bandage) tied around the chest and over the sling to further immobilize the arm, especially for shoulder injuries or collarbone fractures. This is called a "swathe."

**Why this is important (Exam Hint):** Questions might focus on the *purpose* of a sling (immobilization, support, pain reduction) and the *steps* involved in application, including checking for circulation.

**Relatable Example:** Imagine you’re hiking and your friend falls, hurting their forearm. It’s clearly painful and swollen. You suspect a fracture. You carefully help them to a comfortable position, support their arm, and using a scarf or even a shirt you have, you fashion a makeshift sling to keep their arm stable until you can get them to a doctor. The sling prevents the injured bone ends from grinding against each other, reducing pain and preventing further damage.

### Qualities of a Good First Aider: A Recap

As we’ve discussed these procedures, remember the qualities that make a good first aider, as outlined in our principles. These aren't just abstract ideas; they are crucial for performing CPR, the Heimlich, or applying a sling effectively:

*   **Calm and Reassuring:** In an emergency, panic helps no one. Staying calm helps you think clearly and reassure the casualty.
*   **Observant:** Noticing the signs of choking or someone not breathing is the first step.
*   **Competent:** Knowing *how* to perform these skills correctly. This comes from learning and practice.
*   **Resourceful:** Knowing how to improvise if you don't have the perfect equipment (like using a shirt as a sling).
*   **Confident (but not overconfident):** Having the belief in your ability to help, but knowing your limitations and when to call for professional help.
*   **Respectful:** Treating the person with dignity and preserving their privacy as much as possible.

### Sample Questions and Answers

**1. Conceptual Question:** Explain why opening the airway is a critical step before performing rescue breaths during CPR.
    *   **Answer:** Opening the airway (typically by tilting the head and lifting the chin) is crucial because the tongue is the most common cause of airway obstruction in an unconscious person. When a person loses consciousness, their tongue can relax and fall back, blocking the pharynx. By tilting the head and lifting the chin, the tongue is pulled forward and upward, clearing the passage for air to reach the lungs during rescue breaths. Without an open airway, the delivered air will not enter the lungs, making the rescue breaths ineffective.

**2. Exam-Oriented Question:** A 60-year-old man suddenly collapses while walking. You approach him, find he is unresponsive, and is not breathing normally. What are the immediate steps you should take, and what is the recommended ratio of chest compressions to rescue breaths if you are trained in CPR?
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Immediate Steps:**
            1.  **Check for Safety:** Ensure the scene is safe for you to approach.
            2.  **Check for Responsiveness:** Tap the person's shoulder and shout, "Are you okay?"
            3.  **Call for Help:** Shout for someone nearby to call emergency services (e.g., 911) and get an AED if available. If alone, perform CPR for 2 minutes before calling if no phone is readily accessible.
            4.  **Begin CPR:** If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, immediately start chest compressions.
        *   **Recommended Ratio:** The recommended ratio of chest compressions to rescue breaths for adults is **30 compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths**. If not trained or unwilling to perform rescue breaths, hands-only CPR (continuous chest compressions) is still highly effective.

**3. Conceptual Question:** What is the primary purpose of performing the Heimlich maneuver on someone who is choking and unable to cough or speak?
    *   **Answer:** The primary purpose of the Heimlich maneuver is to create a sudden, forceful expulsion of air from the lungs (an "artificial cough") to dislodge an object that is blocking the airway. This action aims to clear the obstruction, allowing the person to breathe again.

**4. Exam-Oriented Question:** Describe the correct hand placement and the action taken when performing the Heimlich maneuver on a conscious adult who is choking.
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Hand Placement:** Stand behind the choking person. Wrap your arms around their waist. Make a fist with one hand. Place the thumb side of your fist against the person's abdomen, slightly above their navel and well below the rib cage. Grasp your fist with your other hand.
        *   **Action:** Deliver quick, upward thrusts into the abdomen. These thrusts should be firm and directed inwards and upwards, much like a hug from behind with an upward motion. Repeat these thrusts until the object is expelled or the person can breathe or cough forcefully.

**5. Conceptual Question:** Why is it important to check the circulation of the fingers after applying a sling to an injured arm?
    *   **Answer:** Checking the circulation of the fingers after applying a sling is crucial to ensure the sling is not applied too tightly. If the sling constricts blood flow to the hand, it can lead to nerve damage, tissue damage, and long-term complications. Signs of impaired circulation include fingers that are pale, blueish, cold to the touch, or accompanied by numbness or tingling. If these signs are present, the sling needs to be loosened immediately.

**6. Exam-Oriented Question:** Outline the steps for applying a basic triangular sling to an injured arm, assuming a suspected fracture of the forearm.
    *   **Answer:**
        1.  **Support the Arm:** Gently support the injured arm, bending it at the elbow to approximately a 90-degree angle.
        2.  **Place the Bandage:** Position the center of the triangular bandage under the injured arm, from the elbow to the shoulder.
        3.  **Bring Ends Up:** Bring the two ends of the bandage up and over the uninjured shoulder.
        4.  **Tie at Neck:** Cross the ends at the back of the neck and tie them securely, ensuring comfort and avoiding pressure on the neck or shoulder bone.
        5.  **Secure the Tip:** Bring the tip of the bandage that extends in front of the elbow upwards and secure it by tucking it into the knot at the back of the neck or tying it off.
        6.  **Check Circulation:** Ensure the sling is snug but not too tight. Check the fingers for signs of good circulation (warm, pink, good sensation). If circulation is compromised, loosen the sling.
        7.  **(Optional) Swathe:** Consider using a broad bandage (swathe) tied around the chest and over the sling for extra support and immobilization, especially for shoulder injuries.
