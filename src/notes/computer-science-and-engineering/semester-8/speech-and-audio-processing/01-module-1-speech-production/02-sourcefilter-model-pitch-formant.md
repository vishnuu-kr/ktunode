# SPEECH AND AUDIO PROCESSING - Module 1: Speech Production

## Topic: Source/Filter Model - Pitch, Formant

This module introduces the fundamental Source/Filter model of speech production, focusing on the key acoustic correlates of pitch and formants.

---

### **1. Introduction to the Source/Filter Model**

The Source/Filter model is a widely accepted conceptual framework that explains how the complex sounds of speech are generated by the human vocal tract. It proposes that speech production can be viewed as two independent stages:

*   **Source:** The generation of a raw sound signal.
*   **Filter:** The modification of this raw signal by the vocal tract.

#### **1.1 The Source Component**

The source of speech sound is typically the **vocal folds (or vocal cords)** located in the larynx. There are two primary types of sound sources:

*   **Voiced Source:**
    *   **Mechanism:** The vocal folds vibrate rhythmically due to airflow from the lungs. This vibration creates a **periodic** series of puffs of air.
    *   **Acoustic Characteristics:** The resulting sound is characterized by its fundamental frequency (F0), which is perceived as **pitch**.
    *   **Speech Sounds:** Vowels, voiced consonants (e.g., /b/, /d/, /g/, /z/, /v/, /m/, /n/, /l/, /r/, /w/).

*   **Unvoiced (Aperiodic) Source:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Air is forced through a constriction in the vocal tract, creating **turbulent airflow** and noise. The vocal folds do not vibrate.
    *   **Acoustic Characteristics:** The resulting sound is **aperiodic** and characterized by a broad spectral distribution of energy, perceived as **noise**.
    *   **Speech Sounds:** Voiceless consonants (e.g., /p/, /t/, /k/, /s/, /f/, /ʃ/, /θ/).

#### **1.2 The Filter Component**

The vocal tract acts as a **resonator**, modifying the raw sound produced by the source. The vocal tract is a series of cavities (pharynx, oral cavity, nasal cavity) whose shape can be altered by moving the articulators (tongue, lips, jaw, soft palate).

*   **Mechanism:** The shape of the vocal tract determines which frequencies are amplified and which are attenuated in the source signal. This selective amplification and attenuation is governed by the principles of **acoustic resonance**.
*   **Acoustic Characteristics:** The filtering effect results in peaks in the frequency spectrum called **formants**. These formants are crucial for distinguishing different vowel sounds.
*   **Articulation:** The movement of articulators changes the volume and shape of these cavities, thus altering the formant frequencies and the resulting speech sound.

#### **1.3 The Combined Source/Filter Model**

The overall speech sound is the product of the source signal being filtered by the vocal tract.

*   **Voiced Speech:** The periodic pulses from the vocal folds are filtered, resulting in a spectrum with a fundamental frequency (pitch) and amplified harmonic frequencies at the formant frequencies.
*   **Unvoiced Speech:** The aperiodic noise source is filtered, resulting in a spectrum with amplified frequencies at the formant frequencies (though often less distinct than in vowels).

### **2. Pitch (Fundamental Frequency - F0)**

Pitch is a subjective perceptual attribute of sound that is primarily determined by the **fundamental frequency (F0)** of the vocal fold vibration.

#### **2.1 Definition of Pitch**

*   **Pitch:** The perceived highness or lowness of a sound.
*   **Fundamental Frequency (F0):** The lowest frequency component in a complex periodic sound wave, corresponding to the rate at which the vocal folds vibrate.

#### **2.2 Factors Affecting Pitch**

The fundamental frequency of the vocal folds is influenced by several physiological factors:

*   **Tension of Vocal Folds:**
    *   **Higher Tension:** Vocal folds are stretched and thinner, leading to faster vibration and higher F0.
    *   **Lower Tension:** Vocal folds are relaxed and thicker, leading to slower vibration and lower F0.

*   **Length of Vocal Folds:**
    *   **Longer Vocal Folds:** Result in slower vibration and lower F0 (e.g., in males).
    *   **Shorter Vocal Folds:** Result in faster vibration and higher F0 (e.g., in females and children).

*   **Mass of Vocal Folds:**
    *   **Greater Mass:** Slower vibration and lower F0.
    *   **Lesser Mass:** Faster vibration and higher F0.

*   **Air Pressure from Lungs:**
    *   **Higher Subglottal Pressure:** Can lead to increased vocal fold tension and higher F0, up to a certain limit.

#### **2.3 Pitch in Speech**

*   **Intonation:** Variations in pitch across an utterance convey meaning, such as distinguishing between statements, questions, and exclamations.
    *   **Example:** The falling pitch at the end of a declarative sentence vs. the rising pitch at the end of a yes/no question.

*   **Prosody:** Pitch is a key component of prosody, which includes intonation, stress, and rhythm.

#### **2.4 Measurement of Pitch**

Pitch is measured in Hertz (Hz). The typical range of F0 for adult males is 85-180 Hz, for adult females is 165-255 Hz, and for children is higher.

### **3. Formants**

Formants are peaks in the frequency spectrum of speech sounds, particularly prominent in vowels. They represent frequencies that are amplified by the vocal tract due to resonance.

#### **3.1 Definition of Formants**

*   **Formant:** A specific frequency at which a significant amount of acoustic energy is present in a sound, arising from the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract.
*   **Resonance:** The phenomenon where an object (in this case, the vocal tract) vibrates with greater amplitude at certain frequencies when exposed to external vibrations at those same frequencies. These frequencies are called the **resonant frequencies** or **natural frequencies**.

#### **3.2 The Vocal Tract as a Resonator**

The vocal tract can be approximated as a **tube open at one end (the lips) and closed at the other (the glottis)**. The resonant frequencies of such a tube are determined by its length and shape.

*   **Length:** A longer vocal tract has lower resonant frequencies, and a shorter vocal tract has higher resonant frequencies.
*   **Shape:** The distribution of air volume within different cavities (e.g., front oral cavity vs. back pharyngeal cavity) influences the specific formant frequencies.

#### **3.3 Key Formants in Vowel Production**

*   **First Formant (F1):**
    *   **Relationship to Tongue Height:** Primarily determined by the **volume of the pharyngeal cavity**.
    *   **Inverse Relationship:**
        *   **High Tongue (Closed Vowel, e.g., /i/):** Larger pharyngeal volume, **lower F1**.
        *   **Low Tongue (Open Vowel, e.g., /a/):** Smaller pharyngeal volume, **higher F1**.

*   **Second Formant (F2):**
    *   **Relationship to Tongue Backness/Frontness:** Primarily determined by the **volume of the oral cavity anterior to the constriction**.
    *   **Direct Relationship:**
        *   **Front Tongue (e.g., /i/):** Smaller front oral cavity volume, **higher F2**.
        *   **Back Tongue (e.g., /u/):** Larger front oral cavity volume, **lower F2**.

*   **Third Formant (F3) and Higher:** While F1 and F2 are the most crucial for distinguishing vowels, F3 and subsequent formants also contribute to the unique acoustic characteristics of different speech sounds, especially in certain diphthongs or specific consonant contexts.

#### **3.4 Formants and Vowel Perception**

The pattern of formant frequencies (F1, F2, etc.) is the primary acoustic cue for distinguishing different vowel sounds.

*   **Example: The Cardinal Vowels**
    *   **/i/ (as in "see"):** High front tongue, large pharyngeal volume. High F2, Low F1.
    *   **/u/ (as in "too"):** High back tongue, rounded lips. Low F2, relatively low F1.
    *   **/a/ (as in "father"):** Low tongue, small pharyngeal volume. High F1, mid-range F2.
    *   **/ɑ/ (as in "hot"):** Low back tongue, unrounded lips. High F1, low F2.
    *   **/ɔ/ (as in "caught"):** Mid back tongue, rounded lips. Mid F1, low F2.

    (Note: The exact values of formants vary between speakers and contexts, but the relative patterns are consistent.)

#### **3.5 Formants in Consonant Production**

Formants are also present in voiced consonants, where they change rapidly during transitions. These **formant transitions** are important cues for identifying the place of articulation of consonants. For unvoiced consonants, the noise source is filtered, leading to formant-like peaks in their spectrum.

### **4. The Source/Filter Model in Action: Examples**

#### **4.1 Vowel Production**

*   **Source:** Voiced vibration of vocal folds.
*   **Filter:** The shape of the oral cavity (tongue position, lip rounding).
    *   **Example /i/ (see):** Vocal folds vibrate, and the tongue is raised high and forward in the mouth. This creates a narrow front cavity and a large back cavity. This configuration results in a low F1 and a high F2, which our auditory system perceives as the vowel /i/.

#### **4.2 Voiced Stop Consonant Production (e.g., /b/)**

*   **Source:** Voiced vibration of vocal folds.
*   **Filter:** Vocal tract configuration, including a complete closure at the lips.
    *   **Acoustic Event:** A brief period of silence (due to closure), followed by an **abrupt release** of the closure. The release is accompanied by a burst of noise and **rapid formant transitions** as the articulators move from the closed position to the vowel position.
    *   **Example /ba/:** The /b/ sound is produced with voiced vocal folds and lip closure. When the lips open, the articulators move to the vowel /a/ position. The formants change rapidly from frequencies characteristic of the closure to the F1 and F2 values of /a/.

#### **4.3 Voiceless Fricative Consonant Production (e.g., /s/)**

*   **Source:** Aperiodic noise generated by forcing air through a narrow constriction (e.g., between the tongue and the alveolar ridge).
*   **Filter:** The vocal tract shape after the constriction.
    *   **Acoustic Event:** A continuous noise spectrum with prominent peaks (formant-like structures) determined by the resonating cavities anterior and posterior to the constriction.
    *   **Example /sa/:** The /s/ sound is produced with a high-frequency noise spectrum. This noise is then filtered by the vocal tract as it moves towards the vowel /a/. The prominent spectral peak of /s/ is typically at a higher frequency than the spectral peak of /f/.

### **5. Important Points to Remember**

*   The Source/Filter model separates speech production into a **sound generator (source)** and a **sound modifier (filter)**.
*   The primary sources are **voiced (periodic)** from vocal fold vibration and **unvoiced (aperiodic)** from turbulent airflow.
*   **Pitch** is the perceptual correlate of the **fundamental frequency (F0)**, determined by vocal fold vibration rate.
*   **Formants** are resonant frequencies of the vocal tract, crucial for distinguishing vowels.
*   **F1** is primarily related to **tongue height** (inverse relationship).
*   **F2** is primarily related to **tongue backness/frontness** (direct relationship).
*   The vocal tract acts as a filter whose shape is constantly changed by the **articulators** (tongue, lips, jaw, etc.).
*   **Formant transitions** are important acoustic cues for consonants.
*   Different speech sounds are produced by combining different **source types** with different **filter configurations**.

### **6. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:**
What are the two primary components of the Source/Filter model of speech production?

**Answer 1:**
The two primary components are the **Source** (which generates the raw sound, typically the vocal folds or constrictions) and the **Filter** (which modifies the sound, primarily the vocal tract).

---

**Question 2:**
Which acoustic feature is primarily associated with the perceptual quality of "pitch"?

**Answer 2:**
The acoustic feature primarily associated with pitch is the **fundamental frequency (F0)** of the vocal fold vibration.

---

**Question 3:**
Describe the relationship between tongue height and the first formant (F1) during vowel production.

**Answer 3:**
There is an inverse relationship between tongue height and F1. A higher tongue position results in a lower F1, while a lower tongue position results in a higher F1.

---

**Question 4:**
What acoustic phenomenon allows us to distinguish between the vowel sounds /i/ (as in "see") and /u/ (as in "too")?

**Answer 4:**
The primary acoustic difference lies in the **formant frequencies**, specifically the relationship between F1 and F2.
*   **/i/:** High F2, Low F1 (due to high front tongue)
*   **/u/:** Low F2, relatively low F1 (due to high back tongue and lip rounding)

---

**Question 5:**
Differentiate between a voiced and an unvoiced speech source. Provide an example of a speech sound produced by each.

**Answer 5:**
*   **Voiced Source:** Produced by the rhythmic vibration of the vocal folds. This results in a periodic sound.
    *   **Example:** Vowels like /a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/; voiced consonants like /b/, /d/, /g/, /z/.
*   **Unvoiced Source:** Produced by turbulent airflow through a constriction in the vocal tract, without vocal fold vibration. This results in an aperiodic (noise) sound.
    *   **Example:** Voiceless consonants like /p/, /t/, /k/, /s/, /f/.

---

**Question 6:**
Consider the vowel sound /a/ (as in "father"). Would you expect its first formant (F1) to be higher or lower than the vowel sound /i/ (as in "see")? Explain why.

**Answer 6:**
You would expect the first formant (F1) of /a/ to be **higher** than that of /i/. This is because /a/ is produced with a lower tongue position than /i/. A lower tongue position increases the volume of the pharyngeal cavity, which in turn raises the first resonant frequency (F1) of the vocal tract.
