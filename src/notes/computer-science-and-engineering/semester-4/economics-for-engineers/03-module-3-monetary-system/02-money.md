## ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - MODULE 3: MONETARY SYSTEM - TOPIC: MONEY

**Learning Outcomes:**

*   Understand the functions of money.
*   Differentiate between different types of money.
*   Explain the concepts of money supply and its measurement.
*   Describe the demand for money.
*   Analyze the relationship between money supply, inflation, and interest rates.

---

### 1. Functions of Money

Money serves as a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account. Understanding these functions is crucial for understanding how money facilitates economic activity.

*   **Medium of Exchange:**
    *   Definition: Money is widely accepted as a payment for goods and services and the settlement of debts.  It eliminates the need for barter.
    *   Example: You buy a coffee with cash or a debit card (both forms of money). The coffee shop accepts it readily because they know they can use it to pay their suppliers, employees, etc.
    *   Why it's important:  Barter requires a "double coincidence of wants" (you have what the other person wants, and they have what you want).  Money overcomes this inefficiency, enabling specialization and increased trade.

*   **Store of Value:**
    *   Definition:  Money holds its value over time, allowing individuals to save and defer consumption.
    *   Example: You save money in a bank account.  You expect it to retain its purchasing power (though this is affected by inflation).
    *   Why it's important: Without a reliable store of value, saving would be difficult, hindering investment and long-term economic growth.  The extent to which money serves as a store of value is impacted by inflation.  High inflation erodes purchasing power.

*   **Unit of Account (Standard of Value):**
    *   Definition: Money provides a common measure of the value of goods, services, and assets.  It allows for easy comparison of prices.
    *   Example:  A loaf of bread costs $3, a gallon of milk costs $4, and a shirt costs $20.  Money provides a standard unit (dollars) to compare their relative values.
    *   Why it's important:  Simplifies accounting, price comparisons, and economic decision-making.  Imagine trying to compare the value of goods if prices were expressed in terms of other goods (e.g., 2 chickens for a shirt).

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   For something to function effectively as money, it needs to be relatively scarce, durable, portable, divisible, and acceptable.
*   The degree to which an item fulfills these functions determines how well it serves as money.

### 2. Types of Money

Money can be categorized based on its intrinsic value and how it's backed.

*   **Commodity Money:**
    *   Definition: Money that has intrinsic value in itself.  Its value is based on the material it is made of.
    *   Example: Gold coins. The gold itself has value apart from its use as money.  Salt was also used as commodity money historically.
    *   Advantages:  Intrinsic value provides inherent security.
    *   Disadvantages:  Supply is limited by the availability of the commodity.  Can be difficult to transport large values.  Quality may vary.

*   **Representative Money:**
    *   Definition: Money that represents a claim to a commodity held in reserve.
    *   Example:  Gold certificates that were redeemable for a specific amount of gold.
    *   Advantages:  More convenient than carrying the actual commodity.
    *   Disadvantages:  Reliant on the issuer's ability to maintain the reserve and redeem the certificates.

*   **Fiat Money:**
    *   Definition: Money that has no intrinsic value and is not backed by any commodity. Its value is based on government decree (fiat) and public confidence.
    *   Example:  Most modern currencies, such as the US Dollar, Euro, and Yen.
    *   Advantages:  Supply can be managed more easily to meet the needs of the economy.
    *   Disadvantages:  Value is dependent on public confidence in the government and central bank.  Prone to inflation if the money supply is not managed responsibly.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Most modern economies use fiat money. Its success depends on trust in the government and its ability to control inflation.
*   The move from commodity money to fiat money has allowed for greater flexibility in managing the money supply.

### 3. Money Supply and Its Measurement

The money supply refers to the total amount of money in circulation in an economy at a given time. It's measured using different aggregates, each including different types of liquid assets.

*   **Monetary Aggregates:**  Different measures of the money supply. Common aggregates include:
    *   **M0 (Monetary Base/Base Money):**  Currency in circulation + commercial banks' reserves held at the central bank.  It's the most liquid form of money.
    *   **M1 (Narrow Money):**  Currency in circulation + demand deposits (checking accounts) + other checkable deposits.  Represents money readily available for transactions.
    *   **M2 (Broad Money):**  M1 + savings deposits + small-denomination time deposits (CDs) + money market mutual funds.  Less liquid than M1.
    *   **M3 (Broadest Money):** M2 + large-denomination time deposits + institutional money market funds + repurchase agreements. Least liquid.  Some countries have stopped tracking M3.

*   **Central Bank's Role:**  The central bank (e.g., the Federal Reserve in the US) is responsible for managing the money supply through various tools, including:
    *   **Open Market Operations:** Buying or selling government securities to increase or decrease the money supply. Buying injects money into the economy; selling withdraws money.
    *   **Reserve Requirements:**  The fraction of deposits that banks are required to hold in reserve. Lowering reserve requirements increases the money supply; raising them decreases it.
    *   **Discount Rate/Policy Rate:**  The interest rate at which commercial banks can borrow money directly from the central bank. Lowering the discount rate encourages borrowing and increases the money supply; raising it discourages borrowing and decreases it.

*   **Money Multiplier:**  The process by which an initial change in the monetary base leads to a larger change in the money supply. The money multiplier is approximately equal to 1/reserve requirement.
    *   Example: If the reserve requirement is 10% (0.1), the money multiplier is 1/0.1 = 10.  An initial injection of $100 of reserves into the banking system can potentially lead to a $1000 increase in the money supply.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The specific definitions of M1, M2, and M3 can vary from country to country.
*   The central bank's control over the money supply is not perfect due to factors such as banks choosing to hold excess reserves and individuals' preferences for holding cash.

### 4. Demand for Money

The demand for money represents the desire of individuals and firms to hold money balances. There are three primary motives for holding money.

*   **Transactions Motive:**
    *   Definition:  Holding money to finance day-to-day transactions (purchases of goods and services).
    *   Factors affecting:  Level of income (higher income, more transactions), price level (higher prices, more money needed).
    *   Example:  Keeping cash in your wallet or a balance in your checking account to pay for groceries, gas, and other expenses.

*   **Precautionary Motive:**
    *   Definition:  Holding money as a buffer against unexpected expenses or opportunities.
    *   Factors affecting:  Uncertainty about future income and expenses, risk aversion.
    *   Example:  Having an emergency fund in a savings account to cover unexpected medical bills or car repairs.

*   **Speculative Motive:**
    *   Definition:  Holding money in anticipation of future changes in interest rates or asset prices.
    *   Factors affecting:  Expectations about future interest rates, risk tolerance.
    *   Example:  Holding money instead of investing in bonds if you expect interest rates to rise (and bond prices to fall).  This allows you to buy the bonds later at a lower price.

*   **Interest Rate and Demand for Money:** The demand for money is inversely related to the interest rate.  Higher interest rates make it more costly to hold money (because you are foregoing the interest you could earn by investing it), so the quantity of money demanded decreases.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The transactions and precautionary motives are primarily influenced by the level of income.
*   The speculative motive is primarily influenced by interest rates and expectations about future asset prices.

### 5. Money Supply, Inflation, and Interest Rates

The quantity theory of money suggests a strong relationship between the money supply, inflation, and interest rates.

*   **Quantity Theory of Money:**
    *   Equation:  MV = PQ, where:
        *   M = Money supply
        *   V = Velocity of money (the average number of times a dollar is spent in a year)
        *   P = Price level
        *   Q = Real GDP (quantity of goods and services produced)
    *   Assumption:  Velocity of money (V) is relatively stable in the short run.
    *   Implication:  If the money supply (M) increases faster than real GDP (Q), the price level (P) will increase, leading to inflation.  That is, **ΔM > ΔQ  => Inflation**.

*   **Inflation:** A sustained increase in the general price level.
    *   Causes:  Excessive growth in the money supply is a primary driver of inflation (according to the quantity theory).  Other factors include cost-push inflation (rising input costs) and demand-pull inflation (excess demand).
    *   Effects:  Erosion of purchasing power, uncertainty, distortion of relative prices, redistribution of wealth.

*   **Relationship between Money Supply and Interest Rates:**
    *   **Liquidity Preference Theory:**  Interest rates are determined by the supply and demand for money.
    *   Increased Money Supply: An increase in the money supply (holding demand constant) will typically lead to lower interest rates. This is because there is more money available for lending, driving down the price of borrowing (interest rate).
    *   Decreased Money Supply: A decrease in the money supply (holding demand constant) will typically lead to higher interest rates.

*   **Fisher Equation:** Relates nominal interest rate, real interest rate, and inflation rate.
    *   Equation: Nominal Interest Rate = Real Interest Rate + Expected Inflation Rate
    *   Implication: An increase in expected inflation will lead to an increase in the nominal interest rate.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The relationship between money supply, inflation, and interest rates is complex and can be influenced by various factors.
*   Central banks often target inflation rates by adjusting the money supply and interest rates.
*   High inflation can have detrimental effects on an economy, making it important for policymakers to maintain price stability.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

1.  **Identify the function of money being illustrated in each scenario:**

    *   a) A company quotes the price of all its products in US dollars.  (**Unit of Account**)
    *   b) You put money into a savings account to use for a down payment on a house in the future. (**Store of Value**)
    *   c) You pay for groceries with a debit card. (**Medium of Exchange**)

2.  **Which type of money is most susceptible to inflation, and why?**

    *   **Fiat money** is most susceptible to inflation because its value is based on government decree and public confidence, not on any intrinsic value or commodity backing.  If the government prints too much fiat money, its value can be quickly eroded.

3.  **Explain how open market operations can be used to decrease the money supply.**

    *   The central bank can **sell government securities** in the open market. When the central bank sells these securities, commercial banks and individuals purchase them using their reserves and cash. This reduces the amount of money in circulation, decreasing the money supply.

4.  **If the reserve requirement is 20%, what is the money multiplier?**

    *   The money multiplier is 1/reserve requirement = 1/0.20 = **5**.

5.  **What is the primary motivation behind the speculative demand for money?**

    *   The **speculative demand for money** is driven by the desire to profit from anticipated changes in interest rates or asset prices.

6.  **According to the quantity theory of money, if the money supply increases by 10% and real GDP increases by 3%, what will happen to the price level (assuming velocity is constant)?**

    *   According to the quantity theory of money (MV=PQ), if M increases by 10% and Q increases by 3%, then P will increase by approximately 7%.  (10% - 3% = 7%).  This suggests an **inflation rate of approximately 7%**.

7.  **Explain the relationship between interest rates and the demand for money.**

    *   The demand for money and interest rates are **inversely related**. Higher interest rates mean it is more costly to hold money (because you forego the interest you could earn by investing it). Therefore, higher interest rates lead to a lower quantity of money demanded.
