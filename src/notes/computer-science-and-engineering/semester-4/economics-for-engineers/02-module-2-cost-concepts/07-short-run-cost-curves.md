## ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts - Short Run Cost Curves

**Learning Outcomes:**

*   Understand the definition of short run and fixed vs. variable costs.
*   Define and differentiate between Total Cost (TC), Total Fixed Cost (TFC), and Total Variable Cost (TVC).
*   Define and differentiate between Average Fixed Cost (AFC), Average Variable Cost (AVC), Average Total Cost (ATC), and Marginal Cost (MC).
*   Explain the relationship between the different cost curves (AFC, AVC, ATC, MC) and their shapes.
*   Understand the relationship between marginal cost and average variable cost, and marginal cost and average total cost.
*   Apply the concepts of short-run cost curves to engineering decision-making problems.

---

### 1. Short Run, Fixed Costs, and Variable Costs

*   **Short Run:** A period of time in which at least one input (factor of production) is fixed, while others can be varied.  The fixed input often represents a firm's capital (plant size, equipment).  It's important to note that the length of the short run varies by industry. A restaurant might be able to change its size within a couple of months, a power plant, several years.
*   **Fixed Costs (FC):** Costs that do not vary with the level of output in the short run. These costs are incurred even if no output is produced.  Also sometimes referred to as sunk costs, especially when considering long term decisions.
    *   **Examples:** Rent, insurance premiums, property taxes, salaries of permanent staff (even if production is temporarily halted).
*   **Variable Costs (VC):** Costs that vary directly with the level of output in the short run.  As production increases, variable costs increase.
    *   **Examples:** Raw materials, direct labor wages, electricity for running machinery, cost of packaging for each item produced.

**Important Point:**  The distinction between fixed and variable costs is crucial for short-run decision-making.  Firms can influence variable costs by adjusting production levels, but fixed costs are largely predetermined in the short run.

---

### 2. Total Cost (TC), Total Fixed Cost (TFC), and Total Variable Cost (TVC)

*   **Total Fixed Cost (TFC):** The sum of all fixed costs.  TFC is a constant value and does not change with output.  Graphically, it's a horizontal line.
*   **Total Variable Cost (TVC):** The sum of all variable costs. TVC increases as output increases.  The shape of the TVC curve typically reflects the law of diminishing returns (initially increasing at a decreasing rate, then increasing at an increasing rate).
*   **Total Cost (TC):** The sum of total fixed cost and total variable cost.  TC = TFC + TVC.  The shape of the TC curve is similar to the TVC curve, but it's shifted upwards by the amount of TFC.

**Key Relationships:**

*   TC = TFC + TVC
*   Changes in TC are solely due to changes in TVC, as TFC is constant.

**Example:**

| Output (Units) | TFC ($) | TVC ($) | TC ($) |
|-----------------|---------|---------|--------|
| 0              | 100     | 0       | 100    |
| 1              | 100     | 50      | 150    |
| 2              | 100     | 90      | 190    |
| 3              | 100     | 120     | 220    |
| 4              | 100     | 160     | 260    |
| 5              | 100     | 210     | 310    |

In this example, TFC is always $100.  TVC increases with output. TC is calculated as TFC + TVC.

### 3. Average Fixed Cost (AFC), Average Variable Cost (AVC), Average Total Cost (ATC), and Marginal Cost (MC)

These are cost measures expressed per unit of output.

*   **Average Fixed Cost (AFC):** Total Fixed Cost divided by the quantity of output. AFC = TFC / Q. AFC always decreases as output increases because the fixed cost is spread over a larger number of units. The AFC curve is a continuously declining curve (but never reaches zero).
*   **Average Variable Cost (AVC):** Total Variable Cost divided by the quantity of output. AVC = TVC / Q. The AVC curve is typically U-shaped due to the initial benefits of specialization and division of labor, followed by diminishing returns.
*   **Average Total Cost (ATC):** Total Cost divided by the quantity of output. ATC = TC / Q.  Also, ATC = AFC + AVC. The ATC curve is also typically U-shaped, representing economies of scale followed by diseconomies of scale.
*   **Marginal Cost (MC):** The change in total cost resulting from producing one more unit of output. MC = ΔTC / ΔQ  or MC = ΔTVC / ΔQ (since ΔTFC = 0).  The MC curve is also typically U-shaped.  MC is critically important for production decisions because it tells a firm how much it will cost to produce the next unit.

**Key Relationships:**

*   ATC = AFC + AVC
*   MC is calculated based on *changes* in TC or TVC.
*   AFC is always decreasing.
*   AVC and ATC are typically U-shaped.
*   MC is typically U-shaped.

**Example (Continuing from the previous example):**

| Output (Units) | TFC ($) | TVC ($) | TC ($) | AFC ($) | AVC ($) | ATC ($) | MC ($) |
|-----------------|---------|---------|--------|---------|---------|---------|--------|
| 0              | 100     | 0       | 100    | -       | -       | -       | -      |
| 1              | 100     | 50      | 150    | 100.00  | 50.00   | 150.00  | 50     |
| 2              | 100     | 90      | 190    | 50.00   | 45.00   | 95.00   | 40     |
| 3              | 100     | 120     | 220    | 33.33   | 40.00   | 73.33   | 30     |
| 4              | 100     | 160     | 260    | 25.00   | 40.00   | 65.00   | 40     |
| 5              | 100     | 210     | 310    | 20.00   | 42.00   | 62.00   | 50     |

**Important Points:**

*   Pay close attention to the units of measurement (dollars, units of output).
*   Understand the formulas for calculating each cost measure.
*   Remember that MC is a *marginal* measure (change in cost).

### 4. Relationships Between the Cost Curves

The shapes of the cost curves and their relationships are crucial for understanding cost behavior.

*   **MC and AVC:**
    *   When MC < AVC, AVC is decreasing.
    *   When MC > AVC, AVC is increasing.
    *   MC intersects AVC at its minimum point.
*   **MC and ATC:**
    *   When MC < ATC, ATC is decreasing.
    *   When MC > ATC, ATC is increasing.
    *   MC intersects ATC at its minimum point.
*   **ATC and AVC:**
    *   ATC is always greater than AVC (by the amount of AFC).
    *   The gap between ATC and AVC decreases as output increases because AFC decreases.

**Explanation of the Relationships:**

The fact that MC intersects AVC and ATC at their minimum points is a mathematical consequence of the averaging process.  Think of your GPA: if your grade in a new course is *lower* than your current GPA, your GPA will decrease. If your grade is *higher* than your current GPA, your GPA will increase. The same principle applies to costs.

### 5. Application to Engineering Decision-Making

Short-run cost curves are essential for engineers when making decisions related to production levels, pricing, and resource allocation.  Here are some examples:

*   **Determining Optimal Production Level:** Engineers can use MC and MR (Marginal Revenue) to determine the profit-maximizing output level.  Profit is maximized where MC = MR.  (MR is not covered in this module but is essential for making these decisions in a profit-oriented business.)
*   **Evaluating the Profitability of Accepting an Order:** If a company has spare capacity, it might consider accepting a special order, even if the price is below the current ATC, as long as it's above the AVC.  This is because the company will still be covering its variable costs and contributing towards its fixed costs.  The key is to understand if you are better off covering AVC and some amount of TFC than not producing at all.
*   **Shutdown Decisions:** If a firm's revenue is consistently below its AVC, it should shut down production in the short run.  Continuing to produce would mean incurring losses on every unit produced.
*   **Process Improvement Decisions:** By analyzing cost curves, engineers can identify areas where costs can be reduced through process improvements, automation, or better resource utilization.  For example, understanding the shape of the MC curve can help determine the optimal level of input usage.

**Example:**

An engineering firm is contracted to produce 100 customized widgets.  Their TFC is $5,000. Their TVC for producing 100 widgets is $3,000. A new client offers to purchase an additional 20 widgets for $25 each.  The estimated MC of producing these additional 20 widgets is $20 per widget. Should the firm accept the order?

**Solution:**

*   Revenue from the additional order: 20 widgets * $25/widget = $500
*   Cost of the additional order: 20 widgets * $20/widget = $400
*   Profit from the additional order: $500 - $400 = $100

Since the profit from the additional order is positive, the firm should accept the order.  The firm is covering its marginal cost and generating additional profit, regardless of the relationship between the price ($25) and the average costs for producing 100 widgets.

### 6. Practice Questions/Exercises

1.  **Define the short run and explain the difference between fixed and variable costs. Provide three examples of each for an automobile manufacturing plant.**
    *   *Answer:* See Section 1. The short run is a period where at least one input is fixed. Fixed costs don't change with output (e.g., factory rent, insurance, loan payments). Variable costs do change with output (e.g., raw materials, hourly labor wages, energy consumption).

2.  **A company has TFC of $10,000. Their TVC for producing 100 units is $5,000, and their TVC for producing 101 units is $5,040. Calculate the AFC, AVC, ATC, and MC at a production level of 100 units and at a production level of 101 units.**
    *   *Answer:*
        *   **At 100 units:**
            *   AFC = $10,000 / 100 = $100
            *   AVC = $5,000 / 100 = $50
            *   ATC = ($10,000 + $5,000) / 100 = $150
            *   MC = Not applicable (need at least two production levels to calculate change)
        *   **At 101 units:**
            *   AFC = $10,000 / 101 = $99.01 (approx.)
            *   AVC = $5,040 / 101 = $49.90 (approx.)
            *   ATC = ($10,000 + $5,040) / 101 = $148.91 (approx.)
            *   MC = ($5,040 - $5,000) / (101 - 100) = $40

3.  **Explain the relationship between MC and AVC. What does it mean when MC is below AVC? What happens to AVC when MC equals AVC?**
    *   *Answer:* See Section 4. When MC < AVC, AVC is decreasing. When MC = AVC, AVC is at its minimum point.

4.  **A manufacturing company is considering a process improvement that would reduce its variable costs but require a one-time fixed cost investment. How should the company evaluate this investment decision in the short run?**
    *   *Answer:* In the short run, the key is to determine if the reduction in AVC (due to the process improvement) is large enough to offset the increased fixed costs.  The company should calculate the change in total costs at different production levels with and without the process improvement.  If the new process lowers total costs (TC), it is worth implementing.  Remember that the one-time fixed cost investment is "sunk" for the period the calculations are made, so will not affect the MC.

5.  **A company finds that at its current production level, its MC is $10 and its ATC is $12. Should the company increase or decrease production? Why?**
    *   *Answer:* The company should *increase* production. Since MC < ATC, ATC is decreasing. Increasing production will further lower ATC. However, keep in mind it is not this alone that determines the optimal production quantity - MR needs to be included.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   Understand the definitions and formulas for all cost measures.
*   Know the relationships between the cost curves (MC, AVC, ATC, AFC).
*   Remember that fixed costs are constant in the short run, and variable costs change with output.
*   MC is a crucial concept for decision-making, as it represents the cost of producing one more unit.
*   Apply the concepts of short-run cost curves to practical engineering problems.
*   Consider the limitations of short-run analysis.  In the long run, all costs are variable, and firms have more flexibility in adjusting their production processes.
*   These cost concepts are the foundation for more advanced topics in economics, such as cost-benefit analysis, pricing strategies, and market structure.
