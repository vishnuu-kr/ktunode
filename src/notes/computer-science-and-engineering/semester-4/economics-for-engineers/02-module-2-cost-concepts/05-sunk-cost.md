## Economics for Engineers: Module 2 - Cost Concepts: Sunk Cost

**Learning Outcomes:**

*   Define sunk cost and its characteristics.
*   Differentiate sunk cost from other cost concepts (e.g., opportunity cost, marginal cost).
*   Explain why sunk costs are irrelevant in decision-making.
*   Identify sunk costs in engineering projects and business scenarios.
*   Apply the sunk cost fallacy and its impact on decision quality.

---

### 1. Definition of Sunk Cost

*   **Definition:** A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of any future action.  It represents past expenditures that are irreversible.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   **Irrecoverable:** The funds spent are gone and cannot be recouped.
    *   **Past Expenditure:** The cost has already been paid.
    *   **Irrelevant to Future Decisions:**  It should not influence future choices about whether to continue a project, pursue a new one, or abandon an endeavor.  Focus on future costs and benefits.

### 2. Differentiation from Other Cost Concepts

*   **Sunk Cost vs. Opportunity Cost:**
    *   **Sunk Cost:** A cost already incurred and irrecoverable.
    *   **Opportunity Cost:** The potential benefit that is forfeited when choosing one alternative over another.  It represents the *next best* alternative use of resources.
    *   **Example:** You buy a machine for $10,000 (sunk cost).  You could lease it out for $2,000/year instead of using it in your production process.  The $2,000/year is the opportunity cost.
*   **Sunk Cost vs. Marginal Cost:**
    *   **Sunk Cost:** A cost already incurred and irrecoverable.
    *   **Marginal Cost:** The additional cost incurred by producing one more unit of a good or service. It is a future cost.
    *   **Example:** The $10,000 spent on the machine is the sunk cost. The electricity and materials needed to produce one more widget using that machine represent the marginal cost.
*   **Sunk Cost vs. Fixed Cost:**
    *   **Sunk Cost:** A cost already incurred and irrecoverable.
    *   **Fixed Cost:** A cost that does not vary with the level of production or activity (within a relevant range).  Fixed costs are *future* costs that are typically incurred periodically.
    *   **Example:** Rent is a fixed cost.  You might have already paid the first month's rent, in which case, it becomes a sunk cost.
*   **Key Difference:** Sunk costs are *past* costs, while opportunity costs, marginal costs, and fixed costs generally pertain to *future* decisions and their associated cost implications.

### 3. Irrelevance of Sunk Costs in Decision-Making

*   **Rational Decision-Making:** Economic theory dictates that rational decision-makers should ignore sunk costs when making future decisions. The focus should be on incremental costs and benefits moving forward.
*   **The Core Principle:** Decisions should be based on comparing the expected *future* costs and *future* benefits of different alternatives.  Past investments (sunk costs) cannot be changed and are therefore irrelevant to the choice.
*   **Example:** You invested $50,000 in developing a new product. After initial market research, you realize the product is unlikely to be profitable.  Ignoring sunk costs, you should only continue development if the *future* expected revenue exceeds the *future* costs of completion and marketing.  Throwing good money after bad to "recover" the $50,000 is irrational if future prospects are dim.

### 4. Identifying Sunk Costs in Engineering Projects and Business Scenarios

*   **Examples in Engineering Projects:**
    *   **Feasibility Studies:**  The cost of conducting a feasibility study for a project that is ultimately deemed unviable.
    *   **Abandoned Prototypes:**  The resources (labor, materials) spent on a prototype that is discarded during the design phase.
    *   **Obsolete Equipment:**  The purchase price of specialized equipment that becomes outdated before its useful life is over.
    *   **Permitting Costs:** The cost of applying for permits if the project is eventually rejected due to environmental concerns.
*   **Examples in Business Scenarios:**
    *   **Marketing Campaigns:**  Money spent on an unsuccessful advertising campaign.
    *   **Training Programs:**  The cost of training employees who subsequently leave the company.
    *   **Cancelled Projects:**  Expenses incurred in the initial stages of a project that is later cancelled.
    *   **Software Development:** The funds spent on developing a software feature that is ultimately removed due to lack of user adoption.

### 5. The Sunk Cost Fallacy

*   **Definition:** The sunk cost fallacy (also known as the Concorde fallacy) is the tendency to continue investing in a failing project or endeavor simply because significant resources have already been invested. This is an irrational behavioral pattern because it violates the principle that sunk costs are irrelevant.
*   **Why it Occurs:**
    *   **Loss Aversion:** People are generally more averse to losses than they are attracted to gains. Abandoning a project feels like admitting failure and incurring a loss, leading to further investment in the hope of turning things around.
    *   **Ego and Pride:**  Decision-makers may be reluctant to admit that their initial investment was a mistake.
    *   **Justification Bias:** People seek to justify their past decisions, even if those decisions are now demonstrably wrong.
*   **Impact on Decision Quality:** The sunk cost fallacy leads to:
    *   **Resource Misallocation:**  Resources are wasted on projects with low or negative returns, diverting them from more profitable opportunities.
    *   **Delayed Recognition of Failure:**  Problem projects are prolonged, delaying the implementation of corrective actions.
    *   **Increased Overall Losses:** Continued investment in a failing project can exacerbate the initial loss.
*   **Mitigation Strategies:**
    *   **Objective Evaluation:**  Focus on current and future costs and benefits, not on past investments.
    *   **External Perspective:**  Seek advice from independent experts who can provide an unbiased assessment.
    *   **Pre-Defined Exit Criteria:**  Establish clear milestones and triggers for abandoning a project if it fails to meet expectations.
    *   **Separate Decision-Making:**  Ensure that decisions about future investments are made by individuals who were not involved in the initial investment decision.

### Practice Questions/Exercises

1.  **Scenario:** An engineering firm has spent $200,000 on research and development for a new type of bridge cable.  They discover that a competitor has already patented a similar, superior cable.  To complete their cable development and bring it to market will cost another $100,000.  Market analysis suggests they can sell the cable for $150,000.  Should they continue with the project? Why or why not?
    *   **Answer:** No, they should not continue. The $200,000 is a sunk cost. The relevant analysis is whether the *future* revenue of $150,000 exceeds the *future* cost of $100,000.  Since it does, the project is financially viable, and *should* be continued.

2.  **Scenario:** A construction company is halfway through building a skyscraper.  They've already spent $50 million.  Due to unforeseen geological problems, completing the skyscraper will now cost an additional $75 million.  They can sell the completed skyscraper for $100 million.  Alternatively, they can abandon the project now and sell the partially completed structure for $20 million.  What should they do? Show your calculations.
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Option 1: Complete the skyscraper:** Revenue = $100 million, Total Costs = $50 million (sunk) + $75 million (future) = $125 million.  Profit/Loss = $100 million - $125 million = -$25 million.
        *   **Option 2: Abandon the project:** Revenue = $20 million, Total Costs = $50 million (sunk). Profit/Loss = $20 million - $50 million = -$30 million.
        *   **Analysis:** From a rational economic perspective, they should choose to complete the skyscraper, despite the overall loss.  The sunk cost of $50 million is irrelevant. The relevant comparison is between *future* costs and *future* benefits. If they abandon, the future revenue would be 20 million, the costs are 0, giving a profit of 20 million.  If they continue, the future revenue would be 100 million, the costs are 75 million, giving a profit of 25 million. Therefore they should continue.

3.  **What is the sunk cost fallacy, and how does it affect decision-making? Provide a real-world example (different from those listed above).**
    *   **Answer:** The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to continue investing in a failing project because of the significant resources already invested, despite the knowledge that further investment will not make the project successful. It leads to inefficient resource allocation and delayed recognition of failure.
    *   **Example:** A family buys season tickets to a local sports team for $1,000.  After attending several games, they realize the team is terrible, and they don't enjoy watching them. However, they continue to attend the games, saying, "We've already paid for the tickets, so we might as well use them." A more rational decision would be to consider what else they could do with their time and whether that would provide more enjoyment, ignoring the sunk cost of the tickets. They could instead spend their time reading, going to the movies, or pursuing hobbies that they find more rewarding, even though it means "wasting" the money spent on the tickets.

---

### Important Points to Remember

*   Sunk costs are irrelevant to future decisions.
*   Focus on incremental costs and benefits when making decisions.
*   Be aware of the sunk cost fallacy and its potential to distort your judgment.
*   Seek objective evaluations and establish clear exit criteria for projects.
*   Do not let past investments dictate your future actions.
