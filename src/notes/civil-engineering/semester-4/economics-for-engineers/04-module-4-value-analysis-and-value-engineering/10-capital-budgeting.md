## Topic: Capital Budgeting

---

### 1. Introduction to Capital Budgeting

**1.1 What is Capital Budgeting?**

*   **Definition:** Capital budgeting is the process by which a business plans for the acquisition of major assets, typically those with a lifespan of more than one year, such as machinery, equipment, buildings, and land. It involves evaluating and selecting projects or investments that will generate future revenues and cash flows.
*   **Importance for Engineers:** Engineers are often involved in proposing, designing, and implementing capital projects. Understanding capital budgeting allows them to justify project feasibility, select the most economically viable options, and ensure that investments align with the company's strategic goals.
*   **Key Characteristics of Capital Projects:**
    *   **Long-term commitment:** Investments made today have long-lasting effects.
    *   **Significant investment:** Typically involve large sums of money.
    *   **Irreversible decisions:** Once capital is invested, it's difficult and costly to reverse.
    *   **Uncertainty:** Future revenues and costs are not guaranteed.

**1.2 The Capital Budgeting Process**

The capital budgeting process generally involves the following steps:

1.  **Identification of Investment Opportunities:** Brainstorming and identifying potential projects that could enhance the company's profitability or operational efficiency.
2.  **Forecasting Cash Flows:** Estimating the expected cash inflows and outflows associated with each project over its economic life.
3.  **Evaluating Investment Alternatives:** Using various financial techniques to assess the attractiveness of each project.
4.  **Selecting Projects:** Choosing the projects that best meet the company's financial and strategic objectives.
5.  **Implementing and Monitoring the Projects:** Executing the selected projects and tracking their performance against expectations.

### 2. Key Concepts in Capital Budgeting

**2.1 Relevant Cash Flows**

*   **Definition:** The cash flows that are expected to occur if and only if the project is undertaken. This includes both incremental cash inflows (revenues, cost savings) and incremental cash outflows (initial investment, operating costs, maintenance).
*   **Incremental Cash Flows:** Focus on the *additional* cash flows generated by the project, not sunk costs (costs already incurred and unrecoverable) or allocated overhead costs that will be incurred regardless of the project.
*   **Examples of Relevant Cash Flows:**
    *   **Initial Investment:** Cost of new equipment, installation costs, changes in working capital.
    *   **Operating Cash Flows:** Incremental revenues, cost savings from new technology, operating and maintenance expenses.
    *   **Terminal Cash Flows:** Salvage value of the asset, recovery of working capital, tax implications of asset disposal.

**2.2 Time Value of Money (TVM)**

*   **Definition:** The principle that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future due to its potential earning capacity.
*   **Why it's Crucial:** Capital projects involve cash flows spread over many years. TVM helps to bring all future cash flows back to their present value for a fair comparison.
*   **Key TVM Components:**
    *   **Present Value (PV):** The current worth of a future sum of money or stream of cash flows given a specified rate of return.
    *   **Future Value (FV):** The value of a current asset at a future date based on an assumed rate of growth.
    *   **Interest Rate (r) or Discount Rate:** The rate of return used to discount future cash flows to their present value. This often represents the company's cost of capital or required rate of return.
    *   **Number of Periods (n):** The length of time over which the cash flows occur.

**2.3 Discount Rate (Cost of Capital)**

*   **Definition:** The minimum rate of return a company expects to earn on an investment to justify undertaking it. It reflects the riskiness of the investment and the company's cost of financing.
*   **Sources of Capital:** Debt, equity, preferred stock.
*   **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC):** The average rate of return a company expects to pay to its security holders to finance its assets. It's a common discount rate used in capital budgeting.
*   **Importance:** Using an appropriate discount rate is vital for accurately assessing the profitability of long-term projects.

### 3. Capital Budgeting Techniques

These techniques are used to evaluate and compare different investment projects.

**3.1 Net Present Value (NPV)**

*   **Definition:** The difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a period of time.
*   **Formula:**
    $NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$
    Where:
    *   $CF_t$ = Net cash flow during period t
    *   $r$ = Discount rate
    *   $n$ = Total number of periods
    *   $CF_0$ is usually the initial investment (negative value).
*   **Decision Rule:**
    *   **NPV > 0:** Accept the project (expected to generate more value than its cost).
    *   **NPV < 0:** Reject the project (expected to destroy value).
    *   **NPV = 0:** Indifferent (project breaks even).
*   **Advantages:** Considers the time value of money, accounts for all cash flows, provides a direct measure of the increase in shareholder wealth.
*   **Disadvantages:** Requires an accurate discount rate, can be complex to calculate manually.

**Example:** A company is considering a project that requires an initial investment of $10,000 and is expected to generate cash flows of $3,000 in year 1, $4,000 in year 2, and $5,000 in year 3. The company's discount rate is 10%.

*   Year 0: -$10,000
*   Year 1: $3,000 / (1.10)^1 = $2,727.27
*   Year 2: $4,000 / (1.10)^2 = $3,305.79
*   Year 3: $5,000 / (1.10)^3 = $3,756.57

$NPV = -10,000 + 2,727.27 + 3,305.79 + 3,756.57 = \$ -210.37$

Since the NPV is negative, the project should be rejected.

**3.2 Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**

*   **Definition:** The discount rate at which the Net Present Value (NPV) of all the cash flows from a particular project equals zero. It represents the effective rate of return generated by the investment.
*   **Formula:** Solve for 'r' in the equation:
    $\sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t} = 0$
*   **Decision Rule:**
    *   **IRR > Discount Rate (Cost of Capital):** Accept the project.
    *   **IRR < Discount Rate:** Reject the project.
*   **Advantages:** Considers the time value of money, intuitively appealing as it represents a rate of return.
*   **Disadvantages:** Can be difficult to calculate manually (often requires financial calculators or software), may yield multiple IRRs or no IRR for projects with non-conventional cash flows (cash flows that change sign more than once), can lead to incorrect decisions when comparing mutually exclusive projects of different scales.

**Example:** Using the same project as above, we need to find the rate 'r' that makes NPV = 0. This often requires trial-and-error or financial software. If we found that at 9%, the NPV was positive ($263.08) and at 10%, it was negative (-$210.37), the IRR would be somewhere between 9% and 10%.

**3.3 Payback Period**

*   **Definition:** The length of time required for an investment's cumulative cash inflows to equal its initial cost.
*   **Calculation:**
    *   For projects with even cash flows: Initial Investment / Annual Cash Flow
    *   For projects with uneven cash flows: Sum cash flows until the initial investment is recovered. If recovery occurs mid-year, calculate the fraction of the year.
*   **Decision Rule:** Accept projects with a payback period shorter than a predetermined cutoff period.
*   **Advantages:** Simple to understand and calculate, focuses on liquidity and risk (shorter payback is generally less risky).
*   **Disadvantages:** Ignores the time value of money, ignores cash flows beyond the payback period, doesn't measure profitability.

**Example:** A project costs $10,000 and generates cash flows of $3,000, $4,000, and $5,000 in years 1, 2, and 3 respectively.
*   Year 1: $3,000 (Remaining: $10,000 - $3,000 = $7,000)
*   Year 2: $4,000 (Remaining: $7,000 - $4,000 = $3,000)
*   Year 3: $3,000 of the $5,000 cash flow is needed to recover the remaining $3,000.
*   Payback Period = 2 years + ($3,000 / $5,000) = 2.6 years.

**3.4 Discounted Payback Period**

*   **Definition:** Similar to the payback period, but it uses discounted cash flows to determine when the initial investment is recovered.
*   **Calculation:** Sum the discounted cash flows until they equal the initial investment.
*   **Advantages:** Considers the time value of money, focuses on liquidity and risk.
*   **Disadvantages:** Ignores cash flows beyond the discounted payback period, doesn't directly measure profitability.

**Example:** Using the discounted cash flows from the NPV example (Year 1: $2,727.27, Year 2: $3,305.79, Year 3: $3,756.57), and an initial investment of $10,000.
*   End of Year 1: $2,727.27 (Remaining: $10,000 - $2,727.27 = $7,272.73)
*   End of Year 2: $3,305.79 (Remaining: $7,272.73 - $3,305.79 = $3,966.94)
*   Year 3: $3,756.57 (This is more than the remaining $3,966.94)
*   Discounted Payback Period = 2 years + ($3,966.94 / $3,756.57) = 2 + 1.056 = 3.06 years.

**3.5 Profitability Index (PI)**

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the present value of future cash inflows to the initial investment.
*   **Formula:**
    $PI = \frac{\text{Present Value of Future Cash Flows}}{\text{Initial Investment}}$
    or
    $PI = \frac{NPV + Initial Investment}{Initial Investment}$
*   **Decision Rule:**
    *   **PI > 1:** Accept the project.
    *   **PI < 1:** Reject the project.
    *   **PI = 1:** Indifferent.
*   **Advantages:** Useful for ranking projects when capital is rationed (limited). A higher PI indicates more value generated per dollar invested.
*   **Disadvantages:** Can be misleading when comparing mutually exclusive projects of different scales.

**Example:** For the project with an NPV of -$210.37 and an initial investment of $10,000.
The Present Value of Future Cash Flows = $2,727.27 + $3,305.79 + $3,756.57 = $9,789.63
$PI = $9,789.63 / $10,000 = 0.979$

Since PI < 1, the project should be rejected.

### 4. Project Selection and Considerations

**4.1 Mutually Exclusive vs. Independent Projects**

*   **Independent Projects:** Projects whose acceptance or rejection does not affect the acceptance or rejection of other projects. Multiple independent projects can be accepted if they meet the company's criteria.
*   **Mutually Exclusive Projects:** Projects where the acceptance of one project precludes the acceptance of another. This often happens when projects use the same resources or serve the same purpose. In such cases, selection is based on the project that provides the highest NPV or PI (for investment decisions).

**4.2 Capital Rationing**

*   **Definition:** A situation where a company has more acceptable investment opportunities than it has funds available to invest.
*   **Approach:** When capital is rationed, companies often rank projects based on their Profitability Index (PI) and invest in the projects with the highest PIs until the capital budget is exhausted.

**4.3 Qualitative Factors in Capital Budgeting**

While financial metrics are crucial, engineers and managers must also consider non-financial aspects:

*   **Strategic Alignment:** Does the project fit with the company's long-term vision and goals?
*   **Technological Advancement:** Does it incorporate new or improved technology?
*   **Risk Assessment:** Beyond financial risk, consider operational, environmental, or regulatory risks.
*   **Market Conditions:** Current and future market demand for the product or service.
*   **Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) Impact:** Increasingly important for sustainability and corporate responsibility.
*   **Employee Morale and Safety:** Improvements in working conditions or new training opportunities.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A company is considering purchasing new machinery for $50,000. The machinery is expected to generate additional annual cash flows of $15,000 for 5 years. The company's required rate of return is 12%.

a) Calculate the Net Present Value (NPV) of this investment.
b) Calculate the Internal Rate of Return (IRR).
c) Calculate the Payback Period.
d) Calculate the Profitability Index (PI).
e) Based on your calculations, should the company invest in this machinery?

**Answer 1:**

**a) NPV Calculation:**
*   Initial Investment = -$50,000
*   Discounted Cash Flows:
    *   Year 1: $15,000 / (1.12)^1 = $13,392.86
    *   Year 2: $15,000 / (1.12)^2 = $11,958.00
    *   Year 3: $15,000 / (1.12)^3 = $10,676.79
    *   Year 4: $15,000 / (1.12)^4 = $9,532.85
    *   Year 5: $15,000 / (1.12)^5 = $8,511.47
*   Total PV of Cash Flows = $13,392.86 + $11,958.00 + $10,676.79 + $9,532.85 + $8,511.47 = $54,071.97
*   $NPV = $54,071.97 - $50,000 = $4,071.97$

**b) IRR Calculation:**
This requires trial and error or a financial calculator.
Let's test a rate slightly higher than 12%. At 15%:
PV of Cash Flows = $15,000 * [1 - (1.15)^-5] / 0.15 = $53,300.40
NPV at 15% = $53,300.40 - $50,000 = $3,300.40
Let's test a rate slightly higher. At 17%:
PV of Cash Flows = $15,000 * [1 - (1.17)^-5] / 0.17 = $51,035.01
NPV at 17% = $51,035.01 - $50,000 = $1,035.01
Let's test a rate higher. At 19%:
PV of Cash Flows = $15,000 * [1 - (1.19)^-5] / 0.19 = $48,843.83
NPV at 19% = $48,843.83 - $50,000 = -$1,156.17
The IRR is between 17% and 19%. Using a financial calculator, the IRR is approximately **17.76%**.

**c) Payback Period Calculation:**
*   Initial Investment = $50,000
*   Annual Cash Flow = $15,000
*   Payback Period = $50,000 / $15,000 = **3.33 years**

**d) Profitability Index (PI) Calculation:**
*   $PI = $54,071.97 / $50,000 = **1.0814**

**e) Investment Decision:**
*   **NPV:** $4,071.97 > 0, so accept.
*   **IRR:** 17.76% > 12% (discount rate), so accept.
*   **Payback Period:** 3.33 years. If the company's cutoff period is less than 3.33 years, it might be rejected on this basis, but financial metrics are stronger.
*   **PI:** 1.0814 > 1, so accept.

**Overall, based on NPV, IRR, and PI, the company should invest in this machinery.**

**Question 2:**
A project has an initial investment of $100,000 and expected cash flows as follows:
*   Year 1: $30,000
*   Year 2: $40,000
*   Year 3: $50,000
*   Year 4: $60,000

The company's discount rate is 10%.

a) Calculate the NPV.
b) Calculate the Payback Period.
c) Calculate the Discounted Payback Period.

**Answer 2:**

**a) NPV Calculation:**
*   Initial Investment = -$100,000
*   Discounted Cash Flows:
    *   Year 1: $30,000 / (1.10)^1 = $27,272.73
    *   Year 2: $40,000 / (1.10)^2 = $33,057.85
    *   Year 3: $50,000 / (1.10)^3 = $37,565.73
    *   Year 4: $60,000 / (1.10)^4 = $40,917.64
*   Total PV of Cash Flows = $27,272.73 + $33,057.85 + $37,565.73 + $40,917.64 = $138,813.95
*   $NPV = $138,813.95 - $100,000 = $38,813.95$

**b) Payback Period Calculation:**
*   Year 1: $30,000 (Remaining: $100,000 - $30,000 = $70,000)
*   Year 2: $40,000 (Remaining: $70,000 - $40,000 = $30,000)
*   Year 3: $30,000 of the $50,000 cash flow is needed.
*   Payback Period = 2 years + ($30,000 / $50,000) = 2 + 0.6 = **2.6 years**

**c) Discounted Payback Period Calculation:**
*   Year 1 Discounted CF: $27,272.73 (Remaining: $100,000 - $27,272.73 = $72,727.27)
*   Year 2 Discounted CF: $33,057.85 (Remaining: $72,727.27 - $33,057.85 = $39,669.42)
*   Year 3 Discounted CF: $37,565.73 (This is less than the remaining $39,669.42)
*   Discounted Payback Period = 2 years + ($39,669.42 / $37,565.73) = 2 + 1.056 = **3.06 years**

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Focus on Incremental Cash Flows:** Only consider cash flows that are directly attributable to the project. Ignore sunk costs and irrelevant overhead.
*   **Time Value of Money is Key:** Always discount future cash flows to their present value using an appropriate discount rate.
*   **NPV is the Preferred Method:** For most situations, NPV is considered the most reliable method for evaluating projects as it directly measures the increase in shareholder wealth.
*   **Understand the Limitations of Other Methods:** Payback period is simple but ignores TVM and later cash flows. IRR can be problematic with non-conventional cash flows and for comparing mutually exclusive projects.
*   **Consider Qualitative Factors:** Financial analysis is important, but don't overlook strategic fit, risk, and other non-financial aspects.
*   **Consistency in Discount Rate:** Use the company's cost of capital or appropriate risk-adjusted rate consistently across all projects.
