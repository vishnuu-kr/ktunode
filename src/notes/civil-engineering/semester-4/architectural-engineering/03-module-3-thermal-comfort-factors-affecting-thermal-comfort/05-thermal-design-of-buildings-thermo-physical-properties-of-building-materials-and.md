# Architectural Engineering: Module 3 - Thermal Comfort

## Topic: Thermal Design of Buildings: Thermo-physical Properties of Building Materials and Thermal Control

### Learning Outcomes:

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Understand the fundamental thermo-physical properties of building materials relevant to thermal comfort.
*   Explain how these properties influence heat transfer within and through building elements.
*   Identify and describe various methods of thermal control in buildings.
*   Relate thermal control strategies to achieving desired indoor thermal comfort levels.
*   Analyze the role of material selection and thermal control in energy-efficient building design.

---

### 1. Introduction to Thermal Design of Buildings

**Definition:** Thermal design of buildings is the process of designing buildings to maintain comfortable and healthy indoor environments while minimizing energy consumption for heating, cooling, and lighting. It involves understanding how heat interacts with the building envelope and interior spaces.

**Key Concept:** The building envelope (walls, roof, floor, windows, doors) is the primary barrier between the indoor and outdoor environments. Its thermal performance is crucial for thermal comfort and energy efficiency.

---

### 2. Thermo-physical Properties of Building Materials

These properties dictate how materials interact with heat and are fundamental to understanding heat transfer.

#### 2.1 Thermal Conductivity (k)

*   **Definition:** A measure of a material's ability to conduct heat. Higher thermal conductivity means heat flows more easily through the material.
*   **Units:** Watts per meter-Kelvin (W/m·K) or British thermal units per hour per square foot per degree Fahrenheit per foot (Btu/hr·ft·°F).
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:**
    *   Materials with low conductivity (insulators) resist heat flow, keeping interiors warmer in winter and cooler in summer.
    *   Materials with high conductivity (conductors) allow heat to pass through readily, leading to discomfort and energy loss.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Low Conductivity (Good Insulators):** Mineral wool, fiberglass, foam insulation, wood.
    *   **High Conductivity (Good Conductors):** Metals (aluminum, steel), concrete, glass.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Low thermal conductivity is desirable for insulation materials in building envelopes.

#### 2.2 Thermal Resistivity (R)

*   **Definition:** The reciprocal of thermal conductivity (R = 1/k). It measures a material's resistance to heat flow per unit thickness.
*   **Units:** Kelvin-meters per Watt (K·m/W) or hr·ft²·°F/Btu.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** Higher thermal resistivity indicates better insulation.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Resistivity is a material property, while *resistance* (R-value) is a property of a specific building element (e.g., a wall with multiple layers).

#### 2.3 Thermal Resistance (R-value)

*   **Definition:** The ability of a building component (e.g., a wall, roof assembly) to resist heat flow. It's the sum of the thermal resistivities of all materials within the assembly, plus the resistance of air films on the surfaces.
*   **Formula:** $R_{total} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + ... + R_{air}$
*   **Units:** Same as thermal resistivity (K·m/W or hr·ft²·°F/Btu).
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** A higher R-value for a building element means less heat transfer, leading to better thermal comfort and energy efficiency.
*   **Example:** A wall with concrete, insulation, and drywall will have a total R-value that is the sum of the R-values of each layer.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** The R-value is crucial for comparing the thermal performance of different building assemblies.

#### 2.4 Thermal Transmittance (U-value)

*   **Definition:** The rate of heat transfer through a building element per unit area and per degree of temperature difference. It is the reciprocal of the total thermal resistance (U = 1/R_total).
*   **Units:** Watts per square meter-Kelvin (W/m²·K) or Btu/hr·ft²·°F.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** A lower U-value indicates better thermal performance (less heat transfer).
*   **Example:** A well-insulated window will have a lower U-value than a single-pane window.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** The U-value is a common metric used in building codes to specify the thermal performance requirements for building envelopes.

#### 2.5 Specific Heat Capacity (c)

*   **Definition:** The amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of one unit mass of a substance by one degree Celsius (or Kelvin).
*   **Units:** Joules per kilogram-Kelvin (J/kg·K) or Btu/lb·°F.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:**
    *   Materials with high specific heat capacity can store a significant amount of heat energy. This leads to thermal lag, where the material takes longer to heat up and cool down.
    *   This property is important for **thermal mass**.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **High Specific Heat:** Water, concrete, brick.
    *   **Low Specific Heat:** Air, wood, foam insulation.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** High specific heat capacity contributes to thermal mass, which can help stabilize indoor temperatures.

#### 2.6 Density (ρ)

*   **Definition:** The mass of a substance per unit volume.
*   **Units:** Kilograms per cubic meter (kg/m³) or pounds per cubic foot (lb/ft³).
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** Density influences both thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity. Denser materials often have higher thermal conductivity and higher heat capacity (though not always directly proportional).
*   **Examples:** Steel is much denser than wood.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Density plays a role in the overall thermal behavior of materials.

#### 2.7 Thermal Diffusivity (α)

*   **Definition:** A measure of how quickly a material's temperature changes when exposed to a change in temperature of its surroundings. It is related to thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity, and density.
*   **Formula:** $α = k / (ρ \cdot c)$
*   **Units:** Square meters per second (m²/s) or square feet per hour (ft²/hr).
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:**
    *   Materials with low thermal diffusivity will respond more slowly to temperature changes, contributing to thermal lag.
    *   This is important for understanding diurnal (daily) temperature swings.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Low thermal diffusivity indicates a material's ability to dampen temperature fluctuations.

#### 2.8 Thermal Emissivity (ε)

*   **Definition:** A measure of a material's ability to emit thermal radiation. It ranges from 0 (perfect reflector) to 1 (perfect emitter/blackbody).
*   **Units:** Dimensionless.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:**
    *   **Radiant Heat Exchange:** Surfaces emit and absorb radiant heat. High emissivity surfaces emit and absorb radiation more effectively.
    *   Affects the perceived temperature of surfaces and the overall heat balance within a space.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **High Emissivity:** Dark, matte surfaces (e.g., black paint, asphalt).
    *   **Low Emissivity:** Shiny, reflective surfaces (e.g., polished aluminum, gold foil).
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Low-emissivity coatings on windows can reduce radiant heat transfer.

#### 2.9 Absorptivity (α)

*   **Definition:** The fraction of incident radiant energy that is absorbed by a surface.
*   **Units:** Dimensionless.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** Influences how much solar heat is absorbed by a building surface.
*   **Relationship with Emissivity (Kirchhoff's Law of Thermal Radiation):** For an opaque surface in thermal equilibrium, absorptivity equals emissivity ($α = ε$).
*   **Important Point to Remember:** High absorptivity leads to higher surface temperatures when exposed to radiation.

### 3. Thermal Control Strategies

Thermal control aims to manage heat flow to maintain comfortable indoor conditions.

#### 3.1 Building Envelope Design

*   **Insulation:**
    *   **Purpose:** To reduce heat transfer by conduction.
    *   **Methods:** Adding materials with low thermal conductivity (e.g., fiberglass, mineral wool, rigid foam, spray foam) to walls, roofs, and floors.
    *   **Impact:** Increases R-values, reduces U-values, leading to less heat loss in winter and less heat gain in summer.
*   **Air Sealing:**
    *   **Purpose:** To prevent uncontrolled air leakage (infiltration and exfiltration).
    *   **Methods:** Sealing gaps and cracks in the building envelope using tapes, sealants, membranes, and proper detailing around windows, doors, and penetrations.
    *   **Impact:** Reduces heat loss/gain due to convection and the loss of conditioned air. Improves indoor air quality management.
*   **Vapor Control:**
    *   **Purpose:** To control the movement of water vapor through the building envelope, preventing condensation within building materials.
    *   **Methods:** Using vapor barriers (e.g., polyethylene sheeting) on the warm side of the insulation in cold climates, and vapor retarders.
    *   **Impact:** Prevents moisture damage, mold growth, and degradation of insulation performance.
*   **Thermal Break:**
    *   **Purpose:** To interrupt continuous paths for heat flow through conductive materials in the building envelope.
    *   **Methods:** Using materials with low thermal conductivity (e.g., plastic, wood) between conductive materials like metal framing in windows and curtain walls.
    *   **Impact:** Reduces heat transfer through highly conductive elements.

#### 3.2 Window Design

*   **Double and Triple Glazing:**
    *   **Purpose:** To reduce heat transfer by conduction and convection compared to single glazing.
    *   **Methods:** Using multiple panes of glass separated by sealed air or gas-filled cavities.
    *   **Impact:** Significantly lower U-values and better thermal performance.
*   **Low-Emissivity (Low-E) Coatings:**
    *   **Purpose:** To reduce radiant heat transfer.
    *   **Methods:** Applying thin, transparent metallic coatings to the glass surface. These coatings reflect long-wave infrared radiation (heat).
    *   **Impact:** Low-E coatings on the inner surface reduce heat loss from the interior in winter, while coatings on the outer surface can reflect solar heat gain in summer.
*   **Gas Fills (Argon, Krypton):**
    *   **Purpose:** To further reduce heat transfer through the gap between glass panes.
    *   **Methods:** Replacing air with inert gases like Argon or Krypton, which have lower thermal conductivity than air.
    *   **Impact:** Lowers the U-value.
*   **Window Frame Materials:**
    *   **Purpose:** To minimize heat transfer through the window frame.
    *   **Methods:** Using materials with lower conductivity like vinyl, wood, or fiberglass, or incorporating thermal breaks in metal frames.
    *   **Impact:** Improves the overall thermal performance of the window unit.

#### 3.3 Solar Control

*   **Shading Devices:**
    *   **Purpose:** To reduce solar heat gain through windows.
    *   **Methods:**
        *   **External Shading:** Overhangs, awnings, louvers, shutters, fins.
        *   **Internal Shading:** Blinds, curtains, shades.
    *   **Impact:** Reduces cooling loads in summer. External shading is generally more effective as it stops solar radiation before it enters the building.
*   **Solar Reflectance (Albedo):**
    *   **Purpose:** To reflect solar radiation away from the building surface.
    *   **Methods:** Using light-colored or reflective materials on roofs and walls.
    *   **Impact:** Reduces solar heat gain, particularly important for cooling-dominated climates (e.g., "cool roofs").

#### 3.4 Thermal Mass

*   **Definition:** The ability of a material to store heat energy. Materials with high density and high specific heat capacity contribute to thermal mass.
*   **Purpose:** To absorb and store heat during the day (from the sun or internal gains) and release it at night, moderating indoor temperature fluctuations.
*   **Methods:** Using heavy materials like concrete, brick, stone, or even water walls in the interior of the building envelope, typically exposed to indoor air.
*   **Impact:** Can reduce peak heating and cooling loads and improve comfort by stabilizing temperatures. It is most effective when coupled with night ventilation or controlled solar gain.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Thermal mass is most effective in climates with significant diurnal temperature swings and when coupled with appropriate control strategies.

#### 3.5 Ventilation and Air Movement

*   **Natural Ventilation:**
    *   **Purpose:** To use natural air currents (wind-driven and buoyancy-driven) for cooling and fresh air supply.
    *   **Methods:** Operable windows, vents, chimneys, stack effect.
    *   **Impact:** Can provide cooling and improve indoor air quality without mechanical systems.
*   **Mechanical Ventilation:**
    *   **Purpose:** To provide controlled fresh air supply and exhaust stale air.
    *   **Methods:** Fans, air handling units (AHUs), heat recovery ventilators (HRVs), energy recovery ventilators (ERVs).
    *   **Impact:** Ensures adequate indoor air quality, can be used for cooling (e.g., night ventilation), and HRVs/ERVs can pre-condition incoming fresh air.
*   **Air Movement (Fans):**
    *   **Purpose:** To improve perceived comfort by increasing air velocity over the skin, enhancing evaporative cooling.
    *   **Methods:** Ceiling fans, portable fans.
    *   **Impact:** Can allow for higher thermostat setpoints in cooling mode, saving energy, as the increased air speed makes occupants feel cooler.

#### 3.6 Passive Solar Design

*   **Definition:** Designing buildings to utilize the sun's energy for heating and lighting without the use of active mechanical systems.
*   **Key Elements:**
    *   **Orientation:** Placing large windows on the equator-facing side (south in the Northern Hemisphere, north in the Southern Hemisphere) to capture winter sun.
    *   **Glazing:** Using appropriate window sizes and types.
    *   **Thermal Mass:** Incorporating materials to store solar heat.
    *   **Shading:** Controlling summer sun.
*   **Impact:** Reduces reliance on artificial heating and lighting.

#### 3.7 Active Solar Systems

*   **Definition:** Systems that use mechanical or electrical equipment to collect, store, and distribute solar energy.
*   **Examples:** Solar water heaters, solar photovoltaic panels.
*   **Impact:** While not directly related to passive thermal comfort, they contribute to overall energy efficiency and can provide heated water or electricity.

### 4. Integrating Thermo-physical Properties and Thermal Control

*   **Material Selection:** Choosing materials with appropriate thermo-physical properties is the foundation of good thermal design. For instance, selecting insulation with low 'k' and high 'R-value' for walls.
*   **Building Envelope Performance:** The combination of material properties and assembly design (e.g., multiple layers, thermal breaks) determines the overall R-value and U-value of the envelope.
*   **Climate Responsiveness:** Thermal control strategies must be adapted to the local climate.
    *   **Cold Climates:** Focus on high insulation (high R-values), air tightness, and minimizing heat loss. Thermal mass can be beneficial for storing solar heat in winter.
    *   **Hot Climates:** Focus on reducing solar heat gain (shading, reflective surfaces), promoting ventilation, and using lightweight construction or well-insulated envelopes to minimize heat absorption. Thermal mass can be detrimental if not managed with night cooling.
    *   **Mixed Climates:** Requires a balance of strategies to address both heating and cooling needs.
*   **Occupant Behavior:** Understanding how occupants interact with the building (e.g., opening/closing windows, using blinds) is important for effective thermal control.

### 5. Practice Questions/Exercises

**Question 1:** A wall assembly consists of a 100mm brick layer ($R = 0.08$ K·m/W), a 10mm air gap ($R = 0.16$ K·m/W), and a 50mm fiberglass insulation layer ($R = 1.75$ K·m/W). If the inside and outside air film resistances are $R_{in} = 0.13$ K·m/W and $R_{out} = 0.04$ K·m/W respectively, calculate the total R-value of the wall assembly.

**Answer 1:**
$R_{total} = R_{brick} + R_{air\_gap} + R_{insulation} + R_{in} + R_{out}$
$R_{total} = 0.08 + 0.16 + 1.75 + 0.13 + 0.04 = 2.16$ K·m/W

**Question 2:** What is the U-value for the wall assembly calculated in Question 1?

**Answer 2:**
$U = 1 / R_{total}$
$U = 1 / 2.16$ K·m/W
$U \approx 0.46$ W/m²·K

**Question 3:** Explain the primary difference between thermal conductivity and thermal resistance.

**Answer 3:** Thermal conductivity (k) is a property of the *material* itself, indicating how easily heat flows through it per unit thickness and temperature gradient. Thermal resistance (R-value) is a property of a *building component* or assembly, representing its total opposition to heat flow across its entire thickness. R-value is calculated by summing the thermal resistivities (1/k) of all layers and surface resistances.

**Question 4:** Name two types of thermal control strategies related to windows and briefly explain their function.

**Answer 4:**
1.  **Double Glazing:** Uses two panes of glass separated by an air or gas-filled cavity to reduce heat transfer by conduction and convection, lowering the U-value.
2.  **Low-Emissivity (Low-E) Coatings:** Applied to glass surfaces, these coatings reduce radiant heat transfer by reflecting infrared radiation, thus keeping heat inside in winter and outside in summer.

**Question 5:** How does thermal mass contribute to thermal comfort, and in which type of climate is it most effective?

**Answer 5:** Thermal mass contributes to thermal comfort by storing heat energy. During the day, it can absorb excess heat from solar radiation or internal gains, preventing indoor temperatures from rising too high. At night, it can release this stored heat, moderating temperature drops. Thermal mass is most effective in climates with significant diurnal temperature swings (large differences between day and night temperatures), such as desert climates or regions with clear skies and dry air, where it can effectively buffer temperature fluctuations.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Low 'k' and High 'R' are good for insulation.**
*   **High 'U' means poor thermal performance.**
*   **Thermal mass (high specific heat, density) moderates temperature swings.**
*   **Low emissivity surfaces reflect radiant heat.**
*   **Air sealing is critical to prevent uncontrolled heat transfer via convection.**
*   **External shading is generally more effective than internal shading for reducing solar heat gain.**
*   **Climate dictates the most effective thermal control strategies.**
*   **Understanding material properties is essential for designing energy-efficient and comfortable buildings.**
