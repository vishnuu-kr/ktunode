# Architectural Engineering: Module 4 - Architecture Design: Basic Anthropometrics

## Topic: Perspective Views of Form: 2-Point Perspective and 3-Point Perspective

### 1. Learning Outcomes

This module aims to equip you with the fundamental understanding of how to represent three-dimensional forms in a two-dimensional plane, specifically focusing on:

*   **Understanding the principles of perspective drawing.**
*   **Identifying and distinguishing between 2-point and 3-point perspective.**
*   **Recognizing the role of vanishing points and horizon lines in creating realistic depth.**
*   **Applying basic 2-point and 3-point perspective techniques to represent architectural forms.**
*   **Understanding how perspective drawing relates to representing human scale and experience within architectural spaces.**

### 2. Introduction to Perspective Drawing

Perspective drawing is a graphical technique used to represent three-dimensional objects and spaces on a two-dimensional surface (like paper or a screen) in a way that mimics how the human eye perceives them. It creates the illusion of depth, distance, and volume.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Illusion of Depth:** The primary goal of perspective drawing is to create a convincing sense of depth.
*   **Realistic Representation:** It aims to depict objects as they would appear in reality, accounting for how lines converge and forms diminish in size as they recede into the distance.
*   **Viewer's Position:** The way an object is drawn in perspective depends entirely on the viewer's (or artist's) position relative to the object.

### 3. The Picture Plane

*   **Definition:** The picture plane is an imaginary transparent surface that the artist "looks through" to see the object. In drawings, it's essentially the surface of the paper or screen.
*   **Purpose:** All lines and points of the object are projected onto this plane to create the illusion of depth.

### 4. The Horizon Line (Eye Level)

*   **Definition:** The horizon line represents the viewer's eye level. It is a horizontal line across the drawing.
*   **Significance:**
    *   Objects *above* the horizon line will appear to be viewed from below.
    *   Objects *below* the horizon line will appear to be viewed from above.
    *   Objects *at* the horizon line will appear at eye level.

### 5. Vanishing Points (VPs)

*   **Definition:** Vanishing points are points on the horizon line where parallel lines that are not parallel to the picture plane appear to converge.
*   **Purpose:** They are crucial for creating the illusion of depth and accurately representing converging parallel lines.
*   **Key Principle:** Parallel lines will converge to a single vanishing point.

### 6. Two-Point Perspective

Two-point perspective is used when the viewer is looking at an object, such as a building, at an angle, so that two of its principal faces are visible and neither face is directly facing the viewer or parallel to the picture plane.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Two Vanishing Points:** This system utilizes two vanishing points, located on the horizon line.
*   **No Vertical Lines Converge:** Vertical lines remain vertical and parallel to each other, parallel to the edge of the picture plane.
*   **Angle of View:** Typically used when the viewer is positioned to see two sides of a rectangular object (e.g., a cube or a building corner).

**How it Works:**

1.  **Establish the Horizon Line (HL):** Draw a horizontal line across your drawing surface. This represents the viewer's eye level.
2.  **Place Vanishing Points (VP1, VP2):** Mark two vanishing points on the horizon line, spaced apart. The distance between them affects the perceived width of the object and the angle of view.
3.  **Draw the Vertical Edge:** Draw a single vertical line on your picture plane. This represents the closest vertical edge of the object. The height of this line determines the apparent height of the object.
4.  **Connect Vertical Edge to VPs:** Draw lines from the top and bottom of the vertical edge to both VP1 and VP2. These lines represent the receding parallel edges of the object.
5.  **Define Depth:** Draw vertical lines connecting the "receding" lines from VP1 and VP2 to define the other vertical edges of the object. The placement of these lines determines the depth and width of the object.
6.  **Complete the Form:** Draw horizontal lines connecting the points on the receding lines to complete the top and side faces of the object. These horizontal lines are parallel to the horizon line.

**Example:** Imagine standing on a street corner and looking at a building. You see two facades (front and side) of the building, and neither is directly facing you. The building's vertical edges (like corners) are parallel to each other and to the picture plane, while its horizontal edges recede to vanishing points on your left and right.

**Visualisation:** Think of looking down a long, straight road. The edges of the road, which are parallel in reality, appear to converge at a point far in the distance (a vanishing point).

### 7. Three-Point Perspective

Three-point perspective is used when the viewer is looking at an object from a high or low angle, causing the vertical lines to appear to converge as well. This creates a more dramatic or imposing effect.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Three Vanishing Points:** This system utilizes three vanishing points. Two are on the horizon line (like in 2-point perspective), and a third vanishing point is either directly above or below the horizon line.
*   **Converging Vertical Lines:** Vertical lines appear to converge towards the third vanishing point.
*   **High or Low Vantage Point:** Used when the viewer is significantly above (looking down) or significantly below (looking up) the object.

**How it Works:**

1.  **Establish the Horizon Line (HL):** Draw the horizon line representing eye level.
2.  **Place Two Vanishing Points (VP1, VP2):** Mark two vanishing points on the horizon line, spaced apart.
3.  **Place the Third Vanishing Point (VP3):**
    *   **Looking Up (worm's-eye view):** VP3 is located *below* the horizon line.
    *   **Looking Down (bird's-eye view):** VP3 is located *above* the horizon line.
4.  **Draw the Object's Closest Vertex:** Draw a single point representing the vertex (corner) closest to the viewer. This is where all three sets of receding lines will originate.
5.  **Connect Vertex to VPs:** Draw lines from this closest vertex to VP1, VP2, and VP3. These lines represent the three sets of parallel lines (horizontal receding to VP1/VP2, and vertical receding to VP3).
6.  **Define the Form:** To define the object, you'll draw lines parallel to the lines connecting to the VPs.
    *   To create the sides of the object, draw lines parallel to the lines going to VP1 and VP2.
    *   To create the top/bottom of the object, draw lines parallel to the lines going to VP3.
7.  **Complete the Form:** Trim or extend these converging lines to form the edges of your object.

**Example:**

*   **Looking Up at a Skyscraper:** You are standing at the base of a tall building and looking straight up. The vertical edges of the building appear to converge upwards towards a point above your head. The horizontal edges recede to vanishing points on your left and right.
*   **Looking Down from a Tall Building:** You are on a rooftop looking down at the street. The vertical edges of buildings below appear to converge downwards towards a point below your feet.

**Visualisation:** Imagine looking up at a towering structure. The sides seem to lean inwards and meet at a point far above.

### 8. Relation to Anthropometrics (Human Scale)

While perspective drawing primarily deals with geometry and visual perception, it's fundamentally linked to anthropometrics through the concept of **human scale and experience.**

*   **Viewer's Height and Position:** The placement of the horizon line directly relates to the viewer's eye level. In architectural contexts, this means the horizon line can be set at the average height of a standing person, sitting person, or even a child, influencing how the architectural space is perceived.
*   **Perceived Size and Proportion:** Perspective distortion affects how we perceive the size and proportion of architectural elements. Understanding this allows architects to manipulate these perceptions to create specific feelings or emphasize certain features. For instance, a low horizon line can make a space feel grander and taller.
*   **Field of Vision:** Perspective drawing attempts to mimic the human field of vision. The degree of convergence of lines (controlled by the placement of VPs) can influence the feeling of enclosure or openness.
*   **Creating Immersive Experiences:** By accurately depicting how space recedes, perspective drawings help convey the immersive quality of an architectural design and how a human occupant will experience that space. A well-executed perspective can make a viewer feel like they are *in* the designed environment.
*   **Representing Human Presence:** Often, figures are included in perspective drawings to establish scale and give context to the architectural form, directly linking the geometry of the drawing to human dimensions.

### 9. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

A cube is placed such that its front face is parallel to the picture plane, and the viewer is looking at one corner of the cube. Which type of perspective is most suitable for drawing this cube?

**Answer:**
Two-point perspective. Even though one face is parallel, the "corner view" implies that horizontal lines receding from that corner will converge to two distinct vanishing points on the horizon line. If the viewer were looking directly at the face of the cube, it would be one-point perspective.

**Question 2:**

If you are drawing a tall building from street level looking upwards, what will happen to the vertical lines of the building in your drawing, and which type of perspective would you use?

**Answer:**
The vertical lines will appear to converge upwards. You would use **three-point perspective** for this scenario, with the third vanishing point located below the horizon line.

**Question 3:**

What is the primary difference between 2-point perspective and 3-point perspective in terms of vanishing points and the depiction of vertical lines?

**Answer:**
*   **2-point perspective:** Uses two vanishing points on the horizon line, and vertical lines remain vertical.
*   **3-point perspective:** Uses three vanishing points (two on the horizon line, one above or below it), and vertical lines converge towards the third vanishing point.

**Question 4 (Practical Exercise):**

*   **Objective:** Draw a simple rectangular box using both 2-point and 3-point perspective.
*   **Instructions:**
    1.  **For 2-point perspective:** Draw a horizon line. Place two vanishing points on it. Draw a vertical line representing the front edge of your box. Connect the top and bottom of this line to both vanishing points. Then, draw two vertical lines to define the depth of the box. Complete the box by drawing horizontal lines.
    2.  **For 3-point perspective (worm's-eye view):** Draw a horizon line. Place two vanishing points on it. Place a third vanishing point *below* the horizon line. Draw a single point representing the closest corner of your box. Draw lines from this point to all three vanishing points. Use these lines as guides to draw the box, ensuring that lines that are vertical in reality converge towards the lower vanishing point.

### 10. Important Points to Remember

*   **Horizon Line = Eye Level:** Always remember that the horizon line represents the viewer's eye level.
*   **Parallel Lines Converge:** All parallel lines that are not parallel to the picture plane will converge to a single vanishing point.
*   **2-Point vs. 3-Point:** The key distinction is the treatment of vertical lines. They stay vertical in 2-point perspective and converge in 3-point perspective.
*   **Vantage Point Dictates Perspective:** The viewer's position (high, low, or straight on) determines the type of perspective needed and the placement of vanishing points.
*   **Anthropometrics and Perception:** Perspective drawing is crucial for understanding how human scale and the viewer's position influence the perceived size, height, and spatial qualities of architectural designs.

This set of notes provides a foundational understanding of 2-point and 3-point perspective, crucial skills for visualizing and communicating architectural designs. Remember to practice these techniques to develop your spatial reasoning and drawing abilities.
