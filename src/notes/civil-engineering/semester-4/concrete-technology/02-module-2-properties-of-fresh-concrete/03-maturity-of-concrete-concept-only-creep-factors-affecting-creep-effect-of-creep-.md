# CONCRETE TECHNOLOGY - Module 2: Properties of Fresh Concrete

## Topic: Maturity of Concrete (Concept Only)

### Introduction

Maturity of concrete is a concept used to estimate the strength development of concrete over time, taking into account both the **time** and the **temperature** at which it cures. It's particularly useful for predicting when concrete will reach a certain strength for formwork removal, prestressing, or opening to traffic.

### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Hydration:** The chemical reaction between cement and water that leads to the hardening and strength gain of concrete.
*   **Curing:** The process of maintaining adequate moisture content and temperature in concrete for a specific period to allow for proper hydration and strength development.
*   **Temperature:** The ambient temperature during the curing process significantly influences the rate of hydration. Higher temperatures generally accelerate strength gain, but can also lead to undesirable effects like increased shrinkage and reduced ultimate strength if not controlled.
*   **Time:** The duration for which the concrete is allowed to cure.

### The Maturity Concept

The maturity concept is based on the idea that the **total amount of hydration** (and hence strength development) is a function of the **integral of time and temperature** above a certain reference temperature. This reference temperature is typically considered the minimum temperature at which hydration effectively stops (often around -10°C or 10°F).

**Conceptual Formula:**

Maturity Index ($M$) = $\sum_{i=0}^{n} (\Delta t_i \times (T_i - T_0))$

Where:
*   $\Delta t_i$: Time interval (in hours or days).
*   $T_i$: Average temperature of concrete during the time interval $\Delta t_i$ (in °C or °F).
*   $T_0$: Reference temperature below which hydration is assumed to cease (usually 0°C or 32°F, but can vary).

**How it works:**

1.  **Monitoring:** Temperature of the concrete is monitored continuously or at regular intervals.
2.  **Calculation:** The maturity index is calculated by summing the product of time intervals and the corresponding temperature (above the reference temperature).
3.  **Strength Prediction:** A relationship is established between the maturity index and the compressive strength of the concrete through laboratory testing. Once this relationship is known, the maturity index can be used to predict the in-situ strength of the concrete.

### Importance and Applications

*   **Predicting Strength:** Allows for estimation of when concrete will achieve sufficient strength for various construction operations.
*   **Optimizing Construction Schedules:** Enables more accurate planning for formwork removal, post-tensioning, and opening structures to service.
*   **Quality Control:** Provides a means to assess the potential strength of concrete in the field, especially under varying environmental conditions.
*   **Cost Savings:** Can prevent unnecessary delays and ensure efficient use of resources.

### Important Points to Remember (Maturity)

*   Maturity is a **concept**, not a direct measurement of strength. It is a tool for prediction.
*   The accuracy of maturity predictions depends on establishing a reliable **strength-maturity relationship** through calibration.
*   It assumes that **all concrete mixes of the same type** will develop strength at the same rate for a given maturity index.
*   Other factors like **moisture content** and **admixtures** can also influence strength development and may need to be considered.

## Topic: Creep of Concrete

### Introduction

Creep is the **time-dependent increase in strain (deformation) of concrete subjected to a sustained stress**. Unlike elastic deformation, which is instantaneous and reversible, creep is a permanent deformation that occurs over time.

### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Creep:** The progressive increase in strain with time under a constant sustained stress.
*   **Stress:** Force per unit area applied to the concrete.
*   **Strain:** Deformation per unit length.
*   **Sustained Load:** A load that remains constant over a significant period.
*   **Elastic Strain:** Immediate deformation that is recovered when the load is removed.
*   **Creep Strain:** The additional strain that occurs over time due to sustained stress.
*   **Total Strain:** Elastic Strain + Creep Strain.

### Factors Affecting Creep

Creep is a complex phenomenon influenced by several factors:

1.  **Nature of Stress:**
    *   **Magnitude of Stress:** Higher stresses generally lead to higher creep strains. However, creep is generally considered to be proportional to stress up to a certain limit (e.g., 50% of ultimate strength).
    *   **Duration of Stress:** Creep continues to increase with time, although the rate of increase slows down.
    *   **Type of Loading:** Sustained loads cause creep.

2.  **Properties of Concrete:**
    *   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):** Lower w/c ratios generally result in lower creep. This is because lower w/c ratios lead to denser concrete with fewer pores.
    *   **Aggregate Properties:**
        *   **Type of Aggregate:** Stiffer aggregates (e.g., granite, basalt) tend to restrain creep more than weaker or more compressible aggregates (e.g., limestone, lightweight aggregates).
        *   **Quantity of Aggregate:** Higher aggregate content generally reduces creep because aggregates are less susceptible to creep than the cement paste.
        *   **Shape and Size of Aggregate:** Well-graded and angular aggregates can provide better resistance to creep.
    *   **Cement Content:** Higher cement content can lead to higher creep due to the larger amount of cement paste.
    *   **Admixtures:** Certain admixtures, like shrinkage-reducing admixtures or some superplasticizers, can influence creep.
    *   **Concrete Strength:** Higher strength concrete generally exhibits lower creep.

3.  **Properties of the Environment:**
    *   **Humidity:** Creep is significantly higher in drier environments. This is because the loss of moisture from the concrete paste contributes to shrinkage, which interacts with creep.
    *   **Temperature:** Higher curing and service temperatures generally increase the rate of creep.

4.  **Age of Concrete at Loading:**
    *   Concrete loaded at an earlier age (when hydration is still ongoing) exhibits higher creep than concrete loaded at a later age. This is because the paste is less mature and more susceptible to deformation.

5.  **Specimen Characteristics:**
    *   **Size and Shape:** Larger specimens and specimens with smaller surface area-to-volume ratios generally exhibit lower creep rates due to reduced moisture loss.

### Effect of Creep

Creep can have significant implications in concrete structures:

*   **Increased Deflections:** Creep contributes to the long-term deflection of beams, slabs, and other structural elements, potentially leading to serviceability issues.
*   **Stress Redistribution:** In statically indeterminate structures (e.g., continuous beams, portal frames), creep can cause a redistribution of internal stresses. Highly stressed regions may relieve stress through creep, transferring it to less stressed regions.
*   **Prestress Loss:** In prestressed concrete, creep of concrete under sustained prestressing force leads to a loss of prestress, which needs to be accounted for in design.
*   **Cracking:** While creep itself is a deformation, it can induce stresses in restrained members or due to differential creep, potentially leading to cracking.
*   **Damage to Non-Structural Elements:** Increased deflections due to creep can cause damage to partitions, finishes, and other non-structural elements.

### Example of Creep

Consider a concrete beam supporting a constant load. Initially, the beam deflects due to the elastic properties of concrete. Over time, as the concrete is continuously subjected to this load, it will deform further, leading to an additional, permanent increase in deflection. This additional deformation is creep.

## Topic: Shrinkage of Concrete

### Introduction

Shrinkage is the **reduction in volume of concrete that occurs as it dries or due to chemical processes**. Unlike creep, shrinkage is generally not directly related to applied stress but is a property of the material itself. It is an undesirable phenomenon that can lead to cracking if not properly managed.

### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Shrinkage:** The decrease in volume of concrete.
*   **Drying Shrinkage:** The reduction in volume due to the loss of moisture from the concrete to the atmosphere. This is the most common type of shrinkage.
*   **Autogenous Shrinkage:** Shrinkage that occurs in concrete due to the hydration of cement itself, even in the absence of external drying. It's primarily due to the consumption of water in the hydration process and the resulting reduction in pore volume.
*   **Plastic Shrinkage:** Shrinkage that occurs in concrete *before* it has set (in its plastic state). It's caused by rapid surface evaporation of mixing water when the rate of evaporation exceeds the rate at which bleed water rises to the surface.
*   **Thermal Shrinkage:** The reduction in volume of concrete due to a decrease in temperature after it has set.

### Factors Affecting Shrinkage

Shrinkage is influenced by a combination of concrete mix design, environmental conditions, and construction practices:

1.  **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):**
    *   **Higher w/c ratio:** Leads to greater shrinkage because there is more water to evaporate and a more porous structure.

2.  **Aggregate Properties:**
    *   **Type and Content of Aggregate:** Aggregates act as a restraint to shrinkage. A higher volume of well-graded, stiff aggregates generally reduces shrinkage. Aggregates with high thermal expansion or contraction can also influence overall volume changes.
    *   **Modulus of Elasticity of Aggregate:** Aggregates with a higher modulus of elasticity provide greater restraint to shrinkage.

3.  **Cement Content and Type:**
    *   **Higher cement content:** Can lead to higher shrinkage due to increased paste volume.
    *   **Cement Type:** Cements with higher heat of hydration (e.g., Type III) may contribute to higher thermal shrinkage but can also affect drying shrinkage.

4.  **Admixtures:**
    *   **Shrinkage-Reducing Admixtures (SRAs):** These admixtures are specifically designed to reduce drying shrinkage by reducing surface tension of pore water.
    *   **Air-Entraining Agents:** Can slightly increase drying shrinkage due to the increased air voids, but their primary benefit is in frost resistance.

5.  **Environmental Conditions:**
    *   **Relative Humidity:** Lower ambient humidity leads to faster and greater drying shrinkage.
    *   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures accelerate drying and can increase thermal shrinkage if there's a significant temperature drop after setting.
    *   **Wind Speed:** High wind speeds increase the rate of surface evaporation, exacerbating plastic shrinkage.

6.  **Age of Concrete at Drying:**
    *   Drying concrete at an earlier age (while it's still developing strength) results in more significant shrinkage.

7.  **Mix Design and Compaction:**
    *   A richer mix (more cement and water) will shrink more.
    *   Poorly compacted concrete with voids will also be more susceptible to shrinkage.

### Types of Shrinkage

1.  **Drying Shrinkage:**
    *   **Cause:** Loss of moisture from the concrete to the surrounding atmosphere.
    *   **Mechanism:** As water evaporates from the capillary pores in the cement paste, surface tension forces cause the pore walls to move closer together, resulting in a reduction in volume.
    *   **Characteristics:** Occurs over a long period as concrete dries. Can cause significant cracking if not controlled.
    *   **Example:** Concrete pavements, slabs, and walls exposed to air.

2.  **Autogenous Shrinkage:**
    *   **Cause:** Internal consumption of water by the hydration reaction and capillary tension in the pores.
    *   **Mechanism:** As cement hydrates, it consumes water and forms more solid products, reducing the volume of voids. The water in the capillary pores also develops surface tension forces as its volume decreases during hydration.
    *   **Characteristics:** Occurs primarily in the early stages of hydration, especially in concrete with very low w/c ratios or in massive structures where external drying is limited. It can be significant in self-compacting concrete or high-performance concrete.
    *   **Example:** Massive concrete structures like dams or high-strength concrete elements where internal hydration is more dominant.

3.  **Plastic Shrinkage:**
    *   **Cause:** Rapid evaporation of surface moisture from concrete in its plastic state.
    *   **Mechanism:** When the rate of evaporation from the concrete surface exceeds the rate at which bleed water rises to the surface to replenish it, the surface layer of the concrete experiences a volumetric reduction.
    *   **Characteristics:** Occurs very early, typically within the first few hours after placement. The shrinkage is often in the form of fine, irregular cracks on the surface.
    *   **Preventive Measures:** Curing compounds, plastic sheeting, fog spraying, windbreaks.
    *   **Example:** Concrete placed on a hot, dry, windy day without immediate curing.

4.  **Thermal Shrinkage:**
    *   **Cause:** Reduction in volume of concrete due to a decrease in temperature after setting.
    *   **Mechanism:** Concrete, like most materials, contracts as its temperature decreases. If this contraction is restrained, tensile stresses can develop, leading to cracking.
    *   **Characteristics:** Occurs when the concrete cools down from its maximum internal temperature (due to heat of hydration) or from ambient temperature changes.
    *   **Example:** Large concrete pours that generate significant heat of hydration and then cool down, or pavements exposed to significant daily or seasonal temperature fluctuations.

### Example of Shrinkage

Imagine a concrete slab poured on a hot, dry, and windy day. The surface of the concrete loses water rapidly to evaporation. If this evaporation is faster than the bleed water can reach the surface, the top layer of the concrete will shrink while the underlying concrete is still plastic. This differential shrinkage will cause the surface to crack. This is **plastic shrinkage**. If the slab is then exposed to dry air for weeks, it will continue to lose moisture, leading to **drying shrinkage**, which can cause larger, deeper cracks.

### Important Points to Remember (Shrinkage)

*   Shrinkage is **time-dependent** and primarily driven by moisture loss.
*   **Drying shrinkage** is the most significant contributor to cracking in most structures.
*   **Plastic shrinkage** is a concern in early stages and can be mitigated by proper curing.
*   **Restraint** (e.g., by reinforcement, adjacent structural elements, or fixed formwork) is what causes shrinkage to induce tensile stresses and lead to cracking.
*   Minimizing the **water content** in the mix is crucial for controlling shrinkage.

---

### Short Answer Answers

1.  **Maturity Concept:** The maturity of concrete refers to the combined effect of time and temperature on the hydration process, and thus strength development. It's represented by a "maturity index" which is essentially the integral of time and temperature above a reference point where hydration is assumed to cease.
    **Practical Application:** It allows engineers to predict when concrete will reach a certain strength for operations like formwork removal, prestressing, or opening to traffic, especially when ambient temperatures vary.
2.  **Elastic Deformation:** This is the immediate deformation that occurs when a load is applied to concrete. It is proportional to the applied stress (within the elastic limit) and is **reversible**, meaning the concrete returns to its original shape when the load is removed.
    **Creep:** This is a time-dependent, **irreversible** deformation that occurs in concrete when subjected to a sustained load. It is an additional strain that develops over time beyond the initial elastic strain.
3.  **Factors Affecting Creep:**
    *   **Water-Cement Ratio:** Higher w/c leads to higher creep.
    *   **Aggregate Properties:** Stiffer aggregates reduce creep.
    *   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures increase creep rate.
    *   **Age at Loading:** Loading at an earlier age increases creep.
    *   **Humidity:** Lower humidity (drier conditions) can increase creep.
    *   **Magnitude and Duration of Load:** Higher loads and longer durations increase creep.
4.  **Consequences of Creep:**
    *   Increased deflections in beams and slabs.
    *   Stress redistribution in statically indeterminate structures.
    *   Loss of prestress in prestressed concrete.
    *   Potential for cracking due to restrained creep.
    *   Damage to non-structural elements attached to the structure.
5.  **Drying Shrinkage vs. Plastic Shrinkage:**
    *   **Drying Shrinkage:** Occurs after setting due to the loss of moisture from concrete to the atmosphere, causing a reduction in volume.
    *   **Plastic Shrinkage:** Occurs before setting when rapid surface evaporation leads to a volumetric decrease in the plastic concrete.
    **Prevention of Plastic Shrinkage:**
    *   Apply curing compounds or plastic sheeting immediately after finishing.
    *   Use fog spraying or misting.
    *   Erect windbreaks.
    *   Avoid placing concrete during extremely hot, dry, or windy conditions without proper precautions.
6.  **Influence of Water-Cement Ratio:**
    *   **Creep:** A higher w/c ratio results in a more porous cement paste, which is less resistant to deformation under sustained stress, thus leading to higher creep.
    *   **Shrinkage:** A higher w/c ratio means more water is available to evaporate, leading to greater drying shrinkage.

---

### Practical Scenario Answer

a)  **Most Likely Shrinkage:** On a hot, sunny, and windy day, **plastic shrinkage** is the most immediate and likely type of shrinkage to be a problem in the initial stages of the pour. The rapid evaporation of surface moisture will cause differential shrinkage before the concrete has set.

b)  **Mitigation for Plastic Shrinkage:**
    *   Apply a **curing compound** to the surface immediately after finishing.
    *   Cover the slab with **plastic sheeting** or wet burlap.
    *   Employ **fog spraying** or misting over the surface if conditions are extremely severe.
    *   Use **windbreaks** to reduce the wind speed across the surface.

c)  **Long-Term Deformation:** The long-term deformation that will occur under a constant traffic load is **creep**.

    **Factors to Minimize Creep:**
    *   **Lower the water-cement ratio:** This will result in a denser, stronger paste with lower creep potential.
    *   **Increase the aggregate content:** Aggregates are much stiffer than cement paste and restrain creep. Using a well-graded, stiff aggregate (like granite or basalt) and a higher proportion of it will reduce creep.
    *   **Use a richer mix (with caution):** While more cement can sometimes increase creep, ensuring a lower w/c ratio with a slightly richer mix can be beneficial if it leads to better paste quality and density.
    *   **Ensure proper curing:** Concrete loaded at an older age will have lower creep. Good curing helps the concrete achieve better maturity before the load is applied.
    *   **Consider aggregates with higher modulus of elasticity:** As mentioned, stiffer aggregates provide more restraint.
