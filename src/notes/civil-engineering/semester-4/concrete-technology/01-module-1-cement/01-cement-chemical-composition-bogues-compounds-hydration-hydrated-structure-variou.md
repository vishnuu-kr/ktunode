# CONCRETE TECHNOLOGY - Module 1: Cement

This module introduces the fundamental material of concrete: cement. We will explore its chemical makeup, the important compounds that determine its properties, how it reacts with water (hydration), the resulting structure, different types of cement, and essential tests to ensure its quality as per Indian Standards.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

*   Describe the chemical composition of cement.
*   Explain the formation and significance of Bogue's compounds.
*   Detail the process of cement hydration and the resulting hydrated structure.
*   Differentiate between various types of cement and their applications.
*   Understand and explain the Indian Standard tests for cement: standard consistency, setting times, fineness, and specific gravity.

## 1. Chemical Composition of Cement

Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) is the most common type of cement. Its chemical composition is primarily a result of burning a carefully proportioned mixture of calcareous (limestone, chalk) and argillaceous (clay, shale) materials in a kiln at high temperatures (around 1450°C). This process leads to the formation of clinker, which is then ground with a small amount of gypsum.

**Key Constituents and their Approximate Proportions in OPC:**

*   **Lime (CaO):** 60-67% (Provides the essential alkaline component for hydration)
*   **Silica (SiO₂):** 17-25% (Contributes to strength development)
*   **Alumina (Al₂O₃):** 3-8% (Aids in early strength and reduces setting time)
*   **Iron Oxide (Fe₂O₃):** 0.5-6% (Contributes to color and early strength; acts as a flux in the kiln)
*   **Magnesia (MgO):** 0.1-4% (Excessive magnesia can cause unsoundness)
*   **Sulphur Trioxide (SO₃):** 1-3% (Primarily from added gypsum; controls setting time)
*   **Alkalis (Na₂O, K₂O):** 0.2-1.3% (Can cause alkali-aggregate reaction if reactive aggregates are used)
*   **Free Lime (CaO):** < 1.5% (Unburnt lime, indicates inefficient burning and causes unsoundness)
*   **Loss on Ignition (LOI):** < 5% (Volatile matter lost when heated, indicates quality of burning)

**Important Note:** The proportions are crucial for the proper formation of desirable cement compounds.

## 2. Bogue's Compounds

Bogue's compounds are theoretical compounds formed when the main oxides in cement clinker are heated together. They are represented by the following formulae and abbreviations:

| Compound Name                 | Formula       | Abbreviation | Approximate % | Significance                                                                                                       |
| :---------------------------- | :------------ | :----------- | :------------ | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tricalcium Silicate           | 3CaO · SiO₂   | C₃S (Alite)  | 45-65         | Responsible for early strength development (1-7 days). Reacts rapidly with water.                                |
| Dicalcium Silicate            | 2CaO · SiO₂   | C₂S (Belite) | 15-35         | Contributes to ultimate strength at later ages (after 7 days). Reacts slowly with water.                         |
| Tricalcium Aluminate          | 3CaO · Al₂O₃  | C₃A (Celite) | 5-12          | Responsible for flash set (very rapid hardening) if not controlled. Contributes to early heat of hydration.        |
| Tetracalcium Aluminoferrite | 4CaO · Al₂O₃ · Fe₂O₃ | C₄AF (Felite) | 5-15          | Contributes to early strength but less than C₃S. Acts as a flux in the kiln, lowering the clinkering temperature. |

**How they are formed (simplified):**

During the high-temperature burning process, these oxides combine in specific ratios. The presence and proportions of these compounds significantly influence the properties of the cement.

**Example:** A cement with a higher C₃S content will achieve higher early strength compared to a cement with a higher C₂S content.

**Important Points to Remember about Bogue's Compounds:**

*   **C₃S (Alite):** Primary contributor to **early strength**.
*   **C₂S (Belite):** Primary contributor to **ultimate strength**.
*   **C₃A (Celite):** Contributes to **early heat of hydration** and can cause **flash set** if not controlled by gypsum.
*   **C₄AF (Felite):** Contributes to **early strength** and acts as a **flux**.

## 3. Hydration of Cement

Hydration is the chemical reaction between cement compounds and water, forming a hardened cement paste. This process is responsible for the binding properties of cement, which hold aggregates together in concrete.

**The Process:**

1.  **Initial Contact:** When water is added to cement, the anhydrous cement grains begin to dissolve.
2.  **Formation of Hydrates:** The dissolved ions react with water to form hydrated compounds. The primary reactions involve:
    *   **C₃S + Water → Calcium Silicate Hydrate (C-S-H gel) + Calcium Hydroxide (CH)**
        *   This is the most important reaction for strength development. C-S-H gel is a colloidal material that binds particles together. CH is a crystalline byproduct, which contributes to alkalinity but not directly to strength.
    *   **C₂S + Water → C-S-H gel + CH** (Slower reaction than C₃S)
    *   **C₃A + Water → Hydrated Aluminate Compounds (e.g., ettringite, monosulfate) + CH**
        *   This reaction is very rapid, hence the addition of gypsum to control it.
    *   **C₄AF + Water → Hydrated Aluminoferrite Compounds + CH** (Relatively fast reaction)
3.  **Setting and Hardening:** As hydration progresses, the newly formed hydrates fill the space between cement particles, creating a rigid structure and causing the cement paste to set and then harden.

**Key Aspects of Hydration:**

*   **Exothermic Reaction:** The hydration process releases heat. C₃S and C₃A contribute significantly to the early heat evolution, while C₂S contributes to heat at later stages.
*   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c):** Crucial for hydration. Sufficient water is needed for complete hydration, but excess water can reduce strength and durability.
*   **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O):** Added during cement manufacturing (about 2-5%) to control the rapid reaction of C₃A. Gypsum reacts with C₃A to form ettringite, which coats the C₃A particles, retarding the initial reaction and preventing flash set.

## 4. Hydrated Structure

The hardened cement paste is a complex structure consisting of:

*   **Calcium Silicate Hydrate (C-S-H) Gel:** The primary binding phase. It's a poorly crystalline, amorphous gel with a high surface area. It forms a tangled network of fibrous or plate-like particles that interlock and provide strength and cohesion. The properties of C-S-H gel (like its composition and morphology) significantly influence the mechanical properties of concrete.
*   **Calcium Hydroxide (CH) Crystals:** Formed as a byproduct of C₃S and C₂S hydration. CH crystals are typically hexagonal prisms. They contribute to the alkalinity of the pore solution and can provide some stiffness, but they are relatively weak and prone to chemical attack. Their presence can also facilitate the formation of further C-S-H gel through secondary reactions.
*   **Pore Structure:** The hydrated cement paste is porous. The pores are filled with water, air, or gel-like material.
    *   **Capillary Pores:** Larger pores (typically < 1 µm) formed by the spaces between hydrating cement particles. Their size and connectivity significantly influence strength, permeability, and durability.
    *   **Gel Pores:** Very fine pores within the C-S-H gel itself.
*   **Unreacted Cement Grains:** Incomplete hydration means some cement grains may remain unreacted, especially at lower w/c ratios or if the cement is poorly ground.
*   **Other Hydration Products:** Such as calcium sulfoaluminates (from C₃A and gypsum) and calcium aluminoferrites.

**Visualizing the Structure:**

Imagine a dense, interwoven mat of C-S-H gel, with larger, more ordered CH crystals scattered throughout, and a network of interconnected pores.

**Important Point:** The quality and microstructure of the hydrated structure, particularly the development of a dense C-S-H gel and a limited capillary pore network, are key to achieving high-strength and durable concrete.

## 5. Various Types of Cement

While OPC is common, different applications require cements with specific properties. Here are some common types of cement as per Indian Standards:

**A. Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC)**

*   **OPC 33 Grade:** Specified for general construction purposes where moderate strength is required. Characteristic compressive strength at 28 days is 33 MPa.
*   **OPC 43 Grade:** More common for general construction, providing higher early strength. Characteristic compressive strength at 28 days is 43 MPa.
*   **OPC 53 Grade:** Used for high-strength concrete applications, such as pre-stressed concrete, high-rise buildings, and bridges. Characteristic compressive strength at 28 days is 53 MPa.
    *   **Note:** "Grade" refers to the characteristic 28-day compressive strength in MPa.

**B. Portland Pozzolana Cement (PPC)**

*   Produced by grinding OPC clinker with pozzolanic materials (like fly ash, volcanic ash, calcined clay) or by intimately mixing OPC with pozzolanic materials.
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Lower heat of hydration:** Suitable for mass concrete works (e.g., dams).
    *   **Improved durability:** Pozzolanic reaction leads to denser microstructure and better resistance to chemical attack (sulfates, chlorides).
    *   **Slower strength gain:** Initial strength gain is slower than OPC, but ultimate strength can be comparable or even higher.
*   **Applications:** General construction, mass concrete, marine structures, wastewater treatment plants.

**C. Portland Slag Cement (PSC)**

*   Produced by grinding OPC clinker, granulated blast furnace slag (a byproduct of iron manufacturing), and gypsum.
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Low heat of hydration:** Similar to PPC, suitable for mass concrete.
    *   **Good durability:** Improved resistance to chemical attack.
    *   **Slower strength gain:** Initial strength gain is slower than OPC.
*   **Applications:** Mass concrete, foundations, general construction, marine structures.

**D. Rapid Hardening Cement**

*   Similar to OPC but with a higher percentage of C₃S and finer grinding.
*   **Properties:** Achieves high early strength much faster than OPC.
*   **Applications:** Pavements, precast concrete units, cold weather concreting, and situations where early formwork removal is needed.

**E. Sulphate Resisting Cement (SRC)**

*   Produced by reducing the C₃A content (ideally < 5%) in the clinker and ensuring a higher C₂S content.
*   **Properties:** High resistance to attack by sulfates present in soil and groundwater.
*   **Applications:** Structures in contact with sulfate-bearing soils or water, such as foundations, sewage systems, and marine structures.

**F. High Alumina Cement (HAC)**

*   Produced by fusing a mixture of bauxite and lime.
*   **Properties:** Very rapid hardening, high ultimate strength, good resistance to chemical attack (acids, sulfates) and high temperatures.
*   **Applications:** Refractory concrete, rapid repairs, precast concrete products, and environments with high chemical exposure. (Note: Can be susceptible to conversion at elevated temperatures, reducing strength).

**G. White Cement**

*   Similar to OPC but with iron oxide and manganese content reduced to very low levels.
*   **Properties:** White color.
*   **Applications:** Architectural purposes, decorative concrete, tile grouts, terrazzo finishes.

**Important Distinction:** The difference between these cements lies in their chemical composition (particularly C₃S, C₂S, C₃A) and fineness, which dictate their hydration rates and resulting properties.

## 6. Testing of Cement as per Indian Standard

Standard tests are crucial to ensure that cement meets the specified quality and performance requirements for its intended use. The primary Indian Standard for cement testing is **IS: 4031 (Methods of Physical Tests for Cement)**.

### 6.1 Standard Consistency

*   **Objective:** To determine the percentage of water required to be added to a cement sample to produce a plastic paste that conforms to the Vicat Apparatus requirements. This value is known as the "Standard Consistency" of the cement.
*   **Apparatus:**
    *   Vicat Apparatus (as per IS: 5513)
    *   Balance
    *   Weighing dish
    *   Stirring implement (spatula)
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Weigh a specific amount of cement (e.g., 400g for OPC, 300g for other types).
    2.  Add varying percentages of water (starting from around 22% for OPC and increasing in steps of 1-2%) to separate batches of cement.
    3.  Mix the cement and water thoroughly for a specified time (e.g., 3-5 minutes) to form a homogeneous paste.
    4.  Fill the Vicat mould (cylindrical or conical, depending on the test) with the paste, ensuring it's compacted without excessive shaking.
    5.  Place the Vicat apparatus with the plunger attached.
    6.  Release the plunger. If the paste has standard consistency, the plunger should penetrate to a depth of **5 to 7 mm from the bottom of the mould**.
    7.  Adjust the percentage of water in subsequent trials until the specified penetration is achieved.
*   **Result:** The percentage of water that yields the standard consistency.
*   **Example:** If a cement sample requires 26% water to achieve the standard consistency, its standard consistency is 26%.

### 6.2 Setting Times

*   **Objective:** To determine the time taken for cement paste to lose its plasticity and become a rigid solid. This is important for handling, placing, and compacting concrete.
*   **Apparatus:** Vicat Apparatus (with different needles)
*   **Types of Setting Times:**
    *   **Initial Setting Time:** The time elapsed from the addition of water until the paste starts losing its plasticity.
    *   **Final Setting Time:** The time elapsed from the addition of water until the paste completely loses its plasticity and offers a definite resistance to the attachment of the final setting needle.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Prepare a cement paste of standard consistency (determined earlier).
    2.  Fill the Vicat mould with this paste.
    3.  **For Initial Setting Time:** Insert the **initial setting needle** (a larger, un-shouldered needle) into the Vicat apparatus. Lower the needle gently until it just touches the surface of the paste. Release the needle. The initial setting time is reached when the needle **fails to pierce the paste**.
    4.  **For Final Setting Time:** Insert the **final setting needle** (a smaller needle with an annular ring) into the Vicat apparatus. Lower the needle gently. The final setting time is reached when the needle **pierces the paste, but the annular ring does not leave an impression**, or when the needle simply fails to pierce the paste.
*   **Indian Standard Requirements (IS: 4031):**
    *   **Initial Setting Time:** Not less than 30 minutes (for OPC).
    *   **Final Setting Time:** Not more than 10 hours (for OPC).
*   **Importance of Gypsum:** Gypsum (added to cement) slows down the C₃A reaction, thus increasing the initial setting time and preventing flash set.

### 6.3 Fineness

*   **Objective:** To determine the surface area of cement particles. Finer cement hydrates faster and provides higher early strength, but can also lead to increased shrinkage and heat generation.
*   **Methods:**
    1.  **Sieve Test (IS: 4031 - Part 1):**
        *   **Apparatus:** IS Sieve No. 9 (90 micron aperture), balance, oven.
        *   **Procedure:** Weigh a known quantity of cement (e.g., 100g). Sieve it through the specified sieve for a set time (e.g., 15 minutes) with adequate sieving action. Weigh the residue retained on the sieve.
        *   **Calculation:** Fineness (%) = (Weight of residue / Original weight of cement) * 100.
        *   **Requirement (OPC):** Not more than 10% residue on IS Sieve No. 9.
    2.  **Blaine Air Permeability Test (IS: 4031 - Part 2):** This method measures the surface area directly in cm²/g. It's a more precise method for fineness.
        *   **Procedure:** A known weight of cement is packed into a permeable bed under specific pressure. Air is forced through this bed, and the time taken for a certain volume of air to pass through is measured. The surface area is calculated using the permeability data.
        *   **Requirement (OPC):** Minimum 225 cm²/g for OPC.

### 6.4 Specific Gravity

*   **Objective:** To determine the density of cement relative to water. This is important for calculating the mix proportions by weight for concrete.
*   **Apparatus:**
    *   Le Chatelier flask (as per IS: 5516)
    *   Balance
    *   Stalagmometer (optional, for dropping kerosene)
    *   Kerosene or similar liquid (sp. gr. ~0.8)
    *   Water bath (for temperature control)
    *   Oven
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Fill the Le Chatelier flask with water up to the mark 'O' (0.5 ml).
    2.  Add a known weight of dry cement (e.g., 64g, as per IS code) to the flask.
    3.  Add kerosene to fill the flask up to the mark '24' (24 ml). The purpose of kerosene is to fill the air voids in the cement powder, preventing it from absorbing water.
    4.  Gently shake or stir to remove trapped air.
    5.  The difference in the water level in the flask indicates the volume occupied by the cement.
    6.  **Calculation:** Specific Gravity = (Weight of cement) / (Volume of cement). The volume of cement is determined from the change in liquid level in the flask.
*   **Indian Standard Requirement (OPC):** Not less than 3.15.
*   **Example:** If the liquid level rises from 0.5 ml to 10.5 ml after adding 64g of cement and kerosene, the volume of cement is 10.5 - 0.5 = 10 ml. Specific Gravity = 64g / 10 ml = 6.4 (This is not a correct calculation; the IS code provides specific ways to calculate the volume of cement).

    **Correct Calculation for Specific Gravity using Le Chatelier Flask:**
    Let:
    *   $W$ = Weight of cement = 64 g
    *   $V_w$ = Initial volume of water in flask = 0.5 ml
    *   $V_L$ = Final volume of liquid (water + kerosene + cement) in flask = 24 ml (up to the mark)
    *   $V_{cement}$ = Volume of cement

    The volume of the liquid added from 0.5 ml to 24 ml without cement is $24 - 0.5 = 23.5$ ml.
    When cement is added, the volume of liquid rises due to the volume occupied by cement. The volume of kerosene added is such that the total liquid level reaches the 24 ml mark.

    Let $V_{kerosene}$ be the volume of kerosene added.
    The total volume occupied by water + kerosene + cement = $V_w + V_{kerosene} + V_{cement}$
    When the liquid level reaches the 24 ml mark, it means that the volume of the liquid itself (water + kerosene) is $V_w + V_{kerosene}$ and this liquid, along with the cement, occupies a total volume within the flask.

    The IS code method is a bit more nuanced. It's often simplified by considering the total volume of liquid added to reach the 24ml mark when cement is present.

    A simpler way to think about it is that the displacement of the liquid by the cement powder gives us the volume of the cement.

    **Standard Method Outline:**
    1.  Fill Le Chatelier flask with water up to the 0.5 ml mark.
    2.  Add 64g of cement. Add kerosene to fill up to the 24 ml mark. Note the reading on the graduated scale for kerosene added. Let this be $V_k$.
    3.  The volume of cement is $V_{cement} = (23.5 - V_k) \times \frac{1}{0.8}$ if kerosene was used as the displacing liquid and its specific gravity is 0.8. (This is a simplified representation, actual calculation might involve more precise density corrections).

    **The key is that the change in liquid level, corrected for the liquid's specific gravity, gives the volume of cement.**

    **Practical Approach for Understanding:** The test essentially measures how much space the cement powder occupies when displacing a liquid. If the specific gravity is high, a given weight of cement will occupy a smaller volume.

## Answers to Practice Questions

**1. Multiple Choice Questions:**

    a) (ii) C₃S
    b) (ii) 30 minutes
    c) (ii) Low heat of hydration
    d) (iii) 3.15

**2. Short Answer Questions:**

    a) **Role of Gypsum:** Gypsum is added to cement to control the rapid reaction of Tricalcium Aluminate (C₃A) with water. It forms ettringite, which coats the C₃A particles, retarding the initial setting and preventing "flash set" (premature stiffening).
    b) **Significance of C₂S:** Dicalcium Silicate (C₂S or Belite) is responsible for the **ultimate strength** of cement. It hydrates slowly, contributing to the strength development at later ages (after 7 days).
    c) **Fineness Tests:**
        *   **Sieve Test:** Measures the amount of cement retained on a sieve of a specific aperture (e.g., 90 microns). It indicates the percentage of coarser particles.
        *   **Blaine Air Permeability Test:** Measures the specific surface area of cement particles (in cm²/g). It's a more precise indicator of fineness and relates directly to the rate of hydration.
    d) **Cement for Mass Concrete:**
        *   **Portland Pozzolana Cement (PPC)** and **Portland Slag Cement (PSC)** are suitable for mass concrete.
        *   **Reason:** They have a lower heat of hydration compared to OPC. Mass concrete structures (like dams) generate significant heat due to the large volume. Low heat of hydration reduces the risk of thermal cracking and improves durability.

**3. Calculation/Problem:**

    **Calculation of Fineness:**
    Fineness (%) = (Weight of residue / Original weight of cement) * 100
    Fineness (%) = (8 g / 100 g) * 100 = **8%**

    **IS Requirement for OPC:** Not more than 10% residue on IS Sieve No. 9.

    **Conclusion:** Yes, this cement **passes** the IS requirement for OPC fineness.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Bogue's compounds (C₃S, C₂S, C₃A, C₄AF)** are crucial for understanding cement's performance. C₃S for early strength, C₂S for ultimate strength.
*   **Hydration** is the chemical reaction that turns cement powder into a hardened binder. C-S-H gel is the primary strength-giving product.
*   **Gypsum's role** is vital to prevent flash set by controlling C₃A.
*   Different **types of cement** (PPC, PSC, SRC, etc.) are engineered for specific applications by altering their chemical composition and fineness.
*   Standard tests (consistency, setting times, fineness, specific gravity) are **non-negotiable** for quality control in cement manufacturing and usage.
*   **Standard Consistency** is the water content for a workable paste, determined by Vicat's apparatus.
*   **Setting times** indicate when cement loses plasticity (initial) and becomes rigid (final).
*   **Fineness** affects hydration rate and strength development.
*   **Specific gravity** is essential for mix design calculations.
