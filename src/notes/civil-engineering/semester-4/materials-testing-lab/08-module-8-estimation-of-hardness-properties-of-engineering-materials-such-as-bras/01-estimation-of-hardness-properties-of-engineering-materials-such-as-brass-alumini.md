# MATERIALS TESTING LAB - Module 8: Estimation of Hardness Properties

## Topic: Estimation of Hardness properties of engineering materials such as brass, aluminium, copper, steel etc. by performing (a) Rockwell hardness test (b) Vicker’s hardness test

---

### 1. Introduction to Hardness and its Importance

*   **Definition of Hardness:** Hardness is the resistance of a material to localized plastic deformation, such as scratching, abrasion, indentation, or cutting. It is a measure of a material's stiffness and resistance to surface damage.

*   **Why is Hardness Testing Important?**
    *   **Quality Control:** Ensures that materials meet specified hardness requirements, which are often directly related to their performance characteristics.
    *   **Material Selection:** Helps engineers choose the appropriate material for a given application based on its expected wear and tear.
    *   **Process Control:** Monitors the effectiveness of heat treatments (e.g., hardening, tempering) and manufacturing processes.
    *   **Predicting Other Mechanical Properties:** Hardness often correlates with other important properties like tensile strength, yield strength, and wear resistance.
    *   **Non-Destructive Testing (NDT):** In many cases, hardness testing causes minimal damage to the material, allowing for testing of finished or semi-finished components.

*   **Common Engineering Materials Tested:**
    *   **Steel:** Various alloys, heat-treated steels, stainless steels.
    *   **Aluminum Alloys:** Heat-treatable and non-heat-treatable aluminum.
    *   **Copper Alloys (Brass, Bronze):** Used in plumbing, electrical components, decorative items.
    *   **Other Non-Ferrous Metals:** Titanium alloys, magnesium alloys.
    *   **Polymers:** Though less common in these specific tests, hardness is crucial for polymer applications.

### 2. The Rockwell Hardness Test

#### 2.1. Principle of the Rockwell Test

*   **Concept:** The Rockwell test measures the **depth of penetration** of an indenter under a specific load. It's a "depth-measuring" hardness test.
*   **Two Stages of Loading:**
    1.  **Minor Load (Preload):** A small initial load is applied to seat the indenter and eliminate surface imperfections. This sets a datum for measurement.
    2.  **Major Load:** A significantly larger load is applied for a specified dwell time, causing further indentation.
*   **Hardness Value Calculation:** The hardness number is inversely proportional to the depth of penetration measured *after* the major load is removed, relative to the depth achieved under the minor load.
    *   **Formula (Conceptual):** Hardness ∝ 1 / (Depth of permanent indentation)
*   **Key Feature:** The minor load ensures that the measurement is unaffected by surface roughness or slight variations in seating.

#### 2.2. Types of Rockwell Scales and Indenters

*   **Indenter Types:**
    *   **Diamond Cone:** Used for testing harder materials. Has a specific tip angle.
    *   **Steel Ball:** Used for testing softer materials. Available in various diameters (e.g., 1/16", 1/8", 1/4", 1/2").
*   **Rockwell Scales (Combinations of Loads and Indenters):** Different scales are used for different material types and hardness ranges. Each scale is designated by a letter.
    *   **Common Scales:**
        *   **HRC (Rockwell C Scale):** Diamond cone indenter, 150 kgf major load. Used for hardened steels, carbides.
        *   **HRB (Rockwell B Scale):** 1/16" steel ball indenter, 100 kgf major load. Used for softer steels, aluminum alloys, copper alloys.
        *   **HRA (Rockwell A Scale):** Diamond cone indenter, 60 kgf major load. Used for very hard materials, thin case-hardened surfaces.
        *   **HRF (Rockwell F Scale):** 1/16" steel ball indenter, 60 kgf major load.
        *   **HRD (Rockwell D Scale):** Diamond cone indenter, 100 kgf major load.
        *   **HRBW (Rockwell B, Waterbury):** Similar to HRB but with specific application to brass.
        *   **HRBS (Rockwell B, Steel):** Similar to HRB but with specific application to steel.
*   **Choosing the Correct Scale:** Critical for obtaining accurate and meaningful results. Depends on the material's expected hardness and thickness.

#### 2.3. Rockwell Test Procedure

1.  **Specimen Preparation:**
    *   Surface must be clean, smooth, and free from scale, dirt, oil, or oxide layers.
    *   The surface should be flat and perpendicular to the direction of the indenter.
    *   For curved surfaces, specific correction factors or special fixtures may be needed.
2.  **Machine Setup:**
    *   Select the appropriate indenter and major load based on the material and expected hardness.
    *   Ensure the machine is calibrated and functioning correctly.
3.  **Specimen Placement:**
    *   Place the specimen on the anvil, ensuring stable contact.
4.  **Applying the Minor Load:**
    *   Gently apply the minor load until the indicator registers the "set" position.
5.  **Applying the Major Load:**
    *   Apply the major load smoothly and without shock for the specified dwell time (typically a few seconds).
6.  **Removing the Major Load:**
    *   Remove the major load carefully, leaving the minor load applied.
7.  **Reading the Hardness Value:**
    *   The hardness value is directly read from the dial or digital display on the Rockwell hardness tester.

#### 2.4. Advantages and Disadvantages of Rockwell Test

*   **Advantages:**
    *   **Fast and Simple:** Relatively quick to perform and requires minimal training.
    *   **Direct Reading:** The hardness value is displayed directly, eliminating complex calculations.
    *   **Versatile:** Numerous scales allow testing of a wide range of materials.
    *   **Minimal Specimen Preparation:** Often requires less surface preparation than other methods.
    *   **Non-Destructive (relatively):** Causes a small indentation, allowing testing of finished parts.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Limited to a Specific Range:** Certain scales are not suitable for extremely hard or soft materials.
    *   **Surface Sensitivity:** While less sensitive than Brinell, a smooth surface is still important for accuracy.
    *   **Less Accurate for Very Soft Materials:** The depth measurement can be less precise for highly deformable materials.
    *   **Can't measure very thin materials:** The indenter might penetrate completely through the specimen.

### 3. The Vicker's Hardness Test (also known as Diamond Pyramid Hardness Test)

#### 3.1. Principle of the Vicker's Test

*   **Concept:** The Vicker's test measures the **surface area of the indentation** made by a square-based diamond pyramid indenter under a specific load.
*   **Indenter:** A perfectly square-based diamond pyramid with an angle of 136° between opposite faces.
*   **Load Application:** A single load is applied for a specified dwell time.
*   **Hardness Value Calculation:** The Vicker's hardness number (HV) is calculated based on the applied load and the geometric mean of the two diagonals of the indentation.
    *   **Formula:** $HV = \frac{1.8544 \times P}{d^2}$
        *   Where:
            *   $P$ = Applied Load (in kgf)
            *   $d$ = Average length of the two diagonals of the indentation (in mm)
*   **Key Feature:** The geometric shape of the indenter ensures that the hardness value is largely independent of the applied load (within specified ranges). This is a significant advantage.

#### 3.2. Vicker's Hardness Scales and Loads

*   **Load Range:** Vicker's testing can be performed with a wide range of loads, from very light loads (microhardness testing, e.g., 10-200 gf) to heavier loads (macrohardness testing, e.g., 1 kgf to 120 kgf).
*   **Designation:** The Vicker's hardness number is often followed by a number indicating the load used, e.g., HV100, HV30, HV10.
    *   **Macrohardness:** Loads ≥ 1 kgf (e.g., HV100, HV30). Used for most metals.
    *   **Microhardness:** Loads < 1 kgf (e.g., HV10, HV50). Used for very thin materials, surface layers, brittle materials, and for observing hardness variations across a microstructure.

#### 3.3. Vicker's Test Procedure

1.  **Specimen Preparation:**
    *   Requires a **highly polished, flat surface**. Surface roughness is critical as the diagonals of the indentation must be accurately measured.
    *   The surface must be free of contaminants.
2.  **Machine Setup:**
    *   Select the appropriate load for the material.
    *   Mount the diamond pyramid indenter on the testing machine.
    *   Ensure the machine is calibrated.
3.  **Specimen Placement:**
    *   Place the specimen on the stage.
4.  **Applying the Load:**
    *   Apply the selected load smoothly and without shock for the specified dwell time.
5.  **Removing the Load:**
    *   Remove the load carefully.
6.  **Measuring the Indentation:**
    *   The indentation is typically a square (or rhombus if the surface is not perfectly normal to the indenter).
    *   Using a calibrated microscope attached to the hardness tester, measure the lengths of the two diagonals of the indentation.
7.  **Calculating Hardness:**
    *   Calculate the average diagonal length ($d$).
    *   Use the formula $HV = \frac{1.8544 \times P}{d^2}$ to determine the Vicker's hardness number.

#### 3.4. Advantages and Disadvantages of Vicker's Test

*   **Advantages:**
    *   **Load Independence:** The hardness value is largely independent of the applied load, making it versatile.
    *   **Applicable to Wide Range of Materials:** Suitable for very soft to very hard materials.
    *   **Microhardness Capability:** Excellent for testing thin layers, case hardening, and microscopic features.
    *   **Accurate Measurement:** Provides a precise measure of indentation size.
    *   **Can Measure Hardness Gradients:** By making indentations at different locations on a surface.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Requires Careful Surface Preparation:** The need for a highly polished surface makes it time-consuming.
    *   **Slower than Rockwell:** Measuring the diagonals under a microscope takes longer than direct reading.
    *   **Potential for Indentation Overlap:** When testing materials with varying hardness, careful spacing of indentations is required.
    *   **Brittle Materials:** Can cause cracking or chipping under high loads.

### 4. Material Examples and Typical Hardness Values

*   **Aluminum Alloys:**
    *   Annealed (e.g., 1100-O): Relatively soft, lower hardness. (e.g., HRB 20-40)
    *   Heat-Treated (e.g., 6061-T6): Significantly harder. (e.g., HRB 90-100)
*   **Copper Alloys (Brass):**
    *   Annealed Brass: Softer. (e.g., HRB 40-60)
    *   Work-Hardened Brass: Harder due to strain hardening. (e.g., HRB 70-90)
*   **Steel:**
    *   Mild Steel (Annealed): Low hardness. (e.g., HRB 60-75)
    *   Medium Carbon Steel (Normalized): Moderate hardness. (e.g., HRC 20-30)
    *   Hardened High Carbon Steel: Very high hardness. (e.g., HRC 55-65)
    *   Tempered Steel: Hardness adjusted by tempering.
*   **Important Note:** Always refer to material specifications and standards for exact hardness requirements and acceptable ranges.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A component made of hardened steel needs to be tested for its surface hardness. Which Rockwell scale would be most appropriate, and why?
*   **Answer:** The Rockwell C scale (HRC) would be most appropriate. This scale uses a diamond cone indenter and a high major load (150 kgf), making it suitable for testing hard materials like hardened steels. The HRB scale, which uses a ball indenter, is designed for softer materials and would likely produce inaccurate results or an incomplete penetration for hardened steel.

**Question 2:**
You are testing a thin sheet of aluminum alloy. What are the potential issues with using the Rockwell C scale? What would be a better alternative?
*   **Answer:** Using the Rockwell C scale on a thin aluminum sheet is problematic because:
    *   The material might be too soft for the diamond cone indenter and the high loads of the HRC scale, leading to inaccurate readings.
    *   The indenter might penetrate completely through the sheet, or the indentation might be affected by the thickness of the material and the supporting anvil, giving a falsely low hardness value.
    *   A better alternative would be to use the Rockwell B scale (HRB), which uses a steel ball indenter and a lower major load (100 kgf), more suitable for softer materials like aluminum. If the aluminum is very thin, even HRB might be too much, and a different test or scale might be considered depending on the specific alloy and thickness.

**Question 3:**
In a Vicker's hardness test, the applied load (P) is 50 kgf, and the measured diagonals of the square indentation are 0.25 mm and 0.27 mm. Calculate the Vicker's Hardness number (HV).
*   **Answer:**
    *   Average diagonal ($d$) = (0.25 mm + 0.27 mm) / 2 = 0.26 mm
    *   $HV = \frac{1.8544 \times P}{d^2}$
    *   $HV = \frac{1.8544 \times 50}{0.26^2}$
    *   $HV = \frac{92.72}{0.0676}$
    *   $HV \approx 1372$
    *   Therefore, the Vicker's Hardness number is approximately 1372 HV.

**Question 4:**
What is the main advantage of the Vicker's hardness test over the Rockwell hardness test when examining the hardness of a case-hardened steel component?
*   **Answer:** The main advantage of the Vicker's test is its capability for microhardness testing. Case-hardened components have a hard outer layer (case) and a softer core. The Vicker's test, especially with its microhardness variations (using very low loads), allows for the precise measurement of hardness at different depths across this gradient. This reveals how effective the case hardening process was and how the hardness changes from the surface inwards. Rockwell tests, while offering some depth sensitivity by choosing different scales, are generally not precise enough at the very shallow depths required to map out these fine hardness gradients.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Material Selection is Key:** Always choose the hardness test and scale appropriate for the material being tested.
*   **Surface Preparation Matters:** Even for Rockwell, a reasonably smooth and clean surface is crucial for accuracy. Vicker's demands a highly polished surface.
*   **Load and Indenter Choice:** The correct combination of load and indenter is critical for obtaining meaningful results in both Rockwell and Vicker's tests.
*   **Dwell Time:** Ensure the load is applied for the specified dwell time to allow for proper indentation.
*   **Accuracy of Measurement:** For Vicker's, the accuracy of the microscope and the operator's skill in measuring the diagonals are paramount.
*   **Standards and Calibration:** Always follow relevant material testing standards (e.g., ASTM, ISO) and ensure your testing equipment is properly calibrated.
*   **Correlation with Other Properties:** Remember that hardness is often an indicator of other mechanical properties like tensile strength and wear resistance.
