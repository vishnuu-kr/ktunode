# MATERIALS TESTING LAB: Module 6 - Estimation of Toughness of Steel Specimens by Izod & Charpy Impact Tests

## 1. Introduction to Toughness

### What is Toughness?

*   **Definition:** Toughness is a material's ability to absorb energy and deform plastically before fracturing. It's a measure of a material's resistance to fracture when subjected to a sudden impact or stress.
*   **Distinction from Strength:**
    *   **Strength:** A material's resistance to permanent deformation or fracture under static load.
    *   **Toughness:** A material's resistance to fracture under *dynamic* load, often at high strain rates.
*   **Importance of Toughness:**
    *   Crucial for materials used in applications subjected to shock loads, vibrations, or sudden impacts (e.g., automotive parts, aircraft components, structural elements in bridges and buildings).
    *   Helps prevent catastrophic brittle fracture, especially at low temperatures or in the presence of stress concentrations.

### Factors Affecting Toughness

*   **Temperature:** Generally, toughness decreases as temperature decreases (leading to a ductile-to-brittle transition).
*   **Strain Rate:** Toughness decreases with increasing strain rate (impact tests simulate high strain rates).
*   **Microstructure:** Grain size, phase distribution, and presence of inclusions significantly influence toughness.
*   **Notch Sensitivity:** The presence of a notch or crack concentrates stress, making the material more susceptible to fracture.

## 2. Impact Testing: Principles and Objectives

### Objective of Impact Tests

*   To determine the energy absorbed by a material during fracture under a sudden impact load.
*   To assess the material's susceptibility to brittle fracture.
*   To understand the ductile-to-brittle transition behavior of materials.

### How Impact Tests Work

*   A pendulum hammer (or a falling weight) is released from a known height, imparting kinetic energy to the specimen.
*   The specimen is placed in a specific orientation relative to the hammer's path.
*   The hammer strikes and fractures the specimen.
*   The energy absorbed by the specimen during fracture is measured by the reduction in the hammer's potential energy (indicated by the height the hammer swings to after impact).

### Key Concepts in Impact Testing

*   **Impact Energy:** The energy absorbed by the specimen during fracture, typically measured in Joules (J) or foot-pounds (ft-lb).
*   **Ductile-to-Brittle Transition Temperature (DBTT):** The temperature range at which a material's fracture behavior changes from ductile (significant plastic deformation) to brittle (little to no plastic deformation).
*   **Notched Specimen:** Impact tests are typically conducted on specimens with a precisely machined notch to create a stress concentration and ensure fracture initiates reliably.

## 3. Izod Impact Test

### 3.1. Apparatus and Procedure

*   **Apparatus:**
    *   **Impact Testing Machine (Izod type):** Consists of a sturdy base, a pendulum hammer with a calibrated scale, a release mechanism, and a vise to hold the specimen.
    *   **Specimen:** Typically a rectangular bar with a V-shaped or U-shaped notch at the center. Standard dimensions vary (e.g., ASTM E23).
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  **Specimen Preparation:** Ensure the specimen is prepared according to the relevant standard (e.g., notch type, dimensions, surface finish).
    2.  **Temperature Control:** If testing at different temperatures, the specimen is conditioned to the desired temperature before testing.
    3.  **Specimen Placement:** The specimen is firmly clamped in the vise of the testing machine, with the notch facing away from the hammer's direction of travel. The notch should be positioned at a specific location relative to the striking edge of the hammer.
    4.  **Hammer Release:** The pendulum hammer is raised to a specific height, engaging a latch. The latch is released, allowing the hammer to swing down.
    5.  **Impact and Fracture:** The hammer strikes the specimen at the notch, causing it to fracture.
    6.  **Energy Measurement:** The hammer continues to swing after fracture. The height the hammer reaches on the other side is indicated on the calibrated scale. The difference in potential energy between the initial release height and the final height represents the energy absorbed by the specimen.
    7.  **Recording Data:** The absorbed energy is recorded from the machine's scale. The fracture appearance of the specimen should also be observed and recorded.

### 3.2. Specimen Configuration and Notching

*   **Specimen Shape:** Typically a rectangular bar.
*   **Notch Type:** Commonly V-notch or U-notch. The notch geometry (depth, angle, root radius) is critical for consistent results and is standardized.
    *   **V-notch:** Sharp V-shape.
    *   **U-notch:** Rounded U-shape.
*   **Purpose of Notch:** To concentrate stress at a specific point, facilitating controlled fracture and allowing for the measurement of energy absorption.

### 3.3. Interpretation of Results

*   **Impact Energy Value:** The primary result is the energy absorbed, usually in Joules (J).
*   **Fracture Appearance:**
    *   **Ductile Fracture:** Appears fibrous, grey, and dull. Indicates significant plastic deformation before fracture. Higher energy absorption.
    *   **Brittle Fracture:** Appears crystalline, shiny, and granular. Indicates little to no plastic deformation. Lower energy absorption.
    *   **Mixed Fracture:** Exhibits characteristics of both ductile and brittle fracture.
*   **Temperature Dependence:** By testing specimens at various temperatures, a ductile-to-brittle transition curve can be generated, showing how toughness changes with temperature.

## 4. Charpy Impact Test

### 4.1. Apparatus and Procedure

*   **Apparatus:**
    *   **Impact Testing Machine (Charpy type):** Similar to the Izod machine but with a different specimen clamping mechanism. The specimen is supported on two anvils, and the hammer strikes the notch from the opposite side of the support.
    *   **Specimen:** Similar to Izod, a rectangular bar with a V-shaped or U-shaped notch.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  **Specimen Preparation:** As with Izod, ensure proper specimen preparation according to standards.
    2.  **Temperature Control:** Condition specimens to the desired test temperature.
    3.  **Specimen Placement:** The specimen is placed in the Charpy testing machine, resting on two anvils. The notch should be positioned facing away from the striking hammer.
    4.  **Hammer Release:** The pendulum hammer is released from a known height.
    5.  **Impact and Fracture:** The hammer strikes the specimen opposite the notch, causing it to fracture.
    6.  **Energy Measurement:** Similar to Izod, the energy absorbed is determined by the reduction in the hammer's potential energy.
    7.  **Recording Data:** Record the absorbed energy and observe the fracture appearance.

### 4.2. Specimen Configuration and Notching

*   **Specimen Shape:** Typically a rectangular bar.
*   **Notch Type:** V-notch or U-notch are common, with standardized dimensions.
*   **Support Mechanism:** The key difference from Izod is how the specimen is supported – resting on two anvils, with the hammer striking the *back* of the notch.

### 4.3. Interpretation of Results

*   **Impact Energy Value:** Similar to Izod, the primary result is the absorbed energy.
*   **Fracture Appearance:** Interpretation of ductile vs. brittle fracture is the same as in the Izod test.
*   **Temperature Dependence:** Charpy tests are widely used to determine the DBTT of materials.

## 5. Comparison of Izod and Charpy Tests

| Feature          | Izod Impact Test                                 | Charpy Impact Test                               |
| :--------------- | :----------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------- |
| **Specimen Support** | Clamped at one end, cantilevered.                | Supported on two anvils at the ends.             |
| **Hammer Impact** | Strikes the specimen near the unsupported end.   | Strikes the specimen opposite the notch.         |
| **Stress State** | Primarily bending and shear.                     | Primarily bending.                               |
| **Notch Location**| Notch faces the hammer's direction of swing.     | Notch faces away from the hammer's direction of swing. |
| **Common Usage** | Historically significant, still used in some industries. | More widely used today for assessing toughness and DBTT. |
| **Sensitivity**  | Can be more sensitive to material variability. | Generally considered more representative of service conditions for many applications. |

**Important Note:** While both tests measure impact energy, they produce different values for the same material due to the different specimen support and loading conditions. Results from one test cannot be directly converted to the other.

## 6. Ductile-to-Brittle Transition Temperature (DBTT)

### Concept of DBTT

*   Many materials, especially BCC (Body-Centered Cubic) metals like steel, exhibit a significant drop in toughness as temperature decreases.
*   At high temperatures, the material fractures in a ductile manner, absorbing a large amount of energy.
*   As temperature decreases, the fracture mode transitions to brittle, with significantly reduced energy absorption.
*   The DBTT is the temperature range where this transition occurs.

### Determination of DBTT using Impact Tests

*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Conduct a series of impact tests (either Izod or Charpy) on specimens at various temperatures.
    2.  For each temperature, record the absorbed energy and observe the fracture appearance.
*   **Graphical Representation:** Plot the absorbed energy (or percentage of ductile fracture) against temperature.
*   **Defining DBTT:** There are several ways to define the DBTT from the graph:
    *   **Minimum Impact Energy:** The temperature at which a specific minimum impact energy value is achieved.
    *   **Fracture Appearance:** The temperature at which the fracture surface changes from predominantly fibrous (ductile) to predominantly crystalline (brittle).
    *   **Lateral Expansion:** The amount of plastic deformation on the side of the specimen opposite the notch. A decrease in lateral expansion indicates a transition to brittle behavior.

**Example:** For a particular steel, you might test specimens at -40°C, -20°C, 0°C, 20°C, and 40°C. If the energy absorbed increases significantly from 0°C to 20°C, and the fracture appearance changes from crystalline to fibrous in this range, then the DBTT for this steel lies between 0°C and 20°C.

## 7. Factors Influencing Impact Test Results

*   **Temperature:** As discussed, the primary factor.
*   **Strain Rate:** Higher strain rates tend to decrease toughness.
*   **Notch Geometry:** The radius and depth of the notch significantly affect stress concentration and thus absorbed energy.
*   **Specimen Size and Shape:** Standardized dimensions are crucial for comparability.
*   **Surface Finish:** Surface defects can act as crack initiation sites.
*   **Microstructure:** Grain size, alloying elements, heat treatment, and presence of inclusions all play a role.
*   **Pre-existing Defects:** Cracks or voids in the material can lower impact toughness.

## 8. Applications of Impact Testing

*   **Material Selection:** Choosing materials suitable for specific operating temperatures and loading conditions.
*   **Quality Control:** Ensuring consistency in the toughness of manufactured components.
*   **Research and Development:** Investigating the effects of alloying, heat treatment, and processing on material toughness.
*   **Failure Analysis:** Understanding why components failed under impact.
*   **Assessing Service Life:** Predicting how materials will perform in environments with potential for impact loading.

## Answers

**Answer 1:**
Toughness is a material's ability to absorb energy and deform plastically before fracturing under a sudden impact. Strength is a material's resistance to permanent deformation or fracture under a static load. Toughness relates to dynamic loading and energy absorption, while strength relates to static resistance.

**Answer 2:**
The fundamental principle is to measure the energy a material absorbs when it fractures under a sudden, high-speed impact. This is done by releasing a pendulum hammer from a known height to strike and break a notched specimen, and then measuring the residual energy of the hammer.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Specimen Support:** In Izod, the specimen is clamped at one end like a cantilever. In Charpy, the specimen is supported on two anvils at its ends.
*   **Hammer Impact:** In Izod, the hammer strikes the specimen near the unsupported end. In Charpy, the hammer strikes the specimen on the side opposite the notch.

**Answer 4:**
Generally, as the temperature decreases, the impact toughness of steel decreases. This is because the material's fracture mode tends to shift from ductile to brittle at lower temperatures.

**Answer 5:**
The observed results indicate that the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) for this steel lies somewhere between -30°C and 20°C. At -30°C, the fracture is predominantly brittle with low energy absorption, while at 20°C, the fracture is ductile with high energy absorption.

**Answer 6:**
Impact tests are performed on notched specimens to create a localized stress concentration. This ensures that fracture initiates reliably at a specific point, allowing for a consistent measurement of the energy required to fracture the material under impact conditions. The notch geometry is standardized to ensure comparable results.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Toughness vs. Strength:** Understand the fundamental difference between resistance to static load (strength) and resistance to dynamic impact (toughness).
*   **Ductile-to-Brittle Transition:** Be aware that many steels, especially at low temperatures or high strain rates, can transition from ductile to brittle fracture. Impact tests are crucial for characterizing this behavior.
*   **Izod vs. Charpy:** Know the key differences in apparatus, specimen support, and impact location, and understand that their results are not interchangeable.
*   **Notch Importance:** The notch is critical for concentrating stress and ensuring repeatable fracture.
*   **Fracture Appearance:** Correlate absorbed energy with fracture surface appearance (fibrous = ductile, crystalline = brittle).
*   **Temperature Dependence:** Impact toughness is highly sensitive to temperature.
*   **Standardization:** Always adhere to relevant ASTM or other material testing standards for specimen preparation, machine calibration, and testing procedures for reliable and comparable results.
