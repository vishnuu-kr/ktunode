# Materials Testing Lab: Module 11 - Estimation of Compressive Strength of Timber Specimen

## 1. Introduction to Timber and its Properties

Timber, a natural composite material derived from trees, is widely used in construction, furniture manufacturing, and various other applications due to its strength, renewability, and aesthetic appeal. However, its properties can vary significantly depending on the species, growth conditions, and processing methods.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Timber Species:** Different species of timber possess distinct mechanical properties. For example, hardwoods (like oak, maple) are generally stronger and denser than softwoods (like pine, fir).
*   **Grain Direction:** Timber has a natural grain structure. Its strength properties are significantly higher when loaded parallel to the grain (axial compression) compared to perpendicular to the grain (transverse compression).
*   **Moisture Content:** The moisture content of timber greatly influences its strength and stiffness. Higher moisture content generally leads to lower strength.
*   **Defects:** Natural defects like knots, cracks, shakes, and decay can significantly reduce the compressive strength of timber.

**Importance of Compressive Strength:**

Compressive strength is a crucial property for timber, especially when used in structural applications where it supports loads. Understanding its compressive strength helps engineers and designers to:

*   Select appropriate timber species for specific applications.
*   Determine safe working loads for timber components.
*   Design timber structures that can withstand applied compressive forces without failure.

## 2. Types of Compressive Strength in Timber

Timber can be subjected to compressive forces in two primary directions:

### 2.1. Compression Parallel to Grain (Axial Compression)

This is the most common and significant type of compressive strength for structural timber. It refers to the ability of timber to resist crushing forces when applied along the length of the grain.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Axial Compression:** Force applied along the longitudinal axis of the timber specimen.
*   **Crushing:** Failure occurs due to the buckling or crushing of wood fibers.
*   **High Strength:** Timber exhibits its highest compressive strength when loaded parallel to the grain.

**Example:** A wooden column supporting a roof load is experiencing compression parallel to the grain.

### 2.2. Compression Perpendicular to Grain (Transverse Compression)

This refers to the ability of timber to resist crushing forces when applied perpendicular to the length of the grain. This is generally much lower than compression parallel to the grain.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Transverse Compression:** Force applied perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the timber specimen.
*   **Shearing and Crushing:** Failure can involve a combination of shearing of the wood fibers and crushing.
*   **Lower Strength:** Timber is significantly weaker when loaded perpendicular to the grain.

**Example:** The bearing area of a wooden beam resting on a joist is subjected to compression perpendicular to the grain.

## 3. Factors Affecting Compressive Strength

Several factors influence the compressive strength of timber. Understanding these is vital for accurate estimation and application.

**Key Factors:**

*   **Species of Timber:** As mentioned, hardwoods are generally stronger than softwoods.
*   **Moisture Content:**
    *   **Higher moisture content:** Lower compressive strength.
    *   **Lower moisture content (well-seasoned):** Higher compressive strength.
*   **Presence of Defects:**
    *   **Knots:** Reduce the cross-sectional area and introduce stress concentrations, weakening the timber.
    *   **Cracks/Splits:** Act as initiation points for failure.
    *   **Shakes:** Separations between annual rings can lead to buckling.
    *   **Decay/Rot:** Weakens the wood structure significantly.
*   **Grain Angle:** Compressive strength decreases significantly as the angle of the applied load to the grain increases.
*   **Density:** Generally, denser timbers tend to have higher compressive strength.
*   **Temperature:** Extreme temperatures can affect wood's mechanical properties.

**Important Point to Remember:** The strength of timber is highly anisotropic, meaning it varies significantly with the direction of the grain.

## 4. Testing Procedure for Compressive Strength

The estimation of compressive strength is typically done through standardized mechanical testing procedures.

**Key Steps in a Typical Compression Test:**

1.  **Specimen Preparation:**
    *   **Select Uniform Specimens:** Choose timber specimens that are free from visible defects like knots, cracks, and decay.
    *   **Standard Dimensions:** Prepare specimens according to relevant standards (e.g., ASTM D143 for small clear specimens). Common dimensions for compression parallel to the grain are often square cross-sections with a length-to-width ratio that prevents premature buckling. For compression perpendicular to the grain, the specimen dimensions will differ to focus the load on a specific area.
    *   **Moisture Conditioning:** Specimens are usually conditioned to a specific moisture content (e.g., 12% for seasoned timber) in a controlled environment.
    *   **Measure Dimensions:** Accurately measure the cross-sectional dimensions (width and thickness) and the length of the specimen.

2.  **Test Setup:**
    *   **Universal Testing Machine (UTM):** The test is conducted using a UTM equipped with compression platens.
    *   **Platen Alignment:** Ensure the platens are parallel and the specimen is centered on the lower platen.
    *   **Load Cell:** Select an appropriate load cell capacity for the expected strength of the timber.

3.  **Performing the Test:**
    *   **Compression Parallel to Grain:**
        *   Place the specimen vertically between the platens, ensuring the load is applied parallel to the grain.
        *   Apply the load at a constant rate of crosshead movement as specified by the relevant standard.
        *   Record the applied load and the corresponding deformation of the specimen.
        *   The test continues until the specimen fails (e.g., crushing, buckling).
    *   **Compression Perpendicular to Grain:**
        *   Place the specimen on its side between the platens.
        *   Apply the load over a defined area (often using a bearing block) perpendicular to the grain.
        *   Record the load and deformation.
        *   The test continues until a specified deformation is reached or failure occurs.

4.  **Data Analysis:**
    *   **Load-Deformation Curve:** Plot the recorded data to obtain a load-deformation curve.
    *   **Calculate Stress:** Compressive stress is calculated as:
        $$ \text{Compressive Stress} (\sigma) = \frac{\text{Applied Load (P)}}{\text{Cross-sectional Area (A)}} $$
        Where 'A' is the cross-sectional area of the specimen perpendicular to the direction of the applied load.
    *   **Determine Compressive Strength:**
        *   **Maximum Compressive Strength:** The peak load achieved during the test, divided by the original cross-sectional area.
        *   **Proportional Limit:** The point on the load-deformation curve where it deviates from linearity.
        *   **Elastic Modulus (MOE):** Can be calculated from the initial linear portion of the stress-strain curve (though this is more related to stiffness than strength, it's often recorded).
    *   **Calculate Strain:** Strain is calculated as:
        $$ \text{Strain} (\epsilon) = \frac{\text{Change in Length} (\Delta L)}{\text{Original Length} (L_0)} $$
    *   **Moisture Content Correction:** If the tested timber's moisture content differs from the standard (e.g., 12%), the strength values may need to be adjusted using correction factors provided in relevant standards.

## 5. Calculation of Compressive Strength

The primary goal of the testing is to calculate the compressive strength.

**Formulas:**

*   **Cross-sectional Area (for a rectangular specimen):**
    $$ A = \text{width} \times \text{thickness} $$
*   **Compressive Stress:**
    $$ \sigma = \frac{P}{A} $$
*   **Compressive Strength (Maximum):** This is the maximum stress the material can withstand before failure.
    $$ \text{Compressive Strength} = \frac{P_{\text{max}}}{A} $$
    Where $P_{\text{max}}$ is the maximum load applied before failure.

**Units:**

*   Load (P): Newtons (N) or Kilopascals (kPa)
*   Area (A): Square millimeters ($mm^2$) or Square meters ($m^2$)
*   Stress ($\sigma$): Pascals (Pa), Megapascals (MPa), or Gigapascals (GPa)

**Example Calculation:**

Suppose a timber specimen with a cross-section of 20 mm x 20 mm fails under a maximum load of 15,000 N when tested in compression parallel to the grain.

*   **Cross-sectional Area (A):**
    $$ A = 20 \text{ mm} \times 20 \text{ mm} = 400 \text{ mm}^2 $$
*   **Compressive Strength:**
    $$ \text{Compressive Strength} = \frac{15,000 \text{ N}}{400 \text{ mm}^2} = 37.5 \text{ N/mm}^2 = 37.5 \text{ MPa} $$

## 6. Interpretation and Reporting of Results

The results of the compression test are used to evaluate the quality and suitability of the timber.

**Key aspects of interpretation and reporting:**

*   **Compare with Standards:** Compare the obtained compressive strength values with the values specified in relevant timber design codes or standards for the particular species.
*   **Consider Moisture Content:** Report the moisture content at which the test was performed. If adjustments are made for moisture content, clearly state the original and adjusted values.
*   **Note Failure Modes:** Describe the type of failure observed (e.g., crushing, buckling, shear failure) as this provides valuable insight into the timber's behavior.
*   **Report Average Values:** For multiple specimens of the same timber type, report the average compressive strength along with the standard deviation.
*   **Include Visual Observations:** Mention any defects observed in the specimen and their potential impact on the results.
*   **Graphing:** Present the load-deformation or stress-strain curves graphically.

**Reporting Elements:**

*   Timber species
*   Specimen dimensions
*   Moisture content
*   Load applied at failure
*   Calculated compressive strength
*   Type of failure
*   Any relevant observations or deviations from the procedure

## 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

A rectangular timber specimen of pine wood has dimensions 25 mm x 30 mm. It is tested in compression parallel to the grain and fails at a maximum load of 18,000 N. Calculate the compressive strength of the timber.

**Answer:**

*   Cross-sectional Area (A) = 25 mm * 30 mm = 750 $mm^2$
*   Compressive Strength = Load / Area = 18,000 N / 750 $mm^2$ = 24 N/$mm^2$ = 24 MPa

**Question 2:**

What is the primary difference in how a load is applied when testing timber for compression parallel to the grain versus compression perpendicular to the grain?

**Answer:**

When testing for compression parallel to the grain, the load is applied along the longitudinal axis of the timber specimen, following the grain direction. When testing for compression perpendicular to the grain, the load is applied across the width or thickness of the specimen, perpendicular to the grain direction.

**Question 3:**

List at least three factors that can significantly reduce the compressive strength of timber.

**Answer:**

*   Presence of knots
*   Presence of cracks or splits
*   Higher moisture content
*   Decay or rot

**Question 4:**

A timber column is designed to support a load. If the load is applied along the length of the column, what type of compressive strength is primarily being considered?

**Answer:**

Compression parallel to the grain (axial compression).

**Question 5:**

A timber specimen tested at 18% moisture content has a compressive strength of 28 MPa. If the standard strength for that species at 12% moisture content is desired, and assuming a typical correction factor indicates that strength increases by 5% for every 1% decrease in moisture content below 12%, what would be the estimated compressive strength at 12%?

**Answer:**

This question requires a more complex calculation involving correction factors. For simplicity, let's assume a linear relationship where strength increases by 5% for every 1% reduction in moisture.

*   Moisture difference = 18% - 12% = 6%
*   Estimated increase in strength = 6% * 5% = 30%
*   Estimated strength at 12% moisture content = 28 MPa * (1 + 0.30) = 36.4 MPa

**Note:** Real-world moisture content correction factors are more complex and found in standards. This is a simplified illustration.

## 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Anisotropy:** Timber is highly anisotropic; strength varies drastically with grain direction.
*   **Moisture Content is Critical:** Always consider and report the moisture content.
*   **Defects Matter:** Natural defects significantly reduce strength.
*   **Standardized Testing:** Follow established standards (like ASTM D143) for consistent and comparable results.
*   **Failure Mode Analysis:** Observe and report how the specimen fails, as it provides valuable information.
*   **Safety First:** Always adhere to laboratory safety protocols when operating testing machines.
