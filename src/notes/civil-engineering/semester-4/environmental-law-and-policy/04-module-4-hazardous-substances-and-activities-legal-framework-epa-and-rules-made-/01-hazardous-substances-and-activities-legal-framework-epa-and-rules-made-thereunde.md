# Environmental Law and Policy: Module 4 - Hazardous Substances and Activities

## Topic: Hazardous Substances and Activities Legal Framework: EPA and Rules Made Thereunder

---

### 1. Introduction to Hazardous Substances and Activities

This module focuses on the legal and regulatory framework governing hazardous substances and activities, with a particular emphasis on the role of the **Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)** in the United States and the rules it promulgates.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Hazardous Substance:** A substance that, by reason of its quantity, concentration, or physical, chemical, or infectious characteristics, may present a substantial hazard to the public health, safety, or the environment. This definition is broad and can encompass a wide range of materials.
*   **Hazardous Activity:** An activity that involves the generation, storage, treatment, transportation, or disposal of hazardous substances, or activities that pose a significant risk of environmental damage.
*   **Environmental Risk:** The probability that an adverse effect on the environment will occur as a result of exposure to a hazard.

**Importance:**

The regulation of hazardous substances and activities is critical for:
*   Protecting human health from exposure to toxic chemicals.
*   Preventing environmental contamination of air, water, and soil.
*   Ensuring the safe management of wastes and industrial processes.
*   Promoting sustainable development by minimizing the long-term impacts of chemical use.

---

### 2. The Role of the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)

The EPA is the primary federal agency responsible for implementing and enforcing environmental laws in the United States. Its role in regulating hazardous substances and activities is multifaceted.

**Key Functions of the EPA:**

*   **Rulemaking:** Developing and issuing regulations (rules) under various environmental statutes.
*   **Enforcement:** Monitoring compliance with environmental laws and taking action against violators.
*   **Permitting:** Issuing permits to facilities that manage or release hazardous substances.
*   **Research and Development:** Conducting scientific research to understand environmental risks and develop solutions.
*   **Information Dissemination:** Providing guidance and information to the public and regulated entities.

**Important Point to Remember:** The EPA's authority is derived from Congress through legislation. The rules it creates must be consistent with the intent of these laws.

### 3. Key Federal Statutes Governing Hazardous Substances and Activities

While the EPA implements many laws, several cornerstone statutes specifically address hazardous substances and activities.

#### 3.1. Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA)

RCRA provides a "cradle-to-grave" management system for **hazardous waste**.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Hazardous Waste:** Wastes that possess specific characteristics (ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, toxicity) or are listed by the EPA as hazardous.
*   **"Cradle-to-Grave" Management:** Regulates hazardous waste from its generation, through its transportation, treatment, storage, and ultimate disposal.
*   **Solid Waste:** RCRA also regulates non-hazardous solid waste, but the focus for this module is hazardous waste.

**Key Provisions and Rules:**

*   **Identification and Listing of Hazardous Wastes (40 CFR Part 261):**
    *   **Characteristic Wastes:** Wastes exhibiting ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, or toxicity.
    *   **Listed Wastes:** Wastes from specific sources (F, K lists) or discarded commercial chemical products (P, U lists).
*   **Standards for Generators of Hazardous Waste (40 CFR Part 262):**
    *   Requirements for identifying, accumulating, and manifesting hazardous waste.
    *   Different requirements based on the quantity of hazardous waste generated (large quantity generators, small quantity generators, conditionally exempt small quantity generators).
*   **Standards for Transporters of Hazardous Waste (40 CFR Part 263):**
    *   Manifest requirements, placarding of vehicles, and emergency response information.
*   **Standards for Owners and Operators of Hazardous Waste Treatment, Storage, and Disposal Facilities (TSDFs) (40 CFR Parts 264 & 265):**
    *   Detailed requirements for facility design, operation, monitoring, closure, and post-closure care.
    *   **Permitting:** TSDFs must obtain permits under the **Hazardous Waste Management System (HWMS)**.
*   **Land Disposal Restrictions (LDRs) (40 CFR Part 268):**
    *   Prohibits or restricts the land disposal of untreated hazardous wastes, requiring treatment to specific standards before disposal.
*   **Underground Storage Tank (UST) Program (RCRA Subtitle I):**
    *   Sets technical and financial responsibility requirements for owners and operators of underground storage tanks containing petroleum or hazardous substances.

**Example:**

A manufacturing plant generates spent solvents that are flammable and toxic. Under RCRA, this spent solvent would be classified as hazardous waste. The plant, as a generator, must follow specific rules for accumulating and manifesting this waste before it is transported by a licensed transporter to a permitted TSDF for treatment or disposal.

**Important Point to Remember:** RCRA's primary goal is to prevent the mismanagement of hazardous waste that could contaminate the environment.

#### 3.2. Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA), also known as Superfund

CERCLA addresses the cleanup of uncontrolled or abandoned hazardous waste sites and the response to accidental releases of hazardous substances.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Superfund Sites:** Sites contaminated with hazardous substances that require investigation and cleanup.
*   **Release:** Any spilling, leaking, pumping, pouring, emitting, emptying, discharging, injecting, escaping, leaching, dumping, or disposing into the environment of a hazardous substance.
*   **Potentially Responsible Parties (PRPs):** Individuals or entities that may be held liable for the costs of cleaning up a Superfund site.

**Key Provisions and Rules:**

*   **Liability for Cleanup Costs (CERCLA Section 107):**
    *   Establishes strict liability for PRPs, meaning they are liable regardless of fault.
    *   **Classes of PRPs:**
        *   Current owners and operators of a facility.
        *   Owners and operators at the time of disposal.
        *   Generators who arranged for disposal or treatment of hazardous substances.
        *   Transporters who selected the site for disposal.
*   **National Contingency Plan (NCP):**
    *   Outlines the procedures and standards for responding to releases of hazardous substances and for cleaning up hazardous waste sites. The EPA uses the NCP as guidance for Superfund cleanups.
*   **National Priorities List (NPL):**
    *   A list of the nation's most serious uncontrolled hazardous waste sites that are priorities for cleanup under Superfund.
*   **Remedial Actions:** Long-term cleanup activities at Superfund sites.
*   **Removal Actions:** Short-term cleanup actions to address immediate threats.
*   **Community Right-to-Know:** Provisions requiring facilities to report the storage and release of hazardous substances to state and local authorities and to the public.

**Example:**

A former industrial site is found to have soil contaminated with lead and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from past disposal practices. This site might be placed on the NPL. The EPA could then investigate and identify PRPs, such as the company that formerly owned and operated the site and companies that sent waste there. The PRPs could be held liable for the costs of site investigation and cleanup, which might involve soil excavation and off-site treatment.

**Important Point to Remember:** CERCLA's goal is to hold those responsible for contamination accountable for cleanup costs and to protect public health and the environment from hazardous substances.

#### 3.3. Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA)

TSCA provides the EPA with the authority to regulate the manufacturing, processing, distribution, use, and disposal of **chemical substances**.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Chemical Substance:** Any organic or inorganic substance of a particular molecular identity.
*   **Premanufacture Notice (PMN):** Manufacturers must submit a PMN to the EPA before manufacturing or importing a new chemical substance for commercial purposes.

**Key Provisions and Rules:**

*   **Inventory of Chemical Substances (TSCA Section 8(b)):**
    *   The EPA maintains a list of all existing chemical substances manufactured or processed in the United States.
*   **Premanufacture Notification (PMN) (TSCA Section 5):**
    *   Requires companies to notify the EPA at least 90 days before manufacturing or importing a new chemical.
    *   The EPA reviews the chemical for potential risks and can impose restrictions or prohibit its production.
*   **Regulation of Existing Chemicals (TSCA Section 6):**
    *   If a chemical substance presents an unreasonable risk of injury to health or the environment, the EPA can regulate its manufacture, processing, distribution, use, or disposal.
    *   Regulations can include labeling requirements, restrictions, bans, or phase-outs.
*   **Reporting and Recordkeeping (TSCA Sections 8(a), 8(d), 8(e)):**
    *   Requires companies to maintain records and report certain information to the EPA regarding chemical production, use, and health effects.
    *   **Significant New Use Rules (SNURs):** Used to require notification to the EPA before a chemical is used in a way that was not previously reviewed.
*   **Chemical Data Reporting (CDR):** A TSCA rule requiring manufacturers and importers of specified chemical substances to report production volume and use information to the EPA every four years.

**Example:**

A company develops a new type of plasticizer intended for widespread use in consumer products. Before they can begin manufacturing or importing this chemical, they must submit a PMN to the EPA. The EPA will review the submitted data to assess potential risks to human health and the environment. If the EPA determines the chemical poses an unreasonable risk, they can impose restrictions on its use or even ban it.

**Important Point to Remember:** TSCA is a proactive law designed to prevent harm from chemicals before they enter widespread commerce.

#### 3.4. Clean Air Act (CAA) and Clean Water Act (CWA)

While primarily focused on air and water quality, these acts also play a crucial role in regulating hazardous substances through emissions and discharges.

**Clean Air Act (CAA):**

*   **Hazardous Air Pollutants (HAPs) (CAA Section 112):**
    *   Identifies and regulates emissions of toxic air pollutants that may cause or contribute to an increase in mortality or an increase in serious irreversible or incapacitating reversible illness.
    *   EPA establishes **National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAPs)** for specific source categories.
*   **New Source Performance Standards (NSPS):** Standards for controlling emissions from new or modified stationary sources.

**Clean Water Act (CWA):**

*   **Effluent Limitations:** Sets standards for the quality of pollutants that can be discharged into navigable waters.
*   **National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) Permits:** Permits required for any discharge of pollutants from a point source into waters of the United States. These permits often limit the discharge of hazardous substances.
*   **Water Quality Standards:** States establish water quality standards to protect aquatic life and human health, which can influence permit limits for hazardous substances.

**Example:**

A chemical manufacturing plant may have processes that release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into the air. Under the CAA, if these VOCs are classified as HAPs, the EPA will establish NESHAPs that the plant must meet, potentially requiring the installation of pollution control equipment. Similarly, if the plant discharges wastewater containing hazardous chemicals into a river, it will need an NPDES permit that sets limits on the concentration of those chemicals in the discharge.

**Important Point to Remember:** The CAA and CWA regulate hazardous substances by controlling their release into the air and water, respectively, to protect public health and environmental quality.

### 4. EPA Rules and Their Impact

The EPA's rules are the practical implementation of these statutes. They provide specific requirements, standards, and procedures that regulated entities must follow.

**How EPA Rules are Made (Rulemaking Process):**

1.  **Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM):** Published in the Federal Register, detailing the proposed rule and its rationale.
2.  **Public Comment Period:** Allows interested parties to submit written comments, data, and arguments on the proposed rule.
3.  **Public Hearings:** May be held to allow for oral testimony and discussion.
4.  **Review of Comments:** The EPA considers all comments received.
5.  **Final Rule:** Published in the Federal Register, often with revisions based on public comments.

**Impact of EPA Rules:**

*   **Compliance Requirements:** Mandate specific actions, technologies, or management practices.
*   **Permitting Systems:** Establish frameworks for obtaining and maintaining permits.
*   **Reporting Obligations:** Require regular reporting of data and activities to the EPA.
*   **Enforcement Mechanisms:** Outline penalties for non-compliance.

**Examples of Specific EPA Rules (Illustrative - actual CFR numbers vary and are complex):**

*   **40 CFR Part 261:** Lists and defines hazardous wastes under RCRA.
*   **40 CFR Part 122:** Details the requirements for NPDES permits under the CWA.
*   **40 CFR Part 63:** Establishes NESHAPs for various industrial source categories under the CAA.
*   **40 CFR Part 372:** The Toxics Release Inventory (TRI) reporting requirements under EPCRA (Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act).

**Important Point to Remember:** EPA rules are legally binding and must be adhered to by regulated entities. Non-compliance can result in significant penalties.

### 5. Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act (EPCRA)

EPCRA, also known as the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act, focuses on preparing for and responding to chemical emergencies and providing the public with information about hazardous substances in their communities.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Extremely Hazardous Substances (EHSs):** A specific list of chemicals identified by the EPA that could pose a significant risk in case of a release.
*   **Local Emergency Planning Committees (LEPCs):** Committees responsible for developing emergency plans for their jurisdictions.
*   **Toxics Release Inventory (TRI):** An annual public report of environmental releases and other waste management activities for certain toxic chemicals.

**Key Provisions and Rules:**

*   **Emergency Planning (EPCRA Sections 301-303):**
    *   Requires facilities that store or use certain **Extremely Hazardous Substances (EHSs)** above threshold quantities to notify state and local emergency planning authorities.
    *   Requires the creation of **Local Emergency Planning Committees (LEPCs)** to develop emergency response plans.
*   **Emergency Release Notification (EPCRA Section 304):**
    *   Requires facilities to immediately notify relevant authorities of any release of EHSs or certain other hazardous substances that exceeds reportable quantities.
*   **Hazardous Chemical Inventory Reporting (EPCRA Sections 311-312):**
    *   Requires facilities to submit Safety Data Sheets (SDSs) or lists of hazardous chemicals present at the facility to state and local authorities and fire departments.
    *   Requires the submission of annual Tier I or Tier II reports detailing the types and quantities of hazardous chemicals stored.
*   **Toxics Release Inventory (TRI) Reporting (EPCRA Section 313):**
    *   Requires certain facilities to report annually their releases and other waste management activities for specific toxic chemicals. This information is made publicly available.

**Example:**

A chemical plant stores large quantities of ammonia, which is an EHS. The plant must notify its LEPC and participate in the development of a community emergency response plan. If there is a significant accidental release of ammonia, the plant must immediately report it to the LEPC and the National Response Center. Additionally, if the plant manufactures or uses certain listed toxic chemicals above specific thresholds, it must report its releases of those chemicals annually to the EPA for inclusion in the TRI.

**Important Point to Remember:** EPCRA is crucial for ensuring that communities are prepared for chemical emergencies and have access to information about the hazardous chemicals present in their vicinity.

### 6. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
Which federal statute provides a "cradle-to-grave" management system for hazardous waste?
a) CERCLA
b) TSCA
c) RCRA
d) CWA

**Answer:** c) RCRA

**Question 2:**
Under CERCLA, who are the "Potentially Responsible Parties" (PRPs)?
a) Only current owners and operators of a contaminated site.
b) Only companies that generated hazardous waste disposed of at a site.
c) Individuals and entities that may be liable for cleanup costs, including current owners, past owners, generators, and transporters.
d) Only the EPA.

**Answer:** c) Individuals and entities that may be liable for cleanup costs, including current owners, past owners, generators, and transporters.

**Question 3:**
What is the primary purpose of the Premanufacture Notice (PMN) requirement under TSCA?
a) To report accidental releases of chemicals.
b) To obtain a permit for disposing of hazardous waste.
c) To notify the EPA of a new chemical substance before it is manufactured or imported.
d) To report annual chemical releases to the public.

**Answer:** c) To notify the EPA of a new chemical substance before it is manufactured or imported.

**Question 4:**
The Toxics Release Inventory (TRI) is a program established under which act?
a) RCRA
b) CERCLA
c) TSCA
d) EPCRA

**Answer:** d) EPCRA

**Question 5 (Scenario-based):**
A company discovers that a former underground storage tank at its facility is leaking gasoline into the soil. What federal law is most likely to govern the cleanup of this contamination and the company's responsibilities? Explain why.

**Answer:**
The **Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA)**, specifically its **Underground Storage Tank (UST) program (Subtitle I)**, is most likely to govern this situation. RCRA Subtitle I sets technical and financial responsibility requirements for owners and operators of USTs containing petroleum or hazardous substances. The leaking tank and resulting soil contamination would trigger these regulations, requiring the company to investigate the extent of the contamination and conduct appropriate cleanup actions. CERCLA might also be involved if the contamination is severe and poses a significant threat, potentially leading to site remediation under the Superfund program.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **EPA's Central Role:** The EPA is the primary federal agency responsible for implementing and enforcing laws related to hazardous substances and activities.
*   **Statutory Authority:** The EPA's actions are based on the authority granted by Congress through laws like RCRA, CERCLA, TSCA, CAA, CWA, and EPCRA.
*   **"Cradle-to-Grave" vs. Site Cleanup:** RCRA focuses on the ongoing management of hazardous waste, while CERCLA focuses on the cleanup of past contamination.
*   **Chemical Lifecycle:** TSCA regulates chemicals from their introduction into commerce through their use and disposal, aiming for prevention.
*   **Public Information and Emergency Preparedness:** EPCRA emphasizes transparency with the public and readiness for chemical emergencies.
*   **Rulemaking is Key:** The EPA's detailed rules (found in the Code of Federal Regulations, Title 40) are the specific requirements that regulated entities must follow.
*   **Liability:** Under laws like CERCLA, significant financial liability can be imposed on parties responsible for hazardous substance contamination.
*   **Interconnectedness:** Many environmental laws work together; for example, a facility might be regulated under RCRA for waste, CERCLA for past contamination, and CAA/CWA for ongoing emissions/discharges.
