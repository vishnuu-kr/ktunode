# Environmental Law and Policy: Module 4 - Hazardous Substances and Activities

## Topic: Customary Principles

This module delves into the legal framework governing hazardous substances and activities, primarily focusing on the role of the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and its associated rules. Within this framework, we will explore the often-overlooked yet fundamental influence of customary principles that shape environmental law and policy.

### Learning Outcomes:

By the end of this topic, you will be able to:

*   Understand the concept of customary international environmental law and its relevance to hazardous substances and activities.
*   Identify key customary principles that inform domestic environmental law, particularly in relation to hazardous waste management and pollution control.
*   Analyze the relationship between customary principles and codified laws (e.g., EPA regulations).
*   Evaluate the challenges and opportunities in applying customary principles in the context of hazardous substances and activities.
*   Recognize the evolution and impact of customary principles on international agreements and national legislation concerning hazardous substances.

### 1. Understanding Customary International Environmental Law

Customary international law (CIL) is a source of international law that arises from the consistent practice of states followed by them from a sense of legal obligation. It is distinct from treaty law, which is based on express consent between states.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **State Practice:** This refers to the actual conduct of states in their dealings with each other. It must be widespread, consistent, and generally uniform. For environmental matters, this can include national legislation, administrative decisions, diplomatic communications, and judicial pronouncements.
*   **Opinio Juris sive Necessitatis (Opinion of law or necessity):** This is the belief by states that their practice is legally required or permitted. States must believe they are acting out of a sense of legal obligation, not simply out of courtesy or habit.
*   **Generality of Practice:** While not requiring universal participation, the practice must be followed by a significant number of states, representing different legal and political systems.
*   **Consistency:** The practice must be generally consistent, although minor deviations are not necessarily fatal to the existence of a custom.

**Relevance to Hazardous Substances and Activities:**

Customary principles provide a foundational layer for addressing transboundary environmental harm and regulating activities that pose risks to the environment and human health. They often address issues that may not be explicitly covered by treaties or where treaties are still evolving.

**Examples:**

*   **The prohibition of transboundary harm:** While codified in Principle 21 of the Stockholm Declaration and Principle 2 of the Rio Declaration, the underlying principle that states have a responsibility not to cause significant environmental damage to other states or to areas beyond national jurisdiction has long been recognized as a customary norm. This is directly relevant to the management of hazardous waste disposal and the control of industrial emissions that can cross borders.

### 2. Key Customary Principles Informing Domestic Environmental Law

While CIL primarily governs relations between states, its principles often permeate and influence domestic legal frameworks, including those administered by the EPA.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Precautionary Principle:** In the face of uncertainty about the potential for significant harm, states should take precautionary measures to prevent environmental damage. This means that the burden of proof may shift to the proponent of an activity to demonstrate its safety, rather than requiring proof of harm before action is taken.
    *   *Example:* The EPA might require extensive testing and risk assessments for new chemicals before they are approved for use, even if scientific consensus on their harm is not yet absolute.
*   **Polluter Pays Principle:** The costs of preventing and controlling pollution, as well as the remediation of environmental damage, should be borne by the polluter.
    *   *Example:* Under CERCLA (Superfund), the EPA can hold parties responsible for the cleanup of hazardous waste sites, making them financially liable for the remediation costs.
*   **Prevention Principle:** It is generally preferable to prevent pollution at its source rather than to deal with its consequences.
    *   *Example:* EPA regulations often mandate pollution control technologies for industrial facilities to minimize emissions and discharges of hazardous substances before they enter the environment.
*   **Duty of Due Diligence/Due Care:** States have a responsibility to exercise due diligence in preventing activities within their jurisdiction or control from causing significant harm to the environment of other states or areas beyond national jurisdiction.
    *   *Example:* A state might have regulations requiring proper containment and disposal of hazardous waste to prevent seepage into groundwater that could affect neighboring states.
*   **Cooperation and Information Exchange:** States should cooperate with each other in addressing transboundary environmental problems and exchange information related to hazardous substances and activities.
    *   *Example:* The EPA collaborates with other countries through international agreements and shared databases to track the movement of hazardous waste and to develop best practices for its management.

### 3. Relationship Between Customary Principles and Codified Laws (EPA Rules)

Customary principles act as both a source of inspiration and a guiding force for the development of codified environmental laws and EPA regulations.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Codification:** The process of translating customary rules into written laws, treaties, or regulations.
*   **Interpretation:** Customary principles can inform the interpretation of ambiguous provisions in statutes and regulations. Courts and administrative bodies may look to these underlying principles when applying the law.
*   **Gap-Filling:** Where statutory or regulatory law is silent on a particular issue related to hazardous substances, customary principles can provide a basis for decision-making and regulatory action.
*   **Legitimization:** The incorporation of customary principles into domestic law can lend legitimacy and reinforce the ethical underpinnings of environmental protection measures.

**Examples:**

*   **CERCLA (Superfund):** While a statutory law, CERCLA's liability provisions, which often hold parties responsible for cleanup costs regardless of fault, reflect the spirit of the "polluter pays" principle.
*   **RCRA (Resource Conservation and Recovery Act):** RCRA's cradle-to-grave management of hazardous waste embodies the prevention and polluter pays principles. EPA regulations promulgated under RCRA ensure strict handling, treatment, and disposal standards.
*   **Clean Water Act:** The discharge limits and permitting requirements under the Clean Water Act are direct manifestations of the prevention and polluter pays principles in relation to water pollution.

### 4. Challenges and Opportunities in Applying Customary Principles

While crucial, applying customary principles can present certain difficulties, alongside significant opportunities.

**Challenges:**

*   **Proof of Existence:** Establishing the existence and content of a customary principle can be challenging, requiring extensive research into state practice and opinio juris.
*   **Scope and Applicability:** Determining the precise scope and applicability of a customary principle to a specific situation involving hazardous substances can be complex.
*   **State Sovereignty:** Some states may resist the application of customary principles that they perceive as infringing upon their national sovereignty.
*   **Enforcement:** Enforcement of customary principles can be more difficult than enforcing codified laws, as it may rely on international dispute resolution mechanisms or the willingness of states to act.
*   **Evolving Nature:** Environmental challenges are constantly evolving, and customary principles need to adapt and develop alongside them.

**Opportunities:**

*   **Flexibility:** Customary principles offer a degree of flexibility in addressing emerging environmental issues where treaty law is not yet established.
*   **Early Action:** The precautionary principle, a key customary norm, encourages proactive measures to prevent environmental harm, even in the absence of complete scientific certainty.
*   **Global Harmonization:** The widespread acceptance of certain customary principles can foster greater international cooperation and harmonization of environmental standards.
*   **Ethical Foundation:** Customary principles provide an ethical and moral basis for environmental protection, influencing policy decisions and public opinion.
*   **Inspiration for New Treaties:** Customary principles often serve as the bedrock upon which new international environmental treaties are negotiated and adopted.

### 5. Evolution and Impact of Customary Principles

The influence of customary principles on international and national environmental law has grown significantly over time.

**Evolution:**

*   **Early Recognition:** While not always explicitly articulated, notions of avoiding harm to neighbors have been present in international law for centuries.
*   **Post-WWII Development:** The increasing awareness of transboundary pollution and the need for international cooperation led to a more formalized recognition of environmental norms.
*   **Stockholm Conference (1972):** The Stockholm Declaration, while a non-binding declaration, articulated principles that contributed to the formation of customary law, such as Principle 21 on state responsibility.
*   **Rio Summit (1992):** The Rio Declaration further solidified many of these principles, including the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle, contributing to their status as customary law.
*   **Bilateral and Multilateral Agreements:** Treaties addressing hazardous waste (e.g., the Basel Convention) and chemical safety often reflect and reinforce customary principles.

**Impact:**

*   **Shaping National Legislation:** Customary principles have directly influenced the drafting of national environmental laws, including those enforced by the EPA.
*   **Guiding Judicial Decisions:** Courts have increasingly invoked customary principles in environmental cases, particularly those involving transboundary harm.
*   **Influencing Policy Debates:** Customary principles frame the discourse around environmental protection and inform policy decisions at both national and international levels.
*   **Promoting International Cooperation:** Their widespread acceptance encourages collaboration among states in addressing shared environmental challenges.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:** Explain the two essential elements required for the formation of customary international law and provide an example of each in the context of hazardous waste management.

**Answer:** The two essential elements are:
    1.  **State Practice:** This refers to the actual conduct of states.
        *   *Example:* A state consistently enacting and enforcing national legislation that prohibits the export of hazardous waste to countries without adequate disposal facilities.
    2.  **Opinio Juris sive Necessitatis (Opinion of law or necessity):** This is the belief by states that their practice is legally required.
        *   *Example:* States participating in international discussions and signing treaties on hazardous waste management because they believe they have a legal obligation to prevent harm from such waste.

**Question 2:** How does the "precautionary principle" inform the EPA's regulatory approach to new chemicals?

**Answer:** The precautionary principle suggests that when there is a threat of serious or irreversible environmental damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation. For the EPA, this means they may require extensive testing and risk assessments for new chemicals, and may restrict or ban their use if potential harm is identified, even if definitive proof of harm is not yet available.

**Question 3:** Discuss the relationship between the "polluter pays" principle and the liabilities imposed under CERCLA (Superfund).

**Answer:** The "polluter pays" principle asserts that those who cause pollution should bear the costs of preventing or cleaning it up. CERCLA's liability provisions, which often hold potentially responsible parties (PRPs) accountable for the costs of cleaning up hazardous waste sites, directly embody this principle. Even if a PRP did not act negligently, they can be held liable for cleanup costs, reflecting the idea that those who contributed to the problem should finance its solution.

**Question 4:** What are the main challenges in proving the existence of a customary environmental law principle?

**Answer:** The main challenges include:
    *   The difficulty in collecting and analyzing evidence of widespread and consistent state practice.
    *   The need to demonstrate the subjective element of *opinio juris* – that states act out of a sense of legal obligation, not merely convenience or political expediency.
    *   Differentiating between genuine custom and states merely following treaty obligations or engaging in comity.
    *   The amorphous nature of environmental issues, which can make it hard to pinpoint specific state actions that clearly establish a custom.

**Question 5:** Provide an example of how a customary principle might be used to interpret an existing EPA regulation.

**Answer:** Suppose an EPA regulation on industrial emissions is vaguely worded regarding the definition of "significant harm." A court or the EPA itself, when interpreting this regulation, could refer to the customary principle of "no significant transboundary harm" as a guide. This would lead to an interpretation that emphasizes preventing substantial, measurable environmental damage to other jurisdictions or areas beyond national control.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Customary International Law (CIL) is a dual-element concept:** It requires both consistent State Practice and *Opinio Juris*.
*   **Precautionary, Polluter Pays, and Prevention** are key customary principles with significant influence on environmental law.
*   **Customary principles inform and underpin** codified laws and regulations, including those of the EPA.
*   **While challenging to prove,** customary principles offer flexibility and a crucial ethical foundation for environmental protection.
*   **The evolution of CIL reflects** growing global awareness and concern for environmental issues.
*   **The EPA's actions often reflect** the internalization of customary international environmental principles into domestic law.
