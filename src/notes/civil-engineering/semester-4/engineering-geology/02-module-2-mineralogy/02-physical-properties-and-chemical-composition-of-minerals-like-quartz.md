# Engineering Geology: Module 2 - Mineralogy

## Topic: Physical Properties and Chemical Composition of Minerals (Focus on Quartz)

This module delves into the fundamental building blocks of rocks: minerals. Understanding their physical and chemical characteristics is crucial for engineering geologists as these properties dictate a mineral's behavior in construction materials, its influence on geological processes, and its potential hazards. We will focus on **Quartz** as a primary example to illustrate these concepts.

---

### Learning Outcomes:

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Identify** the key physical properties used to describe and identify minerals.
*   **Explain** the relationship between mineral composition and crystal structure.
*   **Describe** the chemical composition and crystal structure of common rock-forming minerals, with a specific focus on quartz.
*   **Relate** the physical properties of minerals to their practical applications and engineering significance.

---

### 1. Introduction to Minerals

*   **Definition:** A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and a characteristic crystalline structure.
    *   **Naturally Occurring:** Formed by geological processes, not man-made.
    *   **Inorganic:** Not derived from living organisms.
    *   **Solid:** Has a definite shape and volume (not liquid or gas).
    *   **Definite Chemical Composition:** Can be expressed by a chemical formula (though some variations are allowed, e.g., solid solution).
    *   **Characteristic Crystalline Structure:** Atoms are arranged in a regular, repeating, three-dimensional pattern called a crystal lattice.

*   **Importance in Engineering Geology:**
    *   Minerals are the constituents of rocks, and therefore, the properties of rocks are directly influenced by the types and proportions of minerals they contain.
    *   Understanding mineral properties helps predict:
        *   Strength and durability of construction materials (e.g., aggregates, cement).
        *   Behavior of soil and rock masses under stress.
        *   Potential for weathering and alteration.
        *   Occurrence of valuable resources (e.g., ores, industrial minerals).
        *   Geological hazards (e.g., swelling clays, reactive aggregates).

### 2. Physical Properties of Minerals

Physical properties are observable characteristics that do not involve chemical change. They are the primary tools for mineral identification in the field and laboratory.

#### 2.1 Crystal Form (Habit)

*   **Definition:** The external shape of a mineral crystal, reflecting its internal atomic arrangement. It is determined by the symmetry of the crystal lattice and the relative growth rates of different crystal faces.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Crystals:** Minerals grow with specific geometric shapes when conditions allow.
    *   **Faces:** Flat surfaces on a crystal.
    *   **Edges:** Lines where two faces meet.
    *   **Vertices:** Points where three or more edges meet.
*   **Common Crystal Forms (Habits):**
    *   **Prismatic:** Elongated, blocky shapes (e.g., quartz, tourmaline).
    *   **Acicular:** Needle-like (e.g., actinolite).
    *   **Bladed:** Flattened and elongated, like a knife blade (e.g., kyanite).
    *   **Tabular:** Flat and sheet-like (e.g., mica).
    *   **Equant:** Roughly equal dimensions in all directions (e.g., garnet, calcite).
    *   **Dendritic:** Branching, tree-like patterns (e.g., manganese oxides).
    *   **Botryoidal:** Grape-like or reniform clusters (e.g., hematite, chalcedony).
    *   **Mammillary:** Similar to botryoidal but with larger, rounded masses.
    *   **Stalactitic:** Hanging, icicle-like formations (often in caves).
*   **Example (Quartz):** Often forms prismatic crystals with pyramidal terminations. Can also occur in massive, granular, or fibrous forms.

#### 2.2 Cleavage

*   **Definition:** The tendency of a mineral to break along specific planes of weakness within its crystal structure, which correspond to planes with weaker atomic bonding.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Cleavage Planes:** Directions along which a mineral breaks.
    *   **Quality of Cleavage:**
        *   **Perfect:** Breaks easily and cleanly along planes (e.g., mica, calcite).
        *   **Good:** Breaks along planes with some effort (e.g., feldspar, amphibole).
        *   **Fair:** Breaks along planes, but surfaces are irregular (e.g., quartz, pyrite).
        *   **Poor:** Does not break along distinct planes (e.g., olivine).
    *   **Number of Cleavage Directions:**
        *   **One direction:** Sheet-like cleavage (e.g., mica).
        *   **Two directions:** May be at 90° (e.g., feldspar, pyroxene) or not at 90° (e.g., amphibole, calcite).
        *   **Three directions:** May be at 90° (cubic, e.g., halite, galena) or not at 90° (rhombohedral, e.g., calcite).
        *   **Four directions:** Octahedral cleavage (e.g., fluorite).
*   **Example (Quartz):** Does *not* have cleavage. It breaks irregularly.

#### 2.3 Fracture

*   **Definition:** The way a mineral breaks when it does *not* cleave. It occurs along planes of irregular weakness.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Conchoidal Fracture:** Smooth, curved surfaces resembling broken glass (e.g., quartz, obsidian).
    *   **Fibrous Fracture:** Breaks into splinters or fibers (e.g., asbestos minerals).
    *   **Hackly Fracture:** Jagged, sharp surfaces (e.g., native copper).
    *   **Uneven Fracture:** Rough and irregular surfaces.
*   **Example (Quartz):** Exhibits prominent conchoidal fracture.

#### 2.4 Hardness

*   **Definition:** A mineral's resistance to scratching or abrasion. It is a measure of the strength of chemical bonds within the mineral.
*   **Key Concept:** **Mohs Hardness Scale:** A relative scale of hardness from 1 to 10, using ten standard minerals.
    *   **1 - Talc:** Can be scratched by a fingernail.
    *   **2 - Gypsum:** Can be scratched by a fingernail.
    *   **3 - Calcite:** Can be scratched by a copper coin.
    *   **4 - Fluorite:** Can be scratched by a steel knife.
    *   **5 - Apatite:** Can be scratched by a steel knife, but is harder than apatite.
    *   **6 - Orthoclase Feldspar:** Scratches glass.
    *   **7 - Quartz:** Scratches steel and glass.
    *   **8 - Topaz:** Scratches quartz.
    *   **9 - Corundum:** Scratches topaz.
    *   **10 - Diamond:** The hardest natural substance.
*   **Practical Application:** Used to determine if a mineral is harder or softer than common objects (fingernail, copper coin, steel knife, glass).
*   **Example (Quartz):** Hardness of 7 on the Mohs scale. This makes it a durable mineral, resistant to weathering and abrasion, and suitable for use as aggregate in concrete and road construction.

#### 2.5 Streak

*   **Definition:** The color of a mineral's powder when rubbed against an unglazed porcelain plate (streak plate).
*   **Key Concept:** Streak is often more consistent than the mineral's visible color, especially for minerals with variable colors.
*   **Observation:** Color of the streak can be white, black, red, brown, green, blue, etc.
*   **Example (Quartz):** Streak is white. Hematite can have a red-brown streak. Pyrite (fool's gold) has a greenish-black streak.

#### 2.6 Luster

*   **Definition:** The way light reflects off the surface of a mineral.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Metallic:** Looks like polished metal (e.g., pyrite, galena). Opaque.
    *   **Non-metallic:** Does not resemble metal.
        *   **Vitreous:** Like glass (e.g., quartz, calcite).
        *   **Adamantine:** Brilliant, like diamond (e.g., diamond, zircon).
        *   **Resinous:** Like resin or plastic (e.g., sulfur).
        *   **Greasy:** Appears coated in oil (e.g., nepheline).
        *   **Silky:** Like silk fibers, usually due to fibrous structure (e.g., gypsum).
        *   **Pearly:** Iridescent, like mother-of-pearl (e.g., talc, some feldspars).
        *   **Earthy/ Dull:** Like soil, no reflection (e.g., kaolinite, limonite).
*   **Example (Quartz):** Typically has a vitreous luster.

#### 2.7 Color

*   **Definition:** The visible color of a mineral.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Idiochromatic:** Color is due to essential elements in its chemical composition (intrinsic color).
    *   **Allochromatic:** Color is due to impurities or defects in the crystal structure (extrinsic color).
*   **Variability:** Color can vary significantly within a single mineral species.
*   **Example (Quartz):** Can be colorless (rock crystal), white (milky quartz), pink (rose quartz), purple (amethyst), brown (smoky quartz), gray, yellow, etc. The color variations are due to trace amounts of different elements or structural imperfections.

#### 2.8 Specific Gravity (Density)

*   **Definition:** The ratio of a mineral's density to the density of water. It's a measure of how heavy a mineral is for its size.
*   **Key Concept:** Can be estimated by "heft" – a mineral that feels heavy for its size has a high specific gravity.
*   **Range:** Most common rock-forming minerals have specific gravities between 2.5 and 3.5. Minerals containing heavy elements (e.g., lead, iron, uranium) will have higher specific gravities.
*   **Example (Quartz):** Specific gravity is approximately 2.65. Minerals like galena (lead sulfide) have a much higher specific gravity (around 7.5).

#### 2.9 Magnetism

*   **Definition:** Whether a mineral is attracted to a magnet.
*   **Key Concept:** Primarily exhibited by minerals containing iron, nickel, or cobalt.
*   **Example:** Magnetite is strongly magnetic. Hematite is weakly magnetic. Quartz is not magnetic.

#### 2.10 Other Properties (less common but important for specific minerals)

*   **Taste:** (e.g., Halite - salty) - **DO NOT TASTE UNKNOWN MINERALS IN A LAB.**
*   **Feel:** (e.g., Talc - greasy/soapy).
*   **Tenacity:** A mineral's resistance to breaking or deforming.
    *   **Brittle:** Breaks or powders easily (e.g., quartz, calcite).
    *   **Malleable:** Can be hammered into thin sheets (e.g., native gold, native copper).
    *   **Ductile:** Can be drawn into wires (e.g., native copper).
    *   **Sectile:** Can be cut into shavings with a knife (e.g., gypsum).
    *   **Flexible:** Bends but does not return to its original shape (e.g., talc).
    *   **Elastic:** Bends and returns to its original shape (e.g., mica).
*   **Striations:** Grooves or lines on crystal faces, often found on minerals with parallel cleavage planes (e.g., feldspars, pyroxenes).
*   **Effervescence:** Reaction with dilute acid (e.g., calcite fizzes with dilute HCl).

### 3. Chemical Composition and Crystal Structure

Minerals are classified based on their dominant anion or anionic group. This classification reflects their chemical composition and often influences their physical properties and crystal structure.

#### 3.1 Chemical Composition of Minerals

*   **Definition:** The specific elements and their proportions within a mineral, expressed by a chemical formula.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Elements:** The basic building blocks of matter.
    *   **Chemical Formula:** Represents the types and ratios of elements in a mineral.
    *   **Cations:** Positively charged ions.
    *   **Anions:** Negatively charged ions.
    *   **Isomorphism:** Minerals with similar crystal structures and chemical formulas, allowing for substitution of ions of similar size and charge (e.g., solid solution series like olivine: Mg₂SiO₄ to Fe₂SiO₄).
    *   **Polymorphism:** Minerals with the same chemical composition but different crystal structures, leading to different physical properties (e.g., Diamond and Graphite are both carbon (C) but have vastly different properties).

#### 3.2 Crystal Structure

*   **Definition:** The ordered, three-dimensional arrangement of atoms within a mineral. This arrangement is dictated by the size, charge, and bonding preferences of the constituent ions.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Crystal Lattice:** The repeating geometric arrangement of points representing the positions of atoms.
    *   **Unit Cell:** The smallest repeating unit of the crystal lattice.
    *   **Coordination Number:** The number of nearest neighboring atoms or ions surrounding a central atom or ion.
    *   **Bonding:** The forces holding atoms together (ionic, covalent, metallic, van der Waals).

### 4. Focus on Quartz: An Example Mineral

*   **Chemical Formula:** SiO₂ (Silicon Dioxide)
*   **Chemical Composition:** Composed of silicon (Si) and oxygen (O) in a 1:2 ratio. Silicon is in a +4 oxidation state, and oxygen is in a -2 oxidation state.
*   **Crystal Structure:**
    *   **Silicate Mineral:** Belongs to the silicate group, specifically a framework silicate.
    *   **Basic Unit:** The [SiO₄]⁴⁻ tetrahedron. In this tetrahedron, a silicon atom is covalently bonded to four oxygen atoms, forming a pyramid-like structure.
    *   **Framework Structure:** In quartz, these [SiO₄]⁴⁻ tetrahedra are linked by sharing all four of their oxygen atoms with adjacent tetrahedra. This creates a three-dimensional framework of interconnected Si-O bonds.
    *   **Crystal System:** Trigonal (a subdivision of Hexagonal).
    *   **Bonding:** Primarily strong covalent bonds between Si and O atoms. This contributes to quartz's hardness and chemical stability.
*   **Physical Properties of Quartz:**
    *   **Hardness:** 7 (Mohs scale). This makes it resistant to scratching and abrasion.
    *   **Cleavage:** None. It fractures conchoidally.
    *   **Fracture:** Conchoidal.
    *   **Luster:** Vitreous (glassy).
    *   **Color:** Variable (clear, white, pink, purple, smoky, etc.) due to impurities or structural defects.
    *   **Streak:** White.
    *   **Specific Gravity:** ~2.65.
    *   **Tenacity:** Brittle.
    *   **Transparency:** Can be transparent to translucent.
    *   **Crystal Habit:** Commonly forms prismatic crystals with six-sided faces and pyramidal terminations. Can also occur in massive, granular, or fibrous forms (like chalcedony).

*   **Engineering Significance of Quartz:**
    *   **Durability:** Its hardness and chemical stability make it an excellent aggregate for concrete, asphalt, and road construction.
    *   **Abrasion Resistance:** Used in sandpaper, grinding wheels, and as an abrasive material.
    *   **Electrical Properties:** Piezoelectric properties (generates an electric charge when stressed) make it useful in electronics (oscillators, sensors).
    *   **Glassmaking:** High-purity quartz sand is the primary ingredient in glass.
    *   **Construction:** As a major component of granite and sandstone, its presence influences the overall strength and durability of these building stones.
    *   **Weaker Forms:** Cryptocrystalline quartz (like chert) can be very hard but sometimes exhibits problematic behavior in concrete aggregates (e.g., alkali-silica reaction).

### 6. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
c) Cleavage

a) 1-2 (Fingernails have a hardness of about 2.5)

b) Conchoidal fracture

**Answer 5:**
Quartz is significant in civil engineering and construction due to:
*   **Durability and Abrasion Resistance:** Its hardness (7 on Mohs scale) makes it an excellent aggregate for concrete and roads, providing strength and resistance to wear.
*   **Resistance to Weathering:** Its chemical stability means it doesn't easily degrade in the environment.
*   **Applications in Glassmaking:** High-purity quartz sand is the primary raw material for producing glass.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Minerals are the building blocks of rocks.** Their properties dictate rock behavior.
*   **Physical properties are key to mineral identification.** Learn to observe and test for them.
*   **Mohs Hardness Scale is a relative scale.** It's about resistance to scratching.
*   **Cleavage vs. Fracture:** Cleavage is breakage along planes of weakness; fracture is irregular breakage. Quartz has fracture, not cleavage.
*   **Color can be misleading.** Streak, hardness, and luster are often more reliable identifying features.
*   **Chemical composition and crystal structure are fundamental.** They determine the mineral's intrinsic properties.
*   **Quartz (SiO₂)** is a ubiquitous and important mineral in engineering due to its hardness, durability, and chemical stability, making it valuable for aggregates and glass. Its piezoelectric properties also have technological applications.
