### 1. Introduction to Igneous Rocks

*   **Definition:** Igneous rocks are formed from the cooling and solidification (crystallization) of molten rock material called **magma** (below the Earth's surface) or **lava** (on the Earth's surface).
*   **Formation Process:**
    *   Melting of existing rocks deep within the Earth.
    *   Ascent of molten material.
    *   Cooling and crystallization.
*   **Key Characteristics:**
    *   Crystalline texture (composed of interlocking mineral crystals).
    *   Absence of fossils (typically).
    *   Can be extrusive (volcanic) or intrusive (plutonic).

### 2. Classification of Igneous Rocks

Igneous rocks are classified based on two primary criteria:

#### 2.1. Texture (Deals with crystal size, shape, and arrangement - covered in other modules, but foundational)

*   **Phaneritic:** Coarse-grained, visible crystals (intrusive, slow cooling).
*   **Aphanitic:** Fine-grained, crystals not visible to the naked eye (extrusive, rapid cooling).
*   **Porphyritic:** Mixed grain sizes, larger crystals (phenocrysts) in a finer-grained matrix.
*   **Glassy:** No crystals, rapid quenching (e.g., obsidian).
*   **Vesicular:** Contains holes (vesicles) from trapped gas bubbles (e.g., scoria, pumice).

#### 2.2. Chemical and Mineralogical Classification

This classification focuses on the **composition** of the igneous rock.

##### 2.2.1. Chemical Classification

*   **Basis:** The overall chemical composition of the magma/lava, particularly the **silica (SiO2) content**.
*   **Categories:**
    *   **Felsic (Acidic):**
        *   High silica content (typically > 63% SiO2).
        *   Rich in feldspar minerals (potassium feldspar, plagioclase feldspar) and quartz.
        *   Light-colored.
        *   Lower melting point.
        *   **Examples:** Granite, Rhyolite, Obsidian.
    *   **Intermediate:**
        *   Moderate silica content (typically 52-63% SiO2).
        *   Contains a mix of felsic and mafic minerals, primarily plagioclase feldspar and amphibole/biotite.
        *   Medium gray or pinkish-gray color.
        *   **Examples:** Diorite, Andesite.
    *   **Mafic (Basic):**
        *   Lower silica content (typically 45-52% SiO2).
        *   Rich in magnesium (Mg) and iron (Fe) bearing minerals (olivine, pyroxene, amphibole, calcium-rich plagioclase feldspar).
        *   Dark-colored.
        *   Higher melting point.
        *   **Examples:** Gabbro, Basalt.
    *   **Ultramafic:**
        *   Very low silica content (typically < 45% SiO2).
        *   Composed almost entirely of mafic minerals, primarily olivine and pyroxene.
        *   Very dark to black color.
        *   Found in the Earth's mantle.
        *   **Examples:** Peridotite, Komatiite.

##### 2.2.2. Mineralogical Classification

*   **Basis:** The **types and proportions of minerals** present in the rock. This is often more precise than chemical classification, as it reflects the actual minerals that crystallized.
*   **Key Rock-Forming Minerals in Igneous Rocks:**
    *   **Silicates:** The most abundant group of minerals in the Earth's crust.
        *   **Quartz (SiO2):**
            *   Hard, resistant mineral.
            *   Composed solely of silica.
            *   Typically hexagonal crystal structure.
            *   Found in felsic rocks.
            *   **Importance in Engineering:** Provides strength and durability.
        *   **Feldspar Group:** The most abundant group of minerals in the Earth's crust.
            *   **Feldspar Alkali (K-Feldspar):**
                *   Potassium aluminum silicates (e.g., Orthoclase, Microcline).
                *   Often pinkish or white.
                *   Found in felsic rocks.
            *   **Plagioclase Feldspar:**
                *   Solid solution series between Albite (NaAlSi3O8) and Anorthite (CaAl2Si2O8).
                *   Composition ranges from sodium-rich (albite) to calcium-rich (anorthite).
                *   White to gray.
                *   Found in all igneous rock types, with composition varying based on silica content (sodic in felsic, calcic in mafic).
        *   **Micas:** Sheet silicates, characterized by their flaky habit.
            *   **Biotite:** Black to brown mica, contains iron and magnesium (mafic).
            *   **Muscovite:** Colorless to silver mica (felsic).
            *   Often found in felsic and intermediate rocks.
        *   **Amphibole Group:** Double-chain silicates, often black or dark green.
            *   **Hornblende:** Common dark mineral, contains iron and magnesium (mafic).
            *   Found in intermediate and mafic rocks.
        *   **Pyroxene Group:** Single-chain silicates, typically dark colored.
            *   **Augite:** Very common mafic mineral, contains iron and magnesium.
            *   Found in mafic and ultramafic rocks.
        *   **Olivine:**
            *   Nesosilicate, magnesium iron silicate ((Mg,Fe)2SiO4).
            *   Typically green.
            *   Found in mafic and ultramafic rocks.
            *   **Importance in Engineering:** Can weather relatively easily in moist environments.
    *   **Oxides:**
        *   **Magnetite (Fe3O4):** Black, magnetic mineral.
        *   **Ilmenite (FeTiO3):** Black mineral.
        *   Often present as accessory minerals in mafic rocks.
    *   **Sulfides:**
        *   **Pyrite (FeS2):** "Fool's gold". Can cause acid rock drainage if oxidized.

*   **Classification Schemes:**
    *   **Based on Quartz Content:**
        *   **Quartz-rich:** Abundant quartz.
        *   **Quartz-bearing:** Some quartz.
        *   **Quartz-free:** No quartz.
    *   **Based on Feldspar Content:**
        *   **Feldspathic:** Abundant feldspar.
        *   **Feldspar-free:** No feldspar.
    *   **Based on Dominant Mafic Minerals:**
        *   **Pyroxenites:** Dominated by pyroxenes.
        *   **Peridotites:** Dominated by olivine.

### 3. Common Igneous Rock Types and Their Classification

| Rock Type   | Texture   | Chemical Category | Dominant Minerals                                                              | Color    | Examples                                   |
| :---------- | :-------- | :---------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| **Granite** | Phaneritic | Felsic            | Quartz, K-feldspar, plagioclase feldspar (sodic), mica (biotite/muscovite)  | Light    | Kitchen countertops, building stone        |
| **Rhyolite**| Aphanitic | Felsic            | Quartz, feldspar (microscopic)                                                 | Light    | Volcanic flows, ash deposits               |
| **Diorite** | Phaneritic | Intermediate      | Plagioclase feldspar (intermediate), amphibole, biotite, some pyroxene        | Medium   | Building stone, decorative stone           |
| **Andesite**| Aphanitic | Intermediate      | Plagioclase feldspar, amphibole, pyroxene                                      | Medium   | Volcanic arcs, common in mountainous regions |
| **Gabbro**  | Phaneritic | Mafic             | Plagioclase feldspar (calcic), pyroxene, olivine, amphibole                    | Dark     | Foundation stone, aggregate                |
| **Basalt**  | Aphanitic | Mafic             | Plagioclase feldspar, pyroxene, olivine                                        | Dark     | Volcanic plains, oceanic crust             |
| **Peridotite**| Phaneritic | Ultramafic        | Olivine, pyroxene                                                              | Very Dark| Upper mantle rock, source of basalt magma  |

### 4. Significance in Engineering Geology

*   **Strength and Durability:** Felsic rocks like granite, due to their quartz content, are generally strong and durable, making them suitable for foundations, dams, and high-strength concrete aggregate.
*   **Weathering Resistance:** Quartz-rich rocks are resistant to chemical weathering. Mafic minerals (olivine, pyroxene) can be more susceptible to weathering, potentially impacting the long-term stability of rock structures.
*   **Abrasiveness:** The hardness of minerals like quartz and feldspar can affect drilling and excavation equipment.
*   **Porosity and Permeability:** While generally less porous than sedimentary rocks, the presence of vesicles in extrusive rocks can influence their behavior as aquifers or barriers.
*   **Aggregate Quality:** The mineralogy and texture of igneous rocks are crucial for their suitability as aggregate in concrete and asphalt, affecting strength, durability, and workability.
*   **Geotechnical Properties:** The interlocking crystalline structure of igneous rocks generally results in good shear strength and stability.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Which of the following igneous rock types is typically considered felsic and has a phaneritic texture?
a) Basalt
b) Andesite
c) Granite
d) Peridotite

**Question 2:**
Identify the dominant rock-forming mineral in ultramafic rocks.
a) Quartz
b) Feldspar
c) Olivine
d) Biotite

**Question 3:**
A dark-colored igneous rock with fine-grained texture and containing plagioclase feldspar and pyroxene is likely to be:
a) Granite
b) Diorite
c) Basalt
d) Rhyolite

**Question 4:**
Explain the difference between chemical and mineralogical classification of igneous rocks.

**Question 5:**
Why is the silica content of an igneous rock an important factor in its classification and engineering applications?

---

### 6. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
c) Granite. Granite is felsic and has a coarse-grained (phaneritic) texture. Basalt is mafic and aphanitic, Andesite is intermediate and aphanitic, and Peridotite is ultramafic and phaneritic.

**Answer 2:**
c) Olivine. Olivine is the primary mineral in ultramafic rocks.

**Answer 3:**
c) Basalt. Basalt is a dark-colored, fine-grained (aphanitic) igneous rock with a mafic composition, typically containing plagioclase feldspar and pyroxene.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Chemical Classification:** Classifies igneous rocks based on their overall chemical composition, primarily the silica (SiO2) content. This leads to categories like felsic, intermediate, mafic, and ultramafic.
*   **Mineralogical Classification:** Classifies igneous rocks based on the specific types and proportions of minerals present in the rock. This provides a more detailed and accurate description of the rock's composition.

**Answer 5:**
Silica content is important because it directly correlates with the types of minerals that will crystallize from the melt.
*   **High silica (felsic rocks):** Rich in quartz and feldspar, leading to lighter colors, lower melting points, and generally higher strength and durability (e.g., granite). These are good for structural applications.
*   **Low silica (mafic rocks):** Rich in iron and magnesium-bearing minerals, leading to darker colors, higher melting points, and potentially greater susceptibility to weathering (e.g., basalt). Mafic rocks are often used for aggregate and in areas where heat resistance is desired.

---

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Igneous rocks form from molten material (magma/lava).**
*   **Classification is based on texture (grain size/arrangement) and composition (chemical/mineralogical).**
*   **Silica content is the primary basis for chemical classification (Felsic, Intermediate, Mafic, Ultramafic).**
*   **Quartz and Feldspar are key felsic minerals.**
*   **Olivine, Pyroxene, and Amphibole are key mafic minerals.**
*   **Mineralogy dictates many engineering properties like strength, durability, and weathering resistance.**
*   **Granite (felsic, phaneritic) and Basalt (mafic, aphanitic) are two very common and important igneous rock types.**
