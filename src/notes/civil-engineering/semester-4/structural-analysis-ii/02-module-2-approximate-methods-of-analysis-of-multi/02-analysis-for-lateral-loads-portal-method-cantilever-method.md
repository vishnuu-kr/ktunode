# Structural Analysis - II: Approximate Methods of Analysis for Lateral Loads

## Module 2: Approximate Methods of Analysis of Multi-Storey Buildings

---

### 1. Introduction to Lateral Load Analysis

**What are Lateral Loads?**

Lateral loads are forces that act horizontally on a structure, perpendicular to its longitudinal axis. Common sources of lateral loads include:

*   **Wind Loads:** Caused by the pressure of wind acting on the building's surfaces.
*   **Seismic Loads (Earthquake Loads):** Generated by the ground motion during an earthquake, causing inertia forces within the structure.

**Why are Approximate Methods Used?**

While exact methods (like the stiffness matrix method) provide precise solutions, they can be computationally intensive for large and complex multi-storey buildings. Approximate methods offer a simplified approach to estimate the distribution of forces and moments, providing reasonably accurate results for initial design and understanding structural behavior.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Shear Walls:** Vertical structural elements designed to resist lateral forces.
*   **Bracing:** Diagonal members that enhance the lateral stiffness and strength of a structure.
*   **Moment Resisting Frames:** Frames where connections between beams and columns are rigid, allowing them to resist bending moments caused by lateral loads.
*   **Diaphragms:** Horizontal structural elements (like floor slabs) that transfer lateral loads to vertical resisting elements.
*   **Story Shear:** The total horizontal shear force acting on a particular storey.
*   **Story Drift:** The horizontal displacement of one storey relative to the adjacent storey.

### 2. The Portal Method

The Portal Method is a simple approximate method for analyzing multi-storey, multi-bay frames subjected to lateral loads. It assumes that the structure behaves like a series of unbraced portals.

**Assumptions of the Portal Method:**

1.  **Inflection Points:**
    *   Horizontal members (beams): An inflection point exists at the mid-span of each beam.
    *   Vertical members (columns): An inflection point exists at the mid-height of each column in the interior bays and at the mid-height of the exterior columns.
2.  **Shear Force Distribution:**
    *   The total shear force in any storey is distributed among the vertical members (columns) in proportion to their cross-sectional areas. **However, a simplified approach often assumes equal distribution of shear in interior columns and half of the interior column shear in exterior columns.**
3.  **Moment Distribution:**
    *   For a typical bay, the horizontal shear force is resisted by a couple formed by the axial forces in the columns.
    *   The moment at the beam-column connection is transferred to the columns, causing bending.

**Procedure for Analysis using the Portal Method:**

**Step 1: Determine Story Shear**

*   Calculate the total lateral force acting on each storey. This is often done by dividing the total lateral load at each floor level.

**Step 2: Distribute Column Shear**

*   **Simplified Assumption (Most Common):**
    *   Exterior columns resist 25% of the story shear.
    *   Interior columns resist 50% of the story shear.
    *   **Note:** For multi-bay frames, the 50% for interior columns is further distributed equally among them.
*   **More General Assumption (Based on Area):**
    *   The shear force in each column is proportional to its cross-sectional area. $V_{column} \propto A_{column}$
    *   $\sum V_{columns} = V_{story\_shear}$

**Step 3: Locate Inflection Points**

*   Assume inflection points at mid-span for all beams and at mid-height for all columns.

**Step 4: Determine Internal Forces (Axial Force, Shear Force, Bending Moment)**

*   **Consider a typical storey and cut through the beams at their mid-spans (inflection points).** This creates free bodies of the upper and lower halves of the beams.
*   **Consider a free body of a complete bay (or a portion of it) cut through the beams at their inflection points and the columns at their inflection points.**
*   **Apply equilibrium equations to determine axial forces in the columns.**
    *   For a portal frame (one bay): The horizontal shear in the columns is resisted by a couple formed by equal and opposite axial forces in the columns. $H_{column} \times L_{bay} = M_{beam-column}$ (This is a bit simplified, we typically look at shear in columns first).
    *   Let's rephrase: The shear in a column ($V_{column}$) induces an axial force couple within the column to satisfy moment equilibrium in the free body of the bay.
*   **Determine Shear Forces in Beams:**
    *   Consider a free body of a beam cut at its mid-span. The shear in the beam is equal to the moment at the beam-column connection divided by the distance from the column to the beam's inflection point (which is half the beam length). $V_{beam} = M_{beam-column} / (L_{beam}/2)$
*   **Determine Bending Moments in Beams:**
    *   The bending moment at the beam-column connection is determined by the shear in the beam and the location of its inflection point. $M_{beam-column} = V_{beam} \times (L_{beam}/2)$
*   **Determine Bending Moments in Columns:**
    *   Consider a free body of a column cut at its inflection point. The bending moment at the beam-column connection and the shear in the column contribute to the moment at the column's inflection point.

**Example - Single Bay Portal Frame:**

Consider a single-bay, three-storey portal frame subjected to a uniformly distributed lateral load of $w$ per unit length along the height.

*   **Storey Shear:** For the top storey, $V_1 = w \times H$. For the second storey, $V_2 = w \times H$. For the ground storey, $V_3 = w \times H$.
*   **Column Shear (Simplified):** Exterior columns each resist $V_{story}/2$.
*   **Inflection Points:** Mid-span of beams, mid-height of columns.
*   **Bending Moments in Beams:**
    *   Shear in beam: $V_{beam} = (V_{story}/2) / (L_{beam}/2) = V_{story} / L_{beam}$
    *   Moment at connection: $M_{beam-column} = V_{beam} \times (L_{beam}/2) = (V_{story}/2) \times (L_{beam}/2) = V_{story} \times L_{beam} / 4$
*   **Axial Forces in Columns:**
    *   Consider the free body of the top storey bay, cut at beam inflection points. The couple from axial forces in columns equals the sum of moments at beam-column joints.
    *   $2 \times (Axial Force) \times L_{bay} = 2 \times M_{beam-column}$ (assuming symmetrical beam moments)
    *   $Axial Force = M_{beam-column} / L_{bay}$
    *   These axial forces are compressive in one column and tensile in the other, balancing the moment. The distribution of these axial forces along the height of the column is linear, peaking at the beam-column connections.

**Important Points to Remember (Portal Method):**

*   It's best suited for frames where the bays are relatively similar in width.
*   It tends to overestimate moments in beams and underestimate moments in columns.
*   It assumes that the frame acts as a series of independent portals.

### 3. The Cantilever Method

The Cantilever Method assumes that the multi-storey building frame acts as a **vertical cantilever beam** fixed at the base and subjected to lateral loads. This method is particularly suitable for tall, slender buildings.

**Assumptions of the Cantilever Method:**

1.  **Vertical Cantilever Behavior:** The entire building frame acts like a single, tall cantilever beam fixed at the base.
2.  **Inflection Points:**
    *   Inflection points in beams occur at mid-span.
    *   Inflection points in columns occur at the mid-height of the storey.
3.  **Stress Distribution:** The axial stresses in the columns are proportional to their distance from the centroid of the column cross-sections at a given floor level. This is analogous to the bending stress distribution in a cantilever beam.
4.  **Shear Force Distribution:** The shear force in any storey is distributed among the columns in proportion to their cross-sectional areas. **Similar to the Portal Method, a simplified assumption is often used where exterior columns take a smaller share of the shear.**

**Procedure for Analysis using the Cantilever Method:**

**Step 1: Determine Story Shear**

*   Same as in the Portal Method – calculate the total lateral force acting on each storey.

**Step 2: Distribute Column Shear**

*   **Simplified Assumption:**
    *   Exterior columns resist a smaller proportion of the story shear (often assumed to be 5% to 10% of the story shear per exterior column, or based on relative stiffness).
    *   Interior columns resist the remaining shear, distributed based on their relative stiffness or cross-sectional area.
*   **General Assumption (Proportional to Area):**
    *   $V_{column} \propto A_{column}$

**Step 3: Locate Inflection Points**

*   Assume inflection points at mid-span for all beams and at mid-height of all columns.

**Step 4: Determine Axial Forces in Columns**

*   This is the core of the Cantilever Method.
*   Consider a free body of a column at a specific floor level.
*   The axial force in a column is directly proportional to its distance from the **centroid of the column cross-sections** at that floor level.
    *   $F_{axial, column} = M_{base\_resisting} \times (e_{column} / I_{overall})$
    *   Where:
        *   $M_{base\_resisting}$ is the total overturning moment at the base of the cantilever.
        *   $e_{column}$ is the distance of the column from the centroid of all columns at that level.
        *   $I_{overall}$ is the "moment of inertia" of the building's columns about the centroidal axis (sum of $A_{column} \times e_{column}^2$ for all columns).
*   **Simplified Calculation for Axial Forces:**
    *   Calculate the centroid of the columns at each floor level.
    *   Calculate the total overturning moment at the base of the building for each storey.
    *   $Axial Force = (Total\,Overturning\,Moment) \times (Distance\,from\,Centroid) / (\sum (Distance\,from\,Centroid)^2 \times Area)$ - This is getting complex for a simple note.
    *   **A more practical approach:**
        *   Calculate the overturning moment at the base of the building ($M_{base}$).
        *   Calculate the sum of the squares of the distances of each column from the centroid of the columns at that level ($\sum (e_i)^2$).
        *   The axial force in a column $i$ is $F_{axial,i} = (M_{base} \times e_i) / (\sum (e_j)^2)$. This assumes unit area for simplicity if areas are not considered for shear distribution. If shear is distributed proportionally to area, then the approach needs refinement.
        *   **A commonly used simplified approach:**
            *   Consider the total overturning moment at a given level.
            *   Axial force in a column = $(Total\,Overturning\,Moment) \times (Distance\,of\,column\,from\,centroid) / (\sum |Distance\,of\,column\,from\,centroid|)$ (This is a simplification and assumes a linear stress distribution).

**Step 5: Determine Shear Forces in Beams and Columns**

*   **Shear in Beams:**
    *   Consider a free body of a beam cut at its mid-span (inflection point).
    *   The shear force in the beam is equal to the difference in axial forces in the columns framing into that beam, divided by the length of the beam. $V_{beam} = (F_{axial,col1} - F_{axial,col2}) / L_{beam}$
*   **Shear in Columns:**
    *   The shear in the columns of a storey is the story shear for that storey, distributed as per the assumption (Step 2).

**Step 6: Determine Bending Moments**

*   **Bending Moments in Beams:**
    *   The bending moment at the beam-column connection is equal to the shear in the beam multiplied by half the length of the beam. $M_{beam-column} = V_{beam} \times (L_{beam}/2)$.
*   **Bending Moments in Columns:**
    *   Consider a free body of a column cut at its inflection point.
    *   The bending moment at the inflection point is the sum of the moments transferred from the beams framing into the column above and below the inflection point, plus the moment due to the column shear.

**Example - Two Bay, Two Storey Frame:**

Consider a frame with two equal bays of length $L$ and two storeys of height $H$. A lateral load $P$ is applied at the top.

*   **Story Shear:** Top storey: $V_1 = P$. Bottom storey: $V_2 = P$.
*   **Column Centroid:** For a symmetric frame with columns $C_1, C_2, C_3$, the centroid is typically at the center of the middle column ($C_2$).
*   **Column Distances from Centroid ($e_i$):**
    *   $C_1$: $e_1 = -L$
    *   $C_2$: $e_2 = 0$
    *   $C_3$: $e_3 = +L$
*   **Sum of Squares of Distances ($\sum (e_i)^2$):** $S = (-L)^2 + (0)^2 + (L)^2 = 2L^2$
*   **Axial Force in Columns (Top Storey):**
    *   Overturning moment at the base of the frame due to load $P$ on the top storey: $M_{base} = P \times H$.
    *   $F_{axial, C1} = (P \times H \times (-L)) / (2L^2) = -PH / (2L)$ (Tensile)
    *   $F_{axial, C2} = (P \times H \times 0) / (2L^2) = 0$
    *   $F_{axial, C3} = (P \times H \times L) / (2L^2) = PH / (2L)$ (Compressive)
*   **Shear in Beams (Top Storey):**
    *   Consider the free body of the top beam cut at mid-span.
    *   Let's assume shear in exterior columns is 5% of story shear, so $V_{C1} = V_{C3} = 0.05 P$. Interior column $V_{C2} = 0.9 P$.
    *   This distribution is often simplified. A common simplification is to distribute based on the axial forces calculated by the cantilever method for the shear distribution *as well*, which is not strictly correct.
    *   **Let's follow the common simplification:**
        *   The change in axial force in the columns is related to the beam shear.
        *   If we consider the free body of the beam cut at the column $C_1$. The axial force $F_{axial, C1}$ is resisted by the moment at the connection.
        *   A better approach for beam shear using cantilever method: Consider the difference in axial forces between adjacent columns and relate it to beam shear.
        *   For the top beam, cut at mid-span: $V_{beam1} = (F_{axial, C1} - F_{axial, C2}) / L_{beam}$ (This is for a bay cut longitudinally).
        *   **Consider the free body of the top storey, cut through the beams at their mid-spans.**
        *   For the left bay (between $C_1$ and $C_2$): The difference in axial forces in $C_1$ and $C_2$ divided by the beam length gives the shear.
        *   $V_{beam\_left} = (F_{axial, C1} - F_{axial, C2}) / L = (-PH/(2L) - 0) / L = -PH/L^2$
        *   $V_{beam\_right} = (F_{axial, C2} - F_{axial, C3}) / L = (0 - PH/(2L)) / L = -PH/L^2$
*   **Bending Moments in Beams (Top Storey):**
    *   $M_{beam-col} = V_{beam} \times (L_{beam}/2) = (-PH/L^2) \times (L/2) = -PH/(2L)$

**Important Points to Remember (Cantilever Method):**

*   Most suitable for tall, slender buildings where the cantilever action is dominant.
*   Generally provides more accurate results for bending moments in columns compared to the Portal Method.
*   Can be less accurate for shorter, wider buildings.
*   The distribution of shear in columns is a critical assumption and can significantly affect results.

### 4. Comparison of Portal and Cantilever Methods

| Feature                 | Portal Method                                     | Cantilever Method                                       |
| :---------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Primary Analogy**     | Unbraced Portals                                  | Vertical Cantilever Beam                                |
| **Suitability**         | Moderate height, regular frames                   | Tall, slender buildings                                 |
| **Column Shear**        | Assumed to be distributed based on bays/area.   | Often simplified as a small % for exterior, rest for interior. |
| **Axial Forces in Columns** | Calculated based on beam shear/moments.       | Proportional to distance from centroid, resisting overturning. |
| **Moments in Beams**    | Generally over-estimated.                         | Generally under-estimated compared to columns.          |
| **Moments in Columns**  | Generally under-estimated.                        | Generally more accurate.                                |
| **Computational Effort**| Relatively simpler.                               | Can be more involved in calculating axial forces.       |
| **Accuracy**            | Reasonable for moderate heights.                  | Better for tall, slender structures.                    |

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1: Portal Method**

Consider a three-storey, single-bay portal frame with each storey height $H=3$ m and the bay width $L=5$ m. The lateral load on each floor is $10$ kN. Using the Portal Method:

a) Calculate the story shear for each storey.
b) Determine the shear force in each column.
c) Calculate the bending moment at the beam-column connections.
d) Calculate the axial forces in the columns of the top storey.

**Solution 1:**

a) **Story Shear:**
   *   Top Storey: $V_1 = 10$ kN
   *   Middle Storey: $V_2 = 10$ kN
   *   Bottom Storey: $V_3 = 10$ kN

b) **Column Shear (Simplified):**
   *   Each column resists $V_{story}/2$.
   *   Exterior Columns ($C_1, C_2$): $V_{column} = 10 \text{ kN} / 2 = 5$ kN per column per storey.

c) **Bending Moment at Beam-Column Connections:**
   *   Shear in beam ($V_{beam}$) = $V_{column} / (L/2) = 5 \text{ kN} / (5 \text{ m} / 2) = 2$ kN.
   *   Moment at connection ($M_{beam-column}$) = $V_{beam} \times (L/2) = 2 \text{ kN} \times (5 \text{ m} / 2) = 5$ kNm.

d) **Axial Forces in Columns (Top Storey):**
   *   Consider the free body of the top storey bay, cut at beam inflection points.
   *   The couple formed by axial forces in columns balances the moments at the beam-column connections.
   *   $2 \times (Axial Force) \times L_{bay} = 2 \times M_{beam-column}$ (Assuming symmetrical beam moments)
   *   $2 \times (Axial Force) \times 5 \text{ m} = 2 \times 5 \text{ kNm}$
   *   $10 \times (Axial Force) = 10$
   *   Axial Force = 1 kN.
   *   So, one column has 1 kN axial tension, the other has 1 kN axial compression.

**Question 2: Cantilever Method**

Consider a two-storey, two-bay frame with equal bay widths of $L=6$ m and storey height $H=3$ m. A lateral load of $20$ kN is applied at the top floor and $15$ kN at the second floor. The columns are all of the same cross-section. Using the Cantilever Method:

a) Determine the centroid of the columns at each floor level.
b) Calculate the axial forces in the columns of the top storey.
c) Calculate the bending moments at the beam-column connections of the top storey.
d) Calculate the shear forces in the columns of the top storey.

**Solution 2:**

a) **Centroid of Columns:** Assuming columns are placed at $x=0$, $x=L$, $x=2L$.
   *   For a symmetric frame with columns at $x=0, 6, 12$ m, the centroid is at $x = (0+6+12)/3 = 6$ m.

b) **Axial Forces in Columns (Top Storey):**
   *   Total lateral load on top storey = $20$ kN.
   *   Overturning moment at the base of the frame due to the top storey load = $20 \text{ kN} \times 3 \text{ m} = 60$ kNm.
   *   Distances from centroid ($e_i$):
      *   Column 1 ($x=0$): $e_1 = -6$ m
      *   Column 2 ($x=6$): $e_2 = 0$ m
      *   Column 3 ($x=12$): $e_3 = +6$ m
   *   Sum of squares of distances ($\sum (e_i)^2$) = $(-6)^2 + (0)^2 + (6)^2 = 36 + 0 + 36 = 72 \text{ m}^2$.
   *   Axial Force in Column 1: $F_{axial, C1} = (60 \text{ kNm} \times -6 \text{ m}) / 72 \text{ m}^2 = -5$ kN (Tensile).
   *   Axial Force in Column 2: $F_{axial, C2} = (60 \text{ kNm} \times 0 \text{ m}) / 72 \text{ m}^2 = 0$ kN.
   *   Axial Force in Column 3: $F_{axial, C3} = (60 \text{ kNm} \times 6 \text{ m}) / 72 \text{ m}^2 = +5$ kN (Compressive).

c) **Bending Moments at Beam-Column Connections (Top Storey):**
   *   We need beam shear first. Let's assume the common simplification where shear distribution in columns is proportional to the axial forces calculated. This is a compromise.
   *   For a more direct calculation of beam moments using the axial forces:
   *   Consider the free body of the top storey, cut through the beams at their mid-spans.
   *   For the left bay (between C1 and C2):
      *   Change in axial force = $F_{axial, C1} - F_{axial, C2} = -5 - 0 = -5$ kN.
      *   This change in axial force is balanced by the shear in the beam. Assuming the beam shear is distributed linearly to the columns, the internal shear in the beam at its end is half of the total shear.
      *   A more precise way: Consider the moment equilibrium of the bay segment. The couple formed by axial forces in columns supports the overturning moment from beam shear.
      *   Let's use the relationship: $V_{beam} \times L_{beam} = \Delta F_{axial} \times L_{beam}$ where $\Delta F_{axial}$ is the difference in axial force between adjacent columns. This is not quite right.
      *   **Correct approach for beam shear and moment from axial forces:**
         *   Consider the free body of the top storey, cut through the beams at their mid-spans.
         *   For the left bay (between C1 and C2), the shear in the beam is related to the difference in axial forces.
         *   $V_{beam, left} = (F_{axial, C1} - F_{axial, C2}) / L_{beam}$ is incorrect for beam shear itself.
         *   **The axial forces in columns create a couple that resists the moment at the beam-column connection.**
         *   Consider the free body of the beam cut at the column $C_1$. The axial force in $C_1$ creates a moment at the column-beam joint.
         *   $M_{beam-col} = F_{axial, column} \times (L_{bay} / 2)$ (This is a simplification derived from portal method logic applied to cantilever axial forces, which is an approximation).
         *   Let's use the concept that beam shear resists the difference in column axial forces.
         *   Consider the free body of the top storey, cut at the beam-column connections.
         *   The horizontal forces in the columns at the beam-column connection are $M_{col} / (H/2)$.
         *   **A simpler approach is to consider the shear in the beam is the story shear divided by the number of bays for interior bays, and a fraction for exterior.**
         *   Let's revert to the axial force-beam shear relationship: The shear in the beam at the column is half of the total shear it carries.
         *   $V_{beam} = (\text{Axial force in column } C_1 \text{ at connection} - \text{Axial force in column } C_2 \text{ at connection}) / L_{beam}$. This is still not right.

         *   **Let's use the Cantilever Method's typical simplification for beam moments:** The moment in the beam is related to the axial force in the columns.
         *   The axial force in the column is $F_{axial}$. This force is distributed along the column. At the beam connection, the column has an axial force and a bending moment.
         *   **The shear in the beam at the column connection is the force that creates the moment at the beam-column joint.**
         *   Consider the free body of the beam cut at the column connection. The shear in the beam is equal to the bending moment at the connection divided by the distance from the inflection point (mid-span).
         *   The bending moment at the beam-column connection is derived from the axial forces in the columns.
         *   For the top beam, connecting $C_1$ and $C_2$: The difference in axial forces between $C_1$ and $C_2$ at the connection level is what the beam shear is related to.
         *   Let's consider the equilibrium of the bay. The axial forces in columns resist the overturning.
         *   **Let's go with a common simplification:** The shear in the beam is the difference in axial forces in the columns connected by the beam, divided by the beam length. This is **not strictly correct** but often used in approximate methods.
         *   $V_{beam, left} = (F_{axial, C1} - F_{axial, C2}) / L_{beam} = (-5 - 0) / 6 = -0.833$ kN.
         *   $V_{beam, right} = (F_{axial, C2} - F_{axial, C3}) / L_{beam} = (0 - 5) / 6 = -0.833$ kN.
         *   $M_{beam-col, left} = V_{beam, left} \times (L_{beam}/2) = -0.833 \text{ kN} \times (6 \text{ m} / 2) = -2.5$ kNm.
         *   $M_{beam-col, right} = V_{beam, right} \times (L_{beam}/2) = -0.833 \text{ kN} \times (6 \text{ m} / 2) = -2.5$ kNm.

d) **Shear Forces in Columns (Top Storey):**
   *   **Simplified Assumption:** Exterior columns resist 5% of story shear each, interior columns resist the rest.
   *   Story Shear $V_1 = 20$ kN.
   *   Shear in Exterior Columns ($C_1, C_3$): $V_{ext} = 0.05 \times 20 \text{ kN} = 1$ kN.
   *   Shear in Interior Columns ($C_2$): $V_{int} = 20 \text{ kN} - 1 \text{ kN} - 1 \text{ kN} = 18$ kN.
   *   So, $|V_{C1}| = 1$ kN, $|V_{C2}| = 18$ kN, $|V_{C3}| = 1$ kN. (The direction would depend on the lateral load direction).

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Purpose of Approximate Methods:** To quickly estimate force distribution in indeterminate structures under lateral loads, useful for preliminary design and understanding.
*   **Portal Method:** Best for frames of moderate height. Assumes portal action. Tends to overestimate beam moments.
*   **Cantilever Method:** Best for tall, slender buildings. Assumes cantilever action. Generally provides more accurate column moments.
*   **Assumptions are Key:** The accuracy of these methods heavily relies on the validity of their underlying assumptions.
*   **Verification:** Results from approximate methods should ideally be verified by more rigorous methods for critical designs.
*   **Shear Distribution:** The distribution of story shear among columns is a significant assumption in both methods and can be varied (e.g., based on area, stiffness). The simplified distributions are common for ease of calculation.
*   **Inflection Points:** The assumed locations of inflection points are crucial for simplifying the analysis.
