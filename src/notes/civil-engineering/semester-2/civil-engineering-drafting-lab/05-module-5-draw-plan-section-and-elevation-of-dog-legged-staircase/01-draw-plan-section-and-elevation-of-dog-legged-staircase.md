# CIVIL ENGINEERING DRAFTING LAB

## Module 5: Draw Plan, Section and Elevation of a Dog-Legged Staircase

Welcome, everyone! In this module, we're going to dive into one of the most fundamental and frequently encountered elements in building design: the staircase. Specifically, we’ll be focusing on the **Dog-Legged Staircase**. This is a type of staircase where the two flights are separated by a half-space landing, and they run parallel to each other, creating a distinctive "dog-leg" appearance. Think of it like a dog bending its leg – the flights bend around a central point.

As we progress, you'll see how our understanding of drawing these staircases directly ties into the broader goals of our course. You’ll be able to organize your drawings professionally (CO1), detail essential building components (CO2), develop sketches from basic diagrams (CO3), and ultimately, draft these elements using software (CO4). This module is crucial for building a strong foundation in architectural drafting.

### 1. Understanding Staircases: The Basics

Before we draw, we need to understand the language of staircases. What are the key components we need to know?

*   **Tread:** This is the horizontal part of a step that you step on. Imagine walking up a staircase – the tread is where your foot lands.
*   **Riser:** This is the vertical part of a step, connecting one tread to the next. It’s the height you lift your foot.
*   **Going (or Run):** This is the horizontal distance covered by a single step. It's the depth of the tread plus any nosing (we'll get to nosing later if it's detailed).
*   **Width of Stair:** This is the clear width of the staircase, the space available for walking.
*   **Pitch (or Slope):** This is the angle of the staircase. A comfortable pitch is key for usability.
*   **Winder:** These are wedge-shaped steps used to change direction, often used in spiral staircases or to navigate tight corners. We won't see winders in a true dog-legged staircase *between* the flights, but understanding them is good general knowledge.
*   **Landing:** This is a flat, horizontal platform at the top or bottom of a flight of stairs. It provides a resting place and is essential for changing direction. In a dog-legged staircase, the landing between the two parallel flights is called the **half-space landing**.
*   **Newel Post:** This is a vertical post that supports the handrail at the beginning, end, and turns of a staircase.
*   **Handrail:** The rail supported by newel posts and balusters, designed to provide support while climbing.
*   **Baluster (or Spindle):** These are the vertical bars that support the handrail and prevent people from falling through the open spaces.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:** Knowing these terms is fundamental to CO2 (illustrating the detailing of building components). When you're asked to draw a staircase, you need to know *what* to draw and *what* the parts are called.

#### 1.1. Ideal Dimensions and Regulations

Building codes are incredibly important in drafting. They ensure safety and usability. While we'll refer to the National Building Code of India (NBC) and local building rules like the Kerala Panchayat Building Rules and Kerala Municipality Building Rules, here are some commonly accepted ideal dimensions. Remember, these can vary based on specific building use and local regulations, so always check the latest codes.

*   **Riser Height:** Ideally between 150 mm to 180 mm. Too high, and it's tiring; too low, and you might trip. Think about climbing a step at home; it's usually a comfortable height.
*   **Tread Going:** Ideally between 250 mm to 300 mm. This gives you enough space for your foot.
*   **Width of Stair:** For residential buildings, a minimum of 900 mm is common. Public buildings might require wider stairs.
*   **Headroom:** This is the vertical clearance from the edge of a tread to the ceiling or any overhead obstruction. A minimum of 2.1 meters is usually required. This is crucial; you don't want people hitting their heads!

**Textbook Reference:** Shah, Kale, and Patki, in their book "Building Drawing With An Integrated Approach to Built Environment," often provide detailed discussions on these functional aspects of staircases and reference codes. Prabhu's "Building Drawing and Detailing" also delves into these parameters for practical design.

**Exam Tip:** Be prepared to quote typical riser and going dimensions. Understanding *why* these dimensions are chosen (comfort, safety) is as important as memorizing them.

### 2. The Dog-Legged Staircase: Design and Layout

The dog-legged staircase is popular because it's space-efficient and avoids the need for intermediate landings except for the half-space landing between the two parallel flights. This makes it ideal for connecting two floors within a building.

Imagine a typical residential building where you need to go from the ground floor to the first floor. You start on the ground floor, walk up one flight, reach a landing, and then turn 180 degrees to walk up another flight to the first floor. The two flights run parallel to each other.

#### 2.1. Key Features of a Dog-Legged Staircase

*   **Parallel Flights:** The two flights are positioned one after another, parallel to each other.
*   **Half-Space Landing:** A landing is provided between the two flights, occupying the same width as the staircase and extending for the length of one tread (or slightly more). This landing connects the end of the first flight to the beginning of the second flight.
*   **No Winders:** Unlike a quarter-turn staircase, a dog-legged staircase typically doesn't use winders to change direction. The change in direction is facilitated by the half-space landing.
*   **Central Well (Optional but common):** Often, there's a central "well" or void around which the staircase turns. The width of this well is determined by the staircase width and the thickness of the supporting walls or structural elements.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:** Understanding these features is the first step towards CO3 (developing a sketch from a line diagram). If you're given a simple outline of where a staircase needs to be, knowing it's a dog-legged type tells you the general arrangement of flights and landings.

### 3. Drafting the Dog-Legged Staircase: Plan View

The plan view shows the staircase as if you're looking down from above. It's like an aerial view. This is where you see the footprint of the staircase within the building.

#### 3.1. What to Show in the Plan

*   **Flights:** You'll see the treads and risers depicted as lines. Each tread is a horizontal line, and the risers are shown at the end of each flight.
*   **Landings:** The horizontal landing will be clearly visible.
*   **Direction of Travel:** Arrows indicate the direction of ascent. This is crucial for clarity.
*   **Walls and Openings:** The surrounding walls and any openings (like doorways on landings) are shown.
*   **Nosing:** The projection of the tread beyond the riser. This is often shown in detailed plans.
*   **Section Lines:** Lines indicating where a section cut will be taken for the sectional elevation.

#### 3.2. Step-by-Step Drafting (Plan)

Let’s imagine a scenario: a residential building with a ground floor and first floor. We need to design a dog-legged staircase connecting them. Assume the total rise between floors is 3 meters, and we want a comfortable riser height of 175 mm.

1.  **Calculate Number of Risers:**
    Total Rise = 3000 mm
    Riser Height = 175 mm
    Number of Risers = Total Rise / Riser Height = 3000 / 175 = 17.14. Since we can't have a fraction of a riser, we'll adjust. We can have 17 risers and a slightly larger final tread, or 18 risers with a slightly reduced riser height. Let's aim for 18 risers.
    Adjusted Riser Height = 3000 mm / 18 = 166.67 mm (Let's round this to 170 mm for practical purposes and see if it works with the tread).

2.  **Calculate Number of Treads:**
    For a dog-legged staircase, the number of treads is generally one less than the number of risers in a flight if the flight starts and ends on a landing. If a flight starts on a floor and ends on a landing, it has one less tread than the number of risers it contains.
    Let's assume each flight has 9 risers. This means 8 treads in each flight.
    If we have 18 risers in total, we can have two flights of 9 risers each.
    This means 8 treads per flight.

3.  **Determine Going:**
    If riser height is ~170 mm, and we want a total going of ~250 mm per step (tread + nosing), the tread going (horizontal projection of the tread) would be around 250 mm.

4.  **Layout:**
    *   Define the flight width (e.g., 1 meter).
    *   Draw the first flight: Lay out 8 treads with their respective risers. The total horizontal length of the flight will be 8 treads * 250 mm/tread = 2000 mm (2 meters).
    *   Draw the half-space landing: This landing will be at the end of the first flight. Its length is equal to the going of one tread (250 mm) plus any necessary clearance. Its width is the width of the stair (1000 mm).
    *   Draw the second flight: Parallel to the first flight, starting from the half-space landing. Again, lay out 8 treads and their risers.
    *   Indicate the direction of ascent with arrows.
    *   Add surrounding walls.
    *   Mark the section line.

**Example Analogy:** Imagine you're laying out a path in a garden. You lay down stepping stones (treads). The distance between them horizontally is the going. The height you step up is the riser. The dog-legged staircase is like laying out two straight paths of stones, one after the other, with a small patio (landing) in between to change direction.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:** This process directly addresses CO3. You're taking calculated dimensions and laying them out visually. It also touches on CO1 by emphasizing systematic and professional drawing.

### 4. Drafting the Dog-Legged Staircase: Sectional Elevation

The sectional elevation shows the staircase as if a vertical plane has cut through it. This view is crucial for understanding the rise and run, the headroom, and the overall structure.

#### 4.1. What to Show in the Sectional Elevation

*   **Flights and Risers:** You'll clearly see the profile of the steps, showing the risers and treads.
*   **Landings:** The horizontal landing will be visible in section.
*   **Headroom:** The vertical clearance above the stairs and landings is clearly indicated.
*   **Total Rise and Going:** The overall dimensions of the staircase are evident.
*   **Structure:** You might show the supporting beams, slabs, or brickwork.
*   **Material Indications:** Shading or hatching to represent different materials.
*   **Balustrade:** If the section cuts through the balustrade, you'll see the handrail and balusters.

#### 4.2. Step-by-Step Drafting (Sectional Elevation)

Assuming we've defined our plan:

1.  **Draw the Ground Floor Level:** Establish a baseline representing the finished ground floor.
2.  **Draw the First Flight:** Starting from the ground floor, draw the risers vertically and the treads horizontally, ensuring the correct pitch. You'll be drawing a series of steps, one above the other.
3.  **Draw the Landing:** At the end of the first flight (after 9 risers), draw the horizontal landing.
4.  **Draw the Second Flight:** Parallel to the first flight, starting from the landing, draw the second series of risers and treads.
5.  **Indicate the First Floor Level:** Show the finished first floor level at the top of the second flight.
6.  **Show Headroom:** Measure and indicate the minimum headroom (e.g., 2.1 meters) from the edge of the treads and landings. This often involves drawing a horizontal line indicating the soffit (underside) of the floor slab or ceiling above.
7.  **Show Sectional Details:** If the section cuts through a wall, show the wall in section. If it cuts through the balustrade, show the handrail and balusters.

**Example Analogy:** Think about cutting a cake. A sectional elevation is like looking at the layers and arrangement inside the cake after you've made a cut. You see the sponge (treads), the filling (risers), and how it's all stacked.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:** This is a prime example of CO3. You are visualizing and representing the structure in 2D from a conceptual understanding. It also contributes to CO2 by detailing the profile of the steps and landings.

### 5. Drafting the Dog-Legged Staircase: Elevation

The elevation view shows the staircase from the side. This view is less common for detailing the *steps* themselves unless it's an external staircase, but it's crucial for showing the overall form, the supporting structure, and the relationship with surrounding walls. In the context of a dog-legged staircase within a building, a side elevation *might* show one of the flights and the landing, depending on the cutting plane. More often, we're interested in the elevation of the *well* or the *overall facade* if the stairs are visible externally.

However, if we interpret "elevation" as a view showing the face of the staircase (which might be how it's presented in some contexts, especially if the staircase is open on one side), it would resemble a detailed sectional view but without the cut.

Let's consider the elevation of the *staircase void* or the visible face of the staircase.

#### 5.1. What to Show in the Elevation

*   **Profile of Flights:** The sloped lines representing the nosings of the treads.
*   **Landing:** The horizontal landing.
*   **Balustrade:** The handrail, newel posts, and balusters are clearly visible.
*   **Surrounding Walls:** The enclosing walls or open space.
*   **Dimensions:** Overall height, width, and flight lengths.

#### 5.2. Step-by-Step Drafting (Elevation)

1.  **Establish Ground and First Floor Levels:** Draw horizontal lines for the floor levels.
2.  **Draw the Profile of the First Flight:** From the ground floor, draw a series of diagonal lines representing the nosings of the treads. For each riser, there's a horizontal step back, and for each tread, there's a vertical rise.
3.  **Draw the Landing:** Draw the horizontal landing at the appropriate level.
4.  **Draw the Profile of the Second Flight:** Parallel to the first, starting from the landing.
5.  **Draw the Balustrade:** Show the newel posts, the handrail running along the incline and on the landing, and the vertical balusters spaced evenly. The spacing of balusters is important – typically, the gap between them should not allow a 100 mm sphere to pass through, again for safety.
6.  **Add Walls:** Show the surrounding walls.

**Example Analogy:** If the plan is like looking down on a snake's path, and the section is like slicing through the snake to see its insides, the elevation is like looking at the snake's side profile. You see its curves and length.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:** This directly relates to CO3 by showing the external appearance of the staircase. It also reinforces CO2 by detailing the balustrade and handrail. If you were to use software (CO4), you'd be constructing these 3D forms from these 2D views.

### 6. Importance of Codes and Standards (IS 962:1989)

Remember, our drawings must comply with standards. IS 962:1989 (Indian Standard Code of practice for architectural and building drawings) provides guidance on how to represent various building elements, including staircases, in drawings. It specifies line types, conventions for showing sections, and how to annotate dimensions. Always refer to the latest reaffirmed version of the code.

**Textbook Reference:** M.V. Chitawadagi and S.S. Bhavikatti’s "Building Planning and Drawing" often includes chapters dedicated to building by-laws and drawing standards, which are essential for exam preparation and practical work.

**Exam Tip:** Be familiar with the basic symbols and line conventions for staircases as specified in IS codes. Knowing how to represent a hidden line for a tread below or above, or a section line, is important.

### 7. Practical Considerations and Common Pitfalls

*   **Headroom Clearance:** This is a major point. Ensure there's adequate headroom, especially at the turning point of the staircase and at the top and bottom. Students often forget to check this or draw it incorrectly.
*   **Consistency in Dimensions:** Make sure your riser heights and tread goings are consistent throughout the staircase.
*   **Scale:** Always draw to a specified scale (e.g., 1:50 or 1:100).
*   **Clarity of Lines:** Use appropriate line weights. Hidden lines for treads below the cutting plane should be distinct from visible lines.
*   **Direction Arrows:** Ensure direction arrows are clear and unambiguous.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:** Paying attention to these details is crucial for CO1 (systematic and professional organization) and CO4 (accurate software drafting).

### Sample Questions and Answers

**Q1. Define the terms 'riser' and 'going' in the context of a staircase. Why are specific ranges for these dimensions important?**

**Answer:**
*   **Riser:** The vertical surface of a step, representing the height one lifts their foot.
*   **Going:** The horizontal surface of a step, representing the depth your foot lands on.

These dimensions are important for **user comfort and safety**. A riser that is too high can be strenuous and lead to falls, while one that is too low might not be intuitive. Similarly, a going that is too small doesn't provide adequate foot support, increasing the risk of tripping, whereas a very large going can make the ascent awkward. Building codes specify preferred ranges (e.g., riser 150-180mm, going 250-300mm) to ensure a good balance between space efficiency and ergonomic design for most users.

**Q2. What is the main distinguishing feature of a dog-legged staircase compared to other types like a quarter-turn or half-turn staircase?**

**Answer:** The primary distinguishing feature of a dog-legged staircase is that it consists of **two parallel flights of stairs** separated by a **half-space landing**. Unlike quarter-turn or half-turn stairs which use winders to change direction, the dog-legged type uses this parallel arrangement and the intermediate landing for the 180-degree turn.

**Q3. In the plan view of a dog-legged staircase, what elements are essential to show for clarity?**

**Answer:** For clarity in the plan view, it is essential to show:
*   The outline of the flights, typically indicating the treads and risers.
*   The full extent of the half-space landing.
*   Clear **arrows indicating the direction of ascent** for each flight.
*   The width of the staircase.
*   Surrounding walls and any openings on the landings.
*   The line indicating where a sectional cut is taken for the sectional elevation.

**Q4. A staircase has a total rise of 3.6 meters. If you aim for a riser height of 180 mm, how many risers are required, and what would be the number of treads in one flight if the staircase is divided into two equal flights?**

**Answer:**
*   **Number of Risers:**
    Total Rise = 3.6 m = 3600 mm
    Riser Height = 180 mm
    Number of Risers = Total Rise / Riser Height = 3600 mm / 180 mm = 20 risers.

*   **Number of Treads:**
    If the staircase is divided into two equal flights, each flight will have 20 risers / 2 = 10 risers.
    For a flight that starts and ends on a landing, the number of treads is typically one less than the number of risers in that flight.
    Therefore, each flight will have 10 - 1 = **9 treads**.

    *(Note: This assumes a standard construction where the last step is effectively the landing itself, or the final step is a tread connected to the landing. If the landing is considered the final "step up," the tread count might be different. Always clarify assumptions based on the specific context or diagram.)*

**Q5. Explain the significance of headroom in staircase design and how it is typically represented in a sectional elevation.**

**Answer:**
Headroom is the vertical clearance between the walking surface of a step (or landing) and any overhead obstruction, such as a ceiling or the underside of a flight of stairs above. It is crucial for **safety and comfort**, preventing users from hitting their heads, especially at the beginning, end, and turning points of a staircase.

In a sectional elevation, headroom is typically represented by showing the clear vertical distance from the edge of a tread or the surface of a landing up to the soffit (underside) of the slab or beam above. A dimension line with arrows would be used to indicate this clearance, ensuring it meets the minimum requirements stipulated by building codes (e.g., 2.1 meters).
