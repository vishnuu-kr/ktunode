# Environmental Engineering Lab - Module 10: Determination of B.O.D.

## Topic: Determination of B.O.D.

**Introduction:**

Biochemical Oxygen Demand (B.O.D.) is a crucial parameter in wastewater treatment and water quality assessment. It represents the amount of dissolved oxygen (DO) required by aerobic biological organisms to break down organic material present in water under controlled laboratory conditions. This module will delve into the principles, procedures, and importance of B.O.D. determination.

## 1. Fundamental Principles of B.O.D.

*   **Definition:** Biochemical Oxygen Demand (B.O.D.) is the quantity of oxygen required by aerobic biological organisms to decompose organic matter in a water sample in the absence of sunlight. It is a measure of the biodegradable organic pollution.

*   **Mechanism:**
    *   Microorganisms (bacteria, fungi) in the water sample consume dissolved oxygen to metabolize and break down dissolved and suspended organic compounds.
    *   This process can be broadly divided into two stages:
        *   **Carbonaceous B.O.D. (cBOD):** The oxygen consumed for the oxidation of carbonaceous organic compounds to carbon dioxide. This is the primary focus of the standard B.O.D. test.
        *   **Nitrogenous B.O.D. (nBOD):** The oxygen consumed for the oxidation of nitrogenous compounds (e.g., ammonia) to nitrates by nitrifying bacteria. This occurs after the carbonaceous stage and is usually considered in longer B.O.D. tests (e.g., B.O.D. at 20 days).

*   **B.O.D. Curve:**
    *   The B.O.D. test plots the DO consumed over time.
    *   The curve typically shows a rapid initial decrease in DO followed by a slower, more gradual decrease as the readily biodegradable organic matter is depleted.
    *   The shape of the B.O.D. curve can indicate the presence of inhibitory substances or the acclimatization of the microbial population.

*   **Relationship with Dissolved Oxygen (DO):**
    *   B.O.D. is directly related to the consumption of DO.
    *   A higher B.O.D. value indicates a greater demand for oxygen, potentially leading to oxygen depletion in natural water bodies, which can harm aquatic life.

## 2. Key Components and Reagents for B.O.D. Determination

*   **Sample:** Wastewater, river water, or any water body to be tested.
*   **Microbial Seed (if necessary):**
    *   **Purpose:** To provide a sufficient and acclimatized microbial population for the decomposition of organic matter, especially in samples with low microbial content (e.g., chlorinated wastewater, very dilute samples).
    *   **Sources:** Mature activated sludge, settled sewage, or pond water.
    *   **Seeding Ratio:** The amount of seed added is crucial. Too little seed may result in incomplete biodegradation, while too much can lead to rapid oxygen depletion and inaccurate results.
*   **Dilution Water:**
    *   **Purpose:** To dilute the sample to a concentration where the DO depletion can be accurately measured and to provide essential nutrients and DO for the microorganisms.
    *   **Characteristics:** Must be saturated with DO, free from interfering substances, and have low B.O.D. and ammonium.
    *   **Preparation:** Typically prepared by aerating distilled or deionized water and then buffering it to a neutral pH.
*   **Nutrient Buffer Solution:**
    *   **Purpose:** To maintain the pH of the dilution water within the optimal range (pH 7.0-7.5) for microbial activity.
    *   **Composition:** Typically contains phosphate salts (e.g., potassium dihydrogen phosphate, disodium hydrogen phosphate, sodium sulfate) and sometimes calcium chloride, magnesium sulfate, and ferric chloride.
*   **Dissolved Oxygen (DO) Meter or Winkler Titration Reagents:**
    *   **DO Meter:** Used to measure dissolved oxygen concentrations electrochemically.
    *   **Winkler Titration Reagents:**
        *   **Manganous sulfate solution:** Reacts with dissolved oxygen to form a precipitate.
        *   **Alkaline iodide-azide solution:** Forms manganic hydroxide precipitate. Azide is added to prevent nitrite interference.
        *   **Sulfuric acid (concentrated):** Acidifies the solution, converting precipitated manganese compounds into soluble compounds and liberating iodine.
        *   **Starch indicator solution:** Used to detect the endpoint of the titration with sodium thiosulfate.
        *   **Standardized sodium thiosulfate solution:** Titrates the liberated iodine.

## 3. Performing the B.O.D. Test (Standard 5-Day B.O.D. - BOD5)

**Objective:** To determine the amount of dissolved oxygen consumed by microorganisms in a sample over a 5-day incubation period at 20°C.

**Procedure:**

1.  **Sample Preparation:**
    *   If the sample has a high B.O.D. (e.g., raw sewage), dilution is necessary.
    *   If the sample is likely to have a low B.O.D. or a low microbial population, seeding may be required.

2.  **Dilution (if necessary):**
    *   Calculate the required dilution factor (DF) based on the expected B.O.D. of the sample. A general guideline is to achieve a DO depletion of at least 2 mg/L and a final DO of at least 1 mg/L.
    *   **Formula for Dilution Factor (DF):**
        $DF = \frac{\text{Volume of Dilution Water}}{\text{Volume of Sample}}$

3.  **Seeding (if necessary):**
    *   If seeding is required, prepare the seeded dilution water by adding a known amount of seed to the dilution water.
    *   Measure the DO of the seeded dilution water before and after incubation (blank).

4.  **Filling B.O.D. Bottles:**
    *   Use clean, sterile 300 mL B.O.D. bottles.
    *   Fill the bottles carefully with the diluted sample (or seeded dilution water for the blank) by allowing the water to flow gently from the bottom upwards, displacing air and avoiding air bubbles.
    *   Fill the bottles completely to the brim, ensuring no air bubbles are trapped.
    *   Insert the glass stopper firmly, ensuring it is sealed to prevent air exchange. The excess liquid should form a meniscus around the stopper.

5.  **Initial DO Measurement:**
    *   Immediately after filling the bottles, measure the dissolved oxygen concentration in at least two bottles of the diluted sample (and the blank if seeded) using a DO meter or by Winkler titration. Record these as initial DO ($DO_i$).

6.  **Incubation:**
    *   Place the filled B.O.D. bottles in a dark incubator maintained at a constant temperature of 20 ± 1°C.
    *   Incubate for 5 days.

7.  **Final DO Measurement:**
    *   After 5 days of incubation, carefully remove the bottles from the incubator.
    *   Measure the dissolved oxygen concentration in the remaining bottles of the diluted sample (and the blank if seeded). Record these as final DO ($DO_f$).

## 4. Calculating and Interpreting B.O.D. Value

**Calculations:**

*   **B.O.D. for Unseeded Samples:**

    $BOD_5 = \frac{DO_i - DO_f}{P}$

    Where:
    *   $DO_i$ = Initial dissolved oxygen concentration of the diluted sample (mg/L)
    *   $DO_f$ = Final dissolved oxygen concentration of the diluted sample (mg/L)
    *   $P$ = Decimal volumetric fraction of sample used in the dilution
        $P = \frac{\text{Volume of Sample}}{\text{Volume of Sample} + \text{Volume of Dilution Water}}$

*   **B.O.D. for Seeded Samples:**

    $BOD_5 = \frac{(DO_i - DO_f) - (S_b \times (DO_{bi} - DO_{bf}))}{P}$

    Where:
    *   $S_b$ = Decimal volumetric fraction of seed in the dilution water
        $S_b = \frac{\text{Volume of Seed}}{\text{Volume of Seed} + \text{Volume of Dilution Water}}$
    *   $DO_{bi}$ = Initial dissolved oxygen concentration of the seeded blank (mg/L)
    *   $DO_{bf}$ = Final dissolved oxygen concentration of the seeded blank (mg/L)

*   **B.O.D. for Seeded Samples (using Seed Correction Factor):**

    $BOD_5 = \frac{(DO_i - DO_f) - (DO_{bi} - DO_{bf}) \times \frac{\text{Volume of Seed in Sample}}{\text{Volume of Seed in Blank}}}{P}$

    **Note:** The common practice is to use the seeded blank to calculate the oxygen demand exerted by the seed itself, which is then subtracted from the total oxygen demand observed in the seeded sample.

**Interpretation:**

*   The calculated B.O.D.5 value represents the amount of oxygen (in mg/L) consumed by the microorganisms in the sample over 5 days at 20°C.
*   **High B.O.D. values** indicate a high concentration of biodegradable organic matter, suggesting significant pollution and a high potential to deplete dissolved oxygen in receiving waters.
*   **Low B.O.D. values** indicate a lower concentration of biodegradable organic matter.
*   **Typical B.O.D. ranges:**
    *   Clean rivers: < 1 mg/L
    *   Moderately polluted: 5-15 mg/L
    *   Heavily polluted: > 15 mg/L
    *   Treated wastewater: 5-20 mg/L
    *   Untreated domestic sewage: 150-300 mg/L

## 5. Factors Influencing B.O.D. and Potential Sources of Error

**Factors Influencing B.O.D.:**

*   **Temperature:**
    *   The B.O.D. test is standardized at 20°C. Higher temperatures generally increase the rate of microbial activity and thus the B.O.D., but can also lead to faster DO depletion.
    *   Lower temperatures slow down microbial activity.
*   **Microbial Population:**
    *   The presence of a healthy and acclimatized microbial population is essential for accurate B.O.D. determination.
    *   Samples with inhibitory substances (e.g., heavy metals, chlorine) may require special pretreatment or may not yield valid B.O.D. results.
*   **pH:**
    *   Optimal pH for microbial activity is around 7.0-7.5. Significant deviations from this range can inhibit biological activity.
*   **Nutrients:**
    *   Adequate nitrogen and phosphorus are necessary for microbial growth and metabolism. Dilution water and buffer solutions provide these nutrients.
*   **Dissolved Oxygen:**
    *   Sufficient DO must be present throughout the incubation period for aerobic decomposition. If DO drops to zero, anaerobic conditions will prevail, and the B.O.D. cannot be accurately measured. This is why dilutions are important.
*   **Turbidity/Suspended Solids:**
    *   High levels of suspended solids can shield microorganisms from light (if present) or contribute to oxygen demand directly.
*   **Presence of Inhibitory Substances:**
    *   Some chemicals (e.g., heavy metals, chlorine, certain organic compounds) can inhibit or kill the microorganisms, leading to falsely low B.O.D. values. Pretreatment might be necessary.

**Potential Sources of Error:**

*   **Air Bubbles in B.O.D. Bottles:** Trapped air bubbles can exchange oxygen with the atmosphere, leading to inaccurate final DO readings.
*   **Incorrect Dilution:**
    *   Too little dilution: DO may be depleted to zero before the end of the incubation.
    *   Too much dilution: Insufficient oxygen depletion to measure accurately.
*   **Inadequate Seeding:** If seeding is required but insufficient or non-acclimatized seed is used, the B.O.D. will be underestimated.
*   **Improper Seeding Correction:** Errors in calculating the oxygen demand exerted by the seed.
*   **Temperature Fluctuations:** Failure to maintain the incubator at 20°C can significantly affect the results.
*   **Light Exposure:** Light can promote photosynthesis in samples containing algae, leading to oxygen production and underestimation of B.O.D.
*   **Leakage of Stopper:** Air leakage into or out of the B.O.D. bottle will compromise the DO measurements.
*   **Interference from Nitrites:** Nitrite can be oxidized to nitrate, consuming oxygen. Azide is added to the alkaline iodide solution to prevent this.
*   **Inaccurate DO Measurements:** Errors in the initial or final DO measurements (either with a meter or titration).

## 6. Significance of B.O.D. in Water Quality Management and Wastewater Treatment

*   **Wastewater Treatment Performance:**
    *   B.O.D. is a primary parameter for evaluating the effectiveness of wastewater treatment processes. A reduction in B.O.D. indicates the removal of organic pollutants.
    *   It is used to design and operate biological treatment systems (e.g., activated sludge, trickling filters).
*   **Water Quality Assessment:**
    *   B.O.D. is used to assess the quality of surface waters (rivers, lakes, oceans). High B.O.D. in natural waters indicates organic pollution, which can lead to:
        *   **Oxygen Depletion:** Aquatic organisms, especially fish, require dissolved oxygen to survive. When B.O.D. is high, dissolved oxygen levels can drop below critical thresholds, causing suffocation and fish kills.
        *   **Eutrophication:** Organic pollution can contribute to nutrient enrichment, leading to algal blooms, which further exacerbate oxygen depletion when the algae decompose.
        *   **Aesthetic Degradation:** Decomposing organic matter can produce unpleasant odors and colors.
*   **Regulatory Compliance:**
    *   Environmental regulations often set limits on the B.O.D. concentration allowed in treated wastewater discharges to protect receiving water bodies.
*   **Process Control:**
    *   Monitoring B.O.D. at various stages of a treatment plant helps in optimizing operational parameters.

## Answers to Practice Questions:

**Answer 1:**
**Biochemical Oxygen Demand (B.O.D.)** is the amount of dissolved oxygen required by aerobic biological organisms to decompose the organic matter present in a water sample under controlled laboratory conditions over a specified period (usually 5 days) at a given temperature (usually 20°C).

*   **Carbonaceous B.O.D. (cBOD):** The oxygen consumed for the oxidation of carbon-containing organic compounds to carbon dioxide. This is the primary component measured in a standard BOD test.
*   **Nitrogenous B.O.D. (nBOD):** The oxygen consumed for the oxidation of nitrogen-containing organic compounds (like ammonia) to nitrates by nitrifying bacteria. This typically occurs after the carbonaceous stage and is relevant for longer incubation periods.

**Answer 2:**
Given:
*   Dilution ratio = 1:10
*   Volume of sample used = 1 part
*   Volume of dilution water = 10 parts
*   Total volume = 11 parts
*   $P = \frac{\text{Volume of Sample}}{\text{Total Volume}} = \frac{1}{11} \approx 0.0909$
*   $DO_i = 7.5 \text{ mg/L}$
*   $DO_f = 2.0 \text{ mg/L}$

$BOD_5 = \frac{DO_i - DO_f}{P} = \frac{7.5 - 2.0}{0.0909} = \frac{5.5}{0.0909} \approx 60.5 \text{ mg/L}$

**Answer 3:**
Given:
*   Dilution ratio of sample = 1:50 (meaning P = 1/50 = 0.02)
*   $DO_i = 8.0 \text{ mg/L}$
*   $DO_f = 3.5 \text{ mg/L}$
*   $DO_{bi} = 7.0 \text{ mg/L}$
*   $DO_{bf} = 5.5 \text{ mg/L}$
*   Volume of seed in sample dilution = 1%
*   Volume of seed in blank = 1%

First, calculate the oxygen consumed by the seed in the blank:
Oxygen consumed by seed = $DO_{bi} - DO_{bf} = 7.0 - 5.5 = 1.5 \text{ mg/L}$

Now, use the formula for seeded samples:
$BOD_5 = \frac{(DO_i - DO_f) - (DO_{bi} - DO_{bf}) \times \frac{\text{Volume of Seed in Sample}}{\text{Volume of Seed in Blank}}}{P}$
*Note: Since the volume of seed is the same in both the sample dilution and the blank, the seed correction factor is 1 (or you can simply use the oxygen consumed by the seed calculated above).*

$BOD_5 = \frac{(8.0 - 3.5) - (7.0 - 5.5) \times \frac{1}{1}}{0.02}$
$BOD_5 = \frac{4.5 - 1.5}{0.02}$
$BOD_5 = \frac{3.0}{0.02} = 150 \text{ mg/L}$

**Answer 4:**
Three factors influencing B.O.D. results and their effects:

1.  **Temperature:** The B.O.D. test is standardized at 20°C.
    *   **Higher temperatures:** Increase the rate of microbial metabolism and enzymatic activity, leading to a faster rate of oxygen consumption and potentially higher B.O.D. values if not controlled. Can also lead to faster DO depletion.
    *   **Lower temperatures:** Slow down microbial activity, resulting in a slower rate of oxygen consumption and potentially lower B.O.D. values.
2.  **Presence of Inhibitory Substances:** Chemicals like heavy metals, chlorine, or certain organic compounds can inhibit or kill the microorganisms responsible for decomposition.
    *   **Effect:** This leads to a reduced rate of oxygen consumption and thus falsely low B.O.D. results. Pretreatment might be required.
3.  **Microbial Population (Seeding):** The availability and acclimatization of microorganisms are crucial.
    *   **Insufficient or non-acclimatized seed:** In samples with low microbial density or containing substances unfamiliar to the microbes (e.g., chlorinated wastewater), the biodegradation process will be incomplete, leading to an underestimation of the true B.O.D.
    *   **Too much seed:** Can lead to rapid depletion of DO in the bottle, making accurate measurement impossible.

**Answer 5:**
Maintaining the incubation temperature at 20°C for the B.O.D. test is crucial for standardization and comparability of results.
*   **Standardization:** It ensures that the rate of microbial activity and biological oxidation is consistent across different tests and laboratories, allowing for direct comparison of B.O.D. values.
*   **Biological Activity:** 20°C is considered an optimal temperature for the activity of many mesophilic microorganisms commonly found in wastewater and natural waters, ensuring efficient decomposition of organic matter.
*   **Preventing Extreme Rates:** Deviations from 20°C can lead to either excessively rapid oxygen consumption (at higher temperatures) or very slow consumption (at lower temperatures), both of which can compromise the accuracy and validity of the 5-day test.

## Important Points to Remember:

*   **B.O.D. is a measure of biodegradable organic matter.** It does not measure all organic compounds, only those that can be oxidized by microorganisms.
*   **The B.O.D. test is a biological bioassay.** Its accuracy depends heavily on the health and activity of the microbial population.
*   **Dissolved oxygen is critical.** Ensure sufficient DO remains in the bottle throughout the incubation period. This is why dilutions are often necessary.
*   **Temperature control is paramount.** Use a calibrated incubator set at 20 ± 1°C.
*   **Proper sealing of B.O.D. bottles** is essential to prevent air exchange.
*   **If seeding is necessary, a seed correction** must be applied to the calculated B.O.D.
*   **Always check the DO depletion** in the diluted sample. If the final DO is too high (e.g., > 1 mg/L remaining) or too low (e.g., < 1 mg/L remaining), the dilution may not have been appropriate.
*   **B.O.D.5 is the most common measure**, but longer incubation periods can reveal different aspects of the organic matter's degradability.
*   **B.O.D. is a more sensitive indicator of organic pollution** than Chemical Oxygen Demand (C.O.D.) for readily biodegradable substances, but C.O.D. measures all oxidizable compounds.
