# Environmental Engineering Lab: Determination of Chlorine Demand

## Module 13: Determination of Chlorine Demand

### Topic: Determination of Chlorine Demand

This module focuses on understanding and experimentally determining the chlorine demand of water. Chlorine is a widely used disinfectant in water treatment, but its effectiveness is influenced by the presence of impurities in the water. Chlorine demand quantifies the amount of chlorine that is consumed by these impurities.

---

### Learning Outcomes:

*   **Understand the concept of chlorine demand and its significance in water treatment.**
*   **Identify the factors influencing chlorine demand.**
*   **Learn and apply the standard method for determining chlorine demand.**
*   **Interpret the results of chlorine demand tests to inform disinfection strategies.**
*   **Discuss the practical implications of chlorine demand in real-world water treatment scenarios.**

### 1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Disinfection:** The process of eliminating or inactivating harmful microorganisms (pathogens) from water to make it safe for consumption.
*   **Chlorination:** A common disinfection method that uses chlorine or chlorine-containing compounds to kill or inactivate microorganisms.
*   **Chlorine:** A chemical element (Cl) that is a strong oxidizing agent and is highly effective in killing bacteria, viruses, and other pathogens.
*   **Residual Chlorine:** The amount of chlorine remaining in the water after it has been applied and allowed to react with impurities and microorganisms. A certain level of residual chlorine is necessary to provide continuous disinfection as water travels through the distribution system.
*   **Chlorine Demand (CD):** The difference between the amount of chlorine applied to water and the amount of residual chlorine remaining after a specified contact time. It represents the amount of chlorine consumed by reacting with inorganic and organic compounds in the water.

    **Formula:**
    $CD = \text{Chlorine Applied} - \text{Residual Chlorine}$

*   **Combined Chlorine:** Chlorine that has reacted with ammonia and organic nitrogen compounds to form chloramines. Chloramines are less reactive but provide a more stable and longer-lasting residual compared to free chlorine.
*   **Free Chlorine:** Chlorine that is present in the water as hypochlorous acid (HOCl) and hypochlorite ions (OCl⁻). Free chlorine is a stronger disinfectant than combined chlorine.
*   **Total Chlorine:** The sum of free chlorine and combined chlorine.

---

### 2. Factors Influencing Chlorine Demand

The chlorine demand of water is not a fixed value and can vary significantly depending on several factors:

*   **Nature and Concentration of Impurities:**
    *   **Organic Matter:** Dissolved and suspended organic compounds (from decaying vegetation, sewage, etc.) are major consumers of chlorine, forming combined chlorine and organic byproducts.
    *   **Inorganic Matter:** Reduced inorganic compounds like ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), manganous manganese (Mn²⁺), sulfides (S²⁻), and nitrites (NO₂⁻) readily react with and consume chlorine.
    *   **Ammonia:** Ammonia present in water reacts with chlorine to form chloramines, which contribute to combined chlorine. The presence of ammonia can initially increase chlorine demand.
*   **pH of Water:** The pH affects the speciation of free chlorine. At lower pH values (below ~7.5), hypochlorous acid (HOCl) predominates, which is a stronger disinfectant. At higher pH values, hypochlorite ions (OCl⁻) are more prevalent, which are less effective. This pH dependency can indirectly influence the rate of chlorine consumption.
*   **Temperature:** Higher temperatures generally increase the rate of chemical reactions, including those involving chlorine. Therefore, chlorine demand can be higher at elevated temperatures.
*   **Contact Time:** The duration for which chlorine is allowed to react with the water. Longer contact times allow for more complete consumption of chlorine by demand-causing substances.
*   **Chlorine Application Rate:** The initial concentration of chlorine applied.

### 3. Standard Method for Determining Chlorine Demand

The standard method for determining chlorine demand is typically a series of tests where varying doses of chlorine are added to water samples, and the residual chlorine is measured after a specific contact time. This allows for the construction of a chlorine demand curve.

**Objective:** To determine the chlorine demand of a given water sample at a specific contact time.

**Materials:**

*   Water sample
*   Chlorine solution (e.g., sodium hypochlorite - bleach) of known concentration
*   D.P.D. (N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine) reagent kit or spectrophotometer for chlorine residual determination
*   Burettes, volumetric flasks, beakers
*   Stopwatch
*   pH meter (optional, for monitoring pH changes)

**Procedure:**

1.  **Prepare Chlorine Dosing Solutions:** Prepare a series of chlorine solutions with progressively higher concentrations. For example, if you are aiming for initial chlorine doses of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0 mg/L in your sample, you'll need to calculate the required concentration of your stock chlorine solution and the volume to add to a fixed volume of water sample.
2.  **Prepare Water Samples:** Take several identical volumes (e.g., 200 mL) of the water sample into separate beakers.
3.  **Add Chlorine Doses:**
    *   To the first beaker, add a calculated volume of the lowest concentration chlorine dosing solution to achieve the desired initial chlorine dose (e.g., 0.5 mg/L).
    *   Repeat this for all beakers, adding progressively higher doses of chlorine.
    *   **Crucially, start a stopwatch immediately after adding the chlorine to each beaker.**
4.  **Contact Time:** Allow the chlorine to react with the water for a predetermined contact time (e.g., 15 or 30 minutes). Ensure all samples have the same contact time.
5.  **Determine Residual Chlorine:** After the contact time, measure the residual chlorine in each beaker using the D.P.D. method.
    *   For each sample, take a measured aliquot (e.g., 50 mL) into a test tube or cuvette.
    *   Add the D.P.D. reagent as per the kit's instructions.
    *   If using a colorimetric kit, compare the color developed to a color chart.
    *   If using a spectrophotometer, measure the absorbance and convert to concentration using a calibration curve.
6.  **Record Data:** Record the initial chlorine dose applied and the corresponding residual chlorine concentration for each sample.

**Data Analysis:**

*   **Calculate Chlorine Demand:** For each sample, calculate the chlorine demand using the formula:
    $CD = \text{Initial Chlorine Dose} - \text{Residual Chlorine}$
*   **Plot the Chlorine Demand Curve:** Plot the initial chlorine dose (x-axis) against the calculated chlorine demand (y-axis).
*   **Interpret the Curve:**
    *   The curve will typically show an increasing chlorine demand as the applied dose increases, up to a point.
    *   The **break point** on the curve is the point where further addition of chlorine results in a rapid increase in residual chlorine. This indicates that most of the chlorine-consuming substances have been satisfied.
    *   The **chlorine demand** is often reported as the amount of chlorine consumed at a specific dose that achieves a desired residual (e.g., the demand at the breakpoint or the demand at a dose that yields 1 mg/L residual).

---

### 4. Interpreting Results and Practical Implications

*   **Determining Optimal Chlorination Dose:** The chlorine demand curve helps in determining the appropriate dose of chlorine to achieve effective disinfection.
    *   **Breakpoint Chlorination:** This is a common practice where enough chlorine is added to satisfy the chlorine demand and then slightly exceed it to maintain a free chlorine residual. This process oxidizes ammonia and organic nitrogen compounds, which are less effective disinfectants and can form undesirable disinfection byproducts (DBPs).
    *   **Dosage to Achieve Residual:** If the goal is to achieve a specific residual chlorine level (e.g., 1 mg/L free chlorine residual), you can find the corresponding initial chlorine dose from the curve that yields that residual after the contact time.
*   **Water Quality Assessment:** A high chlorine demand indicates a water source with significant levels of organic and inorganic impurities. This might necessitate pre-treatment steps like coagulation, sedimentation, and filtration to remove these substances before chlorination.
*   **Disinfection Byproduct (DBP) Formation:** The reaction of chlorine with organic matter can lead to the formation of DBPs, which can have adverse health effects. Understanding chlorine demand helps in optimizing chlorination to minimize DBP formation while ensuring effective disinfection. For instance, breakpoint chlorination can help reduce the formation of certain DBPs.
*   **Operational Adjustments:** The results of chlorine demand tests can inform operators about necessary adjustments to the chlorination dosage based on seasonal variations in raw water quality or changes in treatment plant influent.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A water sample was tested for chlorine demand. The following data were obtained after a 20-minute contact time:

| Sample | Chlorine Applied (mg/L) | Residual Chlorine (mg/L) |
| :----- | :---------------------- | :----------------------- |
| A      | 1.0                     | 0.2                      |
| B      | 2.0                     | 0.5                      |
| C      | 3.0                     | 1.0                      |
| D      | 4.0                     | 2.5                      |
| E      | 5.0                     | 4.2                      |

Calculate the chlorine demand for each sample and determine the chlorine demand at the breakpoint. (Assume the breakpoint occurs at the last dose that does not result in a significant jump in residual).

**Answer 1:**

*   **Sample A:** CD = 1.0 - 0.2 = 0.8 mg/L
*   **Sample B:** CD = 2.0 - 0.5 = 1.5 mg/L
*   **Sample C:** CD = 3.0 - 1.0 = 2.0 mg/L
*   **Sample D:** CD = 4.0 - 2.5 = 1.5 mg/L
*   **Sample E:** CD = 5.0 - 4.2 = 0.8 mg/L

To estimate the breakpoint, observe the change in residual chlorine relative to the applied dose. The largest increase in residual for a given increase in applied dose occurs between Sample C (3.0 mg/L applied, 1.0 mg/L residual) and Sample D (4.0 mg/L applied, 2.5 mg/L residual). The increase in residual is 1.5 mg/L for a 1.0 mg/L increase in applied dose.

The chlorine demand at the point where the residual starts to significantly increase (near the breakpoint) can be estimated from the data. If we consider the dose that would *just* satisfy the demand before the residual increases sharply, it's around the dose that results in a plateau or a smaller increase in demand. Based on the calculated demands (0.8, 1.5, 2.0, 1.5, 0.8), the peak demand appears to be around 2.0 mg/L (at an applied dose of 3.0 mg/L).

The breakpoint itself is the *applied dose* where the residual starts to increase rapidly. This occurs *after* the peak demand. A common way to approximate the breakpoint dose is where the chlorine demand curve flattens out or starts to decrease. In this case, it's around the 3.0 to 4.0 mg/L applied dose range. If we assume the goal is to reach the breakpoint, we might apply a dose of around 4.0 mg/L, which leaves a residual of 2.5 mg/L, meaning a demand of 1.5 mg/L at this point.

*   **Chlorine Demand at Breakpoint (estimated):** Around 1.5 mg/L (This is the amount of chlorine consumed when the applied dose is sufficient to reach the breakpoint). The breakpoint *dose* is approximately 3.5-4.0 mg/L.

List three factors that can influence the chlorine demand of water.

**Answer 2:**
1.  Concentration and type of organic matter.

**Question 3:**
What is the significance of breakpoint chlorination in water treatment?

**Answer 3:**
Breakpoint chlorination is significant because it ensures that all chlorine-consuming substances (organic matter, ammonia, etc.) have been reacted. This allows for the establishment of a stable and effective free chlorine residual, which provides continuous disinfection in the distribution system. It also helps in oxidizing ammonia and reducing the formation of undesirable disinfection byproducts (DBPs) compared to chloramination alone.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Chlorine demand is a dynamic parameter:** It changes with water quality and environmental conditions.
*   **Contact time is critical:** The determination of chlorine demand must be performed at a specific, standardized contact time.
*   **Accurate measurement of residuals:** The accuracy of the residual chlorine measurement directly impacts the calculated chlorine demand.
*   **Breakpoint chlorination is key:** Aiming for breakpoint chlorination is often the most effective strategy for disinfection.
*   **Understanding demand informs dosage:** Chlorine demand data is essential for calculating the correct chlorine dosage for effective disinfection and for predicting residual levels.
*   **DBP formation is a concern:** Optimize chlorination practices to balance effective disinfection with minimizing harmful DBP formation.
