# REPAIR AND REHABILITATION OF STRUCTURES

## Module 2: Damage Diagnosis and Assessment

### Topic: Strength and Durability of Concrete Structures

This module focuses on understanding the fundamental properties of concrete, their impact on the long-term performance of structures, and how various environmental factors can lead to degradation. This knowledge is crucial for diagnosing existing damage and planning effective repair and rehabilitation strategies.

---

### 1. Quality Assurance for Concrete

Quality assurance (QA) in concrete construction is a systematic process to ensure that the concrete produced and placed meets the specified requirements for strength, durability, and workability. It involves a series of checks and controls from the raw materials to the hardened concrete.

#### 1.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Quality Assurance (QA):** A planned and systematic process to ensure that a product or service satisfies stated requirements.
*   **Quality Control (QC):** The operational techniques and activities used to fulfill requirements for quality.
*   **Workability:** The ease with which concrete can be mixed, placed, compacted, and finished without segregation.
*   **Strength:** The ability of concrete to resist applied loads. Primarily measured by compressive strength.
*   **Durability:** The ability of concrete to resist weathering action, chemical attack, abrasion, or any other process of deterioration.
*   **Consistency:** Refers to the uniformity of concrete mix, meaning consistent proportions of ingredients.

#### 1.2. Components of QA for Concrete

*   **Raw Material Inspection:**
    *   **Cement:** Checking for type, fineness, setting time, soundness, and strength.
    *   **Aggregates (Coarse & Fine):** Testing for grading, cleanliness, shape, surface texture, strength, durability, and absence of deleterious substances (e.g., organic impurities, silt).
    *   **Water:** Ensuring it's clean and free from impurities that could affect hydration or cause staining.
    *   **Admixtures:** Verifying their type, dosage, and compatibility with cement.
*   **Mix Design:**
    *   Proportioning of cement, aggregates, water, and admixtures to achieve desired properties (strength, workability, durability) economically.
    *   Ensuring the water-cement ratio is controlled to meet strength and durability requirements.
*   **Batching and Mixing:**
    *   Accurate measurement of ingredients by weight (preferred) or volume.
    *   Ensuring thorough and uniform mixing in the concrete mixer for the specified duration.
*   **Transportation:**
    *   Preventing segregation and loss of slump during transit.
    *   Using appropriate equipment (e.g., transit mixers) and keeping the haul time within limits.
*   **Placing and Compaction:**
    *   Placing concrete in lifts to avoid segregation.
    *   Effective compaction (vibration) to remove entrapped air and ensure density.
*   **Curing:**
    *   Maintaining adequate moisture and temperature in the concrete for a specified period to promote hydration and strength development.
    *   Methods include water curing (ponding, spraying, wet coverings) and membrane curing.
*   **Testing of Fresh Concrete:**
    *   **Slump Test:** Measures workability.
    *   **Air Content Test:** Measures the amount of entrained or entrapped air.
    *   **Unit Weight Test:** Determines the density of fresh concrete.
*   **Testing of Hardened Concrete:**
    *   **Compressive Strength Test:** Most common test, performed on concrete cylinders or cubes at specified ages (e.g., 7, 28 days).
    *   **Flexural Strength Test:** Measures the tensile strength in bending.
    *   **Abrasion Resistance Test:** Evaluates resistance to surface wear.
    *   **Permeability Tests:** Assesses the ease with which fluids can penetrate the concrete.

### 2. Strength of Concrete

The strength of concrete is its ability to resist applied forces without fracturing. The most critical strength property for structural design is compressive strength.

#### 2.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Compressive Strength:** The maximum compressive stress a concrete specimen can withstand before failure. It is primarily influenced by the water-cement ratio, cement type, aggregate quality, and curing.
*   **Tensile Strength:** Concrete has very low tensile strength, which is typically about 8-15% of its compressive strength. This is why reinforcement is used in concrete structures to resist tensile stresses.
*   **Flexural Strength (Modulus of Rupture):** The tensile stress in the extreme fiber of a concrete beam or prism subjected to bending.
*   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c ratio):** The ratio of the weight of water to the weight of cement in a concrete mix. A lower w/c ratio generally leads to higher strength and durability.

#### 2.2. Factors Affecting Strength

*   **Water-Cement Ratio:** The most significant factor. Lower w/c ratio leads to higher strength.
*   **Cement Content:** Higher cement content generally increases strength, but only if the w/c ratio is maintained.
*   **Aggregate Properties:**
    *   **Strength and Durability:** Stronger and more durable aggregates contribute to higher concrete strength.
    *   **Shape and Texture:** Angular and rough-textured aggregates provide better bond with cement paste, leading to higher strength.
    *   **Grading:** Well-graded aggregates minimize voids and improve packing density.
*   **Admixtures:** Water-reducing admixtures allow for lower w/c ratios while maintaining workability, thus increasing strength.
*   **Curing:** Proper curing is essential for strength development. Inadequate curing results in significantly lower strength.
*   **Compaction:** Proper compaction removes entrapped air, increasing density and strength.
*   **Age of Concrete:** Strength increases with age as hydration continues.
*   **Temperature:** Higher curing temperatures generally accelerate early strength gain but can reduce ultimate strength if not controlled.

#### 2.3. Example

*   A concrete mix with a w/c ratio of 0.45 will typically achieve higher 28-day compressive strength (e.g., 40-50 MPa) compared to a mix with a w/c ratio of 0.60 (e.g., 25-30 MPa), assuming all other factors are equal.

#### 2.4. Important Points to Remember

*   The w/c ratio is the most critical parameter for controlling concrete strength.
*   Concrete is significantly weaker in tension than in compression.
*   Adequate curing is paramount for achieving the designed strength.

### 3. Durability of Concrete

Durability refers to the concrete's ability to resist environmental influences that can cause deterioration over its service life. It is as important as strength, if not more so, for the long-term performance of structures.

#### 3.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Durability:** The capacity of concrete to maintain its intended performance and appearance over its expected service life.
*   **Permeability:** The measure of a material's ability to allow fluids (water, gases) to pass through it. Lower permeability is crucial for durability.
*   **Porosity:** The volume of void space within the concrete matrix. Higher porosity generally leads to higher permeability.
*   **Chloride Attack:** Ingress of chloride ions (from de-icing salts or marine environments) can lead to reinforcement corrosion.
*   **Sulfate Attack:** Reaction of sulfates (from soil or groundwater) with cement hydration products, causing expansion and cracking.
*   **Carbonation:** Reaction of atmospheric carbon dioxide with calcium hydroxide in the cement paste, forming calcium carbonate and reducing alkalinity.
*   **Freeze-Thaw Resistance:** Ability to withstand damage caused by the freezing and thawing of moisture within the concrete.

#### 3.2. Factors Affecting Durability

*   **Water-Cement Ratio (w/c ratio):** A low w/c ratio is essential for durability as it results in a denser, less permeable concrete matrix.
*   **Cement Type and Content:**
    *   Sulfate-resisting cements (e.g., Type II, Type V) are used in environments with sulfate exposure.
    *   Higher cement content generally improves durability, provided the w/c ratio is controlled.
    *   Use of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) like fly ash and slag can significantly improve durability by reducing permeability and pozzolanic reactions.
*   **Aggregate Quality:** Durable aggregates are essential to prevent surface degradation and internal cracking.
*   **Cover Thickness:** Adequate concrete cover over the reinforcing steel is critical to protect it from corrosive agents.
*   **Curing:** Proper curing ensures a dense, hydrated cement paste, reducing permeability.
*   **Crack Width:** Wide cracks allow easier ingress of aggressive substances.
*   **Environmental Exposure Conditions:**
    *   **Aggressive Chemicals:** Acids, sulfates, chlorides.
    *   **Physical Attack:** Abrasion, erosion, freeze-thaw cycles.
    *   **Marine Environments:** Chlorides and sulfates.
    *   **De-icing Salts:** Chlorides.

#### 3.3. Example

*   Concrete exposed to de-icing salts on a bridge deck requires a low w/c ratio (e.g., 0.40) and sufficient cover (e.g., 50 mm) to prevent chloride-induced corrosion of reinforcement. A higher w/c ratio and thinner cover would significantly reduce its service life.

#### 3.4. Important Points to Remember

*   Low permeability is the cornerstone of concrete durability.
*   Durability is a design consideration, not just a consequence of strength.
*   The service life of a structure is heavily dependent on its durability.

### 4. Thermal Properties of Concrete

Concrete exhibits various thermal properties that are important for its performance, especially under fluctuating temperatures or in fire conditions.

#### 4.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Specific Heat:** The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a unit mass of concrete by one degree.
*   **Thermal Conductivity:** The rate at which heat can be conducted through concrete. It depends on the thermal conductivity of the constituent materials (cement paste, aggregates) and the moisture content.
*   **Coefficient of Thermal Expansion:** The fractional change in volume per degree change in temperature. Concrete expands when heated and contracts when cooled.
*   **Thermal Diffusivity:** A measure of how quickly temperature diffuses through a material. It is related to thermal conductivity, density, and specific heat.

#### 4.2. Factors Affecting Thermal Properties

*   **Aggregate Type:** Different aggregates have different thermal properties, which influence the overall properties of the concrete. For example, lightweight aggregates generally have lower thermal conductivity than normal-weight aggregates.
*   **Moisture Content:** Higher moisture content generally increases specific heat and thermal conductivity.
*   **Cement Paste Composition:** The hydration products and density of the cement paste affect thermal properties.
*   **Porosity:** Higher porosity generally leads to lower thermal conductivity.

#### 4.3. Applications and Significance

*   **Thermal Insulation:** Low thermal conductivity is desirable for insulating buildings. Lightweight concrete often exhibits better thermal insulation properties.
*   **Thermal Stresses:** Differential thermal expansion and contraction can induce stresses in concrete structures, especially in large or massive elements, or where there are temperature gradients. These stresses can lead to cracking if they exceed the tensile strength of the concrete.
*   **Fire Resistance:** The thermal properties of concrete are crucial for its performance during a fire. They determine how quickly heat penetrates the concrete, affecting the temperature of the reinforcement and the potential for spalling (surface layer breakdown).

#### 4.4. Example

*   A massive concrete dam will experience temperature changes due to ambient conditions and the heat of hydration of cement. Understanding its thermal expansion is crucial for designing expansion joints to accommodate these movements and prevent cracking.

#### 4.5. Important Points to Remember

*   Concrete expands and contracts with temperature changes.
*   These movements can induce stresses if unrestrained.
*   Thermal properties are important for insulation and fire resistance.

### 5. Effects Due to Climate, Temperature, and Sustained Elevated Temperature

Climate and temperature have profound effects on the behavior and durability of concrete structures. Sustained elevated temperatures, such as those experienced in fire or industrial processes, can lead to significant degradation.

#### 5.1. Effects of Climate

*   **Freeze-Thaw Cycles:**
    *   Water in the pores of concrete freezes, expands, and can cause internal pressure, leading to cracking and surface scaling.
    *   **Mitigation:** Air entrainment (introducing microscopic air bubbles) provides relief for the expanding ice, significantly improving freeze-thaw resistance.
*   **Wet-Dry Cycles:**
    *   Repeated wetting and drying can cause shrinkage and expansion, leading to micro-cracking and increasing permeability.
    *   **Mitigation:** Use of low w/c ratio concrete and proper curing.
*   **Rainfall and Humidity:**
    *   High humidity and moisture ingress can facilitate the movement of aggressive ions (chlorides, sulfates) into the concrete.
    *   **Mitigation:** Dense concrete with adequate cover and protective coatings.
*   **Wind and Abrasion:**
    *   Wind-borne particles can cause surface abrasion, especially in arid or industrial environments.
    *   **Mitigation:** Use of hard aggregates and abrasion-resistant concrete mixes.

#### 5.2. Effects of Temperature (General)

*   **Thermal Stresses:** As discussed earlier, temperature fluctuations induce stresses due to differential expansion/contraction.
*   **Drying Shrinkage:** As concrete dries, water evaporates from the pores, causing volume reduction and shrinkage. This can lead to cracking if the shrinkage stresses exceed tensile capacity.
*   **Moisture Movement:** Concrete can absorb moisture from a humid environment and release it in a dry environment, causing dimensional changes.

#### 5.3. Effects of Sustained Elevated Temperature (e.g., Fire)

Sustained elevated temperatures, especially those experienced in fires, can cause significant damage to concrete, leading to a loss of strength and stiffness.

*   **Physical and Chemical Changes:**
    *   **Dehydration:** Water is driven out of the cement paste, causing shrinkage and cracking.
    *   **Decomposition of Cement Hydration Products:** At higher temperatures (above ~300°C), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) decomposes, releasing water and weakening the matrix. At even higher temperatures (~600-900°C), calcium silicate hydrates (C-S-H) also start to decompose.
    *   **Aggregate Expansion:** Different aggregates have different thermal expansion characteristics. Some aggregates (like quartz) undergo phase changes at elevated temperatures, causing significant expansion and cracking. This can be more detrimental than the damage to the cement paste itself.
    *   **Cracking:** Due to differential thermal expansion of aggregates and cement paste, and the dehydration process.
    *   **Spalling:** Rapid heating can cause explosive spalling of the surface layers due to the build-up of pore pressure from released water vapor.
*   **Loss of Strength and Stiffness:**
    *   As temperatures increase, the compressive and tensile strengths of concrete decrease significantly.
    *   The modulus of elasticity also reduces, meaning the concrete becomes less stiff.
*   **Color Changes:** Concrete can change color at elevated temperatures (e.g., pinkish or greyish), which can be an indicator of heat exposure.

#### 5.4. Example

*   A concrete bridge deck exposed to frequent de-icing salt application and freeze-thaw cycles will deteriorate much faster if it has a high w/c ratio and inadequate cover, exhibiting surface scaling and reinforcement corrosion.
*   A building structure that has experienced a fire will likely have reduced load-carrying capacity due to the thermal damage, requiring careful assessment for repair or demolition.

#### 5.5. Important Points to Remember

*   Freeze-thaw damage is a major concern in cold climates; air entrainment is key.
*   Sustained elevated temperatures cause significant chemical and physical changes in concrete, leading to strength loss.
*   The type of aggregate can greatly influence fire resistance.

### 6. Corrosion of Reinforcement and Effects of Cover Thickness

Corrosion of steel reinforcement is one of the most common and destructive deterioration mechanisms in reinforced concrete structures, particularly in aggressive environments.

#### 6.1. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Corrosion:** An electrochemical process where steel rusts (oxidizes) in the presence of oxygen and an electrolyte (moisture and ions).
*   **Reinforced Concrete:** Concrete embedded with steel bars (rebar) to carry tensile stresses.
*   **Passive Layer (Passivation):** A thin, protective, passive film of oxides is formed on the surface of steel reinforcement in the highly alkaline environment of concrete (pH ~12.5-13.5). This layer prevents or significantly slows down corrosion.
*   **Depassivation:** The breakdown of the passive layer, allowing corrosion to initiate. This typically occurs due to:
    *   **Carbonation:** CO₂ from the atmosphere penetrates concrete and reacts with calcium hydroxide, reducing the alkalinity (lowering pH) to below about 9.
    *   **Chloride Attack:** Chloride ions penetrate concrete and can break down the passive layer directly, even in the presence of high alkalinity.
*   **Corrosion Products:** Rust (iron oxides and hydroxides) formed during corrosion occupies a larger volume than the original steel. This expansion creates internal tensile stresses in the concrete cover.

#### 6.2. Mechanism of Corrosion

1.  **Depassivation:** The passive layer on the steel breaks down due to carbonation or chloride ingress.
2.  **Electrolyte Present:** Moisture and dissolved salts (chlorides or sulfates) are present in the pores, creating an electrolyte.
3.  **Oxygen Present:** Oxygen from the air is available at the steel surface.
4.  **Electrochemical Cell:** An electrochemical cell is formed with anodic (oxidation) and cathodic (reduction) areas on the steel surface.
    *   **Anode:** Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
    *   **Cathode:** O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
5.  **Rust Formation:** Fe²⁺ ions react with OH⁻ ions and oxygen to form rust.
    *   Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂
    *   4Fe(OH)₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃ (which dehydrates to various iron oxides)

#### 6.3. Effects of Reinforcement Corrosion

*   **Cracking of Concrete Cover:** The expansive forces generated by rust cause tensile stresses in the concrete cover, leading to radial cracking starting from the rebar.
*   **Spalling of Concrete Cover:** As corrosion progresses and the cracking widens, pieces of concrete cover can break off and fall, exposing the reinforcement.
*   **Loss of Reinforcement Cross-Section:** The steel bar is consumed by corrosion, reducing its load-carrying capacity.
*   **Loss of Bond:** The rust layer can reduce the bond between the steel and the concrete, compromising composite action.
*   **Reduced Durability and Service Life:** Ultimately, corrosion leads to structural distress and a reduced service life.

#### 6.4. Effects of Cover Thickness

Concrete cover thickness is a critical design parameter for protecting reinforcement from corrosion.

*   **Adequate Cover:**
    *   **Barrier to Aggressive Agents:** A sufficient thickness of dense, impermeable concrete acts as a barrier to the ingress of CO₂, chlorides, and moisture.
    *   **Protection of Passive Layer:** It maintains the alkaline environment necessary for the passive layer to remain intact.
    *   **Buffering Capacity:** The concrete itself provides a buffering capacity to neutralize acids or resist pH reduction.
*   **Insufficient Cover:**
    *   **Faster Ingress:** Aggressive agents reach the reinforcement more quickly.
    *   **Rapid Depassivation:** The passive layer is destroyed sooner.
    *   **Accelerated Corrosion:** Corrosion initiates and progresses at a much faster rate.

#### 6.5. Cover Requirements

Minimum concrete cover requirements are typically specified in building codes and depend on:

*   **Exposure Conditions:** More severe exposure requires thicker cover.
*   **Type of Element:** Beams, columns, slabs, foundations, etc., may have different requirements.
*   **Fire Resistance:** Higher fire resistance ratings often demand increased cover.

**Example Cover Thicknesses (Illustrative, actual values vary by code):**

*   Mild exposure (interior, protected): 20-25 mm
*   Moderate exposure (exterior, subject to rain): 30-40 mm
*   Severe exposure (marine, de-icing salts): 50-75 mm

#### 6.6. Example

*   A concrete column in a coastal area with a cover thickness of 25 mm will likely experience chloride-induced corrosion of reinforcement much sooner than a similar column with a cover thickness of 50 mm. The initial cracking and spalling will appear much earlier in the 25 mm cover case.

#### 6.7. Important Points to Remember

*   Corrosion of reinforcement is a primary cause of premature deterioration in concrete structures.
*   The passive layer protecting the steel is crucial and can be compromised by carbonation and chlorides.
*   Adequate concrete cover thickness is the first line of defense against corrosion.
*   A lower w/c ratio and the use of SCMs enhance the protective capacity of the concrete cover.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Explain the role of the water-cement ratio in determining both the strength and durability of concrete.

**Answer 1:**
The water-cement ratio (w/c) is a critical parameter.
*   **Strength:** A lower w/c ratio leads to a more efficient hydration reaction, resulting in a denser cement paste with fewer voids and thus higher compressive strength.
*   **Durability:** A lower w/c ratio produces concrete with lower permeability and porosity. This reduced permeability makes it more difficult for aggressive substances like chlorides, sulfates, and carbon dioxide to penetrate the concrete, thereby enhancing its durability and protecting the reinforcement from corrosion.

**Question 2:**
What are the main mechanisms that cause depassivation of steel reinforcement in concrete?

**Answer 2:**
The main mechanisms are:
1.  **Carbonation:** Atmospheric carbon dioxide penetrates the concrete and reacts with calcium hydroxide, reducing the pH of the pore solution from around 12.5-13.5 to below 9.
2.  **Chloride Attack:** Chloride ions, typically from de-icing salts or marine environments, can penetrate the concrete and directly attack the passive film on the steel, even at high pH levels.

**Question 3:**
Describe the likely consequences of inadequate concrete cover over reinforcement in a marine environment.

**Answer 3:**
Inadequate concrete cover in a marine environment will lead to:
1.  **Faster ingress of chlorides:** The thin cover provides less resistance to chloride ions penetrating the concrete.
2.  **Rapid depassivation of steel:** Chlorides will reach the reinforcement sooner, breaking down the protective passive layer.
3.  **Initiation of corrosion:** With the passive layer gone, and in the presence of moisture and oxygen, the steel will begin to corrode.
4.  **Cracking and spalling of concrete:** The expansive nature of rust will cause tensile stresses in the concrete cover, leading to radial cracking and eventually spalling, exposing the reinforcement further and accelerating the deterioration process.
5.  **Reduction in structural capacity:** Loss of steel cross-section and bond will reduce the load-carrying capacity of the element.

**Question 4:**
How does air entrainment improve the freeze-thaw resistance of concrete?

**Answer 4:**
Air entrainment introduces a system of microscopic, stable air bubbles within the concrete matrix. During freezing, water in the capillary pores of the cement paste expands. The entrained air bubbles act as relief reservoirs for this expanding ice, absorbing the pressure and preventing the buildup of internal stresses that would otherwise cause cracking and scaling.

**Question 5:**
Discuss the impact of sustained elevated temperatures on the strength and integrity of concrete.

**Answer 5:**
Sustained elevated temperatures, such as those experienced in a fire, cause significant degradation:
*   **Dehydration and Decomposition:** Water is driven out of the cement paste, and cement hydration products like calcium hydroxide decompose, weakening the matrix.
*   **Aggregate Expansion:** Certain aggregates can undergo phase changes and expand, inducing internal stresses.
*   **Cracking and Spalling:** Differential thermal expansion between paste and aggregates, coupled with pore pressure, leads to cracking and potential surface spalling.
*   **Loss of Strength and Stiffness:** Both compressive and tensile strengths, as well as the modulus of elasticity, are substantially reduced, diminishing the concrete's load-bearing capacity.

### Summary of Key Learning Points

*   **Quality Assurance (QA)** is vital for ensuring concrete meets its performance requirements.
*   **Strength** of concrete is primarily governed by the **water-cement ratio**, aggregate quality, and proper curing.
*   **Durability** is the ability to resist environmental degradation and is crucial for long service life. Low permeability is key.
*   **Thermal properties** (expansion, conductivity) influence thermal stresses and fire resistance.
*   **Climate and temperature** factors like freeze-thaw cycles and sustained heat require specific mitigation strategies.
*   **Reinforcement corrosion** is a major deterioration mechanism, initiated by depassivation (carbonation/chlorides).
*   **Concrete cover thickness** is a critical protective measure against corrosion. Inadequate cover leads to accelerated deterioration.
