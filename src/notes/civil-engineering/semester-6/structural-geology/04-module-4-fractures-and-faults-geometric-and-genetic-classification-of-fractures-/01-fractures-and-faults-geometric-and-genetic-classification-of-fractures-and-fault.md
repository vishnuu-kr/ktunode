# Structural Geology: Module 4 - Fractures and Faults: Geometric and Genetic Classification

## Introduction

Fractures and faults are fundamental structural features in rocks that result from the application of stress. Understanding their geometry (shape, orientation, and arrangement) and genesis (how they formed) is crucial for interpreting geological history, predicting rock mass behavior, and exploring for natural resources.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

*   **Define** fractures and faults and differentiate between them.
*   **Classify** fractures and faults based on their geometric characteristics (e.g., orientation, movement).
*   **Classify** fractures and faults based on their genetic characteristics (e.g., stress regime, origin).
*   **Recognize** and describe common types of fractures and faults.
*   **Apply** classification schemes to real-world geological examples.
*   **Understand** the relationship between stress, strain, and the formation of fractures and faults.

## 1. Fractures

### 1.1. Definition

A **fracture** is a surface or plane along which a rock has experienced tensile or shear stress, resulting in a loss of cohesion. Fractures can be open or closed and may or may not involve displacement.

### 1.2. Types of Fractures

Fractures are primarily classified by their **opening mechanism** and **origin**.

#### 1.2.1. Jointing

**Joints** are fractures where there has been **no significant parallel displacement** of the rock masses along the fracture plane. They are typically caused by tensile or shear stresses that exceed the tensile strength of the rock.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Tensile Stress:** Pulling apart forces.
    *   **Shear Stress:** Sliding forces parallel to the surface.
    *   **Fracture Surface:** The planar or curviplanar surface of the break.
    *   **Joint Set:** A group of parallel or sub-parallel joints.
    *   **Joint System:** Two or more intersecting joint sets.
    *   **Jointing Intensity:** The density or spacing of joints.

*   **Geometric Classification of Joints:**
    *   **Planar Joints:** Flat fracture surfaces.
    *   **Curved Joints:** Concave or convex fracture surfaces (e.g., conchoidal fractures).
    *   **Cross-Joints:** Joints that cut across bedding planes or other pre-existing structures.
    *   **Bedding-Parallel Joints:** Joints that are parallel to the bedding planes in sedimentary rocks.

*   **Genetic Classification of Joints (Origin):**
    *   **Tectonic Joints:** Formed by regional or local tectonic stresses.
        *   **Extension Joints (or Opening Joints):** Formed by tensile stress perpendicular to the fracture surface. The opening is typically parallel to the direction of maximum tensile stress ($\sigma_3$). These often form in sets perpendicular to the maximum horizontal stress ($\sigma_1$) or parallel to the minimum horizontal stress ($\sigma_2$).
        *   **Shear Joints:** Formed by shear stress, often occurring in conjugate sets at approximately 30 degrees to the maximum principal stress ($\sigma_1$).
    *   **Cooling Joints:** Formed during the cooling and contraction of igneous rocks.
        *   **Columnar Jointing:** Forms as polygonal columns (often hexagonal) due to contraction during cooling. Example: Giant's Causeway, Northern Ireland.
        *   **Concentric/Curvilinear Jointing:** Forms in massive igneous bodies (e.g., granite intrusions) due to differential cooling and contraction, often forming curved sheets.
    *   **Exfoliation/Sheeting Joints:** Formed by the unloading of overburden pressure (release of confining pressure) in granitic and other massive rocks. These are typically curved and parallel to the land surface, creating "onion-skin" layers that peel off.
    *   **Gravitational Joints:** Formed by stresses associated with gravity, such as those around a free face or in rock slopes.
    *   **Hydraulic Fractures:** Formed by high pore-fluid pressure exceeding the tensile strength of the rock, often occurring naturally during hydrothermal activity or artificially in hydraulic fracturing.

*   **Examples:**
    *   **Columnar joints in basaltic lava flows:** Regularly spaced polygonal columns.
    *   **Exfoliation domes in granite:** Curved sheets of rock peeling off from a massive outcrop.
    *   **Joint sets in sandstone:** Often oriented perpendicular or parallel to bedding and related to regional tectonic forces.

#### 1.2.2. Faults (Fracture with Displacement)

**Faults** are fractures along which there has been **significant displacement** of the rock masses, typically measured in meters to kilometers. The displacement is generally parallel to the fault plane.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Displacement (Slip):** The net movement of one block relative to another across the fault plane.
    *   **Hanging Wall:** The block of rock above an inclined fault plane.
    *   **Footwall:** The block of rock below an inclined fault plane.
    *   **Fault Plane:** The surface along which the slip occurs.
    *   **Fault Zone:** A broader region of crushed and fractured rock associated with a fault, often containing multiple parallel or sub-parallel fault surfaces.
    *   **Strike:** The compass direction of the line formed by the intersection of a fault plane and a horizontal plane.
    *   **Dip:** The angle between the fault plane and the horizontal plane, measured perpendicular to the strike.
    *   **Dip-Slip:** Movement parallel to the dip of the fault plane.
    *   **Strike-Slip:** Movement parallel to the strike of the fault plane.
    *   **Oblique-Slip:** Movement with components of both dip-slip and strike-slip.

*   **Geometric Classification of Faults (Based on Movement):**

    This classification is based on the relative movement of the hanging wall with respect to the footwall for inclined faults, or the sense of strike-slip for vertical faults.

    *   **Dip-Slip Faults:** Characterized by predominantly vertical movement.
        *   **Normal Faults:** The hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall. This results from **tensional stress** (stretching).
            *   **Geological Significance:** Accommodate extension and thinning of the crust.
            *   **Sub-types:**
                *   **Gravity Faults:** Normal faults that form under the influence of gravity, often associated with rifting.
                *   **Listric Faults:** Normal faults where the fault plane flattens with depth.
        *   **Reverse Faults:** The hanging wall moves up relative to the footwall. This results from **compressional stress** (squeezing).
            *   **Geological Significance:** Accommodate shortening and thickening of the crust.
            *   **Sub-types:**
                *   **Thrust Faults:** Reverse faults with a dip of less than 45 degrees (often much less). They can transport large blocks of rock over others, creating significant crustal shortening.
                    *   **Example:** The Alps, Himalayas, the Canadian Rockies.

    *   **Strike-Slip Faults:** Characterized by predominantly horizontal movement parallel to the strike of the fault plane. These are typically near-vertical.
        *   **Geological Significance:** Accommodate horizontal shear and transform plate boundaries.
        *   **Sub-types (Based on sense of slip):**
            *   **Right-Lateral Strike-Slip Fault:** As you look across the fault, the block on the opposite side moves to your right.
                *   **Example:** San Andreas Fault, California.
            *   **Left-Lateral Strike-Slip Fault:** As you look across the fault, the block on the opposite side moves to your left.
                *   **Example:** Great Glen Fault, Scotland.

    *   **Oblique-Slip Faults:** Exhibit significant components of both dip-slip and strike-slip movement.
        *   **Geometric Description:** Described by their dip-slip component (normal or reverse) and their strike-slip component (right-lateral or left-lateral).

*   **Genetic Classification of Faults (Based on Stress Regime and Origin):**

    This classification links fault types to the prevailing stress conditions under which they formed.

    *   **Extensional Faults:** Formed under tensional stress.
        *   **Normal Faults:** Result from extension perpendicular to the fault strike.
    *   **Compressional Faults:** Formed under compressional stress.
        *   **Reverse Faults and Thrust Faults:** Result from compression parallel to the fault strike.
    *   **Shear Faults:** Formed under shear stress.
        *   **Strike-Slip Faults:** Result from pure shear or wrenching.
        *   **Oblique-Slip Faults:** Result from combinations of shear and other stress components.
    *   **Sliding/Gliding Faults:** Often associated with large-scale crustal movements, particularly thrust faults that allow large crustal blocks to slide.
    *   **Detachment Faults:** Large-scale, low-angle normal faults that mark a boundary between upper and lower crustal blocks, often formed during rifting.

*   **Other Important Fault Features and Concepts:**
    *   **Fault Scarp:** A steep slope or cliff formed by the surface rupture of a fault.
    *   **Drag Folds:** Folds formed in the rock adjacent to a fault due to friction during faulting.
    *   **Fault Breccia:** Angular, fractured rock fragments cemented together within a fault zone.
    *   **Fault Gouge:** Finely ground rock powder (clay-like) within a fault zone, acting as a lubricant.
    *   **Shear Zones:** Broader zones of distributed shear deformation, which may or may not be bounded by distinct fault planes.

## 2. Relationship Between Fractures, Faults, Stress, and Strain

*   **Stress:** Force per unit area applied to a rock.
    *   **Normal Stress ($\sigma$):** Perpendicular to a surface.
        *   **Tensile Stress:** Negative normal stress (pulling apart).
        *   **Compressive Stress:** Positive normal stress (pushing together).
    *   **Shear Stress ($\tau$):** Parallel to a surface.
*   **Strain:** The deformation resulting from stress.
*   **Failure:** When the stress exceeds the rock's strength, it breaks or deforms plastically.
*   **Fracture Formation:**
    *   **Joints:** Typically form when tensile stress exceeds the tensile strength of the rock. Shear joints form when shear stress exceeds the shear strength, but with minimal displacement.
    *   **Faults:** Form when shear stress exceeds the shear strength of the rock, allowing for significant displacement.
*   **Stress State and Fault Type:**
    *   **Tensional Regime ($\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 > \sigma_3$ or $\sigma_3$ is tensile):** Favors **normal faulting**.
    *   **Compressional Regime ($\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 > \sigma_3$):** Favors **reverse and thrust faulting**.
    *   **Shear Regime ($\tau$ is significant):** Favors **strike-slip faulting**.
*   **Pore Fluid Pressure:** High pore fluid pressure can reduce the effective stress acting between rock grains, making the rock weaker and more prone to fracturing and faulting. This is crucial in understanding phenomena like hydraulic fracturing and the role of fluids in earthquake generation.

## 3. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Differentiate between a joint and a fault.

**Question 2:**
Imagine you are examining a rock outcrop. You observe a set of planar fractures that divide the rock into blocks, but the surfaces of the fractures are smooth and show no signs of movement or grinding between the blocks. What type of fracture are you likely observing?

**Question 3:**
A geological map shows a series of parallel linear features cutting across sedimentary layers. Field observations reveal that one block of rock has moved vertically upwards relative to the adjacent block, and the fault plane dips at 30 degrees.
    a) Is this a normal fault or a reverse fault? Explain your reasoning.
    b) Which side is the hanging wall and which is the footwall?
    c) What type of stress regime likely caused this fault?

**Question 4:**
You are hiking in an area known for seismic activity. You notice a prominent, steep fault scarp. Field observations indicate that blocks of rock have moved horizontally past each other. If, as you face the fault, the block on the other side has moved to your right, what type of strike-slip fault is this?

**Question 5:**
Columnar jointing is a common feature in certain igneous rocks. What is the primary mechanism responsible for the formation of columnar joints?

**Question 6:**
List the main types of faults based on their geometric classification and the stress regime typically associated with each.

**Question 7:**
Explain the concept of "unloading" in the context of exfoliation joints.

## Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
A **joint** is a fracture with no significant parallel displacement of the rock masses along the fracture plane. A **fault** is a fracture where there has been significant displacement of the rock masses parallel to the fault plane.

**Answer 2:**
You are likely observing **joints**. The lack of movement or grinding between the rock blocks along the fracture surfaces is a key characteristic of joints.

**Answer 3:**
    a) This is a **reverse fault**. In a reverse fault, the hanging wall moves up relative to the footwall. Since the fault plane dips at 30 degrees (which is less than 45 degrees), if the hanging wall has moved up, it signifies compression and shortening.
    b) The block of rock **above** the 30-degree dipping fault plane is the hanging wall. The block of rock **below** the fault plane is the footwall.
    c) A **compressional stress regime** likely caused this fault. This type of stress leads to shortening and thickening of the crust, resulting in reverse and thrust faulting.

**Answer 4:**
This is a **right-lateral strike-slip fault**. If the opposite block moves to your right as you face the fault, it is classified as a right-lateral strike-slip fault.

**Answer 5:**
Columnar jointing is primarily caused by **contraction** (due to cooling) in igneous rocks, particularly basaltic lava flows. As the lava cools, it shrinks, and stresses develop that lead to the formation of polygonal (often hexagonal) fractures that propagate downwards, forming columns.

**Answer 6:**
*   **Normal Faults:** Associated with **tensional stress** (extension).
*   **Reverse Faults:** Associated with **compressional stress** (shortening).
*   **Thrust Faults:** Associated with **compressional stress** (significant shortening, low angle of dip).
*   **Strike-Slip Faults:** Associated with **shear stress** (horizontal movement).
*   **Oblique-Slip Faults:** Associated with **combined stress regimes** (both horizontal and vertical movement).

**Answer 7:**
Unloading, in the context of exfoliation joints, refers to the **reduction of confining pressure** due to the removal of overlying rock (overburden) through erosion. As the overburden is stripped away, the underlying rock experiences a release of compressive stress. This release of stress can cause the rock, particularly massive igneous rocks like granite, to expand and fracture parallel to the surface, forming curved sheets that peel off like an onion.

## 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **Fracture vs. Fault:** Displacement is the key differentiator.
*   **Joints:** Fractures with no significant displacement, primarily formed by tension or shear (without significant slip).
*   **Faults:** Fractures with significant displacement.
*   **Hanging Wall & Footwall:** Essential for describing dip-slip faults.
*   **Geometric Classification:** Primarily based on the direction and magnitude of displacement (normal, reverse, strike-slip, oblique-slip).
*   **Genetic Classification:** Links fault types to the underlying stress regimes (tensional, compressional, shear).
*   **Stress State Dictates Fault Type:** Tension $\rightarrow$ Normal Faults, Compression $\rightarrow$ Reverse/Thrust Faults, Shear $\rightarrow$ Strike-Slip Faults.
*   **Pore Fluid Pressure:** Can significantly influence rock strength and fracture/fault initiation.
*   **Recognize Common Types:** Columnar joints, exfoliation joints, normal faults, reverse faults, thrust faults, strike-slip faults are fundamental to structural geology.
*   **Application:** Understanding these concepts is vital for interpreting geological history, resource exploration (oil, gas, minerals), and geotechnical engineering.
