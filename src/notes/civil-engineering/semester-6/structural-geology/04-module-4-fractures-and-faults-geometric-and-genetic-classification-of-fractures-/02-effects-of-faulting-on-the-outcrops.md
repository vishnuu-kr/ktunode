# Structural Geology: Module 4 - Fractures and Faults

## Topic: Effects of Faulting on Outcrops

This module delves into the fundamental ways in which faulting deforms the Earth's crust and how these deformations are observable in geological outcrops. We will explore the geometric and genetic classifications of fractures and faults, and then focus specifically on the observable consequences of faulting at the Earth's surface.

---

### Learning Outcomes

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Identify and describe the common surface expressions of faults.**
*   **Explain how different types of faults (strike-slip, dip-slip) produce distinct topographic features.**
*   **Recognize the role of rock type and resistance in influencing the outcrop expression of faults.**
*   **Understand how erosion and deposition interact with faulting to shape the landscape.**
*   **Interpret outcrop features to infer the presence and type of faulting.**

### 1. Introduction to Faulting and Outcrops

**Definition: Fault**
A fault is a planar or curviplanar surface (or zone of surfaces) along which there has been **displacement** of the rocks on either side relative to each other. This displacement can be in any direction and can occur over geological timescales.

**Definition: Outcrop**
An outcrop is any place where bedrock, the solid rock that lies beneath the soil, vegetation, and other surface materials, is exposed at the Earth's surface. Outcrops are crucial for geologists as they provide direct visual access to the subsurface geological structure.

**The Interplay:**
Faulting is a primary process that deforms rock masses. When these deformed rock masses are exposed at the surface as outcrops, they reveal the effects of this deformation. Understanding these effects is key to reconstructing the geological history of an area.

### 2. Common Surface Expressions of Faults

Faults can manifest in various ways on the landscape, often influenced by the type of fault, the nature of the rocks involved, and erosional processes.

#### 2.1. Topographic Features

*   **Fault Scarps:**
    *   **Definition:** A fault scarp is a cliff or steep slope formed by the relative displacement of rocks along a fault. It is the most direct topographic expression of a dip-slip fault (normal or reverse).
    *   **Formation:** When a fault breaks the surface, the differential movement creates a step or drop in the terrain.
    *   **Persistence:** Scarps can be eroded over time, becoming gentler slopes, but fresh scarps are often very distinct.
    *   **Examples:** The scarp of the San Andreas Fault in California, the fault scarps associated with the Basin and Range Province.

*   **Linear Valleys and Troughs:**
    *   **Formation:** Strike-slip faults can create linear valleys where the fault zone is a zone of weakness that is preferentially eroded. Alternatively, compressional forces in restraining bends of strike-slip faults can lead to uplift and mountain building, while extensional forces in releasing bends can form depressions or valleys.
    *   **Examples:** Valleys along the San Andreas Fault, graben (down-dropped blocks) associated with normal faulting.

*   **Offset Streams:**
    *   **Formation:** If a fault cuts across a stream, differential movement can displace the stream channel.
    *   **Observation:** A stream may appear to bend sharply at a fault line, or a segment of the stream channel may be shifted laterally.
    *   **Examples:** Offset stream channels along the Meers Fault in Oklahoma.

*   **Terraces:**
    *   **Formation:** Faulting can cause uplift or subsidence of landmasses, leading to the formation of terraces, particularly in coastal or fluvial environments. For example, uplift along a fault can cause a river to incise deeper, leaving behind abandoned river terraces at a higher elevation.

*   **Faceting of Mountain Fronts:**
    *   **Formation:** In mountainous regions, normal faulting at the edge of a range can lead to a steep, linear mountain front, where the range block has been uplifted against the down-dropped basin block. The fault scarp often creates a distinctive straight or stepped appearance to the mountain base.

#### 2.2. Lithological and Stratigraphic Features

*   **Juxtaposition of Different Rock Types:**
    *   **Formation:** When faults displace rock layers, older or younger, harder or softer rocks can be brought side-by-side.
    *   **Observation:** On an outcrop, you might see a hard, resistant rock unit (like sandstone or granite) next to a softer, less resistant rock unit (like shale or unconsolidated sediment) along a fault line.
    *   **Implication:** This juxtaposition can lead to differential erosion, where the softer rock erodes more quickly, creating a depression or valley at the fault trace.

*   **Fault Zones and Breccias:**
    *   **Definition: Fault Zone:** A region of fractured and crushed rock along a fault.
    *   **Definition: Fault Breccia:** Rock fragments that have been shattered and cemented together along a fault plane.
    *   **Definition: Gouge:** Very finely ground rock material, often clay-like, formed by the grinding action of fault movement.
    *   **Observation:** Outcrops may reveal zones of intensely fractured, pulverized, or brecciated rock, indicating significant movement along the fault. These zones are often less resistant to erosion.

*   **Drag Folds:**
    *   **Formation:** As one block moves past another along a fault, the rocks immediately adjacent to the fault can be bent or folded due to friction.
    *   **Observation:** You might see folded rock layers that are truncated at the fault surface, with the folding "dragging" the layers in the direction of fault movement.

*   **Slickensides and Striae:**
    *   **Definition: Slickensides:** Polished or grooved surfaces on rock faces along a fault plane, created by friction during fault movement.
    *   **Definition: Striae (orslickensides):** Linear grooves or ridges on a fault surface that indicate the direction of slip.
    *   **Observation:** These are definitive indicators of faulting. The direction of the striae can help determine the sense of slip (e.g., dip-slip or strike-slip).

*   **Truncated Strata:**
    *   **Formation:** When a fault cuts through a sequence of rock layers, the upper parts of the layers are removed by the fault displacement.
    *   **Observation:** On an outcrop, rock layers will abruptly terminate at the fault plane, rather than continuing across it.

### 3. Influence of Rock Type and Resistance

The way a fault manifests on an outcrop is heavily influenced by the properties of the rocks involved.

*   **Hard, Resistant Rocks (e.g., Sandstone, Limestone, Granite):**
    *   Tend to form steeper scarps.
    *   Can preserve fault features like slickensides and breccias for longer periods.
    *   May exhibit more brittle fracturing.

*   **Soft, Less Resistant Rocks (e.g., Shale, Claystone, Unconsolidated Sediments):**
    *   Tend to erode more rapidly, smoothing out topographic features like scarps.
    *   Fault zones in these rocks might appear as depressions or subtle linear features rather than distinct cliffs.
    *   Can deform more ductilely, potentially showing more drag folding.

*   **Layered Rocks:**
    *   The relative resistance of different layers will dictate how erosion attacks the fault scarp. A sequence with resistant layers on top of less resistant layers, displaced by a normal fault, will often have benches (formed by resistant layers) and steep slopes (formed by less resistant layers).

---

### 4. Interaction of Erosion and Deposition with Faulting

Faulting doesn't occur in isolation; erosion and deposition constantly act upon the deformed landscape.

*   **Erosion:**
    *   **Scarps:** Erosional processes (rain, wind, ice) will degrade fault scarps, reducing their steepness and eventually burying them under colluvium (slope deposits).
    *   **Fault Zones:** Fault zones, being zones of weakness, are often preferentially eroded, leading to the formation of valleys, saddles, or depressions.
    *   **Offset Features:** Erosion can partially obscure offset streams or other features.

*   **Deposition:**
    *   **Alluvium:** Streams and rivers flowing across faulted terrain will deposit sediment. This alluvium can bury fault scarps and mask surface expressions.
    *   **Colluvium:** Debris from eroding scarps will accumulate at the base of the scarp.
    *   **Graben:** Depressions formed by normal faulting (grabens) can become sites of sediment accumulation.

---

### 5. Interpreting Outcrop Features to Infer Faulting

By carefully observing the features present in an outcrop, geologists can infer the presence and type of faulting.

*   **Key Indicators:**
    *   Abrupt termination of rock layers.
    *   Juxtaposition of dissimilar rock types.
    *   Presence of fault gouge, breccia, or fault zones.
    *   Polished or grooved surfaces (slickensides).
    *   Linear grooves or ridges on fault surfaces (striae) indicating slip direction.
    *   Drag folds adjacent to a potential fault plane.
    *   Offset geological markers (though these are more common at a larger scale but can be inferred from patterns).

*   **Determining Fault Type:**
    *   **Dip-Slip Faults (Normal/Reverse):** Look for fault scarps, truncated strata indicating vertical displacement, and striae that are predominantly vertical. If the hanging wall has moved down relative to the footwall (normal fault), look for evidence of extension. If the hanging wall has moved up (reverse fault), look for evidence of compression.
    *   **Strike-Slip Faults:** Look for linear valleys or features parallel to the fault, offset geological features (if large enough to be seen in the outcrop pattern), and striae that are predominantly horizontal, indicating lateral movement.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
You are examining an outcrop and observe a distinct cliff-like feature. Below the cliff, you see a zone of shattered rock that has been cemented together. The rock layers on either side of this feature are clearly cut off. What is the most likely geological feature you are observing, and what type of fault might it represent?

**Answer 1:**
You are most likely observing a **fault scarp** with an associated **fault breccia**. This feature, especially the cliff-like appearance, strongly suggests a **dip-slip fault** (either a normal or reverse fault) where significant vertical displacement has occurred.

**Question 2:**
An outcrop shows a sequence of sandstone layers. On one side of a planar feature, the sandstone layers are continuous. On the other side, the layers are abruptly cut off and appear to be shifted laterally relative to the first side. You also notice polished surfaces on the fault plane with faint horizontal scratches. What type of faulting is indicated?

**Answer 2:**
The abrupt termination of layers and apparent lateral shift, combined with polished surfaces and **horizontal scratches (striae)**, strongly indicates **strike-slip faulting**. The horizontal scratches are key indicators of lateral movement.

**Question 3:**
Imagine a fault that brings a resistant layer of quartzite next to a less resistant layer of shale. If this fault is exposed at the surface and subjected to erosion, describe the likely topographic expression and outcrop appearance of the fault trace.

**Answer 3:**
The fault trace would likely appear as a **linear depression or valley**, as the softer shale erodes more easily than the quartzite. On the outcrop, the quartzite block might form a subtle ridge or a steeper slope where it is exposed, while the shale would be eroded back, creating a noticeable topographic contrast along the fault line. You might also see the abrupt termination of the quartzite layers against the eroded shale.

**Question 4:**
What are the two primary types of fault-related topographic features, and what type of fault is primarily responsible for each?

**Answer 4:**
The two primary types of fault-related topographic features are:
1.  **Fault Scarps:** Primarily formed by **dip-slip faults** (normal and reverse faults) due to vertical displacement.
2.  **Linear Valleys/Troughs:** Can be formed by both **strike-slip faults** (due to erosion along the fault zone or associated deformation) and **normal faults** (forming grabens).

---

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Outcrops are geological snapshots:** They reveal the effects of subsurface processes like faulting.
*   **Fault scarps are direct evidence of vertical movement:** Their presence strongly suggests dip-slip faulting.
*   **Strike-slip faults often create linear features:** These can be valleys, offset landforms, or alignments of geological structures.
*   **Rock resistance is crucial:** It dictates how fault features are preserved or eroded.
*   **Fault zones (breccias, gouge) are visible evidence of fault activity:** They represent areas of intense deformation.
*   **Slickensides and striae are definitive fault indicators:** They provide direct evidence of movement and its direction.
*   **Erosion and deposition modify fault expressions:** They can obscure or enhance the surface manifestation of faults.
*   **Careful observation and interpretation are key:** Geologists use a combination of topographic, lithological, and structural clues to identify and understand faults from outcrop data.
