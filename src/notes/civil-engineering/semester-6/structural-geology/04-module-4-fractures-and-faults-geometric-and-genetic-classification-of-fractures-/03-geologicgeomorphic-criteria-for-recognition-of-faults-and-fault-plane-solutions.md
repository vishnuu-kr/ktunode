# Structural Geology: Module 4 - Fractures and Faults

## Topic: Geologic/Geomorphic Criteria for Recognition of Faults and Fault Plane Solutions

---

### Learning Outcomes:

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Identify and describe the geological and geomorphic criteria used to recognize faults in the field.

### 1. Geologic and Geomorphic Criteria for Recognition of Faults

Faults are fractures along which there has been significant displacement of the rock masses. Recognizing them in the field often involves observing evidence on both the rock itself (geologic criteria) and the landscape (geomorphic criteria).

#### 1.1 Geologic Criteria (Evidence within the Rocks)

These criteria focus on direct evidence of displacement and deformation within the rock units.

*   **Fault Breccia and Gouge:**
    *   **Definition:** **Fault breccia** is a rock composed of angular fragments of country rock that have been shattered and cemented together along a fault zone. **Fault gouge** is a fine-grained, powdery material, often clay-rich, formed by the pulverization and grinding of rock along a fault.
    *   **Recognition:** These materials are found within the fault zone itself, appearing as distinct bands or lenses within otherwise undisturbed rock layers.
    *   **Example:** Observing a thick, poorly consolidated layer of crushed rock separating two different rock units that should be in continuous contact.

*   **Drag Folds:**
    *   **Definition:** As one block moves past another along a fault, the layers adjacent to the fault can be bent or "dragged" into conformity with the fault plane.
    *   **Recognition:** Layers on one side of a fault are observed to be curved or folded near the fault contact. The curvature is typically in a direction that indicates the sense of movement.
    *   **Example:** If a hanging wall moves up and over a footwall, layers in the footwall near the fault might be tilted upwards towards the fault.

*   **Offset Bedding, Features, or Intrusions:**
    *   **Definition:** The most direct evidence of faulting is the displacement of recognizable geological features. These can include stratigraphic layers (beds), dikes, sills, veins, or even fossils.
    *   **Recognition:** Tracing a specific rock layer or feature across a fault zone and observing that it is no longer at the same elevation or laterally continuous.
    *   **Example:** A distinctive fossil-rich limestone bed is observed on one side of a fault at a certain elevation, but on the other side, it is found significantly higher or lower, or is absent altogether.

*   **Fault Scarps (as erosional remnants):**
    *   **Definition:** While fault scarps are primarily geomorphic features, their presence as steep slopes or cliffs that cut across bedding or other geological structures can be a direct indicator of faulting.
    *   **Recognition:** A linear break in slope or a steep cliff that truncates geological layers or formations.
    *   **Example:** A prominent, straight cliff face cutting through a series of gently dipping sedimentary layers.

*   **Slickenlines and Striations:**
    *   **Definition:** **Slickenlines** are parallel scratches or grooves on the surface of a fault plane, formed by the friction of rocks sliding past each other. **Striations** are similar, but may be more pronounced linear features or even small ridges.
    *   **Recognition:** Observing polished and grooved surfaces on the fault plane, often with a fibrous mineral growth (slickensides) that indicates the direction of movement.
    *   **Example:** A polished surface on a fault plane exhibiting fine, parallel grooves that all point in the same direction.

*   **Fault Zone Slickensides and Facies:**
    *   **Definition:** Beyond individual fault planes, entire fault zones can exhibit characteristic mineral assemblages and textures related to the sliding and deformation.
    *   **Recognition:** Bands of polished and grooved surfaces (slickenfiber) within a brecciated or gouge-filled fault zone.
    *   **Example:** Alternating layers of crushed rock and polished, grooved surfaces within a zone several meters wide.

*   **Hydrothermal Alteration and Mineralization:**
    *   **Definition:** Fault zones can act as conduits for hydrothermal fluids, leading to alteration of the surrounding rocks and deposition of minerals.
    *   **Recognition:** Observing altered rock or mineral veins concentrated along a linear feature that disrupts other geological structures.
    *   **Example:** A linear zone of silicified or pyritized rock cutting across otherwise unaltered sedimentary layers.

#### 1.2 Geomorphic Criteria (Evidence on the Landscape)

These criteria focus on how faulting affects the Earth's surface and its drainage patterns.

*   **Fault Scarps (as depositional or tectonic features):**
    *   **Definition:** A fault scarp is a linear cliff or steep slope formed by the displacement of the Earth's surface along a fault.
    *   **Recognition:** A conspicuous, often straight, break in the landscape that separates different elevations.
    *   **Example:** The scarp of the San Andreas Fault, where one side of the fault is visibly uplifted or downdropped relative to the other.

*   **Offset Drainage Patterns:**
    *   **Definition:** Rivers and streams flowing across active fault zones can be diverted, blocked, or offset due to the ongoing displacement.
    *   **Recognition:**
        *   **Stream Terraces:** Displaced stream terraces indicate vertical fault movement.
        *   **Sag Ponds:** Depressions formed where streams are blocked by fault scarps, creating small lakes.
        *   **Offset Stream Channels:** A stream channel appears to be shifted laterally across a fault.
        *   **Deflected Streams:** Streams that follow fault lines for a distance before crossing them.
    *   **Example:** A river that abruptly changes its course, forming a sharp bend where it encounters a fault scarp.

*   **Linear Valleys and Ridges:**
    *   **Definition:** Fault zones, especially those with softer or more easily eroded rocks, can form linear valleys. Conversely, more resistant rocks along a fault can form linear ridges.
    *   **Recognition:** Straight, elongated valleys or ridges that cut across regional geological trends.
    *   **Example:** A long, straight valley that corresponds to a fault line separating different rock units.

*   **Tectonic Geomorphology:**
    *   **Definition:** The study of landforms created or modified by tectonic processes, including faulting.
    *   **Recognition:** Features such as fault-line scarps (where erosion exposes the fault), triangular facets on mountain fronts, and changes in slope or drainage patterns that can be directly attributed to fault activity.
    *   **Example:** Mountain fronts with a series of parallel, steep triangular slopes (facets) that are interpreted as fault scarps.

*   **Uplifted or Subsidence Features:**
    *   **Definition:** Faulting can cause regional uplift or subsidence, leading to changes in land elevation.
    *   **Recognition:** Evidence of marine terraces at high elevations (indicating uplift) or the presence of swamps or coastal plains where land has subsided.
    *   **Example:** Ancient beach deposits found hundreds of meters above sea level, suggesting significant uplift along fault lines.

### 2. Fault Plane Solutions (Focal Mechanisms)

Fault plane solutions, also known as focal mechanisms, are diagrams that represent the type of faulting that occurred at the source of an earthquake. They are derived from the analysis of seismic waves recorded by seismograph stations around the world.

#### 2.1 Principles of Fault Plane Solutions

*   **Seismic Waves:** Earthquakes generate different types of seismic waves (P-waves and S-waves). The first motion of these waves (compression or dilation) at seismograph stations provides information about the direction of slip on the fault.
*   **First Motion Studies:** Seismologists analyze the initial arrival of P-waves. If a station records a compression (upward movement), it's plotted as a filled circle. If it records a dilation (downward movement), it's plotted as an open circle.
*   **Double Couple Model:** The seismic source of an earthquake is often modeled as a **double couple**, which consists of two pairs of forces acting at right angles to each other. This model effectively represents the slip on a fault.
*   **The Diagram:**
    *   A **Wulff net** (or equal-area projection) is used to plot the seismic data.
    *   Two perpendicular circles represent the **fault plane** and the **auxiliary plane** (or cross-fault).
    *   The fault plane is the actual plane along which the earthquake rupture occurred.
    *   The auxiliary plane is perpendicular to the fault plane and contains the slip vector.
    *   **No-fault solution:** The diagram is divided into four quadrants by the intersection of the fault plane and the auxiliary plane.
    *   **First motions:** Stations that record compression are plotted in one set of diagonally opposite quadrants, and stations that record dilation in the other.

#### 2.2 Interpreting Fault Plane Solutions

The distribution of compressions and dilations on the fault plane solution diagram allows us to determine:

*   **The Orientation of the Fault Plane:** One of the two perpendicular planes represents the actual fault plane.
*   **The Orientation of the Slip Vector (Rake):** The direction of movement along the fault.
*   **The Type of Faulting:**
    *   **Normal Faulting:** Characterized by dominant horizontal extension. The fault plane solution will show a tensional axis (T-axis) oriented horizontally and a compressional axis (P-axis) oriented vertically.
    *   **Thrust Faulting (Reverse Faulting):** Characterized by dominant horizontal compression. The fault plane solution will show a P-axis oriented horizontally and a T-axis oriented vertically.
    *   **Strike-Slip Faulting:** Characterized by dominant horizontal shear. The fault plane solution will show both the P-axis and T-axis oriented horizontally, perpendicular to each other. The fault plane will be steeply dipping.

*   **The Orientation of Principal Stresses:**
    *   **P-axis (Compressional Axis):** Represents the direction of maximum compressive stress ($\sigma_1$).
    *   **T-axis (Tensional Axis):** Represents the direction of maximum extensional stress ($\sigma_3$).
    *   **B-axis (Intermediate Stress Axis):** Perpendicular to both the P-axis and T-axis. Its orientation depends on whether it's $\sigma_2$ or a neutral axis.

#### 2.3 Types of Faulting and Corresponding Stress Regimes

| Fault Type     | Dominant Stress Orientation                     | P-axis Orientation | T-axis Orientation | Relationship between Stress and Fault Plane                                                                                                                                |
| :------------- | :---------------------------------------------- | :----------------- | :----------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Normal**     | $\sigma_1$ vertical, $\sigma_2$ horizontal, $\sigma_3$ horizontal | Vertical           | Horizontal         | Fault plane is steeply dipping (typically < 45 degrees from horizontal). Slip vector is down-dip. $\sigma_1$ is perpendicular to the fault plane, $\sigma_3$ is parallel to slip. |
| **Thrust**     | $\sigma_1$ horizontal, $\sigma_2$ horizontal, $\sigma_3$ vertical | Horizontal         | Vertical           | Fault plane is gently dipping (typically > 45 degrees from horizontal). Slip vector is up-dip. $\sigma_1$ is parallel to slip, $\sigma_3$ is perpendicular to the fault plane. |
| **Reverse**    | $\sigma_1$ horizontal, $\sigma_2$ horizontal, $\sigma_3$ vertical | Horizontal         | Vertical           | Similar to thrust faulting, but generally refers to steeper dip angles.                                                                                                 |
| **Strike-Slip**| $\sigma_1$ horizontal, $\sigma_2$ vertical, $\sigma_3$ horizontal | Horizontal         | Horizontal         | Fault plane is steeply dipping (often near vertical). Slip vector is horizontal and parallel to the strike of the fault. $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$ are in the fault plane.     |

**Important Note:** In strike-slip faulting, the fault plane is one of the two vertical planes. The determination of which is the fault plane and which is the auxiliary plane requires additional information or is conventionally decided.

#### 2.4 Example of Fault Plane Solution Interpretation

Imagine a fault plane solution diagram where:

*   The P-axis is plotted as pointing straight up.
*   The T-axis is plotted as pointing to the east.
*   The fault plane is shown as a steeply dipping plane that strikes north-south and dips to the east.
*   The auxiliary plane is shown as a steeply dipping plane that strikes north-south and dips to the west.

**Interpretation:**

1.  **Fault Plane:** If the fault plane is the one dipping to the east, then compression is vertical ($\sigma_1$) and tension is horizontal to the east ($\sigma_3$). This would imply **normal faulting**, with movement down the dip of the fault.
2.  **Fault Plane:** If the fault plane is the one dipping to the west, then compression is vertical ($\sigma_1$) and tension is horizontal to the east ($\sigma_3$). This scenario doesn't fit the stress orientations directly.

Let's reconsider the stress orientations and their relationship to the fault.

*   **Scenario 1: Normal Faulting**
    *   P-axis (max compression) is vertical ($\sigma_1$).
    *   T-axis (max extension) is horizontal ($\sigma_3$).
    *   If the fault plane is the eastern dipping plane (striking N-S), then the slip vector is down-dip. The P-axis is perpendicular to the fault plane, and the T-axis is parallel to the slip vector. This is consistent with normal faulting.

*   **Scenario 2: Thrust Faulting**
    *   P-axis (max compression) is horizontal ($\sigma_1$).
    *   T-axis (max extension) is vertical ($\sigma_3$).
    *   If the fault plane is a gently dipping plane, the P-axis would be horizontal and the T-axis vertical.

*   **Scenario 3: Strike-Slip Faulting**
    *   P-axis and T-axis are horizontal and perpendicular to each other.
    *   The fault plane is steeply dipping (near vertical).
    *   In this case, both the P and T axes would be in the horizontal plane. The fault plane would be one of the vertical planes, and the slip vector would be parallel to the strike of that plane.

**Crucial Point:** Determining which of the two nodal planes is the *actual* fault plane requires independent geological evidence (e.g., mapped fault orientation) or can sometimes be inferred from the regional tectonic setting.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:** You are mapping a region and observe a linear valley cutting across a series of sedimentary layers. Within this valley, you notice that a specific sandstone bed is displaced vertically by approximately 50 meters, with the western block being lower than the eastern block. What type of faulting is most likely responsible?

**Answer 1:** This scenario suggests **normal faulting**. The linear valley could be a fault-line scarp or a fault-controlled valley, and the vertical displacement with the downthrown western block indicates extension.

**Question 2:** While examining a road cut, you find a section of rock with fine, parallel scratches oriented diagonally across the exposed surface. This surface separates two distinct rock units that appear to have moved relative to each other. What is the term for these scratches, and what do they indicate?

**Answer 2:** The scratches are called **slickenlines** (or striations). They indicate the direction of relative movement (slip) between the two rock blocks along the fault plane.

**Question 3:** A seismic station records the first motion of an earthquake as a compression (upward pulse) on the north side of a fault zone and a dilation (downward pulse) on the south side. If the fault is interpreted as a vertical strike-slip fault striking north-south, how would you interpret this?

**Answer 3:** For a vertical strike-slip fault, the fault plane is vertical. The P-axis (maximum compression) and T-axis (maximum tension) are both horizontal and perpendicular to each other. If the fault is striking north-south, the P-axis would be oriented east-west or west-east, and the T-axis would be oriented in the opposite direction. The observed compressions and dilations on either side of the fault would indicate the relative movement. For instance, if the P-axis is oriented east-west, compression would be felt on the eastern side and dilation on the western side if the fault dips towards the west. *To definitively interpret this, we need to know which of the two possible fault planes is the actual fault.* However, if we assume the fault plane is the one that generates these first motions in this pattern, it implies a certain stress orientation relative to that plane.

**Question 4:** Describe the difference between fault breccia and fault gouge, and explain how each is formed.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Fault breccia:** Formed by the shattering and crushing of rock along a fault zone, resulting in angular fragments of the original rock. It's often cemented together.
*   **Fault gouge:** Formed by the grinding and pulverization of rock along a fault, creating a fine-grained, powdery material. It is typically more pervasive and finer than breccia.
Both are formed by the mechanical deformation (fracturing and grinding) of rock as blocks slide past each other during fault movement.

**Question 5:** In a fault plane solution, what do the P-axis and T-axis represent, and how do their orientations relate to the type of faulting?

**Answer 5:**
*   **P-axis (Compressional Axis):** Represents the direction of maximum compressive stress ($\sigma_1$).
*   **T-axis (Tensional Axis):** Represents the direction of maximum extensional stress ($\sigma_3$).
    *   **Normal Faulting:** P-axis is vertical, T-axis is horizontal.
    *   **Thrust/Reverse Faulting:** P-axis is horizontal, T-axis is vertical.
    *   **Strike-Slip Faulting:** Both P-axis and T-axis are horizontal and perpendicular to each other. The fault plane is typically steeply dipping.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Multiple Criteria:** Faults are rarely recognized by a single criterion. Look for a combination of geological and geomorphic evidence.
*   **Scale Matters:** The criteria observed can vary depending on the scale of observation (hand sample, outcrop, regional landscape).
*   **Erosion's Role:** Geomorphic features like scarps can be modified or eroded over time, making interpretation challenging.
*   **Fault Plane Solutions are Models:** They are based on the double-couple model and represent the *source* of an earthquake, not necessarily the entire fault system.
*   **Ambiguity in Solutions:** There are always two possible fault planes and two possible slip directions in a fault plane solution. Independent geological data is often needed to resolve this ambiguity.
*   **Stress vs. Fault Orientation:** Understand the relationship between the orientation of principal stresses (P and T axes) and the orientation of the fault plane and slip vector for different fault types.
*   **Active Faults:** Geomorphic criteria are particularly useful for identifying active faults that have caused recent surface deformation.
