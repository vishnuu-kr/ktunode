# Structural Geology: Module 2 - Foliation and Lineation

## Topic: Description and Origin of Lineation and Relationship with Major Structures

---

### Learning Outcomes Covered:

*   **Define and describe different types of lineations.**
*   **Explain the processes of lineation formation (origin).**
*   **Discuss the relationship between lineations and major structural features (folds, faults, fabrics).**
*   **Identify and interpret lineations in geological contexts.**

---

### 1. Introduction to Lineation

#### 1.1 Definition of Lineation

*   **Lineation:** A directional geological feature, characterized by the elongation of mineral grains, aggregates of grains, fossils, or structural elements along a specific line within a rock. It represents a directional component of strain.

#### 1.2 Key Characteristics of Lineations

*   **Direction:** The orientation of the lineation, typically described by trend and plunge.
*   **Sense:** In some cases, lineations can have a sense of movement or stretching.
*   **Scale:** Lineations can be microscopic (e.g., aligned mineral grains) or macroscopic (e.g., crenulation hinges).

---

### 2. Description of Different Types of Lineations

Lineations can be broadly classified based on their origin and the geological features they are associated with.

#### 2.1 Mineral Lineation

*   **Description:** Parallel alignment and elongation of individual mineral grains or aggregates of grains. This is often the most common type of lineation observed.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Elongated quartz or mica grains:** In metamorphic rocks, these minerals can recrystallize and align during deformation.
    *   **Elongated amphibole or pyroxene crystals:** Common in igneous and metamorphic rocks.
    *   **Stretched pebbles or clasts:** In sedimentary or volcanic rocks that have undergone deformation.
*   **Visual Clues:** Often appears as a "grainy" texture or shimmering effect in one direction.

#### 2.2 Stretching/Strain Lineation

*   **Description:** Formed by the elongation of any pre-existing feature (grains, fossils, pebbles, pores) due to directional extension during deformation. This is a direct manifestation of finite strain.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Elongated fossils:** Shells or bones stretched and aligned along the maximum extension direction.
    *   **Stretched ooids or clasts in sedimentary rocks:** Indicating significant shortening perpendicular to the lineation and extension parallel to it.
    *   **Stretched vesicles in volcanic rocks:** Indicating flow and deformation.

#### 2.3 Crenulation Lineation

*   **Description:** Formed by the intersection of two sets of cleavages or foliations within a rock, particularly in fine-grained rocks like slates and phyllites undergoing refolding.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Type 1 (cis-dominant):** Formed by the intersection of a penetrative cleavage with a later crenulation cleavage that forms within the domains of the earlier cleavage.
    *   **Type 2 (trans-dominant):** Formed by the intersection of two non-penetrative crenulation cleavages of similar intensity.
*   **Visual Clues:** A series of small, parallel folds or kinks, where the hinges define the lineation.

#### 2.4 Fold Axis Lineation

*   **Description:** The axis of folds, representing a line of no horizontal displacement during folding. This lineation is a geometric consequence of folding.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Axial surface trace of a fold:** Where the axial surface intersects a bedding plane.
    *   **Intersection of axial plane cleavage with bedding:** A common type of lineation observed in folded rocks.
*   **Visual Clues:** Can be observed as the culmination and depression of folds, or the intersection of planar features.

#### 2.5 Boudinage Lineation

*   **Description:** Formed by the stretching and breaking of competent layers within an incompetent matrix, resulting in sausage-like segments (boudins) aligned parallel to the extension direction.
*   **Visual Clues:** Discontinuous, stretched segments of a contrasting rock layer.

#### 2.6 Lithological Lineation

*   **Description:** The intersection of two planar features of different orientations, such as the intersection of a bedding plane with a joint or fault plane.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Intersection of bedding with a fault:** The fault line trace on a bedding surface.
    *   **Intersection of bedding with a joint:** The line where a joint cuts through a layered rock.

#### 2.7 Other Types of Lineations

*   **Scissor Lineation:** Formed by the intersection of two different sets of cleavages.
*   **Striations:** Grooves or lines on fault surfaces, indicating the direction of slip.

### 3. Origin of Lineation

Lineations form through various processes associated with deformation, often involving ductile (plastic) deformation.

#### 3.1 Crystal Plasticity and Recrystallization

*   **Process:** During metamorphism and ductile deformation, individual mineral grains can change their shape (plastic deformation) and/or new, elongated grains can recrystallize parallel to the direction of maximum finite extension.
*   **Key Factors:** Temperature, pressure, presence of fluids, mineralogy.
*   **Example:** Recrystallization of mica flakes or amphibole prisms into long, parallel crystals.

#### 3.2 Mass Transport and Flow

*   **Process:** In ductile shear zones or magmatic flows, rigid markers (e.g., clasts, xenoliths) or even the rock fabric itself can be stretched and aligned parallel to the direction of material transport.
*   **Example:** Elongation of xenoliths within a granite pluton or stretched minerals in a mylonite (a highly deformed rock).

#### 3.3 Folding

*   **Process:** As rocks are folded, linear features like fold axes are created. The intersection of axial planes with bedding also generates lineations.
*   **Example:** The axis of a syncline or anticline.

#### 3.4 Dissolution and Precipitation

*   **Process:** In some cases, dissolution of less resistant material and precipitation of new material along stress concentrations can lead to the formation of linear features.
*   **Example:** Pressure solution features, which can lead to mineral alignment.

#### 3.5 Mechanical Twinning

*   **Process:** Certain minerals can deform by mechanical twinning under stress, which can result in the elongation of crystal segments along specific crystallographic directions.
*   **Example:** Calcite twins in deformed limestones.

### 4. Relationship Between Lineation and Major Structures

Lineations are intimately related to major structural features and provide crucial information about the deformational history of a region.

#### 4.1 Relationship with Folds

*   **Fold Axis:** The axis of a fold is a primary example of a lineation.
*   **Intersection Lineation:** The intersection of the axial plane cleavage with the bedding surface is a common lineation in folded rocks. The orientation of this lineation generally parallels the fold axis.
*   **Symmetry:** The orientation of the lineation can indicate the symmetry of the fold. For example, in cylindrical folds, the lineation is parallel to the fold axis.
*   **Refolding:** Multiple sets of lineations can indicate multiple folding events, with later lineations often refolding earlier ones.

#### 4.2 Relationship with Faults and Shear Zones

*   **Striations:** The prominent lines or grooves on fault surfaces (striations) represent the direction of slip along the fault. These are a type of lineation.
*   **Mylonitic Lineation:** In ductile shear zones (mylonites), mineral grains and rock fabrics are elongated parallel to the direction of shearing and mass transport. This lineation often indicates the direction of bulk movement.
*   **Intersection of Faults/Shear Zones:** The intersection of two fault or shear zone planes can also form a lineation.

#### 4.3 Relationship with Foliation

*   **Intersection Lineation:** The intersection of two foliations (e.g., bedding and cleavage, or two cleavages) forms a lineation. This is a very common type of lineation in multiply deformed rocks.
*   **Syn-Foliation Lineation:** Mineral lineations can form contemporaneously with or during the development of a foliation, often representing the direction of maximum stretching during the development of that foliation.
*   **Perpendicular Relationship:** In some cases, the lineation can be perpendicular to the direction of maximum finite strain or the principal finite extension direction, particularly if there was significant shortening in other directions.

#### 4.4 Strain Ellipsoid and Lineation

*   **Concept:** In a finite strain ellipsoid, the longest axis (X) represents the direction of maximum extension, and the shortest axis (Z) represents the direction of maximum shortening.
*   **Lineation Orientation:** Mineral lineations and stretching lineations generally form parallel to the direction of maximum finite extension (the X axis) of the strain ellipsoid.
*   **Implications:** This allows geologists to infer the bulk strain experienced by the rock.

#### 4.5 Fabrics and Microstructures

*   **Fabric:** Lineations are a component of the overall fabric of a rock, which describes the preferred orientation of its constituent elements.
*   **Microstructures:** Observing the alignment and elongation of microscopic features (e.g., quartz subgrains, mica orientation) can reveal the presence and orientation of lineations.

---

### 5. Identifying and Interpreting Lineations

*   **Field Observations:** Carefully examine rock surfaces for signs of directional elongation of minerals, pebbles, fossils, or structural elements.
*   **Orientation Measurements:** Measure the trend and plunge of identified lineations.
*   **Contextual Analysis:** Consider the surrounding geological structures (folds, faults, foliations) and the rock type to interpret the origin and significance of the lineation.
*   **Petrographic Analysis:** Microscopic examination of thin sections can reveal subtle mineral lineations and the processes of their formation.
*   **Stereographic Projection:** Use stereonets to analyze the orientation of lineations and their relationship with other structural elements (planes, fold axes).

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Lineations are directional features:** They indicate a preferred orientation within a rock.
*   **Lineations are often related to strain:** They represent a component of the overall deformation.
*   **Multiple types of lineations can coexist:** A single rock can exhibit several different lineations, reflecting a complex deformational history.
*   **Intersection lineations are crucial:** They are formed by the intersection of planar features and are often parallel to fold axes or stretching directions.
*   **Lineations provide kinematic information:** They help us understand the direction and sense of movement during deformation.
*   **Interpreting lineations requires careful observation and analysis of context.**

### 8. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
*   **Definition of Lineation:** A lineation is a directional geological feature characterized by the elongation of mineral grains, aggregates of grains, fossils, or structural elements along a specific line within a rock, representing a directional component of strain.
*   **Three Types of Lineations in a Metamorphic Rock:**
    1.  **Mineral Lineation:** This involves the parallel alignment and elongation of individual mineral grains, such as mica flakes or amphibole prisms. Its origin is typically **recrystallization** and **crystal plasticity** under directed stress and elevated temperatures, where new, elongated grains grow parallel to the direction of maximum extension.
    2.  **Stretching Lineation:** This is formed by the elongation of any pre-existing feature, like clasts or stretched mineral aggregates, due to directional extension. Its origin is primarily **mass transport** and **ductile flow**, where the rock is stretched along one direction, causing these features to elongate.
    3.  **Crenulation Lineation:** This is observed in fine-grained rocks and is formed by the intersection of two sets of cleavages (a penetrative cleavage and a later crenulation cleavage). Its origin is associated with **refolding** and the development of new cleavages within older ones.

**Answer 2:**
    a) **Parallel alignment and elongation of mica crystals within a schist:** This is a **mineral lineation**. It indicates the direction of maximum finite extension and reflects processes of **recrystallization** and **plastic deformation** of the mica minerals under directed stress.
    b) **Stretched and flattened pebbles within a conglomerate:** This is a **stretching lineation** (or a strain lineation). It signifies significant **ductile deformation** and **directional extension** of the rock, with the pebbles elongated parallel to the maximum extension direction.
    c) **The intersection of bedding planes with a prominent cleavage plane, forming a linear trace on the bedding surface:** This is an **intersection lineation**. Its origin is the geometric intersection of two planar features. In folded rocks, this lineation is often parallel to the **fold axis**.

**Answer 3:**
For a syncline, you would identify a lineation by looking for the parallel alignment and elongation of any features (e.g., minerals, pebbles) within the rock layers. If no such features are present, you can identify the **fold axis** directly by observing the hinge of the syncline. Alternatively, in a rock with bedding and an axial plane cleavage, the lineation would be the **intersection of the bedding and the cleavage**. The relationship between this lineation and the fold axis is that it is **parallel** to the fold axis in a cylindrical fold.

**Answer 4:**
In a shear zone, a pronounced mineral lineation is typically parallel to the **direction of shear movement** or, more precisely, parallel to the **direction of maximum stretching** within the shear zone. This lineation indicates the bulk flow direction of the material through the shear zone.

**Answer 5:**
An **intersection lineation** is a linear feature formed by the geometric intersection of two planar geological features. For example:
    *   The intersection of a **bedding plane** with an **axial plane cleavage** in a folded sequence.
    *   The intersection of two different **cleavage planes** (e.g., a slaty cleavage and a fracture cleavage).
    *   The intersection of a **bedding plane** with a **fault plane**.
