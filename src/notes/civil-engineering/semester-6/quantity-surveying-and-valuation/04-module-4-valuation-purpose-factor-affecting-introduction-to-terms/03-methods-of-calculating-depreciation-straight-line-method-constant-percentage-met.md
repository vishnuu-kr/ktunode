# Quantity Surveying and Valuation: Module 4 - Valuation

## Topic: Methods of Calculating Depreciation

### 1. Introduction to Depreciation

**Definition:** Depreciation is the **decrease in the value of an asset over time** due to wear and tear, obsolescence, or other factors. It represents the loss of economic usefulness of an asset.

**Purpose of Calculating Depreciation:**

*   **Accurate Asset Valuation:** To determine the true current market value of an asset by accounting for its age and condition.
*   **Financial Reporting:** For accounting purposes, depreciation is expensed over the useful life of an asset, affecting profitability and asset book value.
*   **Taxation:** Depreciation expenses can often be deducted from taxable income.
*   **Replacement Cost Calculation:** Understanding depreciation helps in planning for the future replacement of assets.
*   **Insurance:** For insurance purposes, knowing the depreciated value is crucial for claims.
*   **Investment Decisions:** Investors need to understand the depreciation of assets to assess the long-term viability and profitability of a business or property.

**Factors Affecting Depreciation:**

*   **Physical Deterioration:**
    *   **Wear and Tear:** Normal use and exposure to elements cause physical degradation.
    *   **Breakdown:** Mechanical failures and structural weaknesses.
*   **Functional Obsolescence:**
    *   **Outdated Design/Technology:** The asset becomes less efficient or desirable compared to newer alternatives.
    *   **Inadequacy:** The asset can no longer meet the current demands or scale of operations.
*   **Economic/External Obsolescence:**
    *   **Changes in Market Demand:** A decrease in the demand for the asset or its output.
    *   **Technological Advancements:** New inventions or processes making the asset redundant.
    *   **Legal or Regulatory Changes:** New building codes or environmental regulations that make the asset non-compliant.
    *   **Location Factors:** Changes in the surrounding environment, infrastructure, or economic conditions.
*   **Useful Life:** The estimated period over which an asset is expected to be used. This is influenced by maintenance, usage intensity, and technological advancements.
*   **Salvage Value (Residual Value):** The estimated value of an asset at the end of its useful life. This is the amount the owner expects to sell it for or dispose of it for.

### 2. Methods of Calculating Depreciation

There are several methods to calculate depreciation, each with its own approach. We will explore the most common ones:

#### 2.1. Straight-Line Method

This is the simplest and most widely used method. It assumes that the asset depreciates by an equal amount each year over its useful life.

**Formula:**

**Annual Depreciation = (Original Cost - Salvage Value) / Useful Life (in years)**

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Original Cost:** The initial purchase price of the asset, including any costs to get it ready for use.
*   **Salvage Value:** The estimated residual value of the asset at the end of its useful life.
*   **Useful Life:** The estimated period the asset will be in service.

**Example:**

A building was constructed at a cost of $1,000,000. It is estimated to have a useful life of 50 years and a salvage value of $100,000.

*   **Annual Depreciation:** ($1,000,000 - $100,000) / 50 years = $900,000 / 50 = **$18,000 per year**

**Pros:**

*   Simple to calculate and understand.
*   Provides a consistent depreciation charge each year.

**Cons:**

*   May not accurately reflect the actual pattern of wear and tear, as assets often depreciate more in their earlier years.

#### 2.2. Constant Percentage Method (Reducing Balance Method/Diminishing Balance Method)

This method depreciates the asset at a fixed percentage of its **book value** (original cost minus accumulated depreciation) each year. This results in a higher depreciation charge in the earlier years and a decreasing charge in later years.

**Formula:**

**Annual Depreciation = Depreciation Rate (%) * Book Value at the Beginning of the Year**

*   **Depreciation Rate:** Typically, this is a multiple of the straight-line rate. For example, if the straight-line rate is 2% (1/50 years), the constant percentage rate might be 5% (2.5 times the straight-line rate). The rate is usually determined by regulation or company policy.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Book Value:** The value of an asset recorded in the company's books, which is the original cost less accumulated depreciation.
*   **Depreciation Rate:** A fixed percentage applied to the book value.

**Example:**

Using the same building from the previous example: Original Cost = $1,000,000, Salvage Value = $100,000, Useful Life = 50 years.
Let's assume a depreciation rate of 5% is applied to the book value.

*   **Year 1:**
    *   Book Value: $1,000,000
    *   Depreciation: 5% * $1,000,000 = $50,000
    *   End of Year 1 Book Value: $1,000,000 - $50,000 = $950,000

*   **Year 2:**
    *   Book Value: $950,000
    *   Depreciation: 5% * $950,000 = $47,500
    *   End of Year 2 Book Value: $950,000 - $47,500 = $902,500

**Important Note for Constant Percentage Method:** The asset's book value should not fall below its salvage value. If the calculated depreciation in a given year would reduce the book value below the salvage value, the depreciation for that year is limited to the amount needed to reach the salvage value.

**Pros:**

*   Reflects the reality that assets tend to lose more value in their early years.
*   Can provide tax advantages by allowing larger deductions in the early years.

**Cons:**

*   More complex to calculate than the straight-line method.
*   The depreciation charge is not uniform.

#### 2.3. Sinking Fund Method

This method is based on the concept of setting aside a fixed amount of money each year into a "sinking fund" which, with accumulated interest, will be sufficient to replace the asset at the end of its useful life. This method is more theoretical and less commonly used in general accounting for depreciation itself, but the concept of a sinking fund is relevant for asset replacement planning.

**Formula:**

The annual contribution to the sinking fund can be calculated using the future value of an ordinary annuity formula. However, for depreciation calculation, the focus is on the annual charge.

**Depreciation Charge = Annual Sinking Fund Contribution**

The formula for the future value of an ordinary annuity is:

**FV = P * [((1 + r)^n - 1) / r]**

Where:
*   FV = Future Value (which is the cost of the asset minus its salvage value)
*   P = Periodic Payment (the annual sinking fund contribution, which equals the annual depreciation)
*   r = Interest rate per period
*   n = Number of periods

To find the annual depreciation (P), we rearrange the formula:

**P = FV / [((1 + r)^n - 1) / r]**

**P = FV * [r / ((1 + r)^n - 1)]**

Where FV = Original Cost - Salvage Value.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Sinking Fund:** A fund established for the gradual accumulation of money to pay off a debt or to replace an asset.
*   **Interest Rate:** The assumed rate of return on the investments within the sinking fund.
*   **Annual Contribution:** The fixed amount set aside each year, which represents the depreciation charge.

**Example:**

Using the same building: Original Cost = $1,000,000, Salvage Value = $100,000, Useful Life = 50 years.
Assume an annual interest rate of 4% (0.04).

*   **Amount to be accumulated (FV):** $1,000,000 - $100,000 = $900,000
*   **n = 50 years**
*   **r = 0.04**

**Annual Depreciation (P) = $900,000 * [0.04 / ((1 + 0.04)^50 - 1)]**
**P = $900,000 * [0.04 / ((1.04)^50 - 1)]**
**P = $900,000 * [0.04 / (7.10668 - 1)]**
**P = $900,000 * [0.04 / 6.10668]**
**P = $900,000 * 0.006551** (approximately)
**P = $5,895.90** (approximately)

So, the annual depreciation charge under the sinking fund method would be approximately $5,895.90. This is a much lower annual charge compared to the straight-line method, but it assumes the investment grows at a steady rate.

**Pros:**

*   Accounts for the time value of money and the potential for investment returns.
*   Provides a mechanism for planned asset replacement.

**Cons:**

*   Relies on an assumed interest rate, which may not be achieved.
*   More complex calculation.
*   The depreciation charge is lower in the early years, which may not align with the actual usage pattern.

#### 2.4. Quantity Survey Method (or Straight-Line Depreciation based on Physical Units/Output)

While "Quantity Survey Method" is not a standard accounting term for depreciation calculation, in the context of quantity surveying and valuation, it often refers to methods that directly link depreciation to the **usage or output of the asset**, rather than just the passage of time. This is sometimes called the **Units of Production Method** or **Sum of Digits Method** can also be considered related in terms of linking depreciation to usage patterns.

The concept here is that an asset depreciates as it is used. If an asset's life can be measured in units of production (e.g., hours of operation, number of units produced, miles driven), depreciation can be charged based on its actual usage.

**Formula (Units of Production):**

**Depreciation per Unit = (Original Cost - Salvage Value) / Total Estimated Production Units**

**Annual Depreciation = Depreciation per Unit * Actual Units Produced in the Year**

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Total Estimated Production Units:** The total output or usage expected from the asset over its entire useful life.
*   **Actual Units Produced:** The output or usage of the asset during a specific period.

**Example:**

A machine costs $50,000 and has a salvage value of $5,000. It is expected to produce 200,000 units over its useful life. In its first year, it produces 30,000 units.

*   **Depreciation per Unit:** ($50,000 - $5,000) / 200,000 units = $45,000 / 200,000 units = $0.225 per unit
*   **Year 1 Depreciation:** $0.225/unit * 30,000 units = **$6,750**

In the second year, the machine produces 25,000 units:

*   **Year 2 Depreciation:** $0.225/unit * 25,000 units = **$5,625**

**Pros:**

*   Accurately reflects depreciation based on actual usage.
*   Matches depreciation expense with the revenue generated by the asset.

**Cons:**

*   Requires accurate estimation of total production units and tracking of actual usage.
*   May result in fluctuating depreciation charges.
*   Not suitable for assets whose life is not directly tied to production output (e.g., buildings).

**Note on Quantity Survey Method in a Broader Sense:** In the context of a Quantity Surveyor, when assessing the *current value* of a property for valuation purposes, they might use techniques that consider the *remaining economic life* of building components. This involves estimating the useful life of various parts (e.g., roof, plumbing, finishes) and assessing their current condition, effectively applying a form of depreciation based on the quantity and condition of these components. This is distinct from the accounting methods of depreciation for income statement purposes but is crucial for property valuation.

### 3. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**

A company purchases a piece of machinery for $200,000. The estimated useful life is 10 years, and the salvage value is $20,000. Calculate the annual depreciation using the Straight-Line Method.

**Answer 1:**

*   Annual Depreciation = (Original Cost - Salvage Value) / Useful Life
*   Annual Depreciation = ($200,000 - $20,000) / 10 years
*   Annual Depreciation = $180,000 / 10 = **$18,000 per year**

**Question 2:**

Using the information from Question 1, calculate the depreciation for the second year using the Constant Percentage Method, assuming a depreciation rate of 20% per year.

**Answer 2:**

*   **Year 1:**
    *   Book Value: $200,000
    *   Depreciation: 20% * $200,000 = $40,000
    *   End of Year 1 Book Value: $200,000 - $40,000 = $160,000

*   **Year 2:**
    *   Book Value: $160,000
    *   Depreciation: 20% * $160,000 = **$32,000**

**Question 3:**

A factory building cost $5,000,000 to construct. It has a useful life of 40 years and a salvage value of $500,000. If the company uses the Sinking Fund Method with an assumed interest rate of 5% per annum, calculate the annual depreciation charge. (Formula for P = FV * [r / ((1 + r)^n - 1)])

**Answer 3:**

*   FV = $5,000,000 - $500,000 = $4,500,000
*   r = 0.05
*   n = 40

*   Annual Depreciation (P) = $4,500,000 * [0.05 / ((1 + 0.05)^40 - 1)]
*   P = $4,500,000 * [0.05 / ((1.05)^40 - 1)]
*   P = $4,500,000 * [0.05 / (7.03999 - 1)]
*   P = $4,500,000 * [0.05 / 6.03999]
*   P = $4,500,000 * 0.008278
*   P = **$37,251** (approximately)

**Question 4:**

A delivery truck cost $60,000 and has a salvage value of $10,000. It is estimated to be driven 500,000 miles in its lifetime. In the first year, the truck was driven 60,000 miles. Calculate the depreciation for the first year using the Units of Production Method.

**Answer 4:**

*   Depreciation per Unit = (Original Cost - Salvage Value) / Total Estimated Production Units
*   Depreciation per Unit = ($60,000 - $10,000) / 500,000 miles
*   Depreciation per Unit = $50,000 / 500,000 miles = $0.10 per mile

*   Year 1 Depreciation = $0.10/mile * 60,000 miles = **$6,000**

### 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **Depreciation is a Non-Cash Expense:** It reduces the book value of an asset but does not involve an outflow of cash in the period it is recorded.
*   **Purpose Dictates Method:** The choice of depreciation method often depends on the purpose for which it is being calculated (e.g., accounting, taxation, valuation).
*   **Consistency is Key:** Once a depreciation method is chosen, it should be applied consistently for a particular asset. Changes require justification.
*   **Salvage Value:** Always consider the salvage value when calculating depreciation, as it represents the minimum value the asset should be depreciated to.
*   **Useful Life Estimation:** Accurate estimation of useful life is crucial for all depreciation methods. This is often an estimate and can be subject to revision.
*   **Quantity Surveyor's Role:** While accounting methods are used for financial reporting, Quantity Surveyors are concerned with the physical condition and remaining economic life of assets for valuation purposes, often using observation and expert judgment to estimate depreciation.

This comprehensive set of notes covers the fundamental methods of calculating depreciation relevant to Quantity Surveying and Valuation. Understanding these methods is vital for accurate asset valuation and financial decision-making.
