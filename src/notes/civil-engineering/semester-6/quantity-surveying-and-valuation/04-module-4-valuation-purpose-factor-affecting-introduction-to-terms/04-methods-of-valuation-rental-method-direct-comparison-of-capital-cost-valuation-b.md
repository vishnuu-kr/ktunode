# Quantity Surveying and Valuation: Module 4 - Valuation Methods

## Introduction to Valuation

Valuation is the process of determining the monetary worth of an asset or property at a specific point in time. It's a crucial skill for quantity surveyors and is essential for various financial and legal purposes.

### Purpose of Valuation

Valuation serves numerous purposes, including:

*   **Sale and Purchase:** Determining a fair price for buying or selling property.
*   **Mortgage and Lending:** Assessing property as collateral for loans.
*   **Insurance:** Estimating the cost of rebuilding or replacing a property.
*   **Taxation:** Calculating property taxes, capital gains tax, and inheritance tax.
*   **Compulsory Acquisition:** Determining compensation for properties acquired by government bodies.
*   **Investment Analysis:** Evaluating the potential return on property investments.
*   **Feasibility Studies:** Assessing the economic viability of new developments.
*   **Landlord and Tenant Issues:** Setting rent, assessing dilapidations, and resolving disputes.

### Factors Affecting Valuation

Several factors influence the value of a property:

*   **Physical Characteristics:**
    *   **Size and Shape:** Area, layout, and efficiency of space.
    *   **Age and Condition:** State of repair, maintenance history, and obsolescence.
    *   **Quality of Construction:** Materials used, design, and build quality.
    *   **Amenities:** Features like garages, gardens, bathrooms, heating systems, etc.
    *   **Location:** Proximity to transport, schools, shops, amenities, and desirability of the neighbourhood.
*   **Economic Factors:**
    *   **Market Conditions:** Supply and demand, interest rates, economic growth, inflation.
    *   **Rental Income:** Potential income generation from the property.
    *   **Yields:** Expected return on investment.
    *   **Local Employment and Income Levels:** Affects affordability and demand.
*   **Legal and Planning Factors:**
    *   **Zoning Regulations:** Permitted land use.
    *   **Building Codes and Standards:** Compliance requirements.
    *   **Easements and Covenants:** Restrictions on land use.
    *   **Title Deeds and Ownership:** Clarity and marketability of ownership.
    *   **Planning Permissions:** Approvals for development or alterations.
*   **Environmental Factors:**
    *   **Surrounding Environment:** Noise pollution, pollution levels, views.
    *   **Environmental Regulations:** Restrictions related to contamination or protected areas.

### Introduction to Key Valuation Terms

*   **Valuation:** The process of estimating the market value of an asset.
*   **Market Value:** The estimated amount for which an asset should exchange on the valuation date between a willing buyer and a willing seller in an arm's length transaction, after proper marketing, wherein the parties had each acted knowledgeably, prudently, and without compulsion.
*   **Fair Value:** Similar to market value, often used in accounting contexts.
*   **Investment Value:** The value of a property to a specific investor, considering their individual investment criteria and expected returns.
*   **Mortgage Value:** The value of a property that a lender is willing to accept as security for a loan. This is often a conservative estimate of market value.
*   **Gross Rental Income (GRI):** The total potential rental income a property can generate if fully occupied and all rents are collected.
*   **Effective Gross Income (EGI):** Gross Rental Income minus vacancy and credit losses.
*   **Net Operating Income (NOI):** Effective Gross Income minus all operating expenses (excluding mortgage payments, depreciation, and capital expenditures).
*   **Capitalization Rate (Cap Rate):** The ratio of Net Operating Income to the property's market value. It represents the expected rate of return on an investment.
    *   **Formula:** $Cap Rate = \frac{NOI}{Market Value}$
*   **Yield:** A general term for the return on an investment.
*   **Depreciation:** The decrease in the value of an asset over time due to wear and tear, obsolescence, or damage.
*   **Obsolescence:** A loss in value due to functional or economic factors, rather than physical deterioration.

## Methods of Valuation

There are several common methods used in property valuation, each suited to different types of properties and purposes.

### 1. Rental Method (Income Capitalisation Method)

This method is widely used for income-producing properties such as shops, offices, industrial units, and residential properties let out to tenants. It focuses on the income the property generates.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Gross Rental Income (GRI):** Total potential rent.
*   **Vacancy and Credit Loss:** Estimated loss of rent due to unoccupied periods or non-paying tenants.
*   **Effective Gross Income (EGI):** GRI - Vacancy & Credit Loss.
*   **Operating Expenses:** Costs associated with running and maintaining the property (e.g., property taxes, insurance, repairs, management fees). Excludes mortgage payments, depreciation, and capital expenditures.
*   **Net Operating Income (NOI):** EGI - Operating Expenses.
*   **Capitalization Rate (Cap Rate):** The rate used to convert the NOI into a property's value. This rate reflects the risk associated with the investment and prevailing market yields for similar properties.

**Steps:**

1.  **Estimate the potential Gross Rental Income (GRI):** Based on current market rents for comparable properties.
2.  **Deduct Vacancy and Credit Loss:** Typically a percentage of GRI.
3.  **Calculate Effective Gross Income (EGI):** GRI - Vacancy & Credit Loss.
4.  **Deduct Operating Expenses:** Identify and sum all relevant expenses.
5.  **Calculate Net Operating Income (NOI):** EGI - Operating Expenses.
6.  **Determine an appropriate Capitalization Rate (Cap Rate):** Based on market evidence, investor expectations, and the risk profile of the property.
7.  **Calculate the Value:**
    *   **Formula:** $Value = \frac{NOI}{Cap Rate}$

**Example:**

A commercial property generates a current annual rent of $50,000. Market analysis suggests a 5% vacancy and credit loss. Annual operating expenses are estimated at $10,000. Comparable properties in the area are selling at a 7% capitalization rate.

*   GRI = $50,000
*   Vacancy & Credit Loss = 5% of $50,000 = $2,500
*   EGI = $50,000 - $2,500 = $47,500
*   Operating Expenses = $10,000
*   NOI = $47,500 - $10,000 = $37,500
*   Cap Rate = 7% or 0.07
*   **Value = $37,500 / 0.07 = $535,714 (approx.)**

**When to Use:**

*   Income-producing properties.
*   When reliable rental data and comparable sales data are available.

**Important Points:**

*   The accuracy of the valuation heavily depends on the accuracy of the NOI and the chosen Cap Rate.
*   Cap rates are derived from market analysis of recent sales of comparable properties.

### 2. Direct Comparison of Capital Cost (Sales Comparison Approach)

This method is most suitable for valuing properties where there is a good supply of recent sales of similar properties, such as residential houses, vacant land, and some types of commercial properties.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Comparables:** Recently sold properties that are similar to the subject property in terms of location, size, age, condition, features, and amenities.
*   **Adjustments:** Modifications made to the sale prices of comparables to account for differences between them and the subject property.

**Steps:**

1.  **Identify Comparable Properties:** Select properties that have recently sold and are similar to the subject property.
2.  **Gather Sales Data:** Obtain the sale prices and dates of sale for the comparables.
3.  **Analyze Differences:** Identify key characteristics that differentiate the comparables from the subject property (e.g., size, number of bedrooms/bathrooms, condition, features, location within the area).
4.  **Make Adjustments:** Adjust the sale price of each comparable to reflect its differences from the subject property.
    *   If a comparable is *superior* to the subject property, *deduct* from its sale price.
    *   If a comparable is *inferior* to the subject property, *add* to its sale price.
5.  **Reconcile Adjusted Prices:** Arrive at a final valuation by weighing the adjusted sale prices of the comparables. Often, more weight is given to comparables that are most similar to the subject property.

**Example:**

You are valuing a 3-bedroom house. You find three comparables:

*   **Comparable A:** Sold for $300,000. 4 bedrooms, slightly older, good condition.
*   **Comparable B:** Sold for $280,000. 3 bedrooms, same age, excellent condition, larger garden.
*   **Comparable C:** Sold for $310,000. 3 bedrooms, same age, good condition, slightly smaller garden.

**Adjustments (Hypothetical):**

*   Comparable A: 1 extra bedroom ($15,000 deduction), older condition ($5,000 deduction) = $300,000 - $15,000 - $5,000 = $280,000
*   Comparable B: Larger garden ($10,000 deduction) = $280,000 - $10,000 = $270,000
*   Comparable C: Smaller garden ($5,000 addition) = $310,000 + $5,000 = $315,000

**Reconciliation:**

After adjustments, the prices are $280,000, $270,000, and $315,000. Comparables A and C are closest in features to the subject property. You might give more weight to these, perhaps arriving at a valuation of around $290,000 - $300,000.

**When to Use:**

*   Residential properties.
*   Vacant land.
*   Properties where a sufficient number of recent sales of similar properties are available.

**Important Points:**

*   The accuracy depends on the availability of truly comparable properties and the ability to make accurate adjustments.
*   Adjustments are subjective and require market knowledge.

### 3. Valuation Based on Profit (Profits Method)

This method is used for properties that are operated as businesses and whose value is derived primarily from the profit they generate. Examples include hotels, pubs, cinemas, factories with a strong trading record, and certain types of rental properties where the tenant's business success is paramount.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Gross Profit:** Revenue minus the cost of goods sold.
*   **Net Profit (or Trading Profit):** Gross Profit minus operating expenses and other business-related costs.
*   **Fair Rent:** The rent that a property could command if let on the open market, considering its profitability.

**Steps:**

1.  **Determine the Profit:** Analyze the business's trading accounts to determine its profitability over a representative period. This usually involves calculating Net Profit.
2.  **Identify the "Fair Rent" Element:** Separate the profit attributable to the business (e.g., goodwill, skill of the owner) from the profit attributable to the property itself. This is often done by establishing a "fair rent" that a tenant would pay for the use of the property.
3.  **Capitalize the Fair Rent:** Apply a capitalization rate to the fair rent to determine the property's value attributable to its rental income.
4.  **Value Other Assets:** If there are other fixed assets associated with the property (e.g., plant and machinery), their value might be added separately.

**Example:**

A hotel is valued. The hotel's net profit over the last year was $150,000. The valuer determines that a fair rent for the hotel property itself, independent of the goodwill and management of the current owner, would be $50,000 per year. Comparable hotel properties (where the rent is separable from the profit) are being valued on a 10% yield for the rental income.

*   Fair Rent = $50,000
*   Capitalization Rate = 10% or 0.10
*   **Value of Property (based on rent) = $50,000 / 0.10 = $500,000**

The remaining profit ($150,000 - $50,000 = $100,000) is attributable to the business's goodwill, management, and other intangible assets. This portion would not be included in the property valuation unless the entire business is being sold.

**When to Use:**

*   Properties with a strong trading record where the value is significantly influenced by the business operating within it.
*   Pubs, hotels, cinemas, and some industrial premises with specialized machinery.

**Important Points:**

*   This method requires expertise in business accounts and valuation of intangible assets (goodwill).
*   Separating property profit from business profit can be challenging.

### 4. Depreciation Method (Cost Approach)

This method is often used for specialized properties where direct comparison is difficult, such as schools, hospitals, government buildings, or newly constructed properties. It's based on the principle that a buyer would not pay more for a property than the cost to construct a similar property with equivalent utility.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Replacement Cost:** The cost to construct an exact replica of the subject property using current materials and standards.
*   **Reproduction Cost:** The cost to construct a replica of the subject property using the same materials and design as the original.
*   **Physical Deterioration:** Loss of value due to wear and tear, aging, and lack of maintenance.
*   **Functional Obsolescence:** Loss of value due to outmoded design, features, or inefficiencies (e.g., poor layout, inadequate services).
*   **Economic Obsolescence:** Loss of value due to external factors that are beyond the property itself (e.g., undesirable neighbourhood, changes in market demand, zoning changes).

**Steps:**

1.  **Estimate the Cost of Reproduction or Replacement:** Determine the cost to build a new property similar to the subject property.
2.  **Estimate Accrued Depreciation:** Calculate the total depreciation attributable to the property, considering:
    *   Physical deterioration.
    *   Functional obsolescence.
    *   Economic obsolescence.
3.  **Calculate the Depreciated Value:**
    *   **Formula:** $Value = Cost of Reproduction/Replacement - Accrued Depreciation$

**Example:**

A warehouse was built 20 years ago for $500,000.
Current replacement cost for a similar new warehouse is $800,000.
The valuer estimates total accrued depreciation (physical, functional, economic) to be $200,000.

*   Replacement Cost (New) = $800,000
*   Accrued Depreciation = $200,000
*   **Value = $800,000 - $200,000 = $600,000**

**When to Use:**

*   Specialized properties (schools, hospitals, government buildings).
*   Newly constructed properties.
*   Properties where comparables are scarce.
*   Insurance valuations.

**Important Points:**

*   Estimating depreciation, especially obsolescence, can be challenging and subjective.
*   It assumes a buyer would choose to build new rather than buy existing, which isn't always the case. Often, a hybrid approach combining this with the sales comparison method for land value is used.

## Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

A commercial property has a potential gross rental income of $80,000 per annum. Vacancy and credit losses are estimated at 6%. Operating expenses are $15,000 per annum. If comparable properties are selling at a 7.5% capitalization rate, what is the property's value using the rental method?

**Answer 1:**

*   GRI = $80,000
*   Vacancy & Credit Loss = 6% of $80,000 = $4,800
*   EGI = $80,000 - $4,800 = $75,200
*   Operating Expenses = $15,000
*   NOI = $75,200 - $15,000 = $60,200
*   Cap Rate = 7.5% or 0.075
*   Value = $60,200 / 0.075 = **$802,667 (approx.)**

**Question 2:**

You are valuing a residential property. You have identified the following comparable sales:

*   **Comp 1:** Sold for $450,000. 4 bedrooms, good condition, standard garden.
*   **Comp 2:** Sold for $430,000. 3 bedrooms, excellent condition, large garden.
*   **Comp 3:** Sold for $465,000. 3 bedrooms, good condition, standard garden, recently renovated kitchen.

The subject property is 3 bedrooms, good condition, standard garden, and has a recently renovated kitchen. What would be a reasonable valuation using the direct comparison method? Justify your adjustments.

**Answer 2:**

Let's assume the subject property is identical to Comp 3 except for the kitchen renovation.

*   **Comp 1:** Subject property has fewer bedrooms (inferior) and a standard garden. Let's say an extra bedroom is worth $20,000 and a larger garden is worth $10,000.
    *   Adjustment for fewer bedrooms: $-20,000$
    *   Adjustment for garden: $-10,000$
    *   Adjusted Price of Comp 1: $450,000 - 20,000 - 10,000 = $420,000
*   **Comp 2:** Subject property has a standard garden (inferior) and less excellent condition (inferior). Let's say the garden is worth $10,000 and the condition difference is $5,000.
    *   Adjustment for garden: $-10,000$
    *   Adjustment for condition: $-5,000$
    *   Adjusted Price of Comp 2: $430,000 - 10,000 - 5,000 = $415,000
*   **Comp 3:** Subject property has a recently renovated kitchen, which is an improvement over Comp 3's standard kitchen. Let's assume the value of a recently renovated kitchen is $15,000.
    *   Adjustment for kitchen renovation: $-15,000$
    *   Adjusted Price of Comp 3: $465,000 - 15,000 = $450,000

**Reconciliation:**

The adjusted prices are $420,000, $415,000, and $450,000. Comp 3 is the most similar, with only a minor adjustment needed. Therefore, the valuation would likely be close to the adjusted price of Comp 3.

**Reasonable Valuation:** Around **$450,000**.

**Question 3:**

A factory has a net annual profit of $200,000. The fair rent for the factory premises alone is estimated at $60,000 per annum. If the going capitalization rate for similar industrial property rentals is 9%, what is the valuation of the factory premises?

**Answer 3:**

*   Fair Rent = $60,000
*   Capitalization Rate = 9% or 0.09
*   Value of Property = $60,000 / 0.09 = **$666,667 (approx.)**

**Question 4:**

A public library cost $3,000,000 to build 10 years ago. The current replacement cost for a similar facility is $4,500,000. Accrued depreciation is estimated at $1,200,000. What is the depreciated value of the library using the cost approach?

**Answer 4:**

*   Replacement Cost (New) = $4,500,000
*   Accrued Depreciation = $1,200,000
*   Value = $4,500,000 - $1,200,000 = **$3,300,000**

## Summary of Key Points to Remember

*   **Valuation is an opinion of value**, based on evidence and professional judgment.
*   The **rental method** is ideal for income-producing properties.
*   The **direct comparison method** relies heavily on finding truly comparable properties and making accurate adjustments.
*   The **profit method** is for business-based properties, requiring separation of property profit from business profit.
*   The **depreciation method (cost approach)** is useful for specialized properties or new builds, but depreciation estimation can be subjective.
*   Always consider the **purpose of the valuation** as it can influence the method used and the value reached.
*   Market conditions, economic factors, and legal/planning regulations are crucial influences on property value.
*   Understanding key terms like GRI, NOI, Cap Rate, and depreciation is fundamental.
