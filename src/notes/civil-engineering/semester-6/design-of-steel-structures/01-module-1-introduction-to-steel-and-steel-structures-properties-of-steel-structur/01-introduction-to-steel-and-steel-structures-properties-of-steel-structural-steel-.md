# DESIGN OF STEEL STRUCTURES - MODULE 1: INTRODUCTION TO STEEL AND STEEL STRUCTURES, PROPERTIES OF STEEL, STRUCTURAL STEEL SECTIONS

This module provides a foundational understanding of steel as a structural material, its key properties, and the common types of structural steel sections used in building and infrastructure design.

---

## 1. Introduction to Steel and Steel Structures

Steel has become a dominant material in modern construction due to its unique combination of strength, ductility, and constructability. Understanding its advantages and applications is crucial for any aspiring structural engineer.

### 1.1 What is Steel?

*   **Definition:** Steel is an alloy primarily composed of iron, with carbon being the most important alloying element.
*   **Carbon Content:** The amount of carbon in steel is critical and typically ranges from 0.2% to 2.1% by weight.
    *   **Low-carbon steel (Mild Steel):** Less than 0.25% carbon. Most common for structural applications due to its ductility and weldability.
    *   **Medium-carbon steel:** 0.25% to 0.6% carbon. Stronger but less ductile and harder to weld.
    *   **High-carbon steel:** 0.6% to 2.1% carbon. Very strong and hard, but brittle. Used for tools and springs.
*   **Other Alloying Elements:** While carbon is primary, other elements like manganese, silicon, phosphorus, and sulfur are present and affect the steel's properties.

### 1.2 Advantages of Steel as a Structural Material

*   **High Strength-to-Weight Ratio:** Steel possesses high tensile and compressive strength, allowing for lighter structures compared to concrete or timber for the same load-carrying capacity.
*   **Ductility:** Steel can deform significantly before fracturing, providing a warning of failure and preventing brittle collapse. This is crucial for seismic resistance.
*   **Uniformity and Quality Control:** Steel is manufactured under strict quality control, ensuring consistent properties and predictable performance.
*   **Speed of Construction:** Steel components can be prefabricated off-site, leading to faster erection and reduced on-site labor.
*   **Versatility:** Steel can be fabricated into a wide range of shapes and sizes, allowing for innovative and complex architectural designs.
*   **Recyclability:** Steel is highly recyclable, contributing to sustainable construction practices.
*   **Durability:** When properly protected against corrosion, steel structures can have a very long service life.

### 1.3 Disadvantages of Steel as a Structural Material

*   **Corrosion:** Steel is susceptible to rust (corrosion) when exposed to moisture and oxygen, requiring protective coatings.
*   **Buckling:** Slender steel compression members are prone to buckling under load, requiring careful design to prevent instability.
*   **Fire Resistance:** Steel loses strength and stiffness at elevated temperatures, necessitating fire protection measures in many applications.
*   **Cost:** The initial cost of steel can be higher than some other materials, although this can be offset by faster construction and lighter foundations.

### 1.4 Common Applications of Steel Structures

*   **High-rise Buildings:** Skeletal frameworks for skyscrapers.
*   **Bridges:** Decking, girders, trusses for various types of bridges.
*   **Industrial Buildings:** Warehouses, factories, power plants.
*   **Sports Stadiums:** Roof structures, seating arrangements.
*   **Towers:** Communication towers, transmission towers.
*   **Residential Buildings:** Increasingly used for framing in multi-story residential projects.

## 2. Properties of Steel

Understanding the mechanical properties of steel is fundamental to designing safe and efficient steel structures. These properties are typically determined through standardized material testing.

### 2.1 Stress-Strain Relationship

*   **Definition:** The stress-strain curve graphically represents how a material deforms under applied stress.
*   **Key Points on the Curve:**
    *   **Elastic Limit:** The point up to which stress is directly proportional to strain (Hooke's Law). Deformation is temporary and the material returns to its original shape when the load is removed.
    *   **Yield Strength ($F_y$):** The stress at which the material begins to deform plastically. Beyond this point, deformation is permanent. This is a critical design parameter for steel.
    *   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($F_u$):** The maximum stress the material can withstand before it begins to neck (localize deformation) and fracture.
    *   **Fracture Point:** The point at which the material breaks.

*   **Ductility:** Measured by the elongation at fracture or the reduction in area at fracture. High ductility is essential for seismic performance and to prevent brittle failure.
*   **Modulus of Elasticity ($E$):** The ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region. It represents the stiffness of the material. For most structural steels, $E$ is approximately 200 GPa (29,000 ksi).
*   **Poisson's Ratio ($\nu$):** The ratio of transverse strain to axial strain in the elastic region. For steel, $\nu$ is approximately 0.3.

### 2.2 Types of Steel and Their Properties (as per IS 2062)

In India, structural steel is commonly classified and specified by standards like IS 2062 (Hot Rolled Medium and High Tensile Structural Steel).

*   **IS 2062 E250:**
    *   **Yield Strength ($F_y$):** 250 MPa (N/mm²)
    *   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($F_u$):** 410 MPa (N/mm²)
    *   **Applications:** General structural purposes, fabrication of various steel components.
*   **IS 2062 E300:**
    *   **Yield Strength ($F_y$):** 300 MPa (N/mm²)
    *   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($F_u$):** 440 MPa (N/mm²)
    *   **Applications:** Where higher strength is required, for larger spans or heavier loads.
*   **IS 2062 E350:**
    *   **Yield Strength ($F_y$):** 350 MPa (N/mm²)
    *   **Ultimate Tensile Strength ($F_u$):** 490 MPa (N/mm²)
    *   **Applications:** High-strength applications, significant load-bearing elements.

**Important Note:** The actual design stresses used in calculations are typically lower than the material's yield or ultimate strength to provide a factor of safety.

### 2.3 Other Important Properties

*   **Density:** Approximately 7850 kg/m³. This is important for calculating the self-weight of the structure.
*   **Coefficient of Thermal Expansion:** Approximately $12 \times 10^{-6}$ per degree Celsius. Important for designing expansion joints and considering thermal stresses.
*   **Weldability:** The ease with which steel can be welded without defects. Low-carbon steels generally have excellent weldability.
*   **Toughness:** The ability of a material to absorb energy and deform plastically before fracturing. Important for impact loads and low-temperature applications.

## 3. Structural Steel Sections

Structural steel is manufactured in various standard cross-sectional shapes, each designed for specific structural functions. These sections are produced by hot rolling or cold forming.

### 3.1 Common Types of Structural Steel Sections

#### 3.1.1 Rolled Steel Sections (Hot Rolled)

These are the most common type, produced by passing heated steel billets through a series of rollers to achieve the desired shape.

*   **I-Sections (Beams/Girders):**
    *   **Description:** Consist of two parallel flanges connected by a web.
    *   **Primary Use:** Beams, girders, columns, railway tracks.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Indian Standard (IS) Junior Beams (ISJB):** Lighter sections.
        *   **Indian Standard (IS) Medium Beams (ISMB):** Standard sections for general use.
        *   **Indian Standard (IS) Heavy Beams (ISHB):** Heavier sections for higher loads.
        *   **Wide Flange (W) Sections (American Standard):** Have wider flanges, providing better stiffness in the weak axis.
        *   **H-Sections:** Similar to W-sections.
    *   **Behavior:** Excellent in bending about their major axis (strong axis).

*   **Channels (C-Sections/U-Sections):**
    *   **Description:** Similar to I-sections but with only one flange.
    *   **Primary Use:** Purlins, rafters, lintels, bracing members, secondary beams.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Indian Standard (IS) Channels (ISMC):** Standard channels.
        *   **Indian Standard (IS) Junior Channels (ISJC):** Lighter channels.
        *   **American Standard Channels (C):** Wider flange channels.
        *   **American Standard Junior Channels (MC):** Lighter wide flange channels.
    *   **Behavior:** Efficient in bending and as compression members.

*   **Angles (L-Sections):**
    *   **Description:** L-shaped cross-section.
    *   **Primary Use:** Bracing members, purlins, truss members, connections, supports.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Equal Angles:** Both legs have the same width.
        *   **Unequal Angles:** Legs have different widths.
    *   **Behavior:** Often used in tension or compression in trusses.

*   **Tees (T-Sections):**
    *   **Description:** T-shaped cross-section.
    *   **Primary Use:** Sometimes used as purlins or bracing members, or as built-up sections.
    *   **Behavior:** Can be efficient for certain load cases.

*   **Square and Rectangular Hollow Sections (SHS/RHS):**
    *   **Description:** Hollow tubes with square or rectangular cross-sections.
    *   **Primary Use:** Columns, bracing, trusses, space frames, architectural elements.
    *   **Behavior:** Excellent strength-to-weight ratio, good torsional rigidity, aesthetically pleasing.

*   **Circular Hollow Sections (CHS):**
    *   **Description:** Hollow tubes with circular cross-sections.
    *   **Primary Use:** Columns, truss members, space frames, architectural elements.
    *   **Behavior:** Excellent torsional rigidity, uniform strength in all directions.

*   **Plates:**
    *   **Description:** Flat rectangular sheets of steel.
    *   **Primary Use:** Web plates, flange plates in built-up sections, base plates, gusset plates in connections.

*   **Bars:**
    *   **Description:** Solid round or square cross-sections.
    *   **Primary Use:** Reinforcement, connecting elements, certain bracing members.

#### 3.1.2 Built-up Sections

These are fabricated by welding or bolting together multiple simpler rolled sections or plates to achieve specific cross-sectional properties that are not available as standard rolled sections.

*   **Examples:**
    *   **Box Girders:** Fabricated from plates to form a rectangular or square hollow section, often used for bridge decks or large spans.
    *   **Plate Girders:** Made by welding a web plate to two flange plates.
    *   **Built-up I-Sections/Channels:** Combining angles and plates with standard sections.

### 3.2 Identification of Rolled Steel Sections

*   **Naming Convention:** Rolled steel sections are typically identified by their type and nominal depth or width.
*   **Example (Indian Standards):**
    *   **ISMB 300:** Indian Standard Medium Beam with a nominal depth of 300 mm.
    *   **ISMC 250:** Indian Standard Medium Channel with a nominal depth of 250 mm.
    *   **ISA 100x100x10:** Indian Standard Angle with legs of 100 mm x 100 mm and a thickness of 10 mm.

*   **Steel Section Manuals/Catalogs:** These contain detailed geometric properties (area, depth, width, flange thickness, web thickness, moments of inertia, section moduli, etc.) for all standard sections, which are essential for design calculations.

**Question 10:** Why are hollow sections (SHS, RHS, CHS) increasingly popular in modern construction?

## 5. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
*   **Definition:** Steel is an alloy primarily composed of iron and carbon, with carbon content typically ranging from 0.2% to 2.1% by weight.
*   **Advantages:**
    1.  High Strength-to-Weight Ratio
    2.  Ductility
    3.  Speed of Construction

**Answer 2:** The yield strength ($F_y$) is the stress at which the steel begins to deform plastically, meaning it undergoes permanent deformation. It is a critical parameter in structural design as it represents the maximum stress steel can withstand before permanent yielding occurs.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Low-carbon steel (Mild Steel):** Contains less than 0.25% carbon. It is ductile, easily weldable, and commonly used for structural purposes.
*   **High-carbon steel:** Contains 0.6% to 2.1% carbon. It is very strong and hard but also brittle, making it unsuitable for most general structural applications.

**Answer 4:**
1.  **I-Sections (Beams/Girders):** Used as primary load-bearing members in flexure (bending).
2.  **Channels (C-Sections):** Used as purlins, rafters, secondary beams, and bracing.
3.  **Angles (L-Sections):** Used in trusses as tension or compression members, and in connections.

**Answer 5:** "ISMB 400" refers to an Indian Standard Medium Beam with a nominal depth of 400 mm.

**Answer 6:** Buckling is a phenomenon where a slender structural member under compression suddenly bends or collapses sideways at a load lower than its material's yield strength. This is due to the member's slenderness and the instability of the compression force.

**Answer 7:** The main concern is that steel loses significant strength and stiffness at elevated temperatures encountered in a fire. This can lead to the collapse of the structural elements.

**Answer 8:**
*   **Elastic Region:** Within the elastic limit, stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's Law). Deformation is temporary; the material returns to its original shape when the load is removed.
*   **Plastic Region:** Beyond the yield strength, the steel deforms permanently. It exhibits yielding and strain hardening before reaching its ultimate tensile strength and fracturing.

**Answer 9:** The approximate Modulus of Elasticity ($E$) for structural steel is 200 GPa (or 29,000 ksi).

**Answer 10:** Hollow sections offer a higher strength-to-weight ratio, excellent torsional rigidity, good resistance to local buckling, and are often considered aesthetically pleasing, making them suitable for a wide range of structural applications, including bracing, columns, and space frames.

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Carbon content is key:** It determines the grade and properties of steel.
*   **Yield Strength ($F_y$) is crucial for design:** Most steel design codes are based on yielding.
*   **Ductility is vital:** It provides warning before failure and is essential for seismic resistance.
*   **Steel sections are standardized:** Know the common types (I, C, L, RHS, CHS) and their uses.
*   **Steel properties are predictable:** Due to manufacturing standards.
*   **Corrosion and fire require protection:** These are significant considerations for steel structures.
*   **Always refer to Steel Section Manuals:** For accurate geometric and sectional properties needed for calculations.
*   **IS 2062 is a common Indian standard:** Familiarize yourself with its grades (E250, E300, E350).
