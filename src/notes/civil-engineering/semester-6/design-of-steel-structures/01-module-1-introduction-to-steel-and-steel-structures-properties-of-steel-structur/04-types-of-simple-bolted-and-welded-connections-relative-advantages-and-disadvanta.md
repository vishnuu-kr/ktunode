# DESIGN OF STEEL STRUCTURES - MODULE 1: Introduction to Steel and Steel Structures

## Topic: Types of Simple Bolted and Welded Connections

This module introduces the fundamental concepts of steel structures, focusing on the properties of steel and the various structural steel sections available. A crucial aspect of steel construction is the method of joining these sections, which is achieved through bolted and welded connections. This topic delves into the different types of simple connections, their advantages and disadvantages, the potential failure mechanisms of bolted connections, and the design principles for bearing-type and friction-type connections, including the impact of prying forces and the design of bracket connections.

---

### 1. Introduction to Steel and Steel Structures

Steel is a widely used material in construction due to its high strength, ductility, and durability. Steel structures offer several advantages over traditional materials like concrete and timber.

#### 1.1 Properties of Steel

*   **High Strength-to-Weight Ratio:** Steel is strong and relatively light, allowing for slender and efficient structural elements.
*   **Ductility:** Steel can deform significantly under tensile stress before fracturing. This allows it to absorb energy and provide warning before failure, making it a ductile material.
*   **Uniformity and Quality:** Steel is manufactured under controlled conditions, ensuring consistent properties and quality.
*   **Elasticity:** Steel behaves elastically within a certain stress range, meaning it returns to its original shape after the load is removed.
*   **Stiffness:** Steel has a high Young's Modulus (E), indicating it is a stiff material and undergoes less deformation under load.
*   **Weldability and Workability:** Steel is readily weldable and can be easily fabricated into various shapes.
*   **Fire Resistance:** Steel loses strength at high temperatures, requiring fire protection measures.
*   **Corrosion Resistance:** Steel can corrode if exposed to moisture and air, necessitating protective coatings.

#### 1.2 Structural Steel Sections

Structural steel is manufactured in a variety of standard shapes, each suited for different structural applications.

*   **Rolled Steel Sections:** Produced by passing hot steel through a series of rollers.
    *   **I-Sections (or W-Sections/Universal Beams):** Commonly used as beams and columns due to their efficient distribution of material for resisting bending and axial loads.
    *   **Channel Sections (or C-Sections/Parallel Flange Channels):** Often used for purlins, girts, and secondary beams.
    *   **Angle Sections (or L-Sections):** Used for bracing, purlins, and as connection elements.
    *   **T-Sections:** Can be used as beams or for bracing.
    *   **Square and Rectangular Hollow Sections (SHS/RHS):** Offer good torsional rigidity and are used for columns, beams, and bracing.
    *   **Circular Hollow Sections (CHS):** Similar applications to SHS/RHS, particularly good for compression members and as aesthetic elements.
*   **Built-up Sections:** Fabricated by welding or bolting together rolled sections or plates to achieve larger or more specific cross-sectional properties.

### 2. Types of Simple Bolted and Welded Connections

Connections are critical elements in steel structures that transfer forces between members.

#### 2.1 Bolted Connections

Bolted connections use high-strength bolts to join steel members.

*   **Types of Bolts:**
    *   **Black Bolts (Unfinished):** Lower strength, used for less critical connections.
    *   **High-Strength Friction Grip (HSFG) Bolts:** High tensile strength and designed to transfer loads through friction between the connected members.
    *   **High-Strength Bearing Bolts:** Designed to transfer loads through shear and bearing of the bolt and connected plates.

*   **Types of Bolted Connections based on Load Transfer:**
    *   **Butt Joints (Splices):** Used to join members end-to-end, typically requiring cover plates on both sides.
        *   *Single Cover Butt Joint:* Cover plate on one side only.
        *   *Double Cover Butt Joint:* Cover plates on both sides.
    *   **Lap Joints:** Members overlap and are bolted together. Simpler to make but can introduce eccentricity.
    *   **T-Joints:** A flange or web of one member is joined to the flange or web of another member.
    *   **Corner Joints:** Used to join members at a corner.

*   **Connection Arrangements:**
    *   **Single Row:** Bolts arranged in a single line.
    *   **Multiple Rows:** Bolts arranged in multiple lines.
    *   **Staggered:** Bolts in one row are offset from bolts in adjacent rows, improving load distribution.

#### 2.2 Welded Connections

Welded connections create a continuous joint by fusing steel members using heat.

*   **Types of Welds:**
    *   **Fillet Welds:** Most common type, used to join surfaces at an angle (e.g., web to flange of a beam, leg of an angle).
        *   *Convex, Flat, Concave Profiles.*
    *   **Butt Welds:** Used to join members end-to-end, usually requiring edge preparation and providing full strength.
        *   *Square Butt Weld, V-Groove Butt Weld, U-Groove Butt Weld, etc.*
    *   **Plug Welds and Slot Welds:** Used to connect overlapping members when fillet welds are not feasible.
    *   **Surfacing Welds:** Depositing weld metal on a surface to build it up.

*   **Types of Welded Connections based on Load Transfer:**
    *   **Butt Splices:** Similar to bolted butt joints, joining members end-to-end.
    *   **Fillet Welded Connections:** Joining members at angles, common for beam-to-column, beam-to-beam connections, and lap joints.
    *   **T-Joints:** Welded connections where a member is joined to the face of another.

### 3. Relative Advantages and Disadvantages of Bolted vs. Welded Connections

| Feature          | Bolted Connections                                                                         | Welded Connections                                                                         |
| :--------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Advantages**   | - Easy to erect and disassemble.<br>- Can be done in any weather condition.<br>- Less skilled labor required.<br>- Inspection is relatively straightforward.<br>- Can accommodate minor inaccuracies.<br>- Good for site connections. | - Creates a more rigid and monolithic joint.<br>- Aesthetically pleasing (no exposed bolt heads/nuts).<br>- Potentially stronger if properly designed and executed.<br>- Lighter due to fewer connection plates/bolts. |
| **Disadvantages** | - Can loosen over time due to vibrations.<br>- Can introduce stress concentrations.<br>- Requires holes, which reduce the effective area of members.<br>- Can be bulky due to connecting plates.<br>- Bolt heads and nuts can obstruct access. | - Requires skilled welders.<br>- Sensitive to weather conditions (wind, rain).<br>- Difficult to disassemble.<br>- Can induce residual stresses and distortion.<br>- Requires thorough inspection (NDT).<br>- Can be less forgiving of fabrication errors. |

### 4. Modes of Failure of Bolted Connections

Bolted connections can fail in several ways, depending on the type of bolt, the connected material, and the applied load.

#### 4.1 Failure of Bolts in Shear

*   **Shear Rupture:** The bolt material shears across its cross-section. This is dependent on the shear strength of the bolt and the net shear area.
*   **Shear Bearing:** The bolt tears through the connected material (e.g., a plate) at the point of contact. This is dependent on the bearing strength of the connected material and the bolt diameter.

#### 4.2 Failure of Bolts in Tension

*   **Tensile Rupture:** The bolt material is pulled apart in tension. This is dependent on the tensile strength of the bolt and its net tensile area.
*   **Thread Stripping:** The threads of the bolt or nut are damaged or stripped.

#### 4.3 Failure of Connected Plates/Members

*   **Tension Yielding:** The gross cross-sectional area of the plate yields in tension.
*   **Tension Rupture:** The net cross-sectional area of the plate ruptures in tension.
*   **Shear Tear-out:** The plate tears out along the line of bolts due to shear stress.
*   **Bearing Failure:** The material of the connected plate deforms locally around the bolt due to bearing pressure.

#### 4.4 Other Modes of Failure

*   **Block Shear Failure:** A combination of shear along the edge of the connection and tension across the perpendicular leg.
*   **Prying Action:** In lap joints or connections with flexible plates, tensile forces can be amplified due to lever arm effects.

### 5. Design of Bearing Type Connections

In bearing-type connections, the load is transferred primarily through shear and bearing action between the bolt and the connected members.

#### 5.1 Design Principles

*   **Shear Strength of Bolts:** The connection must have sufficient shear capacity to resist the applied shear forces. The design shear strength is typically governed by the shear failure of the bolt.
*   **Bearing Strength of Bolts/Plates:** The connected plates or the bolt itself must resist the bearing pressure exerted by the bolt.
*   **Tensile Strength of Bolts:** For connections in tension or where bolts are subjected to significant prying, tensile capacity is important.
*   **Tensile Strength of Connected Plates:** The net section of the connected plates must have adequate tensile strength to resist the applied tensile forces.

#### 5.2 Design Steps (Simplified)

1.  **Determine Applied Forces:** Identify the shear and tensile forces acting on the connection.
2.  **Select Bolt Type and Size:** Choose appropriate bolts based on required strength and application.
3.  **Check Bolt Shear Strength:** Calculate the shear capacity of the bolts and ensure it exceeds the applied shear force.
    *   Shear Strength = (Shear strength of bolt material) × (Number of shear planes) × (Nominal area of bolt)
4.  **Check Bolt Bearing Strength:** Calculate the bearing capacity of the connected plates and ensure it exceeds the applied shear force.
    *   Bearing Strength = (Bearing strength of connected material) × (Bolt diameter) × (Thickness of connected plate) × (Factor)
5.  **Check Net Section Tension of Plates:** Ensure the net area of the plates can withstand the applied tensile force.
6.  **Check Gross Section Yielding of Plates:** Ensure the gross area of the plates can withstand the applied tensile force.
7.  **Arrange Bolts:** Determine the bolt layout, considering pitch (distance between bolts along the line of force) and gauge (distance between bolt lines perpendicular to the line of force) for adequate spacing and strength.

**Important Point to Remember:** In bearing-type connections, the strength is generally governed by the shear and bearing capacity of the bolts and plates. The contact pressure between the bolt and the hole is significant.

### 6. Design of Friction Type Connection (HSFG Bolts)

In friction-type connections, the load is transferred by the frictional resistance developed between the faying surfaces (surfaces in contact) of the connected members. This resistance is created by the clamping force of the HSFG bolts.

#### 6.1 Design Principles

*   **Pre-tensioning of Bolts:** HSFG bolts are tightened to a specified pre-tension, creating a high clamping force.
*   **Frictional Resistance:** The frictional force is the product of the clamping force and the coefficient of friction between the faying surfaces.
*   **Shear Strength:** Friction connections are designed to resist shear loads through friction. Bolts are not intended to yield in shear in this type of connection.
*   **Bearing is generally not the governing failure mode:** While bearing can occur, the primary load transfer mechanism is friction.

#### 6.2 Design Steps (Simplified)

1.  **Determine Applied Forces:** Identify the shear and tensile forces acting on the connection.
2.  **Select HSFG Bolt Type and Size:** Choose appropriate HSFG bolts.
3.  **Ensure Sufficient Clamping Force:** The pre-tension in the bolts must be high enough to generate the required frictional resistance.
4.  **Check Frictional Capacity:** The total frictional resistance must exceed the applied shear force.
    *   Frictional Resistance = (Number of bolts) × (Pre-tension force per bolt) × (Coefficient of friction) × (Number of friction surfaces)
5.  **Check Bolt Tension Capacity:** The bolts must be able to withstand the pre-tension force and any additional tensile forces from prying or external loads.
6.  **Check Bearing Capacity (often not critical):** While bearing is not the primary mode, it's good practice to check that it doesn't become critical if there's significant relative movement.
7.  **Ensure Adequate Faying Surface Preparation:** Surfaces must be clean and free from obstructions to achieve the design coefficient of friction. Surface treatments (e.g., grit blasting) are often required.

**Important Point to Remember:** The strength of friction connections relies on the pre-tension of the bolts and the friction coefficient. Proper tightening of HSFG bolts is crucial.

### 7. Prying Forces

Prying forces are tensile forces that develop in bolts or plates due to eccentric application of load or the flexibility of connecting elements, particularly in lap joints or connections with unstiffened elements.

#### 7.1 Causes of Prying

*   **Lap Joints:** The offset between the lines of action of the applied load and the fasteners in a lap joint creates a lever arm, leading to prying.
*   **Flexible Connecting Plates:** If the connecting plates are thin and flexible, they can bend under load, causing additional tension in the bolts.
*   **Uneven Bolt Tension:** Non-uniform tightening of bolts can lead to some bolts taking more load than others.

#### 7.2 Effect of Prying Forces

*   **Increased Bolt Tension:** Prying forces add to the direct tensile load on the bolts, potentially causing them to fail in tension.
*   **Reduced Clamp Force (in friction connections):** Prying can reduce the effective clamping force in friction connections, diminishing the frictional resistance.
*   **Overstressing of Plates:** Prying can lead to localized overstressing of the connecting plates.

#### 7.3 Design Considerations for Prying

*   **Minimizing Eccentricity:** Design connections to minimize the eccentricity of applied loads.
*   **Stiffening Connecting Plates:** Use thicker or webbed plates to reduce flexibility.
*   **Using Multiple Bolts:** Distributing the load over more bolts can reduce the effect of prying on individual bolts.
*   **Using Bearing Type Connections:** For lap joints, bearing-type connections are generally preferred over friction-type connections to avoid reduction in clamping force.
*   **Analytical Methods:** Design codes provide methods to calculate the magnitude of prying forces and incorporate them into the bolt design.

**Example:** Consider a lap joint connecting two plates. The applied load is eccentric, creating a bending moment. This moment is resisted by the tension and compression forces in the bolts, which can lead to prying action on the tension bolts.

### 8. Design of Bracket Connection

Bracket connections (or Cleats) are used to transfer loads between a vertical member (like a column) and a horizontal member (like a beam) or to connect members at an angle. They typically consist of plates or angles.

#### 8.1 Types of Bracket Connections

*   **Beam to Column Connections:** Using a bracket plate or angle to connect the flange or web of a beam to the flange or web of a column.
*   **Beam to Beam Connections:** Connecting the webs or flanges of two beams.
*   **Bracing Connections:** Connecting bracing members to main structural elements.

#### 8.2 Load Transfer in Bracket Connections

Bracket connections typically transfer:

*   **Shear Loads:** Primarily transferred by the bolts in shear or welds.
*   **Axial Loads:** Transferred by bolts in tension or welds.
*   **Bending Moments:** These can induce both shear and tension in the bolts or welds. The bracket plate itself acts as a lever arm.

#### 8.3 Design Considerations

*   **Shear Capacity:** Bolts or welds must resist the shear component of the applied load.
*   **Tensile Capacity:** Bolts must resist the tensile component and any prying action induced by bending moments.
*   **Bearing Capacity:** For bolted connections, bearing capacity of plates and bolts is considered.
*   **Plate/Cleat Design:** The bracket plate itself must have adequate strength and stiffness to resist bending and to distribute forces to the fasteners.
*   **Bolt Arrangement:** The spacing and arrangement of bolts are critical for efficient load distribution and to manage prying effects.
*   **Weld Design:** For welded brackets, the size and length of fillet or butt welds are calculated.

#### 8.4 Design Steps (Simplified for Bolted Bracket)

1.  **Analyze Forces:** Determine the shear, axial, and moment components acting on the bracket.
2.  **Select Bolt Type and Size:** Choose appropriate high-strength bolts.
3.  **Design Bolt Group:**
    *   **Shear Design:** Ensure the bolt group has sufficient shear capacity.
    *   **Tension Design (considering prying):** Determine the tensile forces in each bolt due to the moment and axial load, including prying action. Design each bolt for its maximum tensile and shear stress.
4.  **Check Bearing and Net Section of Plates:** Ensure connected plates and the bracket itself have adequate capacity.
5.  **Design the Bracket Plate:** Ensure the plate is adequately sized to transfer forces and resist bending.

**Example:** A bracket connecting a beam to a column. The beam imposes a shear force and a moment at the connection. The bracket plate is bolted to both the beam and the column. The bolts connecting to the column will experience shear and tension, with the tension bolts being critical due to the bending moment and potential prying.

### Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **Bearing-type:** Load is transferred through shear and bearing of the bolt shank against the hole edges in the connected members. **Friction-type:** Load is transferred by frictional resistance between the faying surfaces, generated by the clamping force of the HSFG bolts.
2.  Three potential modes of failure for a single shear connection with four bolts in a line are:
    *   Shear failure of the bolts.
    *   Bearing failure of the connected plates at the bolt holes.
    *   Tensile failure of the connected plates at the net section (if subjected to tension).
3.  **Prying action** is an additional tensile force induced in bolts (or plates) due to the elastic deformation of the connected members, often occurring in lap joints or connections with flexible elements. It is most commonly encountered in **lap joints**.
4.  **Given:**
    *   Shear force ($V$) = 80 kN
    *   Pre-tension force per bolt ($P_t$) = 70 kN
    *   Coefficient of friction ($\mu$) = 0.45
    *   Number of friction surfaces = 1 (for a lap joint)
    *   Frictional resistance per bolt = $P_t \times \mu \times 1 = 70 \text{ kN} \times 0.45 = 31.5 \text{ kN}$
    *   Total frictional resistance required = 80 kN
    *   Minimum number of bolts ($n$) = Total required resistance / Frictional resistance per bolt
    *   $n = 80 \text{ kN} / 31.5 \text{ kN} \approx 2.54$
    *   Therefore, a minimum of **3 bolts** are required.
5.  **Advantages of welded connections:** More rigid and monolithic joint, aesthetically pleasing, potentially stronger, lighter (fewer connection plates). **Disadvantages:** Requires skilled welders, sensitive to weather, difficult to disassemble, can induce residual stresses/distortion, requires thorough inspection.
6.  **Given:**
    *   Shear force ($V$) = 50 kN
    *   Moment ($M$) = 25 kNm
    *   Bolt diameter = 20mm
    *   Bolt spacing = 50mm
    *   Bracket thickness = 10mm (This is the thickness of the bracket itself, not critical for bolt force calculation here, but relevant for plate design).
    *   Assume the shear force acts at the base of the bracket connection to the column. The moment is also applied at the base.
    *   Consider the bolts are arranged in a single vertical row. Let's assume the shear force is vertical and the moment is about the horizontal axis. The bolts are spaced at 50mm intervals.

    Let the bolts be numbered from bottom to top: Bolt 1, Bolt 2, Bolt 3...
    The shear force is directly resisted by all bolts in shear (in a simplified analysis).
    The moment ($M$) creates an additional tension/compression in the bolts. The tension in a bolt at a distance $y$ from the centroid (which is at the base of the bolt group in this simplified case) is given by:
    $T = (M \times y) / (\Sigma y^2)$

    Assuming the shear force acts at the base and the moment is about the horizontal axis, the bolts further away from the neutral axis of bending will experience higher tensile or compressive forces. If we consider a simple scenario where the connection transfers a vertical shear and a moment, and the bolts are in a single vertical line, the distance $y$ will be the distance of each bolt from the axis of rotation (which can be assumed at the base).

    Let's consider two bolts:
    *   Bolt 1: at $y_1 = 0$ (assuming the base is the reference point for simplicity, though realistically it's the centroid of the bolt group).
    *   Bolt 2: at $y_2 = 50$ mm = 0.05 m.
    *   Bolt 3: at $y_3 = 100$ mm = 0.10 m.

    Total Shear Force ($V$) = 50 kN. If we have 'n' bolts, each takes $50/n$ kN in shear.

    The moment $M$ creates tension or compression.
    Let's assume the critical bolt is the furthest one from the axis of rotation.
    Let's consider the axis of rotation to be at the level of the bottom bolt.

    Moment $M = 25$ kNm.
    Let's assume a group of 3 bolts:
    Bolt 1: $y_1 = 0$
    Bolt 2: $y_2 = 0.05$ m
    Bolt 3: $y_3 = 0.10$ m

    $\Sigma y^2 = 0^2 + (0.05)^2 + (0.10)^2 = 0 + 0.0025 + 0.01 = 0.0125 \text{ m}^2$

    Tension/Compression in Bolt 1 ($T_1$) = $(25 \text{ kNm} \times 0 \text{ m}) / 0.0125 \text{ m}^2 = 0 \text{ kN}$
    Tension/Compression in Bolt 2 ($T_2$) = $(25 \text{ kNm} \times 0.05 \text{ m}) / 0.0125 \text{ m}^2 = 100 \text{ kN}$
    Tension/Compression in Bolt 3 ($T_3$) = $(25 \text{ kNm} \times 0.10 \text{ m}) / 0.0125 \text{ m}^2 = 200 \text{ kN}$

    So, Bolt 3 experiences 200 kN tension.
    Bolt 1 experiences 0 kN tension.
    Bolt 2 experiences 100 kN tension.

    The shear force on each bolt is $50 \text{ kN} / 3 = 16.67 \text{ kN}$.

    The maximum tensile force on a bolt is the direct tension plus the tension due to moment.
    If the loads are such that the top bolt is in tension and the bottom is in compression, then:
    Maximum tensile force in Bolt 3 = Shear force per bolt + Tension due to moment
    = 16.67 kN + 200 kN = **216.67 kN**.

    *Note: This is a simplified analysis. A more rigorous approach involves determining the centroid of the bolt group and calculating the moment of inertia of the bolt group.*
