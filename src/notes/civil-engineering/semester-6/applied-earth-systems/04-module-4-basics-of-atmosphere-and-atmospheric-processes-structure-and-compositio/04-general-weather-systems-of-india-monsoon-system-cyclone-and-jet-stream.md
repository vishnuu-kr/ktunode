# APPLIED EARTH SYSTEMS: Module 4 - Basics of Atmosphere and Atmospheric Processes

## Topic: General Weather Systems of India - Monsoon System, Cyclone, and Jet Stream

This module delves into the fundamental atmospheric systems that significantly influence India's weather patterns. We will explore the intricate workings of the monsoon, the destructive power of cyclones, and the elusive jet stream, understanding their formation, characteristics, and impact on the Indian subcontinent.

---

### 1. The Monsoon System of India

The Indian monsoon is a defining feature of the country's climate, responsible for the majority of its annual rainfall and playing a crucial role in agriculture and the economy. It's a complex phenomenon driven by differential heating of land and sea, influenced by several factors.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Monsoon:** A seasonal change in wind direction, typically associated with a seasonal reversal in precipitation. The term originates from the Arabic word "mausim," meaning season.
*   **Differential Heating:** The difference in the rate at which land and water absorb and release heat. Land heats up and cools down faster than water.
*   **Sea Breeze/Land Breeze:** Local winds caused by differential heating. During the day, land heats up faster, creating lower pressure, and cooler air from the sea flows inland (sea breeze). At night, land cools faster, creating higher pressure, and warmer air from the sea flows towards the land (land breeze). The monsoon is essentially a magnified, large-scale version of this effect.
*   **Low Pressure System:** An area where atmospheric pressure is lower than the surrounding areas, leading to rising air, cloud formation, and precipitation.
*   **High Pressure System:** An area where atmospheric pressure is higher than the surrounding areas, leading to sinking air, clear skies, and stable weather.
*   **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ):** A belt of low pressure around the equator where the trade winds of the Northern and Southern Hemispheres converge. It shifts seasonally.

**Types of Indian Monsoons:**

*   **Summer Monsoon (Southwest Monsoon):**
    *   **Timing:** June to September.
    *   **Mechanism:**
        *   **Intensified Heating of the Tibetan Plateau and Indian Landmass:** During summer, the vast Tibetan Plateau and the Indian subcontinent heat up significantly due to intense solar radiation. This creates a deep low-pressure trough over these regions.
        *   **High Pressure over the Indian Ocean:** Simultaneously, the vast Indian Ocean remains relatively cooler, creating a region of high pressure.
        *   **Wind Shift:** This pressure difference drives moist winds from the high-pressure area over the Indian Ocean towards the low-pressure area over the Indian subcontinent. These winds are predominantly from the southwest.
        *   **Arrival:** The monsoon typically arrives on the southern tip of India around June 1st and progresses northwards.
        *   **Orographic Rainfall:** As these moist southwest winds encounter the Western Ghats mountain range, they are forced to rise. As they rise, they cool, condense, and release heavy rainfall on the western slopes of the Ghats.
        *   **Bay of Bengal Branch:** Another branch of the southwest monsoon enters the Bay of Bengal and moves towards the northeastern and eastern parts of India. It also brings heavy rainfall, particularly to regions like the Northeast Himalayas.
        *   **Rainfall Distribution:** The intensity of rainfall varies across India, with the western coast and northeastern states receiving the highest amounts. The interior parts of the country and the leeward side of mountains receive less rain.
    *   **Impact:** Crucial for agriculture ( Kharif crops), replenishing water sources, and impacting river flows.
*   **Winter Monsoon (Northeast Monsoon):**
    *   **Timing:** October to December.
    *   **Mechanism:**
        *   **Cooling of the Tibetan Plateau and Indian Landmass:** During winter, the landmass cools down significantly, creating a high-pressure system.
        *   **Low Pressure over the Indian Ocean:** The Indian Ocean remains relatively warmer, creating a low-pressure area.
        *   **Wind Shift:** Dry, cool winds blow from the high-pressure area over the landmass towards the low-pressure area over the ocean. These winds are from the northeast.
        *   **Rainfall:** While the Northeast monsoon winds are generally dry, they pick up moisture from the Bay of Bengal as they move southeastwards. This results in rainfall primarily along the southeastern coast of India, particularly Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and parts of Karnataka.
    *   **Impact:** Provides vital rainfall for Rabi crops in some southern regions.

**Factors Influencing Monsoon Strength:**

*   **El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO):** A climate pattern that describes the fluctuations in sea surface temperature in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean.
    *   **El Niño:** Warmer sea surface temperatures in the Pacific are generally associated with weaker monsoons in India (deficient rainfall).
    *   **La Niña:** Cooler sea surface temperatures in the Pacific are generally associated with stronger monsoons in India (excess rainfall).
*   **Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD):** A phenomenon of the sea-surface temperatures in the western and eastern Indian Oceans.
    *   **Positive IOD:** Cooler western Indian Ocean and warmer eastern Indian Ocean. Often associated with stronger monsoons in India.
    *   **Negative IOD:** Warmer western Indian Ocean and cooler eastern Indian Ocean. Can weaken the monsoon.
*   **Jet Streams:** Mentioned later, but their position and strength can influence monsoon dynamics.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The Indian monsoon is a **seasonal wind reversal**, not just a rainy period.
*   Differential heating of land and sea is the **fundamental driving force**.
*   The **Tibetan Plateau** plays a crucial role in intensifying the low-pressure system during summer.
*   The **Western Ghats** cause significant orographic rainfall.
*   **ENSO and IOD** are major teleconnections affecting monsoon variability.

### 2. Cyclones in India

Tropical cyclones are powerful, rotating storms characterized by low-pressure centers, strong winds, and heavy rainfall. India's long coastline is susceptible to cyclones originating in the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Tropical Cyclone:** A rotating storm system characterized by a low-pressure center, strong winds, and a spiral arrangement of thunderstorms that produce heavy rain.
*   **Eye:** The calm, clear area at the center of a tropical cyclone.
*   **Eyewall:** The ring of intense thunderstorms surrounding the eye, where the strongest winds and heaviest rainfall occur.
*   **Rainbands:** Bands of thunderstorms spiraling outwards from the eyewall.
*   **Storm Surge:** An abnormal rise of sea level accompanying a tropical cyclone, caused by the surge of wind and water toward the shore as the cyclone moves inland.
*   **Tropical Depression:** A disorganized collection of thunderstorms that has not yet developed a circulation.
*   **Tropical Storm:** A tropical depression that has organized into a defined circulation with sustained winds of 39 mph (63 km/h) or higher.
*   **Hurricane/Typhoon/Cyclone:** Terms for tropical storms that reach sustained wind speeds of 74 mph (119 km/h) or higher. The term "cyclone" is used in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.

**Formation of Tropical Cyclones:**

1.  **Warm Ocean Waters:** Require sea surface temperatures of at least 26.5°C (80°F) extending down to a depth of at least 50 meters. This provides the energy (latent heat released during condensation) for the storm.
2.  **Low Vertical Wind Shear:** Minimal change in wind speed and direction with height. High wind shear can disrupt the storm's structure.
3.  **Pre-existing Disturbance:** Often a tropical wave or a cluster of thunderstorms.
4.  **Coriolis Effect:** The Earth's rotation is necessary to initiate rotation. This is why cyclones do not form within 5 degrees of the equator.

**Characteristics of Indian Cyclones:**

*   **Origin:** Mostly form in the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea, which are warm bodies of water conducive to cyclone development.
*   **Timing:** Most frequent during the pre-monsoon (April-May) and post-monsoon (October-November) seasons.
*   **Movement:** Generally move from east to west or northwestward, often making landfall on India's east coast. Cyclones in the Arabian Sea are less frequent but can be intense.
*   **Impacts:**
    *   **High Winds:** Can cause widespread destruction of buildings, infrastructure, and vegetation.
    *   **Heavy Rainfall:** Can lead to severe flooding and landslides.
    *   **Storm Surge:** The most devastating impact, causing coastal inundation, destruction, and loss of life.
    *   **Landslides:** In hilly regions, heavy rainfall can trigger landslides.

**Example:** The Odisha Super Cyclone (1999) was one of the most devastating cyclones to hit India, causing immense damage and loss of life. Cyclone Fani (2019) and Cyclone Amphan (2020) are more recent examples of powerful cyclones impacting the Bay of Bengal region.

**Mitigation and Preparedness:**

*   **Early Warning Systems:** Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) plays a crucial role in tracking cyclones and issuing timely warnings.
*   **Evacuation Plans:** Effective evacuation procedures in coastal areas.
*   **Cyclone Shelters:** Building reinforced shelters to protect populations.
*   **Coastal Afforestation:** Planting trees along the coast to act as windbreaks and reduce storm surge impact.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Cyclones require **warm ocean waters** and **low wind shear** to form.
*   The **Bay of Bengal** is more prone to cyclones than the Arabian Sea.
*   **Storm surge** is often the deadliest aspect of a cyclone.
*   **Early warning and preparedness** are crucial for minimizing damage.

### 3. Jet Streams and Their Influence on India

Jet streams are fast-flowing, narrow air currents found in the atmosphere, typically at high altitudes. While not directly "weather systems" in the same way as monsoons or cyclones, their presence and behavior significantly influence large-scale weather patterns, including the Indian monsoon.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Jet Stream:** A high-altitude, fast-flowing current of air that moves in a generally west-to-east direction.
*   **Polar Front Jet Stream:** Forms at the boundary between cold polar air and warmer temperate air.
*   **Subtropical Jet Stream:** Forms at the boundary between warm tropical air and cooler temperate air.
*   **Rossby Waves:** Large-scale undulations in the jet stream that are influenced by the Earth's rotation and topography.
*   **Latent Heat:** Heat released when water vapor condenses into liquid water or ice.
*   **Adiabatic Cooling:** The cooling of air as it rises and expands.

**Types of Jet Streams Relevant to India:**

*   **The Subtropical Westerly Jet Stream:**
    *   **Location:** Flows roughly along the southern edge of the Himalayas at altitudes of 9-13 km.
    *   **Summer:** During summer, the intense heating of the Tibetan Plateau and the surrounding landmass causes this jet stream to shift northward, often moving over the plateau itself. This northward shift is crucial for the onset of the Indian Summer Monsoon.
        *   **Mechanism:** The northward shift of the subtropical westerly jet away from the Indian subcontinent is associated with the development of the low-pressure trough over India. The rising air over the plateau and the subsequent southward flow of moisture-laden winds from the high-pressure areas over the Indian Ocean are facilitated by this northward shift.
    *   **Winter:** During winter, this jet stream is found further south, influencing weather patterns over peninsular India.
*   **The Tropical Easterly Jet Stream (TEJ):**
    *   **Location:** Flows from east to west over the Indian subcontinent at altitudes of about 12-15 km, typically during the summer monsoon season.
    *   **Formation:** It is believed to be formed by the strong upward motion and heating over the Tibetan Plateau and its associated release of latent heat as water vapor condenses at high altitudes.
    *   **Role in Monsoon:** The TEJ is a significant feature associated with the active monsoon phase. It is thought to help in the northward transport of moisture and the maintenance of the monsoon circulation. The divergence of air at the upper levels associated with the TEJ supports rising air at lower levels, which is essential for heavy rainfall.

**Influence on Indian Weather:**

*   **Monsoon Onset and Strength:** The northward shift of the Subtropical Westerly Jet is a key indicator for the arrival of the summer monsoon. The strength and position of both the Westerly and Easterly jets can influence the intensity and distribution of monsoon rainfall.
*   **Western Disturbances:** In winter, the movement of the Subtropical Westerly Jet further south brings Western Disturbances (extra-tropical storms moving from the west) across northwestern India, causing unseasonal rains and occasional snowfall in the plains and mountains, respectively.
*   **Heat Waves and Cold Waves:** The wavy pattern of the jet stream (Rossby waves) can lead to prolonged periods of abnormal temperatures. A southward dip in the jet can bring cold air to lower latitudes, causing cold waves, while a northward bulge can bring warm air, contributing to heat waves.

**Example:** A delayed northward shift of the Subtropical Westerly Jet in a particular year might correlate with a delayed onset and weaker summer monsoon rainfall in India.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Jet streams are **high-altitude wind currents**.
*   The **Subtropical Westerly Jet** shifts northward over the Tibetan Plateau in summer, aiding monsoon onset.
*   The **Tropical Easterly Jet** is a characteristic feature of the active summer monsoon.
*   Jet stream patterns can influence **monsoon strength, Western Disturbances, and extreme temperature events**.

### Practice Questions & Exercises

**Question 1:**
Explain the primary mechanism behind the formation of the Indian Summer Monsoon, highlighting the roles of differential heating and pressure systems.

**Answer:**
The Indian Summer Monsoon is driven by the differential heating of the land and sea. During summer, the Indian subcontinent and the Tibetan Plateau heat up significantly, creating a deep low-pressure trough over them. Simultaneously, the Indian Ocean remains relatively cooler, establishing a high-pressure area. This pressure gradient forces moist winds from the Indian Ocean (blowing from the southwest) to move towards the low-pressure areas over India. As these moist winds encounter the Western Ghats, they are forced to rise, cool, condense, and release heavy rainfall on the western slopes, marking the arrival of the monsoon.

**Question 2:**
What is a storm surge, and why is it considered a particularly dangerous aspect of tropical cyclones?

**Answer:**
A storm surge is an abnormal rise of sea level accompanying a tropical cyclone, caused by the surge of wind and water toward the shore as the cyclone moves inland. It is considered particularly dangerous because it can inundate coastal areas, causing widespread destruction of infrastructure, loss of life, and severe erosion. The rising water levels can penetrate far inland, overwhelming coastal defenses and making escape difficult.

**Question 3:**
Describe the role of the Subtropical Westerly Jet Stream in the onset of the Indian Summer Monsoon.

**Answer:**
The Subtropical Westerly Jet Stream plays a crucial role in the onset of the Indian Summer Monsoon. During summer, the intense heating of the Tibetan Plateau causes this jet stream to shift northward, moving over the plateau itself. This northward shift is associated with the weakening of the westerly flow over India and the establishment of a strong low-pressure system over the subcontinent. This condition is favorable for the establishment of the moist Southwest Monsoon winds flowing from the Indian Ocean.

**Question 4:**
Differentiate between the Summer Monsoon and the Winter Monsoon in India in terms of wind direction and the regions most affected by rainfall.

**Answer:**
*   **Summer Monsoon (Southwest Monsoon):** Winds blow from the southwest, bringing heavy rainfall to most of India, with the western coast and northeastern states receiving the highest amounts.
*   **Winter Monsoon (Northeast Monsoon):** Winds blow from the northeast, but rainfall is primarily concentrated along the southeastern coast of India (Tamil Nadu, Kerala) as these winds pick up moisture from the Bay of Bengal.

**Question 5:**
Which of the following is **NOT** a necessary condition for the formation of a tropical cyclone?
a) Warm ocean waters (at least 26.5°C)
b) Low vertical wind shear
c) The Coriolis effect
d) Strong prevailing winds from the east

**Answer:**
d) Strong prevailing winds from the east

**Explanation:** While tropical cyclones are characterized by rotating winds, a strong prevailing wind from a specific direction is not a prerequisite for their formation. In fact, low vertical wind shear is crucial, meaning wind speeds and directions should not change significantly with height, which would disrupt the storm's structure.
