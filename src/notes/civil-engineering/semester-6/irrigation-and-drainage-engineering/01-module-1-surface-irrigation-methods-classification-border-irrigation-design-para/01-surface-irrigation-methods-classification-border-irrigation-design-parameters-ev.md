# IRRIGATION AND DRAINAGE ENGINEERING

## Module 1: Surface Irrigation Methods: Classification – Border Irrigation: Design Parameters, Evaluation, and Ideal Wetting Pattern

---

### 1. Introduction to Surface Irrigation Methods

Surface irrigation is the oldest and most widely used method of irrigation, where water is applied and distributed over the soil surface by gravity.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Irrigation:** The artificial application of water to land or soil to assist in growing agricultural crops, maintain landscapes, and revegetate disturbed soils in dry areas and during periods of insufficient rainfall.
*   **Surface Irrigation:** A method where water flows over the land surface by gravity.
*   **Advantages of Surface Irrigation:**
    *   Low energy requirement (gravity-powered).
    *   Relatively low initial cost for simpler systems.
    *   Adaptable to various field sizes and shapes.
    *   Can handle saline water effectively in some cases.
*   **Disadvantages of Surface Irrigation:**
    *   Lower water application efficiency compared to pressurized systems.
    *   Requires land leveling, which can be costly.
    *   Susceptible to runoff and deep percolation losses.
    *   Can lead to waterlogging and soil salinization if not managed properly.
    *   Requires skilled labor for operation and maintenance.

---

### 2. Classification of Surface Irrigation Methods

Surface irrigation methods can be broadly classified based on the way water is applied to the field.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Gravity Flow:** All surface irrigation methods rely on gravity for water distribution.
*   **Main Types of Surface Irrigation:**
    *   **Basin Irrigation:** The field is divided into small, level plots surrounded by dikes or bunds. Water is applied to fill the basin to a shallow depth.
        *   *Examples:* Square basins, rectangular basins, contour basins.
    *   **Border Irrigation (or Border Strip Irrigation):** The field is divided into long, narrow strips called borders, separated by dikes or ridges. Water is applied to the top end of each border and flows down the length of the strip.
        *   *Examples:* Wide borders, narrow borders.
    *   **Furrow Irrigation:** The field is divided into parallel channels called furrows, separated by small ridges. Water is applied to the head of each furrow and flows down it, wetting the soil on either side.
        *   *Examples:* Straight furrows, contour furrows, bedding furrows.

---

### 3. Border Irrigation: Detailed Study

Border irrigation is a method where the land is divided into long, narrow, and parallel strips called "borders." Water is introduced at the upper end of each border and flows down the slope by gravity, spreading laterally to wet the entire border strip.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Border Strip:** A long, narrow strip of land, usually rectangular, bounded on the sides by low earth ridges (dykes or bunds).
*   **Purpose of Borders:** To confine the irrigation water and direct its flow along the length of the strip.
*   **Advantages of Border Irrigation:**
    *   Relatively simple to design and construct.
    *   Can be adapted to medium to large farms.
    *   Can achieve good uniformity if designed and operated correctly.
    *   Less labor-intensive than basin irrigation for larger areas.
    *   Can be efficiently used with sprinkler irrigation for initial wetting or supplemental irrigation.
*   **Disadvantages of Border Irrigation:**
    *   Requires relatively uniform field slope.
    *   Dikes occupy land area, reducing the irrigable area.
    *   Water management (flow rate, advance time) is crucial for uniformity.
    *   Can have significant tailwater losses if not managed properly.

### 4. Border Irrigation: Design Parameters

Designing a border irrigation system involves determining several key parameters to ensure efficient and uniform water application.

**Key Parameters and their Significance:**

*   **Border Width (W):** The distance between the centers of adjacent dikes.
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Economic:** Wider borders reduce the cost of dikes per unit area but may require higher flow rates and longer advance times.
        *   **Cropping Pattern:** Width should be suitable for cultivation equipment. Typical widths range from 7 to 20 meters.
        *   **Soil Type:** Wider borders may be suitable for fine-textured soils where water spreads laterally more easily.
    *   **Formula/Guideline:** Generally, 10-20 times the furrow spacing if converting from furrow to border.

*   **Border Length (L):** The length of the border strip from the water source to the end of the field.
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Slope:** Longer borders are possible on steeper slopes.
        *   **Flow Rate:** Higher flow rates can be used on longer borders.
        *   **Water Application Efficiency:** Very long borders can lead to significant differences in water depth between the head and tail ends, reducing uniformity.
        *   **Topography:** Limited by field boundaries and topography.
        *   **Typical Lengths:** 50 to 200 meters, but can be longer.

*   **Border Slope (S):** The longitudinal slope of the border strip.
    *   **Longitudinal Slope:** The slope along the length of the border.
        *   **Importance:** Affects the speed of water advance and the required flow rate.
        *   **Ideal Range:** Generally 0.0005 to 0.005 (0.05% to 0.5%).
        *   **Too Steep:** Can cause erosion and reduce infiltration time at the head end.
        *   **Too Flat:** Can lead to slow advance and ponding.
    *   **Cross Slope:** The slope across the width of the border.
        *   **Importance:** Crucial for ensuring water spreads evenly across the width.
        *   **Ideal Range:** Generally 0.0005 to 0.002 (0.05% to 0.2%).
        *   **Too Steep:** Water will concentrate on one side, leading to non-uniform wetting.
        *   **Too Flat:** Water may not spread effectively, and pooling can occur.

*   **Flow Rate per Border (Q):** The volume of water supplied to the head of each border per unit time.
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Soil Infiltration Characteristics:** Higher flow rates are needed for soils with high infiltration rates.
        *   **Border Width and Length:** Affects the required flow rate to achieve a desired advance time.
        *   **Water Source Capacity:** The available supply from the canal or pump.
        *   **Erosion Potential:** Flow rate should not exceed the soil's erosion resistance.
    *   **Common Formula (Empirical):** Various empirical formulas exist, often relating flow rate to border width and slope. A common one relates to the Manning's equation for open channel flow, but with adaptations for the spreading nature of flow in borders.
        *   A simplified approach is often to use guidelines based on flow per unit width. For example, 0.005 to 0.01 m³/s per meter of border width.

*   **Irrigation Depth (d):** The total amount of water to be applied to the soil to meet the crop's water requirement.
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Crop Water Requirement:** Determined by evapotranspiration (ETc) and rainfall.
        *   **Root Zone Depth:** The depth of soil from which the crop extracts water.
        *   **Irrigation Efficiency:** The required application depth is higher than the net depth needed in the root zone to account for losses.
    *   **Calculation:** $d = (ETc - Rainfall) / Efficiency$ (where $ETc$ is crop evapotranspiration)

*   **Advance Time ($T_a$):** The time it takes for the water to reach the far end of the border.
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Border Length, Slope, and Flow Rate:** Higher flow rates and steeper slopes reduce advance time.
        *   **Soil Infiltration:** Higher infiltration rates increase advance time.
        *   **Water Application Efficiency:** Advance time is a key factor in achieving uniform application.
    *   **Determination:** Can be estimated using empirical equations or measured in the field.

*   **Soaking Time ($T_s$):** The time the water is allowed to flow at the head of the border after the water has reached the far end.
    *   **Importance:** Crucial for allowing water to infiltrate and wet the soil profile to the desired depth.
    *   **Relationship with Advance Time:** Total irrigation time = Advance Time + Soaking Time.
    *   **Determination:** Often determined by allowing water to flow until the soil is adequately wetted at the tail end.

### 5. Border Irrigation: Evaluation

Evaluating the performance of a border irrigation system is essential to identify inefficiencies and make improvements.

**Key Evaluation Techniques and Metrics:**

*   **Water Application Efficiency ($E_a$):** The ratio of the water beneficially used by the crop (stored in the root zone) to the total amount of water applied to the border.
    *   **Formula:** $E_a = \frac{Water\ stored\ in\ root\ zone}{Water\ applied\ to\ border} \times 100\%$
    *   **Factors Affecting $E_a$:** Runoff, deep percolation, evaporation, non-uniform distribution.

*   **Distribution Uniformity ($DU$):** A measure of how uniformly water is distributed across the irrigated area.
    *   **Methods of Measurement:**
        *   **Depth-Area Samplers:** Collecting water in small containers placed across the border.
        *   **Soil Moisture Measurements:** Measuring soil moisture content at various points before and after irrigation.
        *   **Advance/Recession Rates:** Analyzing water flow patterns.
    *   **Calculation (Common Method - Low Quarter Method):**
        $DU = \frac{Average\ of\ lowest\ 25\%\ of\ measurements}{Average\ of\ all\ measurements} \times 100\%$
        *   *Alternatively:* $DU = \frac{Lowest\ average\ reading}{Average\ of\ all\ readings} \times 100\%$ (where readings are typically taken at different locations along the border width and length).

*   **Application Uniformity ($AU$):** Another measure of uniformity, often calculated based on the difference between maximum and minimum water depths.
    *   **Formula:** $AU = \frac{Average\ depth - Absolute\ value\ of\ difference\ between\ max\ and\ min\ depth}{Average\ depth} \times 100\%$
    *   *Note: DU is more commonly used in practice for surface irrigation.*

*   **Tailwater Runoff:** The amount of water that flows off the end of the border without infiltrating.
    *   **Impact:** Reduces water application efficiency and can cause erosion or waterlogging downstream.
    *   **Management:** Can be reduced by cutting off the inflow at the appropriate time or by recycling runoff.

*   **Deep Percolation:** Water that infiltrates below the root zone.
    *   **Impact:** Wastes water and nutrients, can lead to groundwater contamination.
    *   **Management:** Proper irrigation scheduling and design are crucial.

**Typical Performance Indicators:**

*   Good DU: 80-90%
*   Good Ea: 60-85%

### 6. Border Irrigation: Ideal Wetting Pattern

The ideal wetting pattern in border irrigation aims for uniform water distribution across the entire border strip, ensuring all parts of the crop root zone receive the required amount of water.

**Characteristics of an Ideal Wetting Pattern:**

*   **Uniform Depth:** Water depth is uniform across the entire width and length of the border at the end of irrigation.
*   **No Ponding:** Water does not accumulate and form large ponds.
*   **No Dry Spots:** All areas within the border are adequately wetted.
*   **Controlled Runoff:** Minimal runoff from the tail end.
*   **Limited Deep Percolation:** Water infiltrates to the desired depth without excessive deep percolation.

**Visual Representation:**

Imagine a cross-section of the border strip. The ideal wetting pattern would look like a uniform rectangular block of wetted soil extending to the required root zone depth. In a longitudinal view, the water level would be consistently spread across the border.

**Factors Influencing the Wetting Pattern:**

*   **Border Width and Length:** Affects the flow path and spreading.
*   **Border Slope (Longitudinal and Cross):** Critical for flow speed and lateral distribution.
*   **Flow Rate:** Influences advance rate and wetting front progression.
*   **Infiltration Rate:** Soil properties dictate how quickly water enters the soil.
*   **Advance and Recession Characteristics:** The timing of water movement is key.
*   **Irrigation Duration:** The total time water is applied.

**Deviations from Ideal:**

*   **Head End Saturation, Tail End Dryness:** Common if the border is too long, slope is insufficient, or inflow is too low.
*   **Side Saturation, Center Dryness (or vice versa):** Indicates poor cross-slope.
*   **Excessive Runoff:** If irrigation duration is too long.
*   **Excessive Deep Percolation:** If irrigation duration is too long or infiltration is too high.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define Border Irrigation and list its two main advantages.

**Answer:**
Border Irrigation is a surface irrigation method where the land is divided into long, narrow strips called borders, separated by dikes. Water is applied to the head of each border and flows down the length by gravity.
**Advantages:**
1.  Relatively simple to design and construct.
2.  Can achieve good uniformity if designed and operated correctly.

**Question 2:**
A border strip is 15 meters wide and 150 meters long with a longitudinal slope of 0.002. If the required irrigation depth is 75 mm and the application efficiency is estimated to be 70%, what is the volume of water that needs to be applied to this border?

**Answer:**
*   Border Width (W) = 15 m
*   Border Length (L) = 150 m
*   Required Irrigation Depth (net) = 75 mm = 0.075 m
*   Application Efficiency ($E_a$) = 70% = 0.70

The area of the border strip is:
Area = Width × Length = 15 m × 150 m = 2250 m²

The volume of water required in the root zone (net application) is:
Net Volume = Area × Net Depth = 2250 m² × 0.075 m = 168.75 m³

The gross volume of water to be applied ($V_{gross}$) to achieve this net volume with 70% efficiency is:
$E_a = \frac{Net\ Volume}{Gross\ Volume}$
$Gross\ Volume = \frac{Net\ Volume}{E_a} = \frac{168.75\ m³}{0.70} \approx 241.07\ m³$

Therefore, approximately 241.07 m³ of water needs to be applied to this border.

**Question 3:**
What is the most critical factor for ensuring uniform water distribution across the *width* of a border strip?

**Answer:**
The cross slope of the border strip is the most critical factor for ensuring uniform water distribution across the width. An ideal cross slope (typically 0.05% to 0.2%) allows water to spread evenly from the center to the edges of the border.

**Question 4:**
Explain the concept of Distribution Uniformity (DU) in the context of border irrigation and state a typical desirable range.

**Answer:**
Distribution Uniformity (DU) is a measure of how uniformly water is distributed across the irrigated area of a border strip. It quantifies the degree to which the lowest amounts of water applied are close to the average amount applied. A high DU means that most of the border strip receives a similar amount of water, reducing the risk of under-irrigation in some areas and over-irrigation in others.
A typical desirable range for DU in border irrigation is **80-90%**.

**Question 5:**
Consider a border irrigation system with a border length of 100m. If the flow rate at the head is high and the infiltration rate is low, what would be the expected wetting pattern, and what are the potential consequences?

**Answer:**
*   **Expected Wetting Pattern:**
    *   **Advance:** Water will reach the tail end relatively quickly.
    *   **Distribution:** Water will likely be concentrated at the head end and along the flow path, with potentially less water reaching the far end and sides if the flow does not spread adequately.
    *   **Runoff:** Due to rapid advance and potentially insufficient infiltration time at the head end, significant tailwater runoff is likely.
    *   **Dry Spots:** Areas at the tail end or edges might remain relatively dry if the flow front slows down or stops before reaching them adequately.

*   **Potential Consequences:**
    *   **Low Application Efficiency ($E_a$):** High tailwater runoff leads to significant water loss.
    *   **Poor Distribution Uniformity ($DU$):** Significant variations in water depth across the border.
    *   **Erosion:** High flow rates on a slope can cause soil erosion, especially at the head end.
    *   **Under-irrigation:** In the dry spots, crops may suffer from water stress.
    *   **Over-irrigation:** At the head end, excessive water application can lead to waterlogging and nutrient leaching.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Slope is King:** Both longitudinal and cross slopes are critical for successful border irrigation. Incorrect slopes lead to poor uniformity and efficiency.
*   **Flow Rate Management:** The inflow rate to the border directly impacts advance time and wetting pattern. It must be matched to border dimensions and soil infiltration characteristics.
*   **Irrigation Duration:** The total time water is allowed to flow is crucial. It should be long enough to wet the soil to the required depth at the tail end but short enough to minimize tailwater runoff.
*   **Evaluation is Key:** Regularly evaluate your border system's performance using metrics like DU and Ea to identify areas for improvement.
*   **Tailwater Management:** Implement strategies like runoff recycling or adjusting cutoff time to improve efficiency.
*   **Land Leveling:** While border irrigation is less sensitive than basin irrigation, some degree of land leveling is still necessary for optimal performance.
*   **Soil Texture:** The infiltration characteristics of the soil significantly influence design parameters like flow rate and duration. Fine-textured soils (clays) infiltrate slower, requiring lower flow rates and longer soaking times compared to coarse-textured soils (sands).
