# Irrigation and Drainage Engineering: Module 1 - Surface Irrigation Methods

## Topic: Basin Irrigation

### Learning Outcomes:

*   Understand the classification of surface irrigation methods.
*   Identify and describe different types of basin irrigation.
*   Explain the concept of an ideal wetting pattern in basin irrigation.
*   Discuss the factors influencing the shapes and sizes of basins.

---

### 1. Introduction to Surface Irrigation Methods

Surface irrigation is the oldest and most widely used method of irrigation. Water is applied to the soil surface and spreads over the land by gravity. It relies on the natural topography of the land to distribute water.

#### Classification of Surface Irrigation Methods:

Surface irrigation methods can be broadly classified based on how water is conveyed and distributed on the field.

*   **Basin Irrigation:** The field is divided into small, level plots surrounded by levees or bunds. Water is applied to fill these basins.
*   **Border Irrigation:** The field is divided into long, narrow strips (borders) separated by levees. Water is introduced at the upper end of each border and flows down its length.
*   **Furrow Irrigation:** Parallel channels (furrows) are constructed between crop rows. Water is introduced into the furrows and infiltrates into the root zone of the plants.

---

### 2. Basin Irrigation

Basin irrigation is a method where the entire area to be irrigated is enclosed by low earthen bunds or levees. Water is admitted into the basin, and it fills the entire area to a shallow depth, remaining on the surface until it infiltrates into the soil.

#### 2.1. Types of Basins:

The classification of basins is primarily based on the land's topography and how water is managed within them.

*   **Level Basins:**
    *   **Description:** These are the most efficient type of basin where the land surface within the basin is perfectly leveled.
    *   **Advantages:** Uniform water distribution, high application efficiency, minimal runoff.
    *   **Disadvantages:** High cost of land leveling, especially for large areas or undulating terrain.
    *   **Example:** Small vegetable gardens, orchards, or fields with naturally flat topography.

*   **Sloping Basins (Graded Basins):**
    *   **Description:** These basins are designed with a gentle slope along the direction of water flow to facilitate drainage and uniform filling. The slope is usually within acceptable limits (typically 0.2% to 0.5%) to avoid erosion.
    *   **Advantages:** Requires less intensive leveling compared to level basins, can adapt to mildly undulating land.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Potential for uneven distribution if the slope is too steep or inconsistent, risk of erosion.
    *   **Example:** Areas with naturally gentle slopes where complete leveling is uneconomical.

*   **Contour Basins:**
    *   **Description:** Basins constructed along contour lines, particularly useful in hilly or mountainous areas. The bunds follow the natural contour of the land, creating relatively flat areas.
    *   **Advantages:** Minimizes earthwork and reduces erosion in steep areas.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Irregular shapes and sizes are common, leading to potentially uneven water distribution if not carefully designed.
    *   **Example:** Terraced fields in hilly regions.

*   **Ring Basins (Circular Basins):**
    *   **Description:** Typically used for irrigating individual trees (e.g., in orchards). A circular bund is formed around the tree, with the basin being leveled or gently sloped towards the tree trunk.
    *   **Advantages:** Concentrates water around the tree roots, minimizes water loss from evaporation and deep percolation away from the root zone.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Labor-intensive for large orchards.
    *   **Example:** Irrigating fruit trees like mangoes, citrus, or olives.

*   **Contour Furrow Basins:**
    *   **Description:** A hybrid approach where contour furrows are made within a larger basin area, often used for sloping lands. The furrows help in controlling water flow and preventing excessive velocity.
    *   **Advantages:** Combines benefits of contour cultivation and basin irrigation.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires more design and construction effort.
    *   **Example:** Intermittent irrigation on sloping terrains.

#### 2.2. Ideal Wetting Pattern in Basin Irrigation:

The goal of basin irrigation is to achieve uniform infiltration of water throughout the entire soil profile within the basin, down to the desired root zone depth.

*   **Characteristics of an Ideal Wetting Pattern:**
    *   **Uniform Depth:** The water depth across the basin should be as uniform as possible at the end of the irrigation event.
    *   **Complete Coverage:** The entire surface area of the basin should be wetted.
    *   **Target Depth:** Water should infiltrate to the desired root zone depth, meeting the crop's water requirement without excessive deep percolation.
    *   **No Ponding (Excessive):** While water is applied to the surface, prolonged and excessive ponding can lead to anaerobic conditions and waterlogging, especially for sensitive crops. The depth and duration should be managed to allow infiltration.
    *   **Minimal Runoff:** Ideally, all applied water should infiltrate into the soil.
    *   **No Erosion:** Water velocity should be low enough to prevent soil erosion.

*   **Factors Influencing Wetting Pattern:**
    *   **Basin Levelness:** The most crucial factor. Any undulation or slope will lead to uneven water depth and infiltration.
    *   **Soil Type:** Coarser soils (sandy) infiltrate water faster, requiring shorter irrigation durations or higher application depths. Finer soils (clay) infiltrate slowly, requiring longer durations and lower application depths.
    *   **Water Application Rate:** The rate at which water is supplied to the basin.
    *   **Basin Geometry:** Shape and size of the basin.
    *   **Infiltration Rate:** The rate at which water enters the soil.
    *   **Duration of Irrigation:** The time water is allowed to flow into the basin.

#### 2.3. Shapes and Size of Basins:

The shape and size of basins are determined by various factors to optimize water distribution and minimize management effort.

*   **Factors Influencing Shape and Size:**
    *   **Topography:** Leveling costs are a major consideration. For very irregular terrain, smaller, irregularly shaped basins might be more practical.
    *   **Crop Type:** Some crops require specific root zone depths and sensitivity to waterlogging influences basin design.
    *   **Soil Type:** Soil texture affects infiltration rates and the required wetting pattern.
    *   **Water Supply:** The available flow rate of irrigation water influences how quickly a basin can be filled.
    *   **Labor Availability:** The ease of construction, maintenance, and management by available labor.
    *   **Irrigation Equipment:** Size and type of water distribution structures (e.g., gates, siphons).

*   **Common Shapes:**
    *   **Rectangular:** Most common for level basins, allowing for efficient layout and water distribution.
    *   **Square:** Also common for level basins, offering simplicity in layout.
    *   **Irregular (Polygonal):** Often used in contour basins or where topography dictates, requiring careful management.
    *   **Circular (Ring Basins):** Specifically for individual tree irrigation.

*   **Size Considerations:**
    *   **Small Basins (e.g., 1m x 1m to 10m x 10m):**
        *   **Advantages:** Easier to level, easier to manage, very uniform wetting, suitable for high-value crops or areas with limited water.
        *   **Disadvantages:** High labor and construction cost per unit area, higher proportion of land used for bunds.
        *   **Typical Use:** Vegetable crops, orchards, rice paddies.
    *   **Large Basins (e.g., 1 hectare or more):**
        *   **Advantages:** Lower labor and construction cost per unit area, efficient for large-scale farming.
        *   **Disadvantages:** More difficult and expensive to level uniformly, potential for uneven wetting if not perfectly leveled, higher risk of runoff and erosion on slopes.
        *   **Typical Use:** Large-scale grain cultivation, pastures.

**General Rule:** Basin size should be manageable for the farmer, considering leveling costs, water management, and labor. Smaller basins are generally more efficient in terms of uniformity but more costly.

### Practice Questions and Answers:

**Question 1:** What are the primary advantages of using level basins for irrigation?
**Answer:** The primary advantages of level basins are uniform water distribution, high application efficiency, and minimal runoff, leading to efficient water use and optimal crop growth.

**Question 2:** Describe a situation where contour basins would be the most suitable basin irrigation method.
**Answer:** Contour basins are most suitable in hilly or mountainous terrain where the land has significant slopes. By following the contour lines, they minimize earthwork for bund construction and help prevent soil erosion compared to attempting to create level basins.

**Question 3:** What is the main objective of an ideal wetting pattern in basin irrigation, and what factors contribute to achieving it?
**Answer:** The main objective of an ideal wetting pattern is to achieve uniform infiltration of water throughout the entire root zone depth across the entire basin area. Factors contributing to this include a perfectly leveled basin surface, appropriate soil type, correct water application rate, and a basin size that is manageable for uniform filling and infiltration.

**Question 4:** You are designing basin irrigation for a sandy loam soil. How would this soil type influence your choice of basin size compared to a clay soil, assuming all other factors are equal?
**Answer:** For sandy loam soil, which has a higher infiltration rate, you might opt for slightly smaller basins or ensure a faster application rate. This is because water will infiltrate more quickly, and larger basins could lead to water not reaching the far end uniformly before significant infiltration occurs at the inlet end, or the water depth might not be maintained for long enough for adequate wetting. Clay soils, with their lower infiltration rates, can generally accommodate larger basins, as water infiltrates slowly, allowing more time for uniform distribution across a larger area.

**Question 5:** What are the potential disadvantages of very large basins, even if the land is reasonably level?
**Answer:** Potential disadvantages of very large basins include:
*   **Difficulty in achieving uniform leveling:** Small imperfections in leveling become magnified over a large area, leading to uneven water distribution.
*   **Longer filling times:** This can lead to excessive infiltration at the inlet end before the entire basin is covered, or requires a high flow rate that might cause erosion.
*   **Higher risk of runoff:** If the application rate is not matched with infiltration and basin capacity, runoff can occur from the lower parts of the basin.
*   **Management challenges:** Maneuvering equipment and managing water distribution across very large, undivided areas can be difficult.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Leveling is Key:** The success of basin irrigation heavily relies on the levelness of the basin. Any deviation from levelness directly impacts water distribution uniformity.
*   **Soil Properties Matter:** Understand your soil's infiltration rate and water-holding capacity to determine appropriate basin size, irrigation duration, and water depth.
*   **Trade-off between Efficiency and Cost:** Smaller basins offer higher uniformity and efficiency but come with higher construction and labor costs. Larger basins are more economical per unit area but require more careful design and management for uniformity.
*   **Contour Basins for Slopes:** Always consider contour basins for undulating or sloped terrain to manage water flow and prevent erosion.
*   **Ring Basins for Individual Plants:** Ring basins are specialized and highly effective for water management around individual trees or plants, especially in orchards.
*   **Water Management is Crucial:** Beyond design, proper water management during irrigation – controlling the inflow rate and duration – is essential to achieve the desired wetting pattern.
