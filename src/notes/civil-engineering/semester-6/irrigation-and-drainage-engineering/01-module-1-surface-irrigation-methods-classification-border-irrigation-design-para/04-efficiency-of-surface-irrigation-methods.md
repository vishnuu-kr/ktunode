# Irrigation and Drainage Engineering: Module 1 - Surface Irrigation Methods

## Topic: Efficiency of Surface Irrigation Methods

This module focuses on understanding the efficiency of various surface irrigation methods, with a particular emphasis on border irrigation. We will delve into the design parameters, evaluation techniques, and the concept of an ideal wetting pattern for border irrigation.

Upon completion of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Define and explain the concept of irrigation efficiency** in the context of surface irrigation.
*   **Identify and differentiate various types of irrigation efficiencies** relevant to surface irrigation.
*   **Explain the factors influencing the efficiency** of surface irrigation methods.
*   **Understand the principles of border irrigation** and its design considerations related to efficiency.
*   **Describe methods for evaluating the efficiency** of surface irrigation systems, particularly border irrigation.

### 1. Introduction to Irrigation Efficiency

**Definition:** Irrigation efficiency refers to the ratio of the amount of water beneficially used by crops to the total amount of water delivered to the field. It quantifies how effectively irrigation water is utilized to meet the crop's needs while minimizing losses.

**Importance:**
*   **Water Conservation:** Crucial in water-scarce regions.
*   **Economic Benefits:** Reduced water costs, energy costs (for pumping).
*   **Environmental Protection:** Minimizes waterlogging, salinization, and nutrient leaching.
*   **Optimal Crop Growth:** Ensures adequate water is available to the root zone without over or under-watering.

---

### 2. Types of Irrigation Efficiencies (Relevant to Surface Irrigation)

Surface irrigation methods are characterized by water application on the soil surface. Several efficiency parameters are used to assess their performance:

*   **Water Application Efficiency ($E_a$)**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of the average depth of irrigation water beneficially stored in the root zone to the average depth of irrigation water delivered to the field.
    *   **Formula:** $E_a = \frac{V_{s}}{V_{d}} \times 100\%$
        *   $V_s$: Volume of water beneficially stored in the root zone.
        *   $V_d$: Total volume of water delivered to the field.
    *   **Focus:** How well the applied water is stored within the intended root zone.
    *   **Losses considered:** Runoff and deep percolation beyond the root zone.

*   **Water Conveyance Efficiency ($E_c$)**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of the water delivered to the field to the water diverted from the source.
    *   **Formula:** $E_c = \frac{V_{field}}{V_{diverted}} \times 100\%$
        *   $V_{field}$: Volume of water delivered to the field.
        *   $V_{diverted}$: Volume of water diverted from the source (e.g., canal).
    *   **Focus:** Losses during transport from the source to the field (e.g., seepage, evaporation from canals, leaks in pipelines).
    *   **Relevance:** While important for the overall system, this is less about the *surface irrigation method itself* and more about the delivery system.

*   **Water Use Efficiency ($E_u$)**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of the volume of water actually consumed by the crop (evapotranspiration) to the volume of water delivered to the field.
    *   **Formula:** $E_u = \frac{V_{ET}}{V_{d}} \times 100\%$
        *   $V_{ET}$: Volume of water consumed by crop evapotranspiration.
        *   $V_{d}$: Total volume of water delivered to the field.
    *   **Focus:** How much of the delivered water is used by the plant.
    *   **Note:** Often confused with Crop Water Use Efficiency (which relates water use to yield). This definition focuses on the *irrigation delivery* aspect.

*   **Irrigation Efficiency ($E_i$)** (Often used as a broader term, sometimes synonymous with $E_a$)
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of the water stored in the root zone for beneficial use to the total water diverted from the source.
    *   **Formula:** $E_i = \frac{V_{s}}{V_{diverted}} \times 100\%$
    *   **Relationship:** $E_i = E_c \times E_a$ (assuming $E_u$ is considered equivalent to $E_a$ for simplicity). This emphasizes the combined effect of conveyance and application.

*   **Storage Efficiency ($E_{st}$)**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of the deficit in the root zone moisture content before irrigation to the total amount of water applied.
    *   **Formula:** $E_{st} = \frac{\text{Moisture deficit in root zone}}{\text{Water applied}} \times 100\%$
    *   **Focus:** How efficiently the applied water replenishes the depleted soil moisture.

*   **Distribution Uniformity ($DU$)**
    *   **Definition:** A measure of how uniformly the irrigation water is distributed over the irrigated area. It quantifies the variation in water depth applied across the field.
    *   **Formula:** $DU = \frac{\text{Average depth of water in the low quarter}}{\text{Average depth of water applied}} \times 100\%$
    *   **Focus:** The spatial variability of water application. Low DU leads to both under- and over-watered areas, reducing overall application efficiency.

*   **Application Efficiency ($EA$)** (Sometimes used interchangeably with $E_a$)
    *   **Definition:** Ratio of water stored in the root zone to water delivered. Same as $E_a$.

### 3. Factors Influencing Efficiency of Surface Irrigation Methods

Several factors affect how efficiently surface irrigation methods deliver water:

*   **System Design:**
    *   **Field Slope:**
        *   **Steep slopes:** Increase runoff velocity, leading to more runoff and reduced infiltration time.
        *   **Flat slopes:** Can lead to ponding, slow infiltration, and increased deep percolation at the start of the run.
        *   **Ideal slope:** Provides a balance for uniform flow and infiltration.
    *   **Soil Type:**
        *   **Sandy soils:** High infiltration rates, leading to rapid deep percolation losses. Shorter application times are needed.
        *   **Clay soils:** Low infiltration rates, prone to surface ponding and runoff if application rates are high. Longer application times are needed.
    *   **Field Length and Width:**
        *   **Long fields:** Can lead to excessive runoff at the lower end and insufficient water at the upper end due to the time it takes for water to reach the end.
        *   **Wide fields:** Can lead to uneven wetting if flow distribution is not managed properly.
    *   **Water Application Rate:**
        *   **High rates:** Can exceed soil infiltration capacity, causing significant runoff.
        *   **Low rates:** Can lead to prolonged wetting and increased deep percolation losses, especially in permeable soils.
    *   **Irrigation Duration (Run Time):**
        *   **Too short:** Insufficient water stored in the root zone, leading to low $E_a$.
        *   **Too long:** Excessive runoff and deep percolation, reducing $E_a$.

*   **Operational Practices:**
    *   **Irrigation Scheduling:** Applying water only when the crop needs it and in the correct amount.
    *   **Cut-off Time:** Properly determining when to stop the water supply to minimize runoff.
    *   **Flow Rate Management:** Maintaining appropriate flow rates into the field.
    *   **Maintenance:** Keeping channels and field surfaces clear of obstructions.

*   **Water Source Characteristics:**
    *   **Water pressure:** Affects flow rates from outlets.
    *   **Water quality:** Salinity can affect crop tolerance to water stress.

*   **Crop Characteristics:**
    *   **Root depth:** Determines the volume of soil to be wetted.
    *   **Crop water requirements (ETc):** Dictates the amount of water needed.

*   **Environmental Factors:**
    *   **Evaporation:** Losses from the water surface during irrigation.
    *   **Wind:** Can affect water distribution in some methods.

### 4. Border Irrigation: Design Parameters, Evaluation, and Ideal Wetting Pattern

#### 4.1 Border Irrigation: An Overview

**Definition:** Border irrigation is a surface irrigation method where the land is divided into long, parallel strips called borders, separated by levees or dikes. Water is introduced at the upper end of each border and flows down the slope to irrigate the area.

**Types:**
*   **Corrugated Borders:** Borders with small corrugations running parallel to the flow to reduce erosion and improve water distribution.
*   **Level Borders:** Borders with zero slope, relying on gravity to spread water laterally. Less common for significant areas.

#### 4.2 Design Parameters for Border Irrigation

Designing an efficient border irrigation system involves considering several parameters:

*   **Border Width:**
    *   **Range:** Typically 3-15 meters (10-50 feet).
    *   **Considerations:** Influenced by equipment used for cultivation, contouring, and harvesting. Wider borders reduce the number of levees, saving land and construction costs. Narrower borders can improve flow distribution control.

*   **Border Length:**
    *   **Range:** Typically 30-150 meters (100-500 feet).
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Longer borders:** Reduce the number of inlets and associated structures, but increase the risk of uneven distribution and high runoff at the lower end.
        *   **Shorter borders:** Improve uniformity but increase the number of inlet structures and labor.
        *   **Soil type:** Permeable soils require shorter lengths to prevent excessive deep percolation.

*   **Border Slope:**
    *   **Range:** Typically 0.0005 to 0.005 (0.05% to 0.5%).
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Too flat:** Water flows slowly, leading to ponding and uneven infiltration.
        *   **Too steep:** Water flows too fast, causing erosion and excessive runoff before adequate infiltration.
        *   **Influence on runoff:** Steeper slopes increase runoff.
        *   **Influence on uniformity:** A slight slope is generally preferred for better distribution.

*   **Water Application Rate (Flow Rate per Unit Width of Border, $q$)**
    *   **Units:** Liters per second per meter (LPS/m) or cubic feet per second per foot (cfs/ft).
    *   **Considerations:**
        *   **Soil infiltration rate:** The application rate should generally be higher than the initial infiltration rate but lower than the steady-state infiltration rate to allow for filling and adequate wetting without excessive runoff.
        *   **Irrigation time:** The duration of water application.
        *   **Economic optimum:** Balancing the cost of water, energy, and labor.
    *   **Typical values:** 0.5 to 2.0 LPS/m (0.01 to 0.04 cfs/ft).

*   **Irrigation Duration (Advance Time and Recession Time)**
    *   **Advance Time:** The time it takes for the water front to reach the end of the border.
    *   **Irrigation Time (Soaking Time):** The total duration water is applied to allow for infiltration and storage in the root zone. This is often determined by the time required to fill the soil moisture deficit at the lower end of the border.
    *   **Cut-off Time:** The time when water supply is stopped. It should be set such that the required amount of water infiltrates before the water reaches the end of the field and runs off.

*   **Levee Height and Shape:**
    *   **Height:** Must be sufficient to contain the water within the border at the maximum expected depth.
    *   **Shape:** Should be stable to resist erosion and allow easy passage of farm equipment.

#### 4.3 Evaluation of Border Irrigation

Evaluating the performance of a border irrigation system helps identify inefficiencies and guide improvements. Key evaluation metrics are derived from field measurements:

*   **Measurements:**
    *   **Water flow rate ($Q$)** entering the border.
    *   **Border dimensions:** Length ($L$), width ($W$).
    *   **Advance time ($t_a$)** at various points along the border.
    *   **Runoff volume ($V_{runoff}$) or runoff rate** at the end of the border.
    *   **Soil moisture content** at different depths and locations in the field before and after irrigation.
    *   **Water depth infiltrated** at various locations across the border.

*   **Calculations:**
    *   **Total Water Delivered ($V_d$):** $V_d = Q \times t_{irr}$ (where $t_{irr}$ is the total irrigation time).
    *   **Runoff Volume ($V_{runoff}$):** Measured directly at the tail end or calculated based on measured runoff rate and duration.
    *   **Water Stored in Root Zone ($V_s$):** Calculated by measuring soil moisture changes or by summing the infiltrated water depth across the area, adjusted for deep percolation beyond the root zone. A common method involves measuring water depths at various points across the border and averaging them.
        *   $V_s = (\text{Average infiltrated depth} - \text{Deep percolation loss}) \times \text{Area}$
    *   **Water Application Efficiency ($E_a$)**: $E_a = \frac{V_s}{V_d} \times 100\%$
    *   **Distribution Uniformity ($DU$)**:
        1.  Divide the border into several sections along its length.
        2.  Measure the depth of water infiltrated in each section using devices like small catch cans or by taking soil samples.
        3.  Calculate the average depth of water for each section.
        4.  Identify the section with the lowest average depth (often the tail end).
        5.  Calculate the average depth for the low quarter of the border.
        6.  $DU = \frac{\text{Average depth in low quarter}}{\text{Average depth over the entire border}} \times 100\%$

**Example of DU Calculation:**
Suppose in a border, water depths measured at different points are: 120mm, 110mm, 100mm, 90mm.
*   Average depth = (120+110+100+90)/4 = 105mm
*   Low quarter depth = 90mm
*   $DU = (90/105) \times 100\% = 85.7\%$

*   **Advance Ratio ($AR$)**: The ratio of the advance time to the tail end to the irrigation duration.
    *   $AR = \frac{\text{Advance time to tail end}}{\text{Irrigation duration}} \times 100\%$
    *   A high advance ratio (close to 100%) indicates that the entire border is wetted for a significant portion of the irrigation time.

#### 4.4 Ideal Wetting Pattern in Border Irrigation

An ideal wetting pattern aims to maximize the water stored in the root zone while minimizing losses from runoff and deep percolation.

**Characteristics of an Ideal Wetting Pattern:**

*   **Uniform Depth:** The depth of infiltrated water should be as uniform as possible across the entire area of the border, from the head to the tail end, and across the width of the border.
*   **Root Zone Saturation:** The infiltrated water should adequately fill the soil moisture deficit within the intended root zone depth.
*   **Minimal Runoff:** Runoff should be minimized or eliminated. This means the water application should cease before the water front reaches the end of the border, leaving just enough water to infiltrate and reach the tail end.
*   **Minimal Deep Percolation:** Water should not percolate beyond the root zone. This means the infiltration should stop once the root zone is adequately filled.

**Visual Representation:**

*   **Good wetting pattern:** A fairly uniform depth of water across the entire border, with the water level tapering off slightly at the very tail end due to the limited infiltration time.
*   **Poor wetting pattern (due to too short irrigation time):** Dry spots at the tail end, indicating insufficient water reached these areas.
*   **Poor wetting pattern (due to too long irrigation time):** Excessive water pooling at the tail end, indicating significant runoff and deep percolation.
*   **Poor wetting pattern (due to non-uniform slope or soil):** One side of the border is wetter than the other, or the head end is very wet while the tail end is dry.

**How Design Parameters Influence the Ideal Pattern:**

*   **Slope:** A gentle slope is crucial for allowing water to advance steadily and spread laterally, promoting uniform wetting.
*   **Flow Rate ($q$):** A well-matched flow rate to the soil infiltration characteristics ensures that the border fills without excessive ponding or rapid runoff.
*   **Border Length:** Shorter borders are easier to manage for achieving uniform wetting.
*   **Cut-off Time:** This is the critical operational parameter that dictates how close the wetting pattern gets to the ideal. It needs to be precisely calculated to stop the water supply before significant runoff occurs.

### 5. Improving the Efficiency of Surface Irrigation Methods

Several strategies can be employed to enhance the efficiency of surface irrigation, particularly border irrigation:

*   **Improved System Design:**
    *   **Slope Adjustment:** Contour grading or leveling the land to achieve optimal slopes.
    *   **Border Size Optimization:** Selecting appropriate border widths and lengths based on soil type and operational capabilities.
    *   **Levee Construction:** Building well-maintained and adequately sized levees.

*   **Optimized Operation:**
    *   **Accurate Irrigation Scheduling:** Using soil moisture monitoring or crop water requirement models to determine when and how much to irrigate.
    *   **Precise Cut-off Management:** Implementing timed cut-offs based on advance and recession characteristics. This is a key technique to reduce runoff.
    *   **Flow Rate Control:** Regulating the inflow rate to match infiltration capacities.
    *   **Runoff Reuse:** Collecting and re-applying runoff water to other fields or back to the upper end of the same field, significantly improving overall application efficiency.

*   **Technological Aids:**
    *   **Flow meters:** To accurately measure the volume of water applied.
    *   **Advance probes or sensors:** To monitor the water front's progress.
    *   **Automated gates and siphons:** For precise control of water delivery.
    *   **Laser leveling:** For precise land grading.

*   **Soil Management:**
    *   **Improving soil structure:** Through organic matter addition can improve infiltration and water holding capacity, leading to better uniformity.
    *   **Managing soil compaction:** To prevent uneven infiltration.

*   **Tail-Water Reuse Systems:**
    *   **Collection ditches and pumps:** To collect runoff from the end of borders and pump it back to the head of the field or to a storage pond. This is one of the most effective ways to boost application efficiency.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:** Define water application efficiency and list two factors that typically cause low application efficiency in border irrigation.

**Answer 1:** Water application efficiency ($E_a$) is the ratio of the volume of water beneficially stored in the root zone to the total volume of water delivered to the field. Two factors causing low $E_a$ in border irrigation are:
1.  **Excessive runoff:** When water is applied for too long or at too high a rate, a significant amount may flow off the end of the border before it can infiltrate.
2.  **Excessive deep percolation:** If the soil is highly permeable or the irrigation duration is too long, water can drain below the root zone, especially at the head of the border.

---

**Question 2:** A border irrigation system has a measured average water depth in the low quarter of 80 mm and an overall average water depth of 100 mm. Calculate the Distribution Uniformity ($DU$).

**Answer 2:**
$DU = \frac{\text{Average depth of water in the low quarter}}{\text{Average depth of water applied}} \times 100\%$
$DU = \frac{80 \text{ mm}}{100 \text{ mm}} \times 100\% = 80\%$

---

**Question 3:** Explain why a well-designed border irrigation system should have a slight longitudinal slope (e.g., 0.001) rather than being perfectly level or extremely steep.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Not perfectly level:** A perfectly level border relies on water spreading laterally and slowly seeping into the soil. This can lead to uneven wetting, ponding, and slow infiltration, especially in soils with lower infiltration rates. It also makes the process more sensitive to minor surface irregularities.
*   **Not extremely steep:** Extremely steep slopes cause water to flow too rapidly down the border. This reduces the time available for infiltration, leading to significant runoff at the lower end and insufficient wetting of the soil profile, particularly at the upper end. It also increases the risk of soil erosion.
*   **Slight longitudinal slope:** A gentle slope (e.g., 0.001) helps the water to advance steadily along the length of the border, promoting a more uniform distribution of water across the irrigated area and a more manageable infiltration process. It balances the need for water to reach the end with the time required for infiltration.

---

**Question 4:** What is the primary goal of optimizing the cut-off time in border irrigation?

**Answer 4:** The primary goal of optimizing the cut-off time is to **minimize water losses due to runoff while ensuring that the entire root zone of the border, including the tail end, receives adequate moisture.** It aims to stop the water supply when just enough water has infiltrated to meet the soil moisture deficit without allowing significant excess to flow off the field.

---

**Question 5:** Briefly describe two methods to improve the Water Application Efficiency ($E_a$) of an existing border irrigation system.

**Answer 5:**
1.  **Implement Tail-Water Reuse:** Collect the runoff from the end of the borders using collection ditches and pumps, and re-apply it to other fields or back to the upper end of the same field. This directly recovers lost water and significantly boosts $E_a$.
2.  **Improve Irrigation Scheduling and Cut-off Management:** Use field observations (e.g., advance time) or simple tools to determine the optimal time to shut off the water supply to each border, thereby reducing excess runoff and deep percolation.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Efficiency is key:** In irrigation, efficiency is not just about applying water, but applying it where and when it's needed, with minimal loss.
*   **$E_a$ vs. $DU$:** While $E_a$ measures how much of the delivered water is *stored* usefully, $DU$ measures how *uniformly* it's distributed. Low $DU$ will always lead to low $E_a$ because some areas will be over-watered (leading to losses) while others are under-watered.
*   **Border irrigation is sensitive:** Its efficiency is highly dependent on precise design parameters (slope, length, width) and operational management (flow rate, cut-off time).
*   **Runoff is the major enemy:** In surface irrigation, managing runoff is paramount for improving efficiency. Tail-water reuse is a powerful strategy.
*   **Ideal wetting pattern is a concept:** Real-world conditions rarely achieve a perfect wetting pattern, but understanding it guides design and management decisions to get as close as possible.
*   **Continuous evaluation is necessary:** Irrigation systems are not static. Soil conditions, wear and tear, and changing crop needs require periodic evaluation and adjustment.
