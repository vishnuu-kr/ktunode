# FUNDAMENTALS OF BUILDING PLANNING: Module 1 - Definition of Architecture & Historical Development

## Topic: Form and Space in Architecture

This module delves into the fundamental concepts of how architecture shapes and is shaped by the spaces it creates. We will explore the relationship between form and space, the distinction between positive and negative space, and how architectural elements define these spaces, ultimately examining the inherent qualities of architectural space.

---

### 1. Definition of Architecture

While a definitive single definition is elusive, architecture can be understood through its multifaceted nature:

*   **The Art and Science of Designing Buildings:** This encompasses the aesthetic, functional, and technical aspects of creating built environments.
*   **The Practice of Designing and Supervising the Construction of Buildings:** This highlights the professional role of an architect.
*   **The Characteristic Style of Buildings:** This refers to the distinctive qualities of buildings from a particular period, place, or artist (e.g., Gothic architecture, Frank Lloyd Wright's architecture).
*   **The Process of Planning, Designing, and Constructing Buildings and Other Physical Structures:** A broader definition that includes the entire lifecycle.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Aesthetics:** The principles concerned with the nature and appreciation of beauty.
*   **Functionality:** The practical purpose and usefulness of a building or its parts.
*   **Structure:** The arrangement of and relations between the parts or elements of something.
*   **Context:** The circumstances that form the setting for an event, statement, or idea, and in terms of which it can be fully understood. This includes physical, social, cultural, and historical context.

### 2. Historical Development of Architecture

Understanding the historical evolution of architecture provides context for contemporary design principles and reveals how societies have expressed themselves through built form.

**Key Periods and Their Architectural Characteristics:**

*   **Prehistoric Architecture (c. 10,000 BCE - 400 CE):**
    *   **Focus:** Shelter, defense, ritual.
    *   **Materials:** Local stone, timber, mudbrick.
    *   **Examples:** Stonehenge (megalithic structures, ritualistic), Cave dwellings (functional shelter), Huts.
    *   **Key Idea:** Buildings were extensions of natural forms and dictated by immediate needs.

*   **Ancient Architecture (c. 4000 BCE - 400 CE):**
    *   **Civilizations:** Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, Rome.
    *   **Focus:** Religion, power, monumentalism, civic life.
    *   **Materials:** Stone (limestone, granite, marble), brick, concrete (Roman).
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Egyptian:** Pyramids of Giza (tombs, religious, monumental), Temples of Karnak (religious, axiality).
        *   **Greek:** Parthenon (temple, order, proportion, post-and-lintel system, Doric, Ionic, Corinthian orders).
        *   **Roman:** Colosseum (amphitheater, engineering, arches, vaults, domes, concrete), Pantheon (temple, dome, engineering).
    *   **Key Ideas:** Development of sophisticated structural systems, codified architectural orders, emphasis on durability and grandeur.

*   **Medieval Architecture (c. 400 CE - 1400 CE):**
    *   **Periods:** Early Christian, Byzantine, Romanesque, Gothic.
    *   **Focus:** Religion, defense, feudalism.
    *   **Materials:** Stone, timber, brick.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Romanesque:** Durham Cathedral (thick walls, rounded arches, barrel vaults, defensive feel).
        *   **Gothic:** Notre-Dame Cathedral (pointed arches, ribbed vaults, flying buttresses, stained glass, verticality, light).
    *   **Key Ideas:** Rise of the church as a patron, development of new structural techniques to achieve height and light.

*   **Renaissance Architecture (c. 1400 CE - 1600 CE):**
    *   **Focus:** Humanism, revival of classical ideals (Greek and Roman).
    *   **Materials:** Stone, brick.
    *   **Examples:** Florence Cathedral Dome (Brunelleschi), St. Peter's Basilica (Bramante, Michelangelo).
    *   **Key Ideas:** Symmetry, proportion, geometric clarity, return to classical orders and forms.

*   **Baroque Architecture (c. 1600 CE - 1750 CE):**
    *   **Focus:** Drama, grandeur, emotion, theatricality.
    *   **Materials:** Stone, stucco, gilded elements.
    *   **Examples:** Palace of Versailles (opulence, grandeur), St. Peter's Square (Bernini).
    *   **Key Ideas:** Curving forms, elaborate ornamentation, manipulation of light and shadow, dramatic spatial effects.

*   **Neoclassical Architecture (c. 1750 CE - 1850 CE):**
    *   **Focus:** Return to classical simplicity, order, rationality, influence of Enlightenment.
    *   **Materials:** Stone, marble.
    *   **Examples:** United States Capitol Building, British Museum.
    *   **Key Ideas:** Emphasis on symmetry, clean lines, monumental scale, classical motifs.

*   **19th Century Architecture (Eclecticism, Revival Styles, Early Modernism):**
    *   **Focus:** Industrial Revolution, new materials (iron, steel, glass), stylistic revivalism (Gothic Revival, Victorian).
    *   **Materials:** Iron, steel, glass, brick, stone.
    *   **Examples:** Crystal Palace (glass and iron), Eiffel Tower (iron).
    *   **Key Ideas:** Technological advancements enabling new forms and structures, questioning of historical styles.

*   **20th Century Architecture (Modernism, Postmodernism, Deconstructivism, etc.):**
    *   **Modernism (Early to Mid-20th Century):**
        *   **Focus:** Functionality, form follows function, rejection of ornament, new materials and technologies.
        *   **Styles:** Bauhaus, International Style.
        *   **Examples:** Villa Savoye (Le Corbusier), Seagram Building (Mies van der Rohe).
        *   **Key Ideas:** Rationality, universality, mass production, abstraction.
    *   **Postmodernism (Late 20th Century):**
        *   **Focus:** Reaction against Modernism, reintroduction of ornament, historical references, playfulness, context.
        *   **Examples:** AT&T Building (Philip Johnson).
        *   **Key Ideas:** Pluralism, irony, eclecticism.
    *   **Contemporary Architecture (Late 20th Century - Present):**
        *   **Focus:** Diverse, embracing sustainability, technology, globalization, digital design.
        *   **Styles:** High-tech, parametricism, biomorphism, deconstructivism.
        *   **Examples:** Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (Frank Gehry), Beijing National Stadium (Herzog & de Meuron).
        *   **Key Ideas:** Innovation, experimentation, responsiveness to global challenges.

### 3. Form and Space in Architecture

The interplay between form and space is central to the architectural experience.

*   **Form:** Refers to the shape, configuration, and physical manifestation of a building or its elements. It is what we see.
*   **Space:** Refers to the volume, void, or area enclosed or defined by form. It is what we experience and move through.

**The Relationship:**

*   **Form Defines Space:** Architectural forms (walls, roofs, columns) create and contain spaces.
*   **Space Influences Form:** The desired spatial qualities can dictate the form of the building.
*   **Interdependence:** Form and space are inseparable; one cannot exist without the other in architecture.

**Analogy:** Think of a sculpture. The solid material is the form, and the space it displaces or encloses is its spatial aspect. In architecture, the building is the form, and the rooms, courtyards, and plazas are the spaces.

### 4. Positive and Negative Space

This concept, borrowed from the visual arts, is crucial in understanding architectural composition.

*   **Positive Space:** The solid elements of a design, such as walls, columns, and roofs. These are the "things" that occupy space.
    *   **In Architecture:** The building itself, its mass, its structural elements.

*   **Negative Space:** The void or unoccupied area that is defined or enclosed by the positive space. This is the "emptiness" that is given shape.
    *   **In Architecture:** The rooms, corridors, courtyards, and the intangible feeling of the volume within.

**Key Idea:** In architecture, both positive and negative spaces are equally important. A well-designed building balances the solidity of its form with the quality and organization of the space it creates.

**Example:**
*   **Positive Space:** The solid walls and columns of a living room.
*   **Negative Space:** The volume of the living room itself, where people gather and interact.

**Exercise:** Sketch a simple plan of a room. Identify the positive and negative spaces within your sketch.

### 5. Defining Space with Horizontal and Vertical Elements

Architectural elements are the tools used to shape and define space. Horizontal and vertical elements play distinct yet complementary roles.

**A. Horizontal Elements:**

*   **Function:** Create floors, ceilings, planes, and delineate boundaries along the ground plane. They contribute to enclosure, connection, and spatial hierarchy.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Floors:** Define the base of a space, provide support, and dictate circulation. (e.g., the marble floor of a grand hall, the timber floor of a domestic interior).
    *   **Ceilings:** Define the top of a space, influence light and acoustics, and contribute to the sense of enclosure or openness. (e.g., a vaulted ceiling in a cathedral, a dropped ceiling in an office).
    *   **Platforms/Terraces:** Create elevated or distinct zones within a larger space or landscape. (e.g., a raised stage, a garden terrace).
    *   **Walls (low-lying or partial):** Can create partial enclosure or define zones without fully separating spaces. (e.g., a half-wall in an open-plan office, a garden wall).
    *   **Roofs (horizontally perceived):** While primarily enclosing, the perceived horizontal span of a roof can define the extent of a space.

**B. Vertical Elements:**

*   **Function:** Provide support, create enclosure, direct gaze, divide space, and establish rhythm and scale. They contribute to the feeling of height, monumentality, or intimacy.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Walls:** The most common vertical elements, providing enclosure, privacy, and support. (e.g., solid brick walls, glass walls).
    *   **Columns:** Vertical supports that can also define edges of space, create rhythm, and allow for greater openness. (e.g., Greek Doric columns, modern steel columns).
    *   **Piers:** Similar to columns but often integrated into walls.
    *   **Screens/Partitions:** Lighter vertical elements that partially divide space, allowing for visual connection. (e.g., perforated screens, slatted partitions).
    *   **Doors and Windows:** Openings within vertical elements that provide access, light, and views, while still being part of the vertical plane.
    *   **Stairs:** Vertical elements that facilitate movement between levels, often creating dynamic spatial sequences.
    *   **Vertical Fins/Louvers:** Can control light and views while adding a vertical articulation to a facade.

**How they work together:**

*   The **base** (floor) and **top** (ceiling/roof) provided by horizontal elements establish the basic volume.
*   The **sides** (walls, columns) provided by vertical elements enclose this volume and define its boundaries.
*   The **relationship** between the scale of horizontal and vertical elements significantly impacts the quality of the space (e.g., low ceilings create intimacy, high ceilings create grandeur).

**Example:**
*   **A simple rectangular room:** The floor and ceiling are horizontal elements defining the volume. The four walls are vertical elements enclosing the space.
*   **A Greek temple:** The stylobate (steps) and entablature are horizontal elements. The columns are dominant vertical elements that define the perimeter of the cella (inner chamber) and the surrounding peristyle.

### 6. Qualities of Architectural Space

Beyond mere enclosure, architectural space possesses qualities that evoke feelings, influence behavior, and create memorable experiences. These are subjective but can be analyzed and designed for.

**Key Qualities:**

*   **Scale:** The relationship of the size of the space to the size of its occupants or the human body.
    *   **Human Scale:** Spaces that feel comfortable and proportionate to people.
    *   **Monumental Scale:** Spaces that feel vast and awe-inspiring.
    *   *Example:* A cozy reading nook vs. the nave of a cathedral.

*   **Proportion:** The harmonious relationship between the dimensions of a space (length, width, height) and the dimensions of its elements.
    *   **Golden Ratio:** Often cited for its perceived aesthetic appeal.
    *   *Example:* The proportions of a well-designed room that feel balanced.

*   **Light:** The quality, quantity, and distribution of light within a space profoundly affects its mood and perception.
    *   **Natural Light:** Diffuse, direct, filtered.
    *   **Artificial Light:** Task lighting, ambient lighting, accent lighting.
    *   *Example:* The dramatic shafts of light in a Gothic cathedral vs. the bright, even light of a modern office.

*   **Enclosure/Openness:** The degree to which a space is bounded or permeable.
    *   **Highly Enclosed:** Creates intimacy, privacy, and security.
    *   **Open and Permeable:** Creates a sense of freedom, connection, and publicness.
    *   *Example:* A private bedroom vs. a public plaza.

*   **Pathways/Circulation:** The routes people take through a space influence their experience and perception of the spatial sequence.
    *   **Linear:** Direct movement.
    *   **Curving:** More exploratory and revealing.
    *   **Axial:** Directing focus to a specific point.
    *   *Example:* The procession through a museum, the meandering path in a park.

*   **Acoustics:** The way sound behaves within a space, affecting clarity, reverberation, and intimacy.
    *   *Example:* The clear speech in a lecture hall vs. the echoing reverberation in a large, empty stone hall.

*   **Texture and Materiality:** The tactile and visual qualities of the surfaces within a space contribute to its character and sensory experience.
    *   *Example:* The rough texture of exposed brick vs. the smooth finish of polished concrete.

*   **View/Prospect:** The vistas or outlooks offered from a space, connecting it to its surroundings.
    *   *Example:* A window framing a landscape, a courtyard providing a visual focus.

*   **Thresholds and Transitions:** The points of entry and movement between spaces, creating anticipation and defining spatial shifts.
    *   *Example:* Stepping through a grand doorway, a change in floor level.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**1. Multiple Choice:**

Which of the following is NOT typically considered a characteristic of Gothic architecture?
a) Pointed arches
b) Flying buttresses
c) Barrel vaults
d) Stained glass windows

**Answer:** c) Barrel vaults (Romanesque architecture is characterized by barrel vaults; Gothic architecture uses ribbed vaults and pointed arches to achieve greater height and lighter construction.)

**2. Short Answer:**

Briefly explain the concept of "form follows function" and give an example of a building where this principle is evident.

**Answer:** "Form follows function" is a principle of modernist architecture suggesting that the shape of a building should be primarily based on its intended purpose. An example is the Bauhaus school buildings in Dessau, Germany, designed by Walter Gropius, where the form directly reflects the various functional requirements of workshops, studios, and administrative spaces.

**3. Definition:**

Define "negative space" in the context of architecture.

**Answer:** Negative space in architecture refers to the void or unoccupied area that is defined and shaped by the positive elements (forms) of a building, such as walls, columns, and roofs. It is the space that people experience and move through, such as rooms, corridors, and courtyards.

**4. Analysis:**

Consider a public library.
a) Identify examples of positive space within the library.
b) Identify examples of negative space within the library.
c) How might the architects have considered the qualities of scale and light in designing the main reading room?

**Answer:**
a) Positive space: The bookshelves, the circulation desk, the walls, the structural columns, the furniture.
b) Negative space: The aisles between bookshelves, the open areas for seating, the volume of the reading room itself, the corridors.
c) Scale: The reading room might be designed with a scale that feels comfortable for study, perhaps with varied ceiling heights or zones to avoid feeling overwhelmingly vast. It needs to accommodate individual focus while also allowing for a sense of shared intellectual pursuit.
Light: Natural light from large windows would be crucial for reading, providing a connection to the outside and a pleasant ambiance. The quality of light might be diffuse to avoid glare on books and screens, with additional task lighting provided at individual desks.

**5. Comparative Study:**

Compare and contrast the primary focus of Romanesque and Gothic architecture in terms of their spatial experience.

**Answer:**
*   **Romanesque:** Characterized by thick walls, rounded arches, and barrel vaults, Romanesque spaces tend to feel heavy, solid, and enclosed. They often convey a sense of security and groundedness, with a focus on horizontal massing and a more dimly lit, intimate atmosphere.
*   **Gothic:** Through the use of pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses, Gothic architecture achieves soaring heights and allows for thinner walls and larger window openings. This creates a spatial experience that is vertical, light-filled, and aspiring, drawing the eye upwards and fostering a sense of transcendence and divine presence.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Architecture is a synthesis of art, science, and culture.**
*   **Form and space are inextricably linked.** One defines and is defined by the other.
*   **Positive and negative space are equally vital** in creating a successful architectural composition.
*   **Horizontal elements establish floors and ceilings**, contributing to enclosure and ground plane definition.
*   **Vertical elements provide support and enclosure**, shaping the sides of spaces and influencing height.
*   **The qualities of architectural space (scale, light, acoustics, etc.) are designed intentionally** to create specific human experiences and responses.
*   **Historical context informs contemporary architectural thinking.** Understanding past achievements and failures provides valuable lessons for today's designers.
