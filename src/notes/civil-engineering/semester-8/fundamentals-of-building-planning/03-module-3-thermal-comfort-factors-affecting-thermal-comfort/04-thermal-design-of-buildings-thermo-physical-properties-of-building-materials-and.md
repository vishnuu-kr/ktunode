# Fundamentals of Building Planning - Module 3: Thermal Comfort

## Topic: Thermal Design of Buildings: Thermophysical Properties of Building Materials and Thermal Control

---

*   Explain how these properties influence heat transfer within a building.

## 1. Introduction to Thermal Design

Thermal design of buildings is the process of shaping a building and its components to maintain a comfortable and healthy indoor environment for occupants while minimizing energy consumption for heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC). It's about balancing heat gains and losses to achieve desired indoor temperatures.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Thermal Comfort:** A state of mind that expresses satisfaction with the thermal environment. It is influenced by objective environmental factors and subjective personal factors.
*   **Heat Transfer:** The movement of thermal energy from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature. In buildings, this primarily occurs through conduction, convection, and radiation.
*   **Passive Design:** Design strategies that use the building's form, orientation, and materials to naturally regulate the indoor environment without the need for active mechanical systems.

## 2. Thermophysical Properties of Building Materials

The performance of a building in terms of thermal comfort is heavily dependent on the thermophysical properties of the materials used in its construction. These properties dictate how heat interacts with the building envelope (walls, roof, floor, windows).

### 2.1. Thermal Conductivity (k)

*   **Definition:** A measure of a material's ability to conduct heat. It quantifies how easily heat passes through a material.
*   **Units:** Watts per meter-Kelvin (W/m·K) or British Thermal Units per hour-foot-degree Fahrenheit (Btu/hr·ft·°F).
*   **High Thermal Conductivity:** Materials with high 'k' values are good conductors of heat (e.g., metals like aluminum, steel, copper). They allow heat to pass through them easily.
*   **Low Thermal Conductivity:** Materials with low 'k' values are poor conductors of heat (insulators) (e.g., fiberglass, mineral wool, foam, air). They resist heat flow.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** Materials with low thermal conductivity are crucial for insulating the building envelope to prevent heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer.

**Example:**
*   A steel beam will conduct heat much more readily than a fiberglass insulation batt.

### 2.2. Thermal Resistivity (R-value)

*   **Definition:** The reciprocal of thermal conductivity (R = 1/k). It measures a material's resistance to heat flow.
*   **Units:** Kelvin-meter per Watt (K·m/W) or ft²·°F·hr/Btu.
*   **High R-value:** Indicates high resistance to heat flow, meaning it's a good insulator.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** A higher R-value for building components (like walls or roofs) leads to better thermal performance and reduced energy for heating/cooling.

**Note:** The overall R-value of a building assembly (e.g., a wall system) is the sum of the R-values of its individual layers.

### 2.3. Thermal Transmittance (U-value)

*   **Definition:** The reciprocal of the total thermal resistance (U = 1/R_total). It measures the rate of heat transfer through a building component or assembly per unit area and per degree temperature difference.
*   **Units:** Watts per square meter-Kelvin (W/m²·K) or Btu/hr·ft²·°F.
*   **Low U-value:** Indicates low heat transfer, meaning it's a good insulator.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** Building codes often specify maximum U-values for different building components to ensure energy efficiency.

**Relationship:** U = 1 / (R_outside_film + R_material1 + R_material2 + ... + R_inside_film)

### 2.4. Specific Heat Capacity (c)

*   **Definition:** The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 unit mass of a material by 1 degree Celsius (or Kelvin).
*   **Units:** Joules per kilogram-Kelvin (J/kg·K) or Btu/lb·°F.
*   **High Specific Heat Capacity:** Materials can absorb and store a large amount of heat without a significant temperature rise.
*   **Low Specific Heat Capacity:** Materials heat up and cool down quickly.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** Materials with high specific heat capacity, often referred to as having high "thermal mass," can help moderate indoor temperature fluctuations. They absorb heat during the day and release it at night.

**Example:**
*   Concrete and stone have high specific heat capacities, making them effective for thermal mass. Wood has a lower specific heat capacity.

### 2.5. Thermal Diffusivity (α)

*   **Definition:** A measure of how quickly a material's temperature changes when exposed to a temperature difference. It relates thermal conductivity, density, and specific heat capacity (α = k / (ρ * c)).
*   **Units:** Square meters per second (m²/s) or ft²/hr.
*   **High Thermal Diffusivity:** Heat penetrates the material quickly.
*   **Low Thermal Diffusivity:** Heat penetration is slow.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** This property is important for understanding how quickly a material will respond to external temperature changes, affecting the time lag of heat transfer. Materials with low diffusivity will have a longer time lag, meaning their internal temperature will be less affected by rapid external changes.

### 2.6. Density (ρ)

*   **Definition:** Mass per unit volume of a material.
*   **Units:** Kilograms per cubic meter (kg/m³) or pounds per cubic foot (lb/ft³).
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** Density is a component of thermal diffusivity and influences the overall thermal mass of a material. Denser materials often have higher specific heat capacities.

### 2.7. Thermal Expansion

*   **Definition:** The tendency of matter to change its volume or shape in response to changes in temperature.
*   **Impact on Thermal Comfort:** While not directly related to heat flow, significant thermal expansion and contraction can lead to stresses in building materials, potentially causing cracking or joint failures, which can compromise the building envelope's integrity and insulation.

## 3. Thermal Control Strategies Using Material Properties

Effective thermal control involves strategically selecting and using building materials to manage heat flow and maintain comfortable indoor conditions.

### 3.1. Insulation

*   **Principle:** Using materials with low thermal conductivity (high R-value) to minimize heat transfer through the building envelope.
*   **Materials:** Fiberglass, mineral wool, cellulose, rigid foam boards (polystyrene, polyisocyanurate), spray foam.
*   **Application:** Walls, roofs, floors, foundations, around windows and doors.
*   **Benefit:** Reduces heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer, leading to lower energy bills and improved comfort.

### 3.2. Thermal Mass

*   **Principle:** Utilizing materials with high specific heat capacity and density to absorb and store heat, moderating indoor temperature swings.
*   **Materials:** Concrete, brick, stone, rammed earth, water (in some systems).
*   **Application:** Internal walls, floors, and sometimes external walls that receive direct sunlight or are exposed to diurnal temperature variations.
*   **Benefit:**
    *   **Passive Heating:** Stores solar heat gained during the day and releases it slowly at night, warming the space.
    *   **Passive Cooling:** Absorbs excess heat from the interior during warmer periods and releases it when the interior temperature drops, cooling the space.
    *   **Reduces HVAC Load:** Less reliance on mechanical systems for temperature regulation.

**Key Considerations for Thermal Mass:**

*   **Placement:** Thermal mass should be exposed to the conditioned space and ideally receive direct sunlight (in winter) or be shielded from direct sun (in summer) to be most effective.
*   **Interaction with Insulation:** Thermal mass is most effective when it is insulated from the outside. This prevents the stored heat from escaping to the exterior.
*   **Climate Dependence:** Thermal mass is most beneficial in climates with significant diurnal temperature swings. In constantly hot or cold climates, its effectiveness can be reduced.

**Example:** A concrete slab floor exposed to sunlight in a well-insulated house will absorb heat during the day and radiate it back into the room at night.

### 3.3. Reflective Surfaces (High Albedo)

*   **Principle:** Using materials with high reflectivity (low absorptivity) to reduce the amount of solar radiation absorbed by the building.
*   **Materials:** Light-colored paints, specialized roofing materials, radiant barriers.
*   **Application:** Roofs, walls, and sometimes exterior surfaces.
*   **Benefit:** Reduces solar heat gain, especially during hot, sunny periods, lowering cooling loads.

### 3.4. Air Sealing

*   **Principle:** Preventing uncontrolled air leakage through the building envelope.
*   **Materials:** Sealants, tapes, membranes.
*   **Application:** Joints between building components, around windows and doors, penetrations for pipes and wires.
*   **Benefit:** Reduces heat loss/gain due to infiltration and exfiltration, improves HVAC system efficiency, and prevents moisture problems.

### 3.5. Ventilation Control

*   **Principle:** Managing the intentional introduction of fresh air (ventilation) and the uncontrolled leakage of air (infiltration).
*   **Natural Ventilation:** Using windows, doors, and other openings to allow air to flow through the building.
*   **Mechanical Ventilation:** Using fans and ductwork to supply fresh air and exhaust stale air.
*   **Impact:** While ventilation is necessary for indoor air quality, uncontrolled ventilation (infiltration) can significantly impact thermal comfort and energy use. Controlled ventilation strategies (e.g., heat recovery ventilators - HRVs, energy recovery ventilators - ERVs) can pre-condition incoming fresh air, reducing energy penalties.

a) Conduct heat easily.
b) Resist heat flow effectively.
c) Store a large amount of heat.
d) Heat up and cool down quickly.

Explain the concept of thermal mass and how it contributes to thermal comfort. Provide an example of a material commonly used for thermal mass and its application in a building.

b) Convection

b) High thermal conductivity

## 5. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
b) Thermal Conductivity
*   **Explanation:** Low thermal conductivity means the material is a poor conductor of heat, making it a good insulator.

**Answer 2:**
b) Resist heat flow effectively.
*   **Explanation:** The R-value is a measure of thermal resistance. A higher R-value means greater resistance to heat flow.

**Answer 3:**
Thermal mass refers to the ability of a material to absorb, store, and release heat. Materials with high specific heat capacity and density, such as concrete, brick, or stone, possess high thermal mass. This property contributes to thermal comfort by:
*   **Moderating Temperature Swings:** Absorbing excess heat during warmer periods (e.g., from direct sunlight or internal sources) and releasing it slowly when the ambient temperature drops, thus smoothing out temperature fluctuations.
*   **Passive Heating/Cooling:** In winter, thermal mass can absorb solar radiation and release it later to warm the space. In summer, it can absorb heat from the interior during the day and release it to the cooler night air, providing a cooling effect.

**Example:** A concrete slab floor in a well-insulated living space that receives direct sunlight during the winter months acts as thermal mass. It absorbs the sun's heat during the day and gradually releases it into the room as the temperature drops in the evening, helping to maintain a comfortable temperature without relying solely on a heating system.

**Answer 4:**
c) Infiltration
*   **Explanation:** Infiltration is the uncontrolled inward leakage of outdoor air into a building.

**Answer 5:**
c) High albedo (reflectivity)
*   **Explanation:** In a hot, sunny climate, a highly reflective roof (high albedo) will reflect a significant portion of the incoming solar radiation, preventing it from being absorbed and heating up the building, thus reducing cooling loads.

---

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Holistic Approach:** Thermal design is not about single properties but the synergistic effect of material choices, building orientation, form, and occupant behavior.
*   **Climate is Key:** The optimal selection of materials and strategies is heavily influenced by the local climate.
*   **Balancing Act:** Thermal design often involves balancing competing needs, like allowing solar heat gain in winter while preventing it in summer.
*   **Beyond Conductivity:** While conductivity is crucial for insulation, specific heat capacity (thermal mass) and diffusivity play vital roles in managing dynamic temperature changes.
*   **Building Envelope Integrity:** Air sealing and proper detailing are as important as the R-value of insulation for effective thermal control.
