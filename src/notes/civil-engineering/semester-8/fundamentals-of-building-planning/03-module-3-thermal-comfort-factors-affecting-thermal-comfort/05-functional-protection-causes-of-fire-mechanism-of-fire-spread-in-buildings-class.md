# FUNDAMENTALS OF BUILDING PLANNING - MODULE 3: THERMAL COMFORT: FACTORS AFFECTING THERMAL COMFORT

## Topic: Functional Protection: Causes of Fire, Mechanism of Fire Spread in Buildings, Classification of Fire - High Temperature Effects and Combustibility of Building Materials and Structure

### 1. Causes of Fire

Understanding the origins of fire is crucial for effective building planning and fire prevention. Fires in buildings are typically caused by accidental ignition of combustible materials, often due to human error or equipment malfunction.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Fire Triangle:** For a fire to start and continue, three elements are necessary:
        1.  **Fuel:** Any combustible material (e.g., wood, paper, textiles, plastics, flammable liquids).
        2.  **Oxygen:** Usually from the air, acting as an oxidizer.
        3.  **Heat Source:** An ignition source that raises the fuel to its ignition temperature (e.g., sparks, open flames, hot surfaces, electrical faults).
    *   **Fire Tetrahedron:** An expansion of the fire triangle, adding **chemical chain reaction** as the fourth element. This refers to the self-sustaining chemical process of combustion.
*   **Common Ignition Sources in Buildings:**
    *   **Electrical Faults:**
        *   Overheating of wiring due to overloading or improper installation.
        *   Short circuits.
        *   Faulty appliances and equipment.
        *   Damaged electrical cords.
        *   **Example:** A frayed electrical cord in a conference room, touching a flammable curtain, could ignite.
    *   **Human Error & Negligence:**
        *   Careless use of smoking materials (cigarettes, matches).
        *   Unattended cooking.
        *   Improper storage of flammable liquids.
        *   Arson (intentional acts).
        *   **Example:** Discarding a lit cigarette butt into a waste bin filled with paper.
    *   **Heating Equipment:**
        *   Malfunctioning furnaces, boilers, or space heaters.
        *   Chimneys or vents not properly maintained, leading to creosote buildup and ignition.
        *   **Example:** A portable electric heater placed too close to a sofa.
    *   **Open Flames:**
        *   Candles, matches, lighters used carelessly.
        *   Fireworks or pyrotechnics within or near buildings.
        *   **Example:** A decorative candle left burning unattended on a wooden shelf.
    *   **Spontaneous Combustion:**
        *   Certain materials, like oily rags or hay, can self-heat due to oxidation. If this heat is trapped, it can reach ignition temperature without an external spark.
        *   **Example:** Oily rags stored in a pile in a workshop.
    *   **Explosions:**
        *   Ignition of flammable gases or vapors (e.g., natural gas leaks, solvent fumes).
        *   Dust explosions (e.g., in grain silos, flour mills).
        *   **Example:** A gas leak from a faulty stove igniting when a light switch is flicked.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** The majority of building fires are preventable and stem from a combination of an ignition source and available fuel.

### 2. Mechanism of Fire Spread in Buildings

Once a fire starts, it can spread rapidly through various mechanisms, posing significant risks to occupants and property.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Fire Spread:** The process by which fire moves from its point of origin to other areas of a building.
*   **Mechanisms of Fire Spread:**
    *   **Conduction:**
        *   Heat transfer through direct contact between materials.
        *   Metals are good conductors, so structural elements made of steel can transfer heat quickly, igniting combustible materials on the other side.
        *   **Example:** A steel beam in a wall heated by a fire on one side can transfer enough heat to ignite wood framing on the other side.
    *   **Convection:**
        *   Heat transfer through the movement of hot gases and air.
        *   Hot gases rise, carrying heat upwards and horizontally, igniting materials in their path.
        *   This is a primary mechanism for vertical fire spread in multi-story buildings.
        *   **Example:** Hot smoke and gases flowing through ventilation shafts or openings in floors and walls.
    *   **Radiation:**
        *   Heat transfer through electromagnetic waves (infrared radiation).
        *   Even without direct contact or air movement, materials can ignite if they receive enough radiant heat from a fire.
        *   The intensity of radiation increases with temperature.
        *   **Example:** Combustible materials on the opposite side of a room from a fire igniting due to radiant heat.
    *   **Direct Flame Impingement:**
        *   Flames directly coming into contact with combustible materials.
        *   This is the most direct and often fastest way for fire to spread.
        *   **Example:** Flames from a burning sofa directly licking a nearby upholstered chair.
    *   **Transfer through Building Elements (Especially Concealed Spaces):**
        *   Fire can spread through cavities in walls, ceilings, floors, and service penetrations (ducts, pipes, electrical conduits).
        *   These concealed spaces can act as chimneys, allowing rapid fire and smoke spread, often undetected.
        *   **Example:** Fire spreading behind a false ceiling and re-emerging in an adjacent room through an unsealed opening.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Building design features (compartmentation, firestopping) are critical in controlling the spread of fire through these mechanisms.

### 3. Classification of Fire

Fires are classified based on the type of fuel involved, which dictates the appropriate extinguishing agent and methods.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Fire Classes:** A standardized system for categorizing fires.
*   **International Fire Classification System (Commonly used in many countries, e.g., EN 2):**
    *   **Class A:**
        *   **Fuel:** Ordinary combustible solids, usually with a fibrous or cellulosic nature.
        *   **Examples:** Wood, paper, textiles, rubber, plastics.
        *   **Characteristics:** Typically leave an ash residue.
    *   **Class B:**
        *   **Fuel:** Flammable and combustible liquids and gases.
        *   **Examples:** Petrol, oil, paints, solvents, cooking oils, LPG, natural gas.
        *   **Characteristics:** Do not typically leave an ash residue (except for some solids like waxes).
    *   **Class C:**
        *   **Fuel:** Flammable gases.
        *   **Examples:** Propane, butane, methane, acetylene.
        *   **Characteristics:** Burn with a visible flame.
    *   **Class D:**
        *   **Fuel:** Combustible metals.
        *   **Examples:** Magnesium, titanium, sodium, potassium.
        *   **Characteristics:** Require special extinguishing agents and methods; water can react violently.
    *   **Class E (now often superseded by Class C in some systems, but relevant in older contexts):**
        *   **Fuel:** Energized electrical equipment.
        *   **Examples:** Fires involving electrical appliances, wiring, motors.
        *   **Characteristics:** The presence of electricity is the primary hazard; it can also involve Class A or B fuels. Extinguishing agents must be non-conductive.
    *   **Class F (or Class K in North America):**
        *   **Fuel:** Combustible cooking oils and fats.
        *   **Examples:** Vegetable oils, animal fats in kitchen environments.
        *   **Characteristics:** Burn at significantly higher temperatures than other Class B fires and require specific wetting agents.
*   **North American Fire Classification System (NFPA 10):**
    *   Similar to the international system but uses letters A, B, C, D, and K.
    *   Class K specifically addresses cooking oils and fats.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Correctly identifying the class of fire is crucial for selecting the appropriate fire extinguisher and preventing the situation from worsening. Using the wrong extinguisher can be ineffective or even dangerous (e.g., using water on a Class B or Class D fire).

### 4. High-Temperature Effects on Building Materials and Structure

The extreme temperatures generated during a fire can significantly degrade and compromise the performance of building materials and structural elements, leading to collapse.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Fire Resistance:** The ability of a material or building element to withstand fire exposure for a specified period without losing its load-bearing capacity, integrity, or insulating ability.
    *   **Degradation:** The breakdown or weakening of materials due to heat.
*   **Effects of High Temperatures:**
    *   **Steel:**
        *   **Loss of Strength:** Steel loses its load-bearing capacity as temperature increases. At around 500-600°C, it can lose up to 50% of its strength.
        *   **Expansion:** Steel expands significantly when heated, which can cause buckling and distortion of structural members, leading to increased loads on other elements.
        *   **Example:** Steel beams in a building can bend and twist, causing floors to sag and potentially leading to catastrophic collapse.
    *   **Concrete:**
        *   **Spalling:** The explosive shedding of surface layers of concrete due to trapped moisture turning to steam and expanding. This exposes the steel reinforcement to heat.
        *   **Strength Reduction:** High temperatures can weaken concrete by altering its chemical composition and causing thermal stresses.
        *   **Reinforcement Corrosion:** Once exposed, the steel reinforcement can heat up rapidly, lose its protective cover, and eventually fail.
        *   **Example:** The protective concrete cover of a reinforced concrete column spalling off, exposing the steel bars to direct flame impingement.
    *   **Timber (Wood):**
        *   **Combustion:** Wood burns and reduces in cross-section over time.
        *   **Charring:** A layer of char forms on the surface, which acts as an insulator but also reduces the effective cross-section of the structural member.
        *   **Loss of Strength:** As the charring progresses, the remaining uncharred wood weakens and can buckle or fail.
        *   **Example:** A wooden roof truss loses its structural integrity as the timber members char and weaken, leading to roof collapse.
    *   **Glass:**
        *   **Thermal Shock:** Rapid heating can cause glass to crack or shatter, especially annealed glass.
        *   **Melted/Softened:** At very high temperatures, glass can soften and flow.
        *   **Fire-Rated Glass:** Special treated glass is designed to resist fire for a specified time, often by incorporating intumescent layers or being made of toughened materials.
        *   **Example:** Standard window glass shattering when exposed to fire, allowing fire and smoke to spread.
    *   **Plastics:**
        *   **Melting and Dripping:** Most plastics melt at relatively low temperatures, and molten plastic can spread fire and create burn hazards.
        *   **Combustion:** Many plastics are highly combustible and can produce toxic smoke and fumes.
        *   **Example:** PVC piping melting and dripping, spreading fire to materials below.
    *   **Building Sealants and Insulation:**
        *   Many sealants and insulation materials are flammable or release toxic fumes when heated, contributing to fire spread and hazard.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Building materials are often rated for their fire resistance (e.g., 30 minutes, 60 minutes, 90 minutes). This rating indicates how long they can withstand fire while maintaining their structural integrity or fire separation function.

### 5. Combustibility of Building Materials and Structure

The inherent property of building materials to burn or contribute to a fire is a critical factor in building planning.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Combustibility:** The ability of a material to ignite and burn when exposed to a heat source and oxygen.
    *   **Fire Resistance vs. Combustibility:**
        *   **Fire Resistance** is a performance measure of how a material or assembly withstands fire.
        *   **Combustibility** is an inherent property of the material itself.
    *   **Non-combustible Materials:** Materials that do not ignite or burn when exposed to fire under typical building conditions.
        *   **Examples:** Steel, concrete, brick, stone, glass, gypsum board.
    *   **Combustible Materials:** Materials that will ignite and burn when exposed to fire.
        *   **Examples:** Wood, paper, textiles, plastics, asphalt, some insulations.
*   **Factors Affecting Combustibility:**
    *   **Chemical Composition:** Materials made of organic compounds are generally combustible.
    *   **Moisture Content:** Higher moisture content in materials like wood can reduce their immediate combustibility, but as the moisture evaporates, the material becomes more susceptible to ignition.
    *   **Surface Area to Volume Ratio:** Materials with a high surface area (e.g., sawdust, thin wood shavings) ignite more easily and burn faster than solid blocks of the same material.
    *   **Presence of Accelerants:** Certain substances can increase the rate of burning.
*   **Classification of Combustibility:**
    *   **Building Codes** often classify materials based on their combustibility and their contribution to fire spread.
    *   **Surface Burning Characteristics:** Standards like ASTM E84 (Surface Burning Characteristics of Building Materials) or BS 476-6/7 are used to test materials for flame spread and smoke development.
        *   **Flame Spread Index:** A measure of how far fire travels along the surface of a material.
        *   **Smoke Developed Index:** A measure of the amount of smoke a material produces when burning.
    *   **Ignitability:** The ease with which a material ignites.
    *   **Rate of Heat Release:** How quickly a material releases heat when burning, a key factor in fire intensity.
*   **Building Planning Implications:**
    *   **Material Selection:** Choosing non-combustible or fire-retardant materials for critical structural elements and finishes, especially in high-risk areas.
    *   **Compartmentation:** Using fire-rated walls, floors, and doors to create barriers that prevent fire spread.
    *   **Firestopping:** Sealing openings in fire-rated assemblies to prevent the passage of fire and smoke.
    *   **Exterior Finish:** The combustibility of exterior cladding can significantly impact the fire spread from outside to inside, or vice-versa.
*   **Important Point to Remember:** Building codes specify requirements for material combustibility and fire performance based on the building's occupancy, size, and location to ensure occupant safety and limit property damage.

### Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
What are the three essential components of the "Fire Triangle"?

**Answer:**
The three essential components are Fuel, Oxygen, and Heat Source.

**Question 2:**
Describe the difference between conduction, convection, and radiation in the context of fire spread in buildings.

**Answer:**
*   **Conduction:** Heat transfer through direct contact between materials.
*   **Convection:** Heat transfer through the movement of hot gases and air.
*   **Radiation:** Heat transfer through electromagnetic waves.

**Question 3:**
A fire involves cooking oil in a restaurant kitchen. According to international classifications, what class of fire is this?

**Answer:**
Class F (or Class K in North America).

**Question 4:**
Explain how high temperatures affect steel used in building structures.

**Answer:**
High temperatures cause steel to lose its strength, expand, and buckle, which can lead to structural failure and collapse.

**Question 5:**
What is the primary hazard associated with Class D fires, and why is water typically not used as an extinguishing agent?

**Answer:**
Class D fires involve combustible metals. The primary hazard is that many of these metals react violently with water, producing flammable hydrogen gas or exacerbating the fire, making water an unsuitable extinguishing agent.

**Question 6:**
What are "concealed spaces" in a building, and how do they facilitate fire spread?

**Answer:**
Concealed spaces are voids within building elements (e.g., between walls, above ceilings). They facilitate fire spread by acting like chimneys, allowing fire and smoke to travel rapidly and often undetected to other parts of the building through unsealed penetrations.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Fire Safety is Integral to Building Planning:** It's not an afterthought but a fundamental consideration from conceptualization to construction.
*   **Understanding Fire Dynamics is Key:** Knowing how fires start, spread, and affect materials informs design decisions.
*   **Material Selection Matters:** The combustibility and fire resistance properties of materials directly impact a building's safety.
*   **Compartmentation and Firestopping are Crucial:** These design strategies are essential for limiting the extent of fire and smoke spread.
*   **Building Codes Provide Essential Guidance:** Adhering to relevant building codes and standards is paramount for ensuring fire safety.
*   **Human Factors are Significant:** A large percentage of fires are caused by human error or negligence, highlighting the importance of education and awareness.
*   **Thermal Effects are Critical:** The way high temperatures degrade building materials dictates structural behavior during a fire and the potential for collapse.
