# Urban Transportation Planning: Module 4 - Transportation and Land Use

## Topic: Lowry Model - Structure, Features, and Model Equation System

This module explores the fundamental relationship between transportation and land use. The Lowry Model is a crucial tool in understanding how these two elements interact and influence urban development.

*   Identify the key features and assumptions of the Lowry Model.

*   Recognize the model's strengths and limitations in transportation and land use planning.
*   Apply the concepts of the Lowry Model to understand urban spatial processes.

### 1. Introduction to the Lowry Model

The Lowry Model, developed by Ira S. Lowry in the 1960s, is a **spatial interaction model** designed to forecast the distribution of population and employment in a metropolitan area. It provides a framework for understanding how different urban activities, particularly those related to retail and residential growth, are influenced by the spatial distribution of basic employment.

**Key Concept:** **Basic Employment**: These are economic activities that generate income from outside the metropolitan area (e.g., manufacturing, higher education, government). They are considered the "engine" of urban growth.

**Key Concept:** **Service Employment**: These are economic activities that serve the needs of the local population, including those employed in basic industries and their families (e.g., retail, personal services, local government).

---

### 2. Structure of the Lowry Model

The Lowry Model is structured as a sequential process that simulates the spatial allocation of urban activities. It typically involves the following core components and steps:

#### 2.1. Model Components:

*   **Activity Locations:**
    *   **Basic Employment (E_b):** Predetermined locations of industries that export goods or services. These are the initial drivers of urban growth.
    *   **Service Employment (E_s):** Generated by the presence of basic employment and the population it supports.
    *   **Households (H):** Directly related to the total employment (basic + service).
    *   **Retail/Service Floor Space (R):** Required to support the households and their consumption of goods and services.

*   **Spatial Units (Zones):** The metropolitan area is divided into a grid of zones or a set of defined geographical areas.

*   **Interaction Functions:**
    *   **Employment-Serving-Households Function:** Determines the amount of service employment attracted to a zone based on the population in that zone and surrounding zones.
    *   **Households-Locating-Near-Employment Function:** Determines the residential attractiveness of zones based on their proximity to employment centers.
    *   **Retail-Serving-Households Function:** Determines the amount of retail floor space attracted to a zone based on the population in that zone and surrounding zones.

#### 2.2. Model Flow (Sequential Process):

1.  **Locate Basic Employment (E_b):** The initial step involves specifying the fixed locations and magnitudes of basic employment in each zone. This is often based on current data or future projections.
2.  **Generate Service Employment (E_s):**
    *   Service employment is a function of total employment (basic + service) and population.
    *   The model assumes that service employees are also residents of the metropolitan area.
    *   A **multiplier effect** is implicit, where basic employment creates a demand for service employment.
3.  **Generate Households (H):**
    *   Households are generated based on the total employment (basic + service).
    *   A fixed labor participation rate (e.g., number of employed persons per household) is used.
4.  **Locate Service Employment (E_s):**
    *   Service employment is allocated to zones based on its **attractiveness**.
    *   Attractiveness is typically related to:
        *   The presence of basic employment in a zone.
        *   The total population in the zone and its surrounding zones (market potential).
        *   Accessibility to other zones.
    *   The model uses an **iterative process** to simulate this allocation.
5.  **Locate Households (H):**
    *   Households are allocated to zones based on their **attractiveness**.
    *   Attractiveness is typically related to:
        *   Proximity to their places of employment (both basic and service).
        *   Availability of retail and service facilities in the zone and surrounding zones.
        *   Other amenities and land use characteristics.
6.  **Locate Retail/Service Floor Space (R):**
    *   Retail/service floor space is allocated to zones based on the demand generated by households and their purchasing power.
    *   Attractiveness is typically related to:
        *   The number of households in the zone and surrounding zones (market potential).
        *   Accessibility to consumers.

### 3. Key Features and Assumptions of the Lowry Model

The Lowry Model, while influential, relies on several key features and assumptions that shape its output and applicability.

#### 3.1. Key Features:

*   **Spatial Interaction:** The model explicitly models the spatial interaction between different urban activities (employment, households, retail).
*   **Sequential Allocation:** It follows a step-by-step process, simulating how urban development unfolds over time.
*   **Market Potential:** It incorporates the concept of market potential, where activities are attracted to zones with a high concentration of consumers or opportunities.
*   **Attractiveness Factors:** Spatial allocation is driven by attractiveness functions that consider factors like distance, accessibility, and the presence of other activities.
*   **Exogenous Basic Employment:** The growth and location of basic employment are treated as external factors that drive the model.
*   **Iterative Process:** The allocation of service employment, households, and retail is often an iterative process to reach a stable spatial distribution.

#### 3.2. Key Assumptions:

*   **Fixed Basic Employment:** The location and size of basic employment are assumed to be fixed or determined exogenously, not influenced by the model's outputs.
*   **Constant Economic Multipliers:** The relationship between basic employment, service employment, and households is assumed to be constant.
*   **Uniform Population/Household Characteristics:** The model often assumes homogeneous households and consumers, with uniform labor participation rates and consumption patterns.
*   **Accessibility is Key:** Spatial patterns are primarily determined by accessibility and proximity to employment and services.
*   **No Feedback Loops (initially):** Early versions of the model did not explicitly account for how residential location choices might influence the location of future basic employment.
*   **Gravity Models for Interaction:** The spatial interaction between activities is often modeled using gravity-type functions, where interaction decreases with distance.
*   **No Congestion Effects:** The model does not typically account for the disutility of congestion or travel time as a primary determinant of location.
*   **Sufficient Land Availability:** It assumes sufficient land is available in all zones for development.

### 4. The Lowry Model Equation System

The Lowry Model's core is its system of equations that mathematically describe the spatial allocation of activities. While specific formulations can vary, the general structure involves gravity-type models for spatial interaction.

Let:

*   $Z$ be the number of zones in the study area.
*   $E_{b,i}$ be the basic employment in zone $i$.
*   $E_{s,i}$ be the service employment in zone $i$.
*   $H_i$ be the number of households in zone $i$.
*   $R_i$ be the retail/service floor space in zone $i$.

**Generalized Equations:**

**1. Service Employment Allocation:**

Service employment in zone $i$ is attracted by the total employment and population in all zones, discounted by distance or travel impedance.

$E_{s,i} = \alpha \sum_{j=1}^{Z} (E_{b,j} + E_{s,j}) \cdot f(d_{ij})$

Where:

*   $\alpha$: A coefficient representing the ratio of service employment to total employment.
*   $E_{b,j}$: Basic employment in zone $j$.
*   $E_{s,j}$: Service employment in zone $j$.
*   $f(d_{ij})$: A distance decay function (e.g., $1/d_{ij}^k$ or $e^{-\beta d_{ij}}$), representing the impedance of travel between zone $i$ and zone $j$. $d_{ij}$ is the distance between zone $i$ and zone $j$.

**2. Household Allocation:**

Households are attracted by employment opportunities and the availability of retail services, again discounted by distance.

$H_i = \beta \sum_{j=1}^{Z} (E_{b,j} + E_{s,j} + R_j) \cdot g(d_{ij})$

Where:

*   $\beta$: A coefficient representing the number of households per unit of total employment and retail activity (combining labor participation and consumption).
*   $E_{b,j}$: Basic employment in zone $j$.
*   $E_{s,j}$: Service employment in zone $j$.
*   $R_j$: Retail/service floor space in zone $j$.
*   $g(d_{ij})$: Another distance decay function, possibly different from $f(d_{ij})$, representing the impedance for households to travel to employment and retail centers.

**3. Retail/Service Floor Space Allocation:**

Retail floor space in zone $i$ is attracted by the number of households and their purchasing power in all zones, discounted by distance.

$R_i = \gamma \sum_{j=1}^{Z} H_j \cdot h(d_{ij})$

Where:

*   $\gamma$: A coefficient representing the amount of retail floor space required per household (consumption coefficient).
*   $H_j$: Number of households in zone $j$.
*   $h(d_{ij})$: A distance decay function representing the impedance for consumers to travel to retail centers.

**Iterative Process:**

These equations are often solved iteratively. For example:

*   Start with initial $E_{b,i}$ for all $i$.
*   Calculate an initial estimate of $E_{s,i}$ using the current values of $E_{b,j}$ and potentially an initial guess of $E_{s,j}$.
*   Calculate $H_i$ based on the current $E_{b,i}$ and $E_{s,i}$.
*   Recalculate $E_{s,i}$ using the newly calculated $H_j$ and updated employment.
*   Recalculate $H_i$ based on the updated $E_{s,i}$.
*   Allocate $R_i$ based on the latest $H_j$.
*   Repeat until the spatial distributions of employment, households, and retail stabilize.

**Example of Distance Decay Function:**

A common form for the distance decay function is a power function:

$f(d_{ij}) = d_{ij}^{-k}$

Or an exponential function:

$f(d_{ij}) = e^{-\beta d_{ij}}$

Where $k$ and $\beta$ are parameters that need to be calibrated based on observed data.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

What is the primary role of "basic employment" in the Lowry Model?

**Answer 1:**
Basic employment acts as the exogenous driver of urban growth in the Lowry Model. It generates income from outside the metropolitan area, which in turn creates demand for service employment and households within the area.

**Question 2:**

Explain the concept of "market potential" as used in the Lowry Model.

**Answer 2:**
Market potential refers to the attractiveness of a zone for certain activities (like service employment or retail) based on the concentration of potential customers (households) or opportunities (other employment) in that zone and its surrounding areas. The model typically uses gravity functions to measure this potential, where proximity and accessibility play crucial roles.

**Question 3:**

If the Lowry Model predicts a significant increase in service employment in a particular zone, what are the likely subsequent impacts on other urban activities within that zone or nearby?

**Answer 3:**
An increase in service employment would likely lead to:
*   **Increased demand for households:** As more people are employed in the zone, more households will be attracted to live nearby.
*   **Increased demand for retail and services:** The growing number of households will require more retail and service facilities to meet their needs.
*   **Potential for increased traffic and land use pressure:** Higher concentrations of people and activities can lead to congestion and greater demand for land.

**Question 4 (Conceptual Application):**

Imagine a city where a new major university (basic employment) is established in the outer suburbs. Using the principles of the Lowry Model, how might this impact the distribution of retail and households in the metropolitan area over time?

**Answer 4:**
*   **Initial Impact:** The university (basic employment) will directly create jobs and attract students (who might form a type of household or consumer base).
*   **Service Employment Growth:** The university itself will require support staff (service employment), and the growing population of students and employees will create demand for local services (restaurants, shops, housing services).
*   **Household Growth:** People attracted to the university for employment and education will form households. These households will likely seek housing that is reasonably accessible to the university.
*   **Retail Location:** Retail and service businesses will be attracted to zones that offer the highest market potential, meaning areas with a higher concentration of university employees, students, and their families. This could lead to the development of new commercial centers or the expansion of existing ones in areas surrounding the university or along major commuting routes.
*   **Spatial Redistribution:** The model suggests that the distribution of service employment and households will not be confined to the university zone itself but will spread to surrounding areas based on accessibility and attractiveness, potentially leading to suburban growth and the development of new employment and residential clusters.

---

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   The Lowry Model is a **predictive spatial allocation model** for urban activities.
*   It establishes a causal chain: **Basic Employment → Service Employment → Households → Retail/Services**.
*   **Distance and accessibility** are critical factors in all spatial interaction functions.
*   The model is **iterative**, meaning the outputs of one step feed back into subsequent steps to reach equilibrium.
*   Its primary strength lies in understanding the **interdependence of land use and transportation** patterns.
*   **Limitations** include its simplified assumptions about human behavior, the exogeneity of basic employment, and the lack of feedback from land use to basic employment location.
*   The model's parameters (coefficients and distance decay functions) need to be **calibrated** using real-world data.
