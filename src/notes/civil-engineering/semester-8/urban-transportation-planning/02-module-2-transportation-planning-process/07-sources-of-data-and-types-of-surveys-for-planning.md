# Urban Transportation Planning: Module 2 - Transportation Planning Process

## Topic: Sources of Data and Types of Surveys for Planning

This module focuses on the crucial first step in any transportation planning process: understanding the existing situation through data collection and analysis. This topic will equip you with the knowledge of where to find relevant data and how to conduct surveys to gather the information needed for effective transportation planning.

---

### Learning Outcomes:

Upon successful completion of this topic, you will be able to:

*   **Identify and describe various sources of existing transportation data.**
*   **Categorize and explain different types of transportation surveys.**
*   **Understand the purpose and methodology of key transportation surveys.**
*   **Recognize the strengths and limitations of different data sources and survey methods.**
*   **Select appropriate data sources and survey types for specific transportation planning objectives.**
*   **Understand the principles of data sampling and its importance in surveys.**

---

### 1. Sources of Existing Transportation Data

Before embarking on extensive data collection, it's essential to leverage readily available existing data. This saves time, resources, and provides a baseline understanding of the transportation system.

#### 1.1. Government and Public Agencies

*   **National, Regional, and Local Government Departments:**
    *   **Transportation Departments/Ministries:** Often possess comprehensive data on road networks, traffic volumes, public transit ridership, accident records, and infrastructure plans.
        *   **Example:** Ministry of Transport in a country, Department of Transportation in a state/province, Municipal Transportation Agency.
    *   **Planning Departments:** Provide land-use plans, zoning regulations, demographic data, and population projections, which are crucial for understanding travel demand.
        *   **Example:** City Planning Department, Regional Development Authority.
    *   **Statistical Agencies:** Collect and disseminate demographic, economic, and social data that influences travel behavior.
        *   **Example:** National Bureau of Statistics, Census Bureau.
    *   **Law Enforcement Agencies:** Maintain accident records, which are vital for identifying safety hotspots and planning mitigation measures.
        *   **Example:** Police Department Traffic Division.
    *   **Public Transit Authorities/Operators:** Hold detailed information on routes, schedules, ridership, fares, and fleet information.
        *   **Example:** Metropolitan Transit Authority, Regional Bus Company.

*   **Key Information Available:**
    *   Road network inventories (road types, lengths, capacities)
    *   Traffic volume counts (average daily traffic, peak hour volumes)
    *   Public transit ridership statistics
    *   Accident data (locations, severity, causes)
    *   Demographic and socioeconomic data
    *   Land use plans and zoning maps
    *   Existing transportation infrastructure plans and projects

#### 1.2. Private Sector Data Providers

*   **Telecommunications Companies:** Anonymized and aggregated mobile phone location data can provide insights into travel patterns, origin-destination flows, and modal split.
    *   **Example:** Data insights from cellular network usage.
*   **Navigation and Mapping Companies:** Data from GPS devices and navigation apps (e.g., Google Maps, Waze) can provide real-time traffic information, travel times, and origin-destination data.
    *   **Example:** Aggregated historical traffic data for route planning.
*   **Ride-sharing and Taxi Companies:** Data on trip origins, destinations, times, and routes can reveal travel demand patterns, especially for specific trip purposes.
    *   **Example:** Anonymized trip data from Uber or Lyft.
*   **Credit Card and Retail Companies:** Transaction data can sometimes be used to infer travel behavior related to shopping or dining.
    *   **Example:** Aggregated data on spending at retail centers.

*   **Key Information Available:**
    *   Origin-Destination (O-D) matrices (often aggregated)
    *   Travel times and speed data
    *   Modal split insights (e.g., proportion of trips by car, public transit)
    *   High-demand corridors and times

#### 1.3. Academic and Research Institutions

*   **Universities and Research Centers:** Conduct studies on transportation behavior, travel demand modeling, and emerging transportation technologies. Their research papers and reports can be valuable sources of data and analysis.
    *   **Example:** University transportation research institutes, think tanks focused on urban development.

*   **Key Information Available:**
    *   Research findings on travel behavior and demand elasticity
    *   Case studies and best practices
    *   Advanced modeling techniques and data analysis methods

#### 1.4. Open Data Portals and Online Resources

*   **Government Open Data Initiatives:** Many cities and countries are making transportation data publicly available through online portals.
    *   **Example:** data.gov, city-specific open data portals.
*   **Online Mapping Services:** Platforms like Google Maps and OpenStreetMap provide detailed road network information and sometimes traffic data.

*   **Key Information Available:**
    *   Road networks, points of interest, transit routes
    *   Historical traffic data (depending on the platform)

**IMPORTANT POINT TO REMEMBER:** Always verify the accuracy, recency, and completeness of existing data. Understand the methodology used to collect the data and its limitations.

### 2. Types of Transportation Surveys

When existing data is insufficient or outdated, surveys become essential to gather specific, up-to-date information about travel behavior, preferences, and opinions.

#### 2.1. Home-Based Surveys

These surveys collect data on trips made by household members from their residences.

*   **Household Travel Survey (HTS):**
    *   **Purpose:** To understand the travel patterns of residents, including trip purpose, destination, mode of transport, time of travel, duration, and socioeconomic characteristics of travelers. This is a cornerstone of transportation planning.
    *   **Methodology:** Typically involves a sample of households. Each household member records their trips over a defined period (e.g., 24 hours). Data can be collected through diaries, interviews, or online platforms.
    *   **Key Information:** O-D matrices, modal split, trip purpose distribution, travel time, daily travel diaries.
    *   **Example:** A survey asking residents of a city to record all trips they make in a 24-hour period.

*   **Dwelling Unit Survey:**
    *   **Purpose:** To collect data on the characteristics of housing units and their occupants, including household size, vehicle ownership, income, and employment status. This helps in understanding the potential for travel.
    *   **Methodology:** Involves surveying a sample of households.
    *   **Key Information:** Household size, income levels, car ownership, dwelling type.

#### 2.2. On-Board Surveys

These surveys collect data from passengers while they are using a specific mode of transport.

*   **Public Transit On-Board Survey:**
    *   **Purpose:** To understand passenger characteristics, trip origins and destinations, travel purpose, and satisfaction with public transit services.
    *   **Methodology:** Passengers are intercepted on transit vehicles (buses, trains) and asked to complete a questionnaire or be interviewed. Can also include "on-board screen" surveys.
    *   **Key Information:** Origin of the current trip, destination of the current trip, trip purpose, boarding and alighting stops, passenger demographics.
    *   **Example:** Surveying bus passengers as they board or alight.

*   **Taxi/Ride-hailing Survey:**
    *   **Purpose:** To gather information on trip characteristics and passenger satisfaction from taxi and ride-hailing users.
    *   **Methodology:** Can be conducted through in-vehicle questionnaires or post-trip surveys via the app.
    *   **Key Information:** Trip origin, destination, fare, passenger demographics, purpose of the trip.

#### 2.3. Screenline/Cordon Surveys

These surveys focus on counting and classifying traffic crossing a defined line or boundary.

*   **Screenline Surveys:**
    *   **Purpose:** To estimate traffic volumes crossing a conceptual line that divides a study area into zones, often used to estimate inter-zone travel and validate traffic models.
    *   **Methodology:** Manual counts, automatic traffic counters, or observational methods are used to record vehicles crossing specific routes that form a screenline.
    *   **Key Information:** Traffic volumes crossing a specific line, modal split of traffic crossing the line.
    *   **Example:** Counting all vehicles crossing a river or a major arterial road that splits a city into two parts.

*   **Cordon Surveys:**
    *   **Purpose:** To count and classify vehicles and persons entering or leaving a specific geographical area (e.g., a central business district) during a defined period.
    *   **Methodology:** Similar to screenline surveys but focused on a closed boundary. Often involves manual observation at entry/exit points.
    *   **Key Information:** Inbound/outbound traffic volumes, vehicle types, passenger counts in vehicles.
    *   **Example:** Counting all vehicles and people entering and leaving the downtown core of a city.

#### 2.4. Origin-Destination (O-D) Surveys

These surveys directly aim to determine where trips start and end and the routes taken.

*   **Roadside Interview Surveys:**
    *   **Purpose:** To gather information on trip origin, destination, purpose, and the route taken from drivers at specific locations.
    *   **Methodology:** Vehicles are stopped at roadside locations, and drivers are interviewed. This can be time-consuming and may introduce bias due to driver refusal or interview effects.
    *   **Key Information:** Trip O-D, trip purpose, route choice, vehicle type.
    *   **Example:** Stopping cars at major intersections and asking drivers about their trip details.

*   **Tag-on/Tag-off Surveys:**
    *   **Purpose:** To track the movement of vehicles or people over a network.
    *   **Methodology:** A "tag" (e.g., a card, a GPS device) is given to a vehicle or person at their origin, and they are asked to return it or report their destination at a specific point.
    *   **Key Information:** Trip O-D, travel times, route adherence.
    *   **Example:** Distributing survey cards to drivers entering a toll road and asking them to return them when they exit.

#### 2.5. Special Purpose Surveys

These surveys focus on specific aspects of the transportation system.

*   **Parking Surveys:**
    *   **Purpose:** To understand parking demand, duration, occupancy rates, and user characteristics in specific areas.
    *   **Methodology:** Direct observation and counting of parked vehicles in different types of parking facilities (on-street, off-street).
    *   **Key Information:** Parking duration, occupancy rates, types of vehicles parked, parking duration by land use.
    *   **Example:** Observing and recording parking duration for vehicles in a downtown commercial area.

*   **Attitude and Perception Surveys:**
    *   **Purpose:** To gauge public opinion, satisfaction levels, and preferences regarding various transportation services and infrastructure.
    *   **Methodology:** Questionnaires, focus groups, or in-depth interviews.
    *   **Key Information:** User satisfaction with public transit, perceptions of safety, willingness to pay for services, attitudes towards cycling or walking.
    *   **Example:** Surveying residents about their satisfaction with bus service frequency and reliability.

*   **Freight Surveys:**
    *   **Purpose:** To understand goods movement patterns, including origins, destinations, commodities, and modes of transport.
    *   **Methodology:** Surveys of businesses, trucking companies, and logistics providers.
    *   **Key Information:** Freight O-D, types of goods transported, modal split for freight.

**IMPORTANT POINT TO REMEMBER:** The choice of survey method depends on the specific data requirements, available resources, the target population, and the desired level of accuracy.

### 3. Principles of Data Sampling

In most transportation surveys, it's impractical or impossible to survey the entire population. Sampling allows us to collect data from a representative subset of the population.

#### 3.1. Why Sample?

*   **Cost-effectiveness:** Reduces the financial burden of data collection.
*   **Time efficiency:** Speeds up the data collection and analysis process.
*   **Feasibility:** Makes it possible to collect data from large populations.

#### 3.2. Key Concepts in Sampling

*   **Population:** The entire group of individuals, households, or entities that the survey aims to represent.
    *   **Example:** All residents of a city, all users of a specific bus route.

*   **Sample:** A subset of the population selected for the survey.
    *   **Example:** 1,000 households selected from the city's residents.

*   **Sampling Frame:** A list or map from which the sample is drawn.
    *   **Example:** A list of all registered households in a city, a list of all registered vehicles.

*   **Sampling Unit:** The individual member of the population to be selected.
    *   **Example:** A household, an individual, a vehicle.

*   **Sample Size:** The number of sampling units included in the sample. The larger the sample size, generally the greater the precision.

*   **Sampling Error:** The difference between the sample result and the true population value due to random chance in the sampling process.

*   **Bias:** A systematic error introduced in the sampling process that leads to an unrepresentative sample.

#### 3.3. Types of Sampling Methods

*   **Probability Sampling:** Each member of the population has a known, non-zero probability of being selected. This allows for statistical inference.
    *   **Simple Random Sampling:** Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.
        *   **Example:** Drawing names out of a hat.
    *   **Systematic Sampling:** Selecting every k-th member from a sampling frame after a random start.
        *   **Example:** Selecting every 10th household from a list.
    *   **Stratified Sampling:** Dividing the population into subgroups (strata) based on certain characteristics (e.g., income, geographic area) and then drawing a random sample from each stratum. This ensures representation of all subgroups.
        *   **Example:** Stratifying a city into low, medium, and high-income neighborhoods and then sampling from each.
    *   **Cluster Sampling:** Dividing the population into clusters (e.g., blocks, neighborhoods) and then randomly selecting clusters to survey. All members within the selected clusters are then surveyed.
        *   **Example:** Randomly selecting specific city blocks and surveying all households within those blocks.

*   **Non-Probability Sampling:** The selection of sampling units is not based on random chance. This can introduce bias.
    *   **Convenience Sampling:** Selecting individuals who are most accessible.
        *   **Example:** Surveying people who happen to be at a shopping mall.
    *   **Quota Sampling:** Selecting individuals to meet pre-determined quotas for certain characteristics. Similar to stratified sampling but non-random within strata.
        *   **Example:** Interviewing a specific number of men and women, and a specific number of older and younger people.
    *   **Judgmental Sampling:** The researcher uses their judgment to select the sample based on their knowledge of the population.
        *   **Example:** Selecting key informants or influential individuals for interviews.

**IMPORTANT POINT TO REMEMBER:** For robust transportation planning and reliable conclusions, probability sampling methods are generally preferred due to their ability to provide statistical validity.
