# Water and Air Quality Management - Module 1: Water Quality - Impurities in Water

## Topic: Water-borne Diseases

---

### 1. Introduction to Water-borne Diseases

Water-borne diseases are illnesses caused by pathogenic microorganisms that are transmitted through contaminated water. This contamination typically occurs when water sources are exposed to fecal matter from humans or animals, or through inadequate sanitation and sewage disposal.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Pathogens:** Disease-causing microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and helminths.
*   **Contamination:** The introduction of harmful substances or microorganisms into water.
*   **Transmission:** The process by which pathogens move from an infected source to a susceptible host.
*   **Fecal-Oral Route:** The primary mode of transmission for most water-borne diseases, where pathogens are ingested through contaminated food or water that has come into contact with fecal matter.

**Significance:**

Water-borne diseases pose a significant threat to public health globally, particularly in regions with poor sanitation infrastructure and limited access to safe drinking water. They can lead to widespread outbreaks, high morbidity (illness) and mortality (death) rates, especially among vulnerable populations like children and the elderly.

---

### 2. Major Categories of Water-borne Pathogens

Pathogens responsible for water-borne diseases can be broadly classified into the following groups:

#### 2.1. Bacteria

*   **Definition:** Single-celled microorganisms that reproduce rapidly. Some bacteria are beneficial, but many are pathogenic.
*   **Significance:** Many common water-borne diseases are caused by bacteria. They can survive in water for extended periods, especially in warm and nutrient-rich environments.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **_Escherichia coli_ (E. coli):** Primarily found in the intestines of warm-blooded animals. Certain strains can cause severe gastrointestinal illness, including bloody diarrhea and kidney failure.
    *   **_Salmonella_ spp.:** Causes salmonellosis, characterized by diarrhea, fever, and abdominal cramps. Commonly associated with poultry and animal feces.
    *   **_Shigella_ spp.:** Causes shigellosis (dysentery), characterized by bloody diarrhea, fever, and stomach cramps. Highly contagious.
    *   **_Vibrio cholerae_:** Causes cholera, a severe diarrheal disease that can lead to rapid dehydration and death if untreated. Associated with contaminated water, particularly in areas with poor sanitation.
    *   **_Campylobacter jejuni_:** A leading cause of bacterial diarrheal disease worldwide. Found in the intestines of poultry and other animals.
    *   **_Legionella pneumophila_:** Causes Legionnaires' disease, a severe form of pneumonia. Found in warm water systems like cooling towers and hot water tanks.
    *   **_Clostridium botulinum_:** Produces a potent neurotoxin that causes botulism, a severe paralytic illness. Found in soil and sediments, can contaminate water sources.

#### 2.2. Viruses

*   **Definition:** Much smaller than bacteria, viruses require living cells to replicate. They are often shed in large numbers in the feces of infected individuals.
*   **Significance:** Viruses are often more resistant to disinfection than bacteria and can cause outbreaks even at very low concentrations in water.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Hepatitis A Virus (HAV):** Causes hepatitis A, an inflammation of the liver. Symptoms include fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, and jaundice.
    *   **Norovirus:** A highly contagious virus that causes gastroenteritis ("stomach flu"), characterized by vomiting and diarrhea.
    *   **Rotavirus:** A common cause of severe diarrhea in infants and young children.
    *   **Enteroviruses (e.g., Poliovirus):** Can cause a range of illnesses, from mild flu-like symptoms to more serious conditions like meningitis and paralysis (in the case of poliovirus).
    *   **Adenoviruses:** Can cause respiratory illnesses, conjunctivitis (pink eye), and gastroenteritis.

#### 2.3. Protozoa

*   **Definition:** Single-celled eukaryotic microorganisms. Many protozoa are free-living, but some are parasitic and can cause disease.
*   **Significance:** Protozoa, especially their cyst forms, are often highly resistant to disinfection treatments like chlorination.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **_Giardia lamblia_ (Giardiasis):** Causes giardiasis, an intestinal infection characterized by diarrhea, gas, stomach cramps, and nausea. Cysts are shed in feces and can survive in cold water.
    *   **_Cryptosporidium parvum_ (Cryptosporidiosis):** Causes cryptosporidiosis, a diarrheal illness. Oocysts are highly resistant to chlorine.
    *   **_Entamoeba histolytica_ (Amoebiasis):** Causes amoebic dysentery, characterized by bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain, and fever. Can also form abscesses in organs like the liver.
    *   **_Cyclospora cayetanensis_:** Causes cyclosporiasis, an intestinal illness with diarrhea, loss of appetite, and fatigue.

#### 2.4. Helminths (Worms)

*   **Definition:** Multicellular parasitic worms. They are transmitted through ingesting eggs or larvae present in contaminated water or soil.
*   **Significance:** While less common than bacterial or viral infections in developed countries, helminthic infections are prevalent in many parts of the world and can cause chronic health problems.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **_Ascaris lumbricoides_ (Roundworm):** Causes ascariasis. Ingesting eggs from contaminated water or soil can lead to intestinal blockage and malnutrition.
    *   **_Ancylostoma duodenale_ and _Necator americanus_ (Hookworm):** Causes hookworm infection. Larvae in contaminated soil can penetrate the skin, and ingested eggs hatch in the intestine. Can cause anemia due to blood loss.
    *   **_Trichuris trichiura_ (Whipworm):** Causes trichuriasis. Ingesting eggs from contaminated soil or water.
    *   **_Schistosoma_ spp. (Schistosomiasis/Bilharzia):** Caused by parasitic flatworms. Larvae released from freshwater snails penetrate the skin of people who come into contact with contaminated water.

### 3. Transmission Pathways and Sources of Contamination

Water-borne diseases are primarily transmitted through the consumption of water contaminated with feces from infected individuals or animals.

**Key Pathways:**

*   **Contaminated Drinking Water:** This is the most direct route. When water treatment processes are inadequate or fail, or when distribution systems are compromised, pathogens can reach tap water.
*   **Recreational Water Use:** Swimming, bathing, or engaging in water sports in contaminated water bodies (lakes, rivers, swimming pools) can lead to ingestion of water containing pathogens.
*   **Food Contamination:**
    *   **Irrigation:** Using contaminated water to irrigate crops can lead to produce being contaminated with pathogens.
    *   **Washing Food:** Washing fruits and vegetables with contaminated water.
    *   **Seafood:** Consuming raw or undercooked shellfish from contaminated waters.
*   **Poor Personal Hygiene:** Lack of proper handwashing after using the toilet can spread pathogens to food and water.

**Sources of Contamination:**

*   **Human Feces:**
    *   **Untreated or inadequately treated sewage:** Discharge of raw sewage into rivers, lakes, or oceans.
    *   **Overflows from combined sewer systems:** During heavy rainfall, sewage can overflow into waterways.
    *   **Leaking septic systems:** Seepage from poorly maintained septic tanks.
    *   **Excretions from infected individuals in areas without sanitation:** Open defecation.
*   **Animal Feces:**
    *   **Agricultural runoff:** Feces from livestock and poultry farms can wash into water sources.
    *   **Wild animal feces:** Feces from birds, rodents, and other wildlife in watersheds.
*   **Wastewater Treatment Plant Malfunctions:** Incomplete treatment can release pathogens into the environment.
*   **Contaminated Water Distribution Systems:** Leaks in pipes, cross-connections with sewage lines, or backflow from contaminated sources.

### 4. Health Impacts and Symptoms

The health impacts of water-borne diseases vary depending on the pathogen, the dose ingested, and the individual's susceptibility.

**Common Symptoms:**

*   **Gastrointestinal Issues:** Diarrhea (watery or bloody), vomiting, nausea, abdominal cramps, loss of appetite.
*   **Fever:** A common systemic response to infection.
*   **Dehydration:** Significant fluid loss due to vomiting and diarrhea, which can be life-threatening.
*   **Fatigue and Weakness:** General feeling of being unwell.
*   **Specific Symptoms:** Depending on the pathogen, additional symptoms can include jaundice (Hepatitis A), paralysis (Poliovirus), skin rashes, and organ damage.

**Long-term Effects:**

*   **Malnutrition:** Particularly in children, repeated gastrointestinal infections can lead to poor nutrient absorption.
*   **Stunted Growth and Cognitive Impairment:** Chronic infections in childhood can affect development.
*   **Organ Damage:** Some pathogens can cause long-term damage to the liver, kidneys, or other organs.
*   **Death:** Severe dehydration or complications from infection can be fatal.

### 5. Estimation Techniques (for Pathogens and Indicators)

Estimating the presence and concentration of pathogens in water is crucial for assessing its safety. Direct enumeration of all possible pathogens is impractical. Therefore, **indicator organisms** are used.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Direct Detection:** Identifying and counting specific pathogens.
*   **Indicator Organisms:** Organisms that are commonly found in the feces of warm-blooded animals and are generally present in greater numbers than specific pathogens. Their presence suggests fecal contamination and the potential presence of pathogens.

#### 5.1. Indicator Organisms

*   **Definition:** Microorganisms whose presence in water indicates potential fecal contamination and the possible presence of enteric pathogens.
*   **Ideal Characteristics of an Indicator Organism:**
    *   Always present in feces of warm-blooded animals.
    *   Present in numbers at least as great as the enteric pathogens.
    *   Survives in water longer than the hardiest pathogens, but not so long as to be undetectable after pathogens have died off.
    *   Not pathogenic itself.
    *   Easy and inexpensive to detect and quantify.
    *   Does not grow in the environment.

*   **Commonly Used Indicator Organisms:**

    *   **Total Coliforms:**
        *   **Definition:** A broad group of bacteria found in soil, water, and vegetation, as well as in the intestines of warm-blooded animals.
        *   **Significance:** Their presence alone doesn't definitively indicate fecal contamination, as they can be found in non-fecal sources. However, their presence signals potential problems with the water treatment or distribution system.
        *   **Estimation Techniques:**
            *   **Multiple Tube Fermentation (MTF):** A probabilistic method where water samples are inoculated into a series of tubes containing selective culture media. Gas production indicates the presence of coliforms.
            *   **Membrane Filtration (MF):** Water is filtered through a sterile membrane that retains bacteria. The membrane is then placed on a selective agar medium. Colonies that grow are counted.
            *   **Colilert Test (for E. coli and total coliforms):** Uses specific chromogenic and fluorogenic substrates that are metabolized by coliforms and E. coli, producing color or fluorescence.

    *   **Fecal Coliforms (including _E. coli_):**
        *   **Definition:** A subgroup of total coliforms that are preferentially found in the intestinal tract of warm-blooded animals.
        *   **Significance:** Their presence is a stronger indicator of recent fecal contamination than total coliforms. _E. coli_ is considered the most reliable indicator of fecal contamination.
        *   **Estimation Techniques:**
            *   **MTF:** Using specific temperature (e.g., 44.5°C) to favor fecal coliforms.
            *   **MF:** Using selective media incubated at higher temperatures.
            *   **Colilert Test:** Specifically detects _E. coli_.

    *   **Enterococci:**
        *   **Definition:** Bacteria found in the feces of warm-blooded animals, including humans and animals.
        *   **Significance:** Generally more resistant to environmental conditions and disinfection than coliforms, making them a good indicator for longer-term contamination or in marine and freshwater recreational waters.
        *   **Estimation Techniques:**
            *   **MF:** Using selective media.
            *   **Enzyme Assays:** Like Colilert Enterolert.

    *   **_Clostridium perfringens_:**
        *   **Definition:** An anaerobic bacterium found in feces. Its spores are highly resistant to disinfection and environmental stress.
        *   **Significance:** Its presence can indicate past fecal contamination, even when coliforms are absent due to environmental degradation. Useful for assessing the integrity of water treatment and distribution systems.
        *   **Estimation Techniques:**
            *   **MF:** Using selective media.

#### 5.2. Direct Pathogen Detection

While less common for routine monitoring, direct detection methods are used for specific investigations or outbreak situations.

*   **Culture Methods:** Growing specific pathogens in the lab.
*   **Molecular Methods (e.g., PCR - Polymerase Chain Reaction):** Detects specific genetic material of pathogens. Highly sensitive and specific.
*   **Microscopy:** Direct observation of larger parasites like protozoa and helminth eggs under a microscope.

### 6. Prevention and Control Strategies

Preventing water-borne diseases relies on a multi-barrier approach to ensure the safety of drinking water and prevent contamination of water sources.

**Key Strategies:**

*   **Source Water Protection:**
    *   Protecting watersheds from agricultural, industrial, and residential pollution.
    *   Controlling land use in catchment areas.
    *   Preventing direct discharge of untreated sewage or animal waste into water sources.
*   **Adequate Water Treatment:**
    *   **Coagulation and Flocculation:** Removing suspended solids.
    *   **Sedimentation:** Allowing heavier particles to settle.
    *   **Filtration:** Removing remaining suspended particles and some microorganisms.
    *   **Disinfection:** Killing or inactivating pathogens. Common methods include:
        *   **Chlorination:** Effective against many bacteria and viruses, but less effective against some protozoa like _Cryptosporidium_.
        *   **Ozonation:** More potent disinfectant than chlorine, effective against a wider range of pathogens, but doesn't leave a residual.
        *   **UV Irradiation:** Effective against most microorganisms, including protozoa, but requires clear water and doesn't leave a residual.
*   **Safe Water Storage and Distribution:**
    *   Maintaining the integrity of water pipes to prevent recontamination.
    *   Ensuring secure storage of water in homes (e.g., using clean containers).
*   **Sanitation and Hygiene:**
    *   **Proper Sewage Disposal:** Constructing and maintaining effective sewage treatment plants and septic systems.
    *   **Promoting Handwashing:** Encouraging regular handwashing with soap and clean water, especially after using the toilet and before preparing food.
    *   **Safe Food Practices:** Proper cooking, storage, and washing of food.
*   **Public Awareness and Education:**
    *   Educating communities about the risks of contaminated water and promoting safe water practices.
*   **Regular Monitoring and Surveillance:**
    *   Testing water quality regularly for indicator organisms and pathogens.
    *   Monitoring public health for outbreaks of water-borne diseases.
*   **Emergency Response Plans:**
    *   Having plans in place to respond to water contamination incidents and outbreaks.

### 9. Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **Definition:** A water-borne disease is an illness caused by pathogenic microorganisms transmitted through contaminated water.
    **Primary Route of Transmission:** The primary route of transmission is the fecal-oral route, where pathogens are ingested through contaminated water or food that has come into contact with fecal matter.
2.  **Categories of Pathogens:**
    *   **Bacteria:** Single-celled microorganisms. Example: _Vibrio cholerae_ causes cholera.
    *   **Viruses:** Much smaller than bacteria, requiring living cells to replicate. Example: Hepatitis A Virus causes hepatitis A.
    *   **Protozoa:** Single-celled eukaryotic microorganisms. Example: _Cryptosporidium parvum_ causes cryptosporidiosis.
    *   *(Note: Helminths were also discussed)*
3.  **Indicator Organisms:** Indicator organisms are microorganisms commonly found in the feces of warm-blooded animals and are generally present in greater numbers than specific pathogens. They are used because directly testing for all possible pathogens is impractical, time-consuming, and expensive. Their presence suggests fecal contamination and thus the potential presence of pathogens.
4.  **Total Coliforms vs. Fecal Coliforms (_E. coli_):**
    *   **Total Coliforms:** A broader group found in soil, water, and vegetation, as well as intestines. Their presence signals potential issues with water treatment or distribution but not necessarily recent fecal contamination.
    *   **Fecal Coliforms (_E. coli_):** A subgroup specifically found in the intestinal tract of warm-blooded animals. Their presence is a stronger indicator of recent fecal contamination and the likely presence of enteric pathogens. _E. coli_ is considered the most reliable indicator.
5.  **Sources of Fecal Contamination:**
    *   Untreated or inadequately treated sewage (e.g., from faulty sewage treatment plants, leaking pipes, or raw sewage discharge).
    *   Animal feces (e.g., agricultural runoff from livestock farms, wildlife contamination in watersheds).
6.  **Prevention and Control Strategies:**
    *   **Community Level:** Source water protection, effective water treatment (filtration, disinfection), proper sewage disposal, maintaining water distribution systems, regular water quality monitoring.
    *   **Individual Level:** Practicing good personal hygiene (especially handwashing), safe water storage at home, proper food handling and preparation, boiling water if its safety is questionable.
7.  **Pathogen Category and Symptoms:**
    *   _Giardia lamblia_ cysts belong to the **Protozoa** category.
    *   Typical symptoms of Giardiasis include diarrhea, gas, stomach cramps, nausea, and sometimes weight loss.

---

### 10. Important Points to Remember

*   **Fecal contamination is the root cause** of most water-borne diseases.
*   **Pathogens can survive in water** for varying lengths of time, with protozoan cysts and bacterial spores being particularly resilient.
*   **_E. coli_ is the most reliable indicator** for recent fecal contamination.
*   A **multi-barrier approach** is essential for effective water safety.
*   **Sanitation and hygiene are as critical as water treatment** in preventing disease transmission.
*   **Vulnerable populations (children, elderly, immunocompromised)** are at higher risk from water-borne illnesses.
*   **Consistent monitoring and maintenance** of water infrastructure are vital.
