# WATER AND AIR QUALITY MANAGEMENT

## Module 2: Water Treatment: Aeration and Types of Aerators

### Topic: Estimation of Coagulant Dose

---

### 1. Introduction to Coagulation

**Coagulation** is a crucial step in water treatment, primarily used to remove suspended and colloidal particles that are too small to be effectively removed by sedimentation or filtration alone. These tiny particles, often negatively charged, are stabilized by natural repulsive forces. Coagulation involves the addition of chemicals called **coagulants** to neutralize these charges and promote the formation of larger, settleable aggregates.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Suspended Solids:** Particles that are visible to the naked eye and can be settled out by gravity.
*   **Colloidal Solids:** Extremely small particles (ranging from 0.001 to 1 micrometer) that remain dispersed and do not settle by gravity. They often carry a negative surface charge.
*   **Turbidity:** A measure of the cloudiness or haziness of water caused by suspended solids. It's a common indicator of water quality.
*   **Zeta Potential:** A measure of the electrical potential at the slipping plane of a colloidal particle, indicating the degree of electrostatic repulsion between particles. Lower zeta potential (closer to zero) leads to particle destabilization.
*   **Destabilization:** The process of reducing the repulsive forces between colloidal particles, allowing them to aggregate.
*   **Flocculation:** A subsequent process where gentle mixing encourages the destabilized particles (now called microflocs) to collide and form larger, denser aggregates called **flocs**.

**Why is Coagulant Dose Estimation Important?**

*   **Effectiveness:** An insufficient dose will not effectively remove impurities, leading to poor water quality.
*   **Economy:** An overdose of coagulant is wasteful and can lead to increased chemical costs.
*   **Secondary Contamination:** Overdosing can introduce excess chemicals into the water, potentially affecting taste, odor, or even exceeding regulatory limits.
*   **Sludge Production:** The amount of sludge produced is directly related to the coagulant dose. Overdosing can lead to excessive sludge volumes, increasing disposal costs.

### 3. Types of Coagulants

Coagulants are typically metal salts that, when dissolved in water, release positively charged ions that neutralize the negative charges on colloidal particles.

**Common Coagulants:**

*   **Aluminum Sulfate (Alum):**
    *   Formula: Al₂(SO₄)₃·nH₂O
    *   Commonly used.
    *   Effective over a wide pH range but most effective in the pH range of 5.5-7.5.
    *   Can cause a significant drop in alkalinity and pH.
*   **Ferric Chloride:**
    *   Formula: FeCl₃
    *   Effective over a wider pH range than alum, particularly at lower pH.
    *   Less sensitive to temperature variations.
    *   More corrosive than alum.
*   **Ferric Sulfate:**
    *   Formula: Fe₂(SO₄)₃
    *   Similar to ferric chloride but generally less corrosive.
*   **Polyaluminum Chloride (PAC):**
    *   A pre-hydrolyzed aluminum coagulant.
    *   Has a higher charge density than alum, requiring lower doses.
    *   Less impact on pH and alkalinity compared to alum.
    *   More effective over a wider pH range.
*   **Organic Polymers:**
    *   Can be used as primary coagulants or as coagulant aids.
    *   They are long-chain molecules that can bridge particles together, enhancing floc formation and strength.

### 4. Factors Affecting Coagulant Dose

The optimal coagulant dose is not a fixed value and varies depending on several factors:

*   **Raw Water Quality:**
    *   **Turbidity:** Higher turbidity generally requires a higher coagulant dose.
    *   **pH:** Each coagulant has an optimal pH range for effectiveness. If the raw water pH is outside this range, pH adjustment chemicals (acids or bases) may be needed, indirectly affecting the coagulant dose.
    *   **Alkalinity:** Alkalinity (buffering capacity) influences the pH drop caused by coagulants. Sufficient alkalinity is needed for the chemical reactions to occur.
    *   **Temperature:** Coagulation efficiency can decrease at lower temperatures.
    *   **Nature of Suspended Solids:** The type, size, and surface charge of the particles influence the amount of coagulant needed for destabilization.
    *   **Presence of Organic Matter:** Dissolved organic matter can react with coagulants and affect their performance.

### 5. Methods for Estimating Coagulant Dose

Several methods are employed to determine the appropriate coagulant dose for a given water source. These methods range from empirical calculations to laboratory and pilot-scale trials.

#### 5.1. Empirical Methods (Rule-of-Thumb)

These methods are based on historical data and experience with similar water sources. They provide a starting point but are often refined by more accurate methods.

*   **Based on Turbidity:**
    *   **Formula:** Coagulant Dose (mg/L) ≈ K × Turbidity (NTU)
    *   Where 'K' is an empirical factor that varies with the type of coagulant, raw water characteristics, and treatment plant design.
    *   *Example:* For alum, K might range from 1 to 3. If turbidity is 50 NTU, the estimated alum dose could be 50-150 mg/L.

*   **Based on Alkalinity:**
    *   This method considers the alkalinity consumed by the coagulant.
    *   *Example:* Alum reacts with alkalinity as follows:
        Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 6HCO₃⁻ → 2Al(OH)₃↓ + 6CO₂ + 3SO₄²⁻
    *   The alkalinity consumed per mg/L of alum is calculated based on the stoichiometry. This is important for ensuring that the water's pH doesn't drop too low.

**Limitations of Empirical Methods:**
*   Highly dependent on the similarity of the current water quality to historical data.
*   Not precise and can lead to overdosing or underdosing.

#### 5.2. Jar Test (Bench-Scale)

The jar test is the most common and reliable method for determining the optimal coagulant dose and operating conditions (pH, mixing speeds, and times) for coagulation and flocculation.

**Procedure:**

1.  **Collect Raw Water:** Obtain a representative sample of the raw water to be treated.
2.  **Prepare Coagulant Solutions:** Prepare stock solutions of the coagulant at a known concentration.
3.  **Set Up Jar Apparatus:** Use a multiple-stirrer apparatus with beakers (jars) of a standard volume (e.g., 1 liter or 2 liters).
4.  **Add Coagulant:** To each beaker, add a different, incrementally increasing dose of the coagulant. Ensure at least 4-5 different doses are tested.
5.  **Mix:**
    *   **Rapid Mixing (Coagulation):** Stir vigorously for 1-2 minutes (e.g., 100-150 RPM) to disperse the coagulant rapidly and neutralize the charges.
    *   **Slow Mixing (Flocculation):** Reduce the stirring speed (e.g., 30-50 RPM) for 15-30 minutes to promote the formation of flocs.
6.  **Allow to Settle (Sedimentation):** Turn off the stirrers and allow the flocs to settle for a specified period (e.g., 15-30 minutes).
7.  **Evaluate Results:** Observe the clarity of the supernatant water, the size and settleability of the flocs, and measure the residual turbidity.
8.  **pH Adjustment (Optional but Recommended):** Repeat the jar test with pH adjustments to find the optimal pH for coagulation, as pH significantly impacts coagulant performance.

**Determining the Optimal Dose:**

*   The optimal dose is the lowest dose that produces the lowest residual turbidity, along with well-formed, settleable flocs.
*   Plot residual turbidity against coagulant dose to identify the minimum turbidity point.

**Example of a Jar Test Setup:**

| Beaker No. | Coagulant Dose (mg/L) | Observation of Flocs     | Residual Turbidity (NTU) |
| :--------- | :-------------------- | :----------------------- | :----------------------- |
| 1          | 10                    | Small, dispersed         | 45                       |
| 2          | 20                    | Small to medium, settle. | 15                       |
| 3          | 30                    | Large, dense, settle.    | 5                        |
| 4          | 40                    | Large, slightly dispersed | 8                        |
| 5          | 50                    | Floc breakup, dispersed  | 12                       |

*   **Optimal Dose:** 30 mg/L (based on lowest residual turbidity and good floc formation).

**Important Considerations for Jar Tests:**

*   Maintain consistent mixing speeds and times across all beakers.
*   Ensure accurate measurement of coagulant doses.
*   Perform tests at different pH values to determine the optimum pH.

#### 5.3. Pilot Plant Studies

For larger water treatment plants, pilot plants are used to simulate the actual treatment process on a smaller scale. This allows for testing of coagulant doses under more realistic operating conditions, including continuous flow and variations in raw water quality.

*   **Advantages:**
    *   More accurate representation of full-scale plant performance.
    *   Allows evaluation of the interaction between coagulation and subsequent processes (e.g., sedimentation, filtration).
    *   Helps in selecting the most appropriate coagulant and optimizing its application.

*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   More expensive and complex than jar tests.
    *   Requires more time and resources.

#### 5.4. Chemical Treatment Models and Algorithms

Advanced treatment plants may use online sensors and predictive algorithms to continuously monitor water quality parameters and adjust coagulant doses automatically. These models can incorporate factors like turbidity, pH, temperature, and historical performance data.

### 6. Calculations for Coagulant Dose

When using coagulants, the dose is often expressed in mg/L. However, coagulants are typically supplied in a specific form (e.g., anhydrous, hydrated, or as a concentrated solution). Calculations are needed to determine the actual amount of product to add.

**Key Terms:**

*   **Active Ingredient:** The actual coagulating chemical component (e.g., Al³⁺ or Fe³⁺).
*   **Purity:** The percentage of the active ingredient in the commercial product.
*   **Molecular Weight:** The sum of the atomic weights of all atoms in a molecule.

**Calculations for Alum (Al₂(SO₄)₃·14.5H₂O):**

*   Molecular Weight of Al₂(SO₄)₃·14.5H₂O = 2(26.98) + 3(32.07 + 4*16.00) + 14.5(2*1.01 + 16.00) ≈ 594.5 g/mol
*   Molecular Weight of Al₂O₃ (for calculating equivalent dose) = 2(26.98) + 3(16.00) ≈ 101.96 g/mol
*   Purity of commercial alum is often given as % Al₂O₃ (e.g., 17% Al₂O₃).

**To calculate the dose of Alum (solid) required:**

Let:
*   $D_{Al_2O_3}$ = Desired dose of Al₂O₃ (mg/L)
*   $P_{Al_2O_3}$ = Purity of the alum product as % Al₂O₃
*   $MW_{Al_2O_3}$ = Molecular Weight of Al₂O₃
*   $MW_{Al_2(SO_4)_3 \cdot 14.5H_2O}$ = Molecular Weight of hydrated alum

**Equation:**

Dose of Alum (mg/L) = $D_{Al_2O_3}$ × $\frac{MW_{Al_2(SO_4)_3 \cdot 14.5H_2O}}{MW_{Al_2O_3}}$ × $\frac{100}{P_{Al_2O_3}}$

**Example Calculation:**

*   Suppose the jar test indicates an optimal dose of 20 mg/L of Al₂O₃.
*   The commercial alum product is 17% Al₂O₃.
*   $MW_{Al_2O_3}$ = 101.96 g/mol
*   $MW_{Al_2(SO_4)_3 \cdot 14.5H_2O}$ = 594.5 g/mol

Dose of Alum (mg/L) = 20 mg/L × $\frac{594.5 \text{ g/mol}}{101.96 \text{ g/mol}}$ × $\frac{100}{17}$
Dose of Alum (mg/L) = 20 mg/L × 5.83 × 5.88
Dose of Alum (mg/L) ≈ 686 mg/L

**This means that to deliver 20 mg/L of Al₂O₃, you need to add approximately 686 mg/L of the commercial alum product.**

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

A jar test was conducted on a raw water sample with an initial turbidity of 80 NTU. The following results were obtained:

| Beaker No. | Alum Dose (mg/L) | Residual Turbidity (NTU) | Floc Description   |
| :--------- | :--------------- | :----------------------- | :----------------- |
| 1          | 20               | 55                       | Poor, small        |
| 2          | 30               | 25                       | Fair, small        |
| 3          | 40               | 10                       | Good, medium       |
| 4          | 50               | 12                       | Good, breaking     |
| 5          | 60               | 20                       | Very dispersed     |

Based on these results, what is the optimal alum dose for this water sample?

**Answer 1:**

The optimal alum dose is **40 mg/L**, as it yields the lowest residual turbidity (10 NTU) with good floc formation.

**Question 2:**

A treatment plant needs to apply a dose of 15 mg/L of Al₂O₃ using a commercial ferric chloride product that is 98% FeCl₃·6H₂O.
Given:
*   $MW_{FeCl_3 \cdot 6H_2O}$ = 270.3 g/mol
*   $MW_{Fe_2O_3}$ = 159.7 g/mol (equivalent for ferric compounds)
*   Purity of FeCl₃ is often considered based on Fe³⁺ content, but for dose estimation purposes, we can relate it to Fe₂O₃. A common approach is to use the Fe₂O₃ equivalent.

Calculate the dose of the commercial ferric chloride product (in mg/L) required.

**Answer 2:**

First, determine the equivalent dose of Fe₂O₃ needed. Since the coagulant is ferric chloride, we'll use Fe₂O₃ as the basis for comparison, assuming the coagulating species is effectively Fe³⁺.

The relationship between Fe₂O₃ and Fe³⁺ is: $Fe_2O_3$ (159.7 g/mol) contains 2 moles of Fe (2 * 55.845 = 111.69 g).
The molecular weight of FeCl₃·6H₂O is 270.3 g/mol. This contains 1 mole of Fe (55.845 g).

To express the dose of ferric chloride in terms of Fe₂O₃ equivalent:

Dose of Fe₂O₃ equivalent (mg/L) = Desired dose of Fe³⁺ (mg/L) × $\frac{MW_{Fe_2O_3}}{2 \times MW_{Fe}}$
However, a more direct approach is to calculate the mass of the product needed to deliver the required Fe³⁺ ions, and then relate it to the Fe₂O₃ equivalent for standardized comparison.

Let's assume the desired dose of 15 mg/L is equivalent to Fe₂O₃.
We need to find the dose of FeCl₃·6H₂O that provides the same amount of Fe³⁺ as 15 mg/L of Fe₂O₃.

Moles of Fe₂O₃ in 15 mg = $\frac{15 \text{ mg}}{159.7 \text{ g/mol}} = 0.0939 \text{ mmol/mg}$
This contains $2 \times 0.0939 = 0.1878$ mmol of Fe³⁺.

Now, we need to find how much FeCl₃·6H₂O contains 0.1878 mmol of Fe³⁺.
Moles of FeCl₃·6H₂O = 0.1878 mmol (since each molecule has one Fe).
Dose of FeCl₃·6H₂O (mg) = $0.1878 \text{ mmol} \times 270.3 \text{ g/mol}$
Dose of FeCl₃·6H₂O (mg) = $0.1878 \times 270.3 \approx 50.77$ mg

This 50.77 mg of FeCl₃·6H₂O has a purity of 98%.

So, the dose of the commercial product is:
Dose of Commercial Product (mg/L) = $\frac{\text{Dose of Pure Product (mg/L)}}{\text{Purity (%)}}$
Dose of Commercial Product (mg/L) = $\frac{50.77 \text{ mg/L}}{0.98}$
Dose of Commercial Product (mg/L) ≈ **51.8 mg/L**

*Alternatively, using a direct ratio:*
To deliver a dose of 15 mg/L as Fe₂O₃, which contains 2 moles of Fe, we need to consider the molar masses.
Mass of Fe in 15 mg Fe₂O₃ = 15 mg * (2 * 55.845) / 159.7 = 10.48 mg Fe

We need to find the mass of FeCl₃·6H₂O (MW = 270.3) containing 10.48 mg of Fe.
Mass of FeCl₃·6H₂O = 10.48 mg Fe * (270.3 g/mol FeCl₃·6H₂O / 55.845 g/mol Fe) = 50.77 mg FeCl₃·6H₂O

Since the commercial product is 98% pure:
Dose of Commercial Product = 50.77 mg / 0.98 = 51.8 mg/L.

**Question 3:**

What is the primary purpose of performing a jar test in water treatment?

**Answer 3:**

The primary purpose of a jar test is to **determine the optimal coagulant dose and other operating conditions (like pH, mixing speeds, and times) required to effectively remove turbidity and other impurities from a raw water sample.**

---

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Jar tests are essential for optimizing coagulant application.** They provide the most reliable data for dose determination under specific raw water conditions.
*   **pH is a critical factor** affecting coagulant performance. Always consider testing at various pH levels during jar tests.
*   **The nature of the particles (turbidity, colloidal matter)** in the raw water significantly influences the required coagulant dose.
*   **Overdosing and underdosing of coagulants** both lead to inefficient treatment and potential problems.
*   **Always account for the purity and form of the commercial coagulant** when calculating the actual amount to be added.
*   **Pilot plant studies** provide more accurate predictions of full-scale performance than jar tests.
