## 1. Indian Contract Act, 1872: Layout and Salient Features

The Indian Contract Act, 1872, is the principal legislation governing contracts in India. It lays down the rules and principles for the formation, performance, and enforcement of contracts.

### 1.1. Layout/Structure of the Indian Contract Act, 1872

The Act is divided into chapters and sections. Originally, it comprised 11 chapters and 266 sections. However, the Contract Act was later split into two parts:

*   **The Indian Contract Act, 1872 (Sections 1-75):** Deals with general principles of contract law, including formation, performance, discharge, and remedies for breach.
*   **The Sale of Goods Act, 1930:** Deals specifically with contracts for the sale of goods. (Sections 76-123 of the original Act were repealed and replaced by the Sale of Goods Act).

**Key Chapters in the First Part (Sections 1-75):**

*   **Chapter I: Preliminary (Sections 1-2A):** Defines scope, application, and key terms like "agreement," "contract," "offer," "acceptance," etc.
*   **Chapter II: Of Contracts, Voidable Contracts and Void Agreements (Sections 3-10):** Deals with the essential elements of a valid contract, free consent, capacity of parties, lawful object, etc.
*   **Chapter III: Of Contingent Contracts (Sections 31-36):** Covers contracts whose performance depends on the happening or non-happening of a future uncertain event.
*   **Chapter IV: Of the Performance of Contracts (Sections 37-67):** Outlines the rules regarding how contracts are to be performed, by whom, and when.
*   **Chapter V: Of the Satisfaction of Contracts Otherwise than by Performance (Sections 62-67):** Deals with methods of discharging a contract other than by actual performance, such as novation, rescission, alteration, etc.
*   **Chapter VI: Of the Consequences of Breach of Contract (Sections 73-75):** Specifies the remedies available to the aggrieved party when a contract is broken.

### 1.2. Salient Features of the Indian Contract Act, 1872

*   **Codified Law:** It is a comprehensive piece of legislation that codifies the law relating to contracts in India.
*   **Based on English Common Law:** The Act draws heavily from the principles of English Common Law, adapted to Indian conditions.
*   **Defines Essential Elements of a Valid Contract:** It clearly outlines what constitutes a valid contract, including offer, acceptance, lawful consideration, capacity to contract, free consent, lawful object, and certainty of meaning.
*   **Distinguishes between Void and Voidable Contracts:** It differentiates between agreements that are void ab initio (from the beginning) and contracts that are voidable at the option of one party.
*   **Provides for Remedies for Breach:** It specifies the legal remedies available to an injured party when a contract is breached.
*   **Covers Specific Types of Contracts:** While the general principles are laid down, specific aspects like sale of goods are now governed by separate acts. However, earlier it included provisions for bailment, pledge, and agency.
*   **Emphasis on Free Consent:** The Act prioritizes the element of free consent and provides remedies against coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, and mistake.

## 2. Performance and Non-Performance of a Contract

### 2.1. Performance of a Contract

**Definition:** Performance of a contract means that the parties to the contract have fulfilled their respective promises or obligations as agreed upon. It is the fulfillment of the contractual duties by the promisor.

**Key Principles of Performance:**

*   **Who must perform?**
    *   **Promisor himself:** If the contract is personal in nature (e.g., a painter agrees to paint a portrait), it must be performed by the promisor himself.
    *   **Promisor's agent:** If the nature of the contract allows, it can be performed by a competent agent appointed by the promisor.
    *   **Legal representative:** Upon the death of the promisor, their legal representatives are bound to perform the contract, provided it is not of a personal nature.
*   **By whom must performance be made?**
    *   Promisor
    *   Promisor's agent
    *   Legal representative (in certain cases)
    *   Third party (if accepted by the promisee)
*   **Time and Place of Performance:**
    *   If a specific time and place are fixed in the contract, performance must adhere to those terms.
    *   If no time or place is specified, performance must be reasonable and at a suitable place as determined by custom or law.
*   **Performance of Reciprocal Promises:**
    *   Where the contract consists of reciprocal promises, one of which depends on the prior performance of the other, the prior promise must be performed first.
    *   **Example:** A agrees to sell his car to B for Rs. 5,00,000. B agrees to buy the car for Rs. 5,00,000 and pay on delivery. A must deliver the car before B pays.
*   **Performance of Joint Promises:**
    *   Joint promisors must perform the promise together.
    *   If any joint promisor dies, their legal representative and the surviving promisors must perform.
    *   Upon the death of all joint promisors, their legal representatives must perform the promise.

**Effect of Performance:**
Once a contract is duly performed by both parties, the contract is discharged and the parties are relieved of their obligations.

### 2.2. Non-Performance of a Contract

**Definition:** Non-performance of a contract occurs when one or more parties fail to perform their contractual obligations or perform them in a manner that is not in accordance with the terms of the contract.

**Types of Non-Performance:**

*   **Actual Breach:** Occurs when a party refuses to perform or makes it impossible to perform their obligations *at the time of performance*.
    *   **Example:** A contracts to deliver 100 kg of wheat to B on December 1st. On December 1st, A fails to deliver the wheat.
*   **Anticipatory Breach:** Occurs when a party repudiates their obligations *before the time for performance has arrived*. The innocent party can treat this as an immediate breach.
    *   **Example:** A agrees to sell his goods to B on March 1st. On February 15th, A informs B that he will not be able to supply the goods. B can sue A immediately on February 15th.

**Modes of Non-Performance:**

*   **Refusal to perform:** A party outright refuses to carry out their promise.
*   **Impossibility of performance:** Circumstances arise making performance impossible.
*   **Defective performance:** Performance is made, but it is not as per the agreed terms (e.g., delivering wrong goods or fewer quantity).
*   **Failure to perform within the stipulated time:** If time is of the essence.

## 3. Breach of Contract: Consequences and Remedies

### 3.1. Breach of Contract

**Definition:** A breach of contract is the wrongful failure of a party to perform its contractual obligations. It is a violation of the terms of the agreement.

### 3.2. Consequences of Breach of Contract

The primary consequence of a breach of contract is that the aggrieved (innocent) party becomes entitled to remedies against the party in default.

### 3.3. Remedies for Breach of Contract

The Indian Contract Act, 1872, provides several remedies for the aggrieved party:

#### a) Damages

**Definition:** Damages are monetary compensation awarded by a court to the aggrieved party to put them in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed.

**Types of Damages:**

*   **Ordinary/General Damages (Section 73):**
    *   These are damages that arise naturally and directly in the usual course of things from the breach of contract.
    *   The aim is to compensate for the loss directly and naturally caused.
    *   **Principle:** Hadley v. Baxendale – damages are recoverable if they are reasonably foreseeable at the time the contract was made.
    *   **Example:** A contracts to sell 100 tonnes of steel to B at Rs. 50,000 per tonne. The market price on the day of delivery is Rs. 55,000 per tonne. B can recover Rs. 5,00,000 (5 * 1000 * 50) from A for the difference in price.

*   **Special Damages (Section 73):**
    *   These are damages that arise from special or extraordinary circumstances not arising naturally from the breach.
    *   They are recoverable only if the special circumstances were communicated to the defaulting party at the time of making the contract, so that they could have reasonably foreseen the loss.
    *   **Example:** A agrees to carry B's machinery to a factory. A delays the delivery, causing B's factory to remain idle for a day. If B had informed A that the factory would be shut down if the machinery wasn't delivered on time, B could claim damages for the loss of profit during that day.

*   **Nominal Damages:**
    *   Awarded when there is a breach of contract, but the aggrieved party has not suffered any actual loss.
    *   These are usually a small sum, awarded to acknowledge the legal right that has been violated.
    *   **Example:** A agrees to sell a specific book to B. A breaches the contract, but B could easily buy the same book from another seller at the same price.

*   **Exemplary/Punitive Damages:**
    *   These are awarded to punish the wrongdoer, not just to compensate the injured party.
    *   Generally, these are not awarded in breach of contract cases, except in rare circumstances like breach of promise to marry where substantial damages may be awarded for injury to reputation.

*   **Consequential Damages:**
    *   These are damages that flow indirectly from the breach but are a direct result of the breach. They often fall under special damages.

#### b) Specific Performance (Section 10 of Specific Relief Act, 1963)

**Definition:** Specific performance is a decree by the court ordering the defaulting party to actually perform the contract as promised.

**When is it granted?**
It is an equitable remedy and is granted at the discretion of the court, usually when monetary compensation (damages) is inadequate to provide relief. This is typically in cases involving:
*   Contracts relating to immovable property.
*   Contracts involving the sale of unique goods.
*   Where the act agreed to be done is of such a nature that monetary compensation cannot adequately recompense the injured party.

**Example:** A agrees to sell a unique antique painting to B. If A fails to deliver the painting, B can seek specific performance, i.e., compel A to deliver the painting, as monetary compensation cannot replace the unique item.

#### c) Injunction

**Definition:** An injunction is a court order restraining a party from doing a particular act or compelling them to do a particular act.

**Types:**
*   **Prohibitive Injunction:** Restrains a party from doing something.
*   **Mandatory Injunction:** Compels a party to do something.

**When is it granted?**
It is also an equitable remedy granted when damages are not an adequate remedy, usually to prevent a threatened or apprehended breach of contract.

**Example:** A musician contracted to perform exclusively for a particular promoter. If the musician threatens to perform for another promoter, the first promoter can seek an injunction to prevent the musician from doing so.

#### d) Quantum Meruit

**Definition:** "Quantum meruit" means "as much as earned." It is a claim for compensation for the work done, where the contract is found to be void or not performed by the other party.

**When is it applicable?**
*   When a person has done something for another, but without any intention of doing it gratuitously.
*   When a contract becomes void and the person has received any benefit thereunder.
*   When an act is done by one person in pursuance of a promise made by another.

**Example:** A agrees to build a house for B for a lump sum. A has partially built the house when the contract becomes void due to some unforeseen reason. A can claim payment for the work done by him on the basis of quantum meruit.

## 4. Liquidated Damages

### 4.1. Definition of Liquidated Damages

**Definition:** Liquidated damages are a sum of money agreed upon by the parties at the time of entering into the contract, which shall be payable by the party who commits a breach to the other party. It is a pre-estimate of the probable loss.

### 4.2. Liquidated Damages vs. Penalty

It is crucial to distinguish between liquidated damages and a penalty. The Indian Contract Act, 1872, clarifies this:

*   **Section 74:** "When a contract has been broken, if a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach, or if any forfeiture is stipulated for in case of such breach, the party aggrieved by the breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract, reasonable compensation for the injury or loss actually suffered by him, *not exceeding the amount so named or forfeited.*"

**Key distinction:**

| Feature             | Liquidated Damages                                | Penalty                                               |
| :------------------ | :------------------------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Purpose**         | Genuine pre-estimate of likely loss.              | To deter a party from breaching the contract.         |
| **Nature**          | Compensation for loss.                            | Punishment for breach.                                |
| **Court's Power**   | Court generally upholds the stipulated sum.      | Court is not bound by the stipulation; awards actual loss. |
| **Wording**         | "Liquidated damages," "agreed damages."           | "Penalty," "forfeiture."                              |
| **Assessment**      | Based on reasonable forecast of loss at contract formation. | Arbitrary or excessive sum, unrelated to probable loss. |

**Important Note:**
The courts will not simply rely on the label used by the parties. They will examine the substance of the stipulation to determine whether it is a genuine pre-estimate of loss (liquidated damages) or a penalty. If it is a penalty, the court will award only reasonable compensation for the loss actually suffered.

**Example of Liquidated Damages:**
A construction contract states that if the contractor fails to complete the project by the agreed date, they will pay the owner Rs. 5,000 per day for every day of delay. This sum is a reasonable pre-estimate of the loss the owner might suffer due to the delay (e.g., lost rental income). This would likely be considered liquidated damages.

**Example of Penalty:**
A contract states that if the contractor fails to complete a Rs. 1 crore project by the agreed date, they will pay the owner Rs. 1 crore per day of delay. This sum is clearly excessive and not a genuine pre-estimate of loss. It is intended to punish the contractor and deter them from delaying. A court would likely treat this as a penalty and award only the actual damages suffered by the owner.

### Practice Questions and Answers

**1. What are the two main parts into which the Indian Contract Act, 1872 was later divided?**
    **Answer:** The Indian Contract Act, 1872 was later divided into:
    *   The Indian Contract Act, 1872 (Sections 1-75) dealing with general principles of contract.
    *   The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 (derived from Sections 76-123 of the original Act) dealing with contracts for the sale of goods.

**2. Differentiate between an actual breach and an anticipatory breach of contract.**
    **Answer:**
    *   **Actual Breach:** Occurs at the time when performance is due, where a party fails to perform or makes performance impossible.
    *   **Anticipatory Breach:** Occurs before the time for performance is due, where a party repudiates their obligation. The innocent party can treat it as an immediate breach.

**3. Define "damages" as a remedy for breach of contract.**
    **Answer:** Damages are monetary compensation awarded by a court to the aggrieved party to put them in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed.

**4. Under what circumstances can special damages be claimed?**
    **Answer:** Special damages can be claimed only if the special circumstances leading to the loss were communicated to the defaulting party at the time of contract formation, so they could reasonably foresee the loss.

**5. What is the difference between liquidated damages and a penalty according to Indian Contract Law?**
    **Answer:** Liquidated damages are a genuine pre-estimate of likely loss agreed upon by the parties, while a penalty is an excessive sum intended to deter breach. Section 74 of the Act states that in case of breach, the aggrieved party is entitled to reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named or forfeited, meaning penalties are not strictly enforced, and only actual loss is compensated.

**6. If A agrees to sell his house to B, and then fraudulently sells it to C, what remedies might B have against A?**
    **Answer:** B might have remedies such as:
    *   **Damages:** For the loss incurred due to A's breach.
    *   **Specific Performance:** If monetary compensation is inadequate (e.g., the house is unique and B wanted it for personal reasons), B could seek specific performance to compel A to sell the house to him.
    *   An **injunction** could be sought to prevent A from selling the house to C if the sale to C has not yet been completed.

### Important Points to Remember

*   The Indian Contract Act, 1872, is the foundational law for all contracts in India (except specific areas covered by other laws).
*   A valid contract requires offer, acceptance, lawful consideration, capacity, free consent, lawful object, and certainty.
*   Performance discharges a contract. Non-performance leads to a breach.
*   Breach of contract entitles the aggrieved party to remedies, with damages being the most common.
*   Damages aim to compensate, not punish, except in rare circumstances.
*   Specific performance and injunctions are equitable remedies granted when damages are insufficient.
*   Liquidated damages are a pre-agreed sum as compensation for breach. Penalties are not enforced as agreed.
*   The court will assess whether a stipulated sum is liquidated damages or a penalty based on the intention and reasonableness at the time of contract formation.
