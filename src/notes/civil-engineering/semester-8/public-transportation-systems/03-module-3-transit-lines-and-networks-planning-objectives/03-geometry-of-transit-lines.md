# Public Transportation Systems: Module 3 - Transit Lines and Networks: Planning Objectives

## Topic: Geometry of Transit Lines

---

### **1. Introduction to Transit Line Geometry**

Transit line geometry refers to the spatial characteristics of transit routes, including their alignment, shape, length, and curvature. Understanding transit line geometry is crucial for effective transit network planning, as it directly impacts:

*   **Passenger Accessibility:** How easily can people reach transit stops?
*   **Operational Efficiency:** How smoothly and quickly can vehicles operate?
*   **Ridership:** How attractive is the service to potential passengers?
*   **Cost of Infrastructure:** How much will it cost to build and maintain the route?
*   **Environmental Impact:** How does the route interact with the urban landscape?

---

### **2. Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Alignment:** The precise path or course of a transit line. This can be a straight line, curved, or follow existing road infrastructure.
*   **Route:** The designated path a transit vehicle follows, typically defined by a sequence of streets, avenues, or dedicated guideways.
*   **Stop Location:** The specific point where passengers board or alight from a transit vehicle. The placement of stops significantly influences the overall geometry.
*   **Headway:** The time interval between successive transit vehicles operating on the same route.
*   **Speed:** The rate at which a transit vehicle travels along its route. This is influenced by traffic conditions, vehicle type, and geometric constraints.
*   **Curvature:** The degree to which a transit line deviates from a straight line. Measured by radius of curvature.
*   **Grade:** The slope of the transit line.

### **3. Learning Outcomes Covered**

This section will detail the geometry of transit lines and its impact on transit network planning objectives.

#### **3.1 Understanding the Impact of Geometric Characteristics on Transit Network Performance**

The geometric characteristics of transit lines have a direct and significant impact on various aspects of transit network performance:

*   **Speed and Travel Time:**
    *   **Directness:** Straighter routes generally allow for higher average speeds and shorter travel times.
    *   **Curvature:** Sharp curves necessitate slower speeds, increasing travel time and reducing operational efficiency.
    *   **Stop Spacing:** Closely spaced stops increase dwell times (time spent at stops for boarding/alighting), slowing down the overall service.
*   **Accessibility:**
    *   **Route Directness:** A more direct route may serve fewer potential origins/destinations compared to a winding one.
    *   **Stop Placement:** Strategically placed stops along the route are critical for passenger access. Geometric alignment can influence where stops can be effectively located.
*   **Capacity:**
    *   **Turning Movements:** Sharp turns can limit the size of vehicles that can operate on a route and can increase delays at intersections.
    *   **Queueing:** The geometry of intersections and the flow of traffic (influenced by route alignment) can create bottlenecks and queuing, impacting capacity.
*   **Ridership and Demand:**
    *   **Coverage:** The geometric layout of a route determines the area it serves, directly influencing the potential ridership.
    *   **Convenience:** Shorter travel times and easy access (proximity to stops) make the service more convenient and attractive, leading to higher ridership.
*   **Cost of Operation and Infrastructure:**
    *   **Vehicle Wear and Tear:** Frequent acceleration, deceleration, and sharp turns increase wear and tear on vehicles, leading to higher maintenance costs.
    *   **Fuel Consumption:** Slower speeds and frequent stops increase fuel consumption.
    *   **Infrastructure Costs:** Building new transit lines with specific geometric requirements (e.g., dedicated guideways with gentle curves) can be significantly more expensive than using existing road networks.

#### **3.2 Analyzing Different Types of Transit Lines Based on Geometry**

Transit lines can be broadly categorized based on their geometric characteristics and how they interact with the urban environment:

*   **Radial Lines:**
    *   **Description:** Originate from a central point (e.g., CBD) and extend outwards into the surrounding suburbs. They often follow major arterial roads.
    *   **Geometric Characteristics:** Tend to be relatively straight and direct for much of their length, facilitating faster travel towards the center. May have more curves as they approach the periphery or navigate existing urban grids.
    *   **Example:** A bus route that starts downtown and goes directly out to a suburban neighborhood.
*   **Circumferential (or Orbital) Lines:**
    *   **Description:** Connect different radial corridors on the periphery of a metropolitan area, bypassing the central city.
    *   **Geometric Characteristics:** Can be more varied, often following major ring roads or existing arterial networks. May involve more gradual curves to connect different sectors.
    *   **Example:** A bus route that circles the outer suburbs, connecting several residential areas without going into the city center.
*   **Cross-Town Lines:**
    *   **Description:** Connect different radial or circumferential lines within the metropolitan area, facilitating transfers and providing non-radial travel options.
    *   **Geometric Characteristics:** Often follow existing street patterns, which can lead to more complex geometries with numerous turns and intersections.
    *   **Example:** A bus route that runs east-west across a city, connecting two north-south bus lines.
*   **Feeder Lines:**
    *   **Description:** Short routes designed to collect passengers from less dense areas and bring them to major trunk lines or transit hubs.
    *   **Geometric Characteristics:** Typically follow local street networks, meaning they can have irregular shapes and frequent turns.
    *   **Example:** A small shuttle bus route serving a residential subdivision and connecting to a main bus route at a designated stop.
*   **Dedicated Guideway Lines (e.g., Light Rail, Bus Rapid Transit - BRT):**
    *   **Description:** Utilize dedicated infrastructure separate from general traffic.
    *   **Geometric Characteristics:** Can be designed with optimized geometry (gentle curves, minimal grade changes) to achieve higher speeds and capacities. However, their alignment is constrained by the need for new infrastructure construction.
    *   **Example:** A light rail line that has its own tracks, often elevated or in a tunnel, with specially designed stations. BRT lanes on major arterials.

#### **3.3 Evaluating Stop Placement and Its Geometric Implications**

The placement of transit stops is a critical geometric decision with significant consequences:

*   **Stop Spacing:**
    *   **Close Spacing:** Improves accessibility by placing stops within shorter walking distances for more people. However, it leads to more frequent stops, increasing dwell times, slowing down the service, and reducing overall speed.
    *   **Wide Spacing:** Allows for higher average speeds and faster travel times. However, it reduces accessibility, requiring longer walks to reach stops, potentially deterring some riders.
*   **Stop Location Relative to Intersections:**
    *   **"On" vs. "Before" or "After" an Intersection:**
        *   **"On" Intersection (Bus stop is within the intersection limits):** Can cause congestion if buses block traffic.
        *   **"Before" Intersection (Bus approaches and stops before turning):** Allows the bus to clear the intersection before stopping, potentially reducing intersection delays.
        *   **"After" Intersection (Bus stops after completing a turn):** Allows the bus to align with the street before stopping, but the bus may be delayed by traffic on the new street.
    *   **Pedestrian Access:** Stops should be located where pedestrians can safely and easily access them, considering sidewalks, crosswalks, and shelter amenities.
*   **Stop Placement and Visibility:**
    *   Good visibility of approaching vehicles is important for passengers.
    *   Geometric features like curves or buildings can obstruct visibility.
*   **Stop Placement and Efficiency:**
    *   The layout of stops should minimize unnecessary maneuvers or detours for the vehicle.
    *   For BRT or light rail, station design and placement are integrated with the guideway geometry for efficient boarding and alighting.

#### **3.4 Designing Transit Lines for Optimal Geometric Alignment**

Designing transit lines for optimal geometric alignment involves balancing various competing objectives:

*   **Directness and Speed:** Aim for straighter alignments where possible to minimize travel times and improve operational efficiency.
*   **Accessibility and Coverage:** Ensure stops are placed within reasonable walking distances of residential areas, employment centers, and other key destinations. The route itself should cover a significant portion of the demand.
*   **Minimizing Curves and Grades:**
    *   **Curvature:** Use the largest possible radii for curves to allow higher speeds and accommodate larger vehicles.
    *   **Grades:** Avoid excessively steep grades, as they can slow down vehicles, increase energy consumption, and pose challenges for certain types of vehicles (especially for accessibility).
*   **Integration with Existing Infrastructure:** Often, transit lines must follow existing road networks. This requires adapting the geometric design to the constraints of the urban fabric, including intersections, buildings, and other utilities.
*   **Dedicated vs. Shared Right-of-Way:**
    *   **Dedicated:** Offers greater control over geometry, allowing for optimized alignments.
    *   **Shared:** Requires careful geometric planning to minimize conflicts with other traffic and ensure smooth operation.
*   **Future Expansion and Flexibility:** Consider how the geometric design might accommodate future network expansions or changes in demand.

### **5. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**
A proposed bus route needs to serve two neighborhoods and a shopping mall. Neighborhood A is to the west, Neighborhood B is to the north, and the mall is to the east. The main arterial road runs north-south.
*   Describe two possible geometric layouts for this bus route, considering the trade-offs between directness and coverage.
*   For each layout, what are the potential geometric challenges related to speed and stop placement?

**Answer 1:**
*   **Layout 1: Primarily North-South Radial with a Loop:**
    *   The route could run north along the main arterial, serving Neighborhood B.
    *   It could then deviate east to serve the shopping mall.
    *   A loop or a return segment could be designed to serve Neighborhood A before returning to the arterial.
    *   **Geometric Challenges:**
        *   The deviation east to the mall might involve a curve.
        *   The loop to Neighborhood A could involve tighter turns and more complex street navigation, impacting speed.
        *   Stop placement would need to consider access to both neighborhoods and the mall, potentially leading to shorter, high-frequency stops in denser areas, slowing the route.
*   **Layout 2: Cross-Town with Radial Extensions:**
    *   The route could run east-west across town, connecting Neighborhood A and the shopping mall.
    *   From the main arterial, it could extend north to serve Neighborhood B.
    *   **Geometric Challenges:**
        *   The east-west segment might involve following existing grid streets, leading to frequent 90-degree turns, which are geometrically challenging for larger vehicles and reduce speed.
        *   Stop placement needs to balance access to the mall, Neighborhood A, and then provide a connection to Neighborhood B.
        *   The north extension might also involve navigating local streets.

**Question 2:**
You are planning a new Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) line along a major avenue. The avenue has several intersections with smaller streets. You have the option to place bus stations either immediately before or immediately after each intersection.
*   Discuss the geometric and operational implications of placing stations **before** an intersection versus **after** an intersection for a BRT system.
*   Which placement strategy might generally be preferred for improving travel speed and minimizing delays in a BRT context, and why?

**Answer 2:**
*   **Placing Stations Before an Intersection:**
    *   **Geometric Implications:** The bus stops and platforms are situated on the approach to the intersection.
    *   **Operational Implications:**
        *   **Pros:** The bus can queue at the intersection without being blocked by the station. If the intersection signal is favorable, the bus can proceed directly after passenger boarding/alighting. Can prevent buses from blocking cross-traffic if they dwell longer than anticipated.
        *   **Cons:** If the bus is delayed at the station (long dwell time) and the intersection is clear, the bus might miss a favorable signal phase, leading to a delay. May also lead to the bus blocking the right-turn lane if not carefully managed.
*   **Placing Stations After an Intersection:**
    *   **Geometric Implications:** The bus stops and platforms are situated on the exit side of the intersection.
    *   **Operational Implications:**
        *   **Pros:** Allows the bus to clear the intersection first, and then pull into the station. This can be more efficient if traffic signals are synchronized, as the bus can proceed through the intersection without interference. Can also allow for easier alignment for boarding on the correct side.
        *   **Cons:** If the bus is delayed at the station (long dwell time), it might miss the subsequent signal phase on the new street, causing a delay. This placement can also lead to buses bunching up after the intersection if multiple buses are running close together.
*   **Preferred Placement for BRT:**
    *   Generally, placing stations **after** the intersection is often preferred for BRT systems aiming for higher speeds and efficiency.
    *   **Reasoning:** This allows the BRT vehicle to navigate the intersection without immediate obstruction. The assumption is that the BRT has a dedicated lane or signal priority, enabling it to clear the intersection promptly. Once clear, it can then pull into the station at its leisure to pick up passengers, without directly impacting the intersection's traffic flow. This strategy contributes to smoother overall travel and minimizes the chance of a BRT vehicle blocking intersection capacity, which is crucial for maintaining travel times. However, careful intersection design (e.g., adequate approach length for buses) and signal timing are still critical for optimal performance.

### **6. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Geometry is not just about curves; it's about directness, stop placement, and interaction with the environment.**
*   **Straighter lines = faster speeds = less travel time = happier passengers and lower operational costs.**
*   **Stop spacing is a critical trade-off between accessibility and speed.**
*   **Dedicated guideways offer more control over geometry, leading to potentially better performance, but at a higher infrastructure cost.**
*   **Understanding existing urban fabric is crucial when planning transit line geometry in built-up areas.**
*   **The placement of stops relative to intersections significantly impacts traffic flow and operational efficiency.**
