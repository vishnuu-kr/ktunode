# Advanced Design of Steel Structures

## Module 3: Design of Plate Girders Subjected to Uniformly Distributed Loads – Design of Stiffeners

### Topic: Design of Gantry Girders

This module focuses on the design of gantry girders, a crucial component in industrial buildings, often supporting overhead cranes. We will explore the specific loading conditions, selection criteria, and design procedures for these specialized structures.

---

### Introduction to Gantry Girders

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Gantry Girder:** A primary structural member that spans between the main columns of an industrial building and supports the rails on which an overhead travelling crane moves.
*   **Overhead Crane:** A machine used for lifting and moving heavy loads within a factory, warehouse, or other industrial setting.
*   **Function:** Gantry girders are designed to withstand not only the static weight of the crane and its load but also the dynamic forces generated by the crane's movement.

**Importance:**

*   Gantry girders are critical for the efficient operation of many industrial facilities.
*   Their design requires careful consideration of dynamic loads and potential fatigue issues.

---

### Loading Considerations for Gantry Girders

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Dead Load (DL):** The self-weight of the gantry girder itself, including any appurtenances like rails, walkways, etc.
*   **Live Load (LL):** The load imposed by the crane and its lifted load. This is the primary load of concern for gantry girder design.
*   **Impact Load:** Dynamic effects caused by the acceleration and deceleration of the crane and the hoisting of the load. This is usually expressed as a percentage of the static crane load.
*   **Braking Forces:** Horizontal forces acting along the longitudinal direction of the gantry, caused by the braking action of the crane.
*   **Centrifugal Forces:** Horizontal forces acting perpendicular to the longitudinal direction, caused by the crane's lateral movement around curves (if applicable).
*   **Wind Load:** If the gantry girder is exposed to the elements.
*   **Seismic Load:** If the structure is in a seismically active zone.

**Loading Components from Crane Operation:**

1.  **Vertical Load:**
    *   **Static Vertical Load:** Weight of the crane's trolley and the lifted load.
    *   **Dynamic Vertical Load (Impact):**
        *   **Impact Factor:** Often taken as 25% of the static crane load, but can vary based on codes and manufacturer recommendations. This accounts for the shock of lifting and the inherent vibrations.
        *   **Formula:** $W_{vertical, dynamic} = W_{static} \times (1 + \text{Impact Factor})$
2.  **Horizontal Load (Longitudinal):**
    *   **Braking Force:** Typically taken as 10% to 20% of the static hook load, applied at the level of the crane wheel. This acts in the direction of crane travel.
3.  **Horizontal Load (Lateral):**
    *   **Centrifugal Force:** Applied when the crane moves laterally. Usually taken as 5% to 10% of the static hook load. This acts perpendicular to the direction of lateral travel.
    *   **Side Pull:** Force applied due to the side movement of the trolley, often resisted by the crane structure itself, but can induce forces on the gantry girder.

**Important Point to Remember:** The combination of these loads needs to be considered for worst-case scenarios. The impact and braking forces are critical in gantry girder design.

### Selection of Gantry Girder

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Span:** The distance between the main columns.
*   **Crane Span:** The distance between the crane's wheels.
*   **Load Capacity:** The maximum weight the crane can lift.
*   **Crane Speed and Frequency of Operation:** Affects dynamic load intensity and fatigue.
*   **Location and Environment:** Influences material selection and protective coatings.
*   **Economic Considerations:** Cost of fabrication, erection, and maintenance.

**Common Gantry Girder Profiles:**

*   **Rolled I-beams:** Suitable for lighter duty cranes or when span and load are moderate.
*   **Plate Girders (Fabricated I-beams):** Most common for heavier duty cranes and longer spans. They offer greater flexibility in section properties.
*   **Box Girders:** Used for very heavy loads and long spans, offering high torsional rigidity.
*   **Built-up Sections with Web Reinforcement:** Another form of fabricated girder.

**Factors Influencing Selection:**

*   **Load Magnitude:** Heavier loads necessitate stronger and often fabricated sections.
*   **Span Length:** Longer spans generally require deeper sections or built-up sections to limit deflection and bending stresses.
*   **Lateral Bracing:** The availability and effectiveness of lateral bracing from roof trusses or bracing systems influence the design.
*   **Stiffener Requirements:** Plate girders often require web stiffeners, which add to the complexity and cost.

### Position of Moving Load for Maximum Effects

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Influence Lines:** Diagrams that show the variation of a specific force (bending moment, shear force, deflection) along a beam due to a unit load moving across it.
*   **Maximum Bending Moment:** Occurs when the load is positioned to maximize the area under the influence line. For a uniformly distributed load, it's when the load covers the region of positive influence. For a concentrated load, it's when the load is at the peak of the influence line.
*   **Maximum Shear Force:** Occurs when the load is at the support, or at the point of maximum shear on the influence line.
*   **Maximum Vertical Deflection:** Occurs when the load is positioned at the point of maximum deflection on the influence line.

**Specifics for Gantry Girders with Cranes:**

The crane is not a single point load but a system of wheel loads. The maximum effects are determined by considering the worst-case positions of the crane's trolleys and the entire crane.

1.  **Maximum Bending Moment:**
    *   Occurs when the two main crane wheels are positioned to produce the maximum bending moment at a critical section, typically near mid-span.
    *   This often involves positioning the crane such that one wheel is slightly to one side of the mid-span, and the other wheel is positioned to maximize the resultant moment.
    *   **Procedure:**
        *   Determine the span of the gantry girder ($L$).
        *   Determine the wheel spacing of the crane ($d$).
        *   Consider the position of the crane such that the resultant moment from the two wheels is maximized. This often happens when the center of the girder is roughly between the two wheels, or when one wheel is at a specific distance from the center.
        *   **Rule of Thumb:** The maximum bending moment usually occurs when the center of the girder is between the two wheels, and the distance between the center and the nearest wheel is minimized.
        *   **Practical Approach:** For design, one can approximate this by considering the two most critical load positions relative to the girder's span.
2.  **Maximum Shear Force:**
    *   The maximum shear occurs near the supports.
    *   It's typically dominated by the shear from the crane wheel closest to the support.
    *   **Positioning:** Place the crane as close to the support as possible, so that the wheel causing the maximum shear is directly over the support.
3.  **Maximum Vertical Deflection:**
    *   The maximum vertical deflection for a simply supported beam under a uniformly distributed load occurs at mid-span.
    *   For a moving load (like a crane), the maximum deflection can occur at mid-span or under one of the crane wheels.
    *   **Positioning:** To maximize deflection at mid-span, the crane should be positioned so that its center is at mid-span. To maximize deflection under a wheel, the crane should be positioned such that the wheel is at the location of maximum deflection.
    *   **Critical Deflection:** Usually, the deflection at mid-span due to the crane's load is the critical one to check.

**Important Point to Remember:** The critical load combination for bending moment might not be the same as for shear or deflection. Always consider the most unfavorable combination for each design check.

### Profile of Gantry Girder

**Key Concepts:**

*   **I-Sections (Rolled or Fabricated):**
    *   **Rolled I-beams (Universal Beams):** Economical for moderate loads and spans. Limited by available section sizes.
    *   **Plate Girders (Fabricated I-beams):**
        *   **Web:** Vertical plate, usually with stiffeners.
        *   **Flanges:** Horizontal plates, providing bending resistance.
        *   **Flange-to-Web Connection:** Welded.
    *   **Advantages:** Efficient in resisting bending, relatively simple to fabricate.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Prone to lateral-torsional buckling if not adequately braced.
*   **Box Girders:**
    *   Two parallel webs connected by top and bottom flanges (or a single web with flanges forming a box).
    *   **Advantages:** High torsional rigidity, good resistance to lateral buckling, can be efficient for very long spans.
    *   **Disadvantages:** More complex fabrication, can be heavier.
*   **Crane Rail Position:**
    *   The crane rail is typically positioned on the top flange of the gantry girder. This creates an eccentric loading condition for the top flange.
    *   **Wear Plate:** A wear plate is often welded to the top flange to protect it from wear caused by the crane wheels. This wear plate needs to be accounted for in the flange design.

**Design Considerations for Profile:**

*   **Effective Depth:** The overall depth of the girder is crucial for limiting deflection and bending stresses.
*   **Flange Width and Thickness:** Dictated by bending moment, shear force, and local buckling criteria for the top flange supporting the rail.
*   **Web Thickness:** Governed by shear capacity and buckling resistance.
*   **Lateral Bracing:** The frequency and location of lateral bracing points (e.g., from roof purlins) significantly influence the choice of section and the need for additional stiffeners.

### Limitation on Vertical Deflection

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Serviceability Limit State:** Deflection limits are primarily concerned with the usability and appearance of the structure, not its ultimate strength.
*   **Reasons for Deflection Limits:**
    *   **Crane Operation:** Excessive deflection can cause the crane wheels to lose contact with the rail, leading to misalignment, jerky movements, and potential damage.
    *   **Rail Alignment:** Maintaining proper alignment of the crane rail is crucial for smooth operation and to prevent excessive wear.
    *   **Building Appearance:** Excessive deflection can lead to sagging in floors or ceilings, impacting aesthetics and functionality.
    *   **Adjacent Structures:** Preventing excessive differential settlement that could affect adjacent structures.

**Typical Deflection Limits (refer to relevant codes like IS 800, Eurocode, AISC):**

*   **Under Working Load (Static + Dynamic):**
    *   Span/500 to Span/750 is a common range.
    *   Specific limits may be imposed by crane manufacturers or the nature of the operation. For instance, a limit of Span/750 might be specified for the deflection due to the static crane load plus half the dynamic load.
    *   The total load considered for deflection calculations typically includes the static crane load, a portion of the dynamic load, and potentially the self-weight of the girder.
*   **Lateral Deflection:** The lateral deflection of the gantry girder due to horizontal forces (braking, centrifugal) also needs to be limited, typically to Span/500.

**Calculation of Deflection:**

*   Deflection can be calculated using standard beam deflection formulas, but it's crucial to use the correct load combinations and effective section properties.
*   **Effective Section Properties:** When considering deflection, the "effective" section properties might be used, particularly if there are localized stresses or if the web is relatively thin. For plate girders, the behavior of the web under shear can affect its stiffness.
*   **Impact of Load Positions:** As discussed earlier, the position of the crane loads significantly affects the magnitude and location of maximum deflection.
*   **Stiffness of the Entire Gantry System:** The stiffness of the main columns and their connections also contribute to the overall deflection behavior.

**Important Point to Remember:** Always refer to the specific design codes and manufacturer's recommendations for precise deflection limits, as these can vary significantly.

### Design of Gantry Girders

The design process involves checking the girder for various failure modes under the critical load combinations.

**General Design Steps:**

1.  **Determine Loads:** Calculate all applicable loads (dead, live, impact, braking, etc.) based on crane specifications and codes.
2.  **Select Preliminary Section:** Based on span, load capacity, and deflection limits, select a trial section (rolled I-beam or plate girder).
3.  **Load Combinations:** Identify critical load combinations for bending moment, shear force, and deflection.
    *   **Maximum Bending Moment:** Often the combination of dead load + crane live load + impact load.
    *   **Maximum Shear:** Often the combination of dead load + crane live load (with one wheel at support) + impact load.
    *   **Maximum Deflection:** Often dead load + crane live load + impact load, positioned for maximum deflection.
    *   **Horizontal Forces:** Consider braking and centrifugal forces for lateral stability and flange/web design.
4.  **Check Bending Strength:**
    *   Calculate the section modulus required ($Z_{xx,req}$) based on the maximum bending moment ($M_{max}$) and the allowable bending stress ($\sigma_{ba}$).
    *   $\sigma_{ba}$ is typically the yield stress ($f_y$) divided by a partial safety factor for material ($\gamma_{m0}$) and a bending stress reduction factor if lateral-torsional buckling is a concern.
    *   Compare the actual section modulus ($Z_{xx,actual}$) of the selected section with $Z_{xx,req}$.
    *   **Lateral-Torsional Buckling (LTB):** This is a critical check for gantry girders. The top flange is usually braced by the crane rail and potentially by roof purlins. However, the bottom flange might not be adequately braced.
        *   **Effective Length:** The unbraced length of the bottom flange needs to be determined.
        *   **Bending Stress Reduction Factor (C_b):** Calculated based on the distribution of bending moment along the unbraced length.
        *   **Check:** $M_{max} \leq \frac{\beta_b Z_{xx} f_{y}}{\gamma_{m0}}$ (where $\beta_b$ is the bending stress reduction factor).
5.  **Check Shear Strength:**
    *   Calculate the maximum shear force ($V_{max}$).
    *   Check the shear capacity of the web. For plate girders, this involves checking web slenderness ratios and shear buckling.
    *   For I-sections, the shear capacity is often governed by the web's ability to resist shear.
    *   **Check:** $V_{max} \leq \frac{A_w \tau_{w}}{\gamma_{m0}}$ (where $A_w$ is the web area and $\tau_{w}$ is the allowable shear stress, which depends on web slenderness).
6.  **Check Vertical Deflection:**
    *   Calculate the maximum vertical deflection under the specified load combination.
    *   Ensure that the calculated deflection is within the allowable limits (e.g., Span/500 or Span/750).
    *   If deflection is excessive, a deeper section or a section with a higher moment of inertia will be required.
7.  **Check Flange Design:**
    *   **Top Flange:** Needs to be strong enough to support the concentrated load from the crane wheel. This involves checking local buckling (flange buckling) and bearing pressure on the web. A wear plate is usually added.
    *   **Bottom Flange:** Needs to be designed for bending and also to resist lateral-torsional buckling.
8.  **Check Web Design:**
    *   **Shear:** As mentioned above, check shear capacity and shear buckling.
    *   **Bearing:** Check the web's bearing capacity under the concentrated loads from the crane wheels at the point of contact.
    *   **Stiffeners:** If the web is too slender, intermediate transverse stiffeners will be required to prevent shear buckling. Longitudinal stiffeners might also be used.
9.  **Design of Stiffeners (Intermediate Transverse Stiffeners):**
    *   **Purpose:** To increase the shear buckling resistance of the web, particularly in situations where the web slenderness ratio ($h/t_w$) is high.
    *   **Placement:** Typically placed at a certain spacing along the girder, often dictated by shear force distribution or code requirements.
    *   **Design Criteria:**
        *   **Shear Buckling Resistance:** The stiffener's area and moment of inertia contribute to the web's resistance to shear buckling.
        *   **Stiffener Stiffness:** The stiffener must have sufficient stiffness (moment of inertia) to provide adequate support to the web.
        *   **Stiffener Strength:** The stiffener must be able to carry any concentrated loads (e.g., from braking forces transmitted through the top flange) and also provide a path for the forces to be distributed into the web.
        *   **Bearing:** The stiffener must also provide adequate bearing area where it contacts the top and bottom flanges to prevent web crippling.
        *   **Moment of Inertia Requirement:** $I_{st} \geq (h/50)^4$ (where $h$ is the web depth and $I_{st}$ is the moment of inertia of the stiffener about the web's center line).
        *   **Area Requirement:** The stiffener's area ($A_{st}$) must be sufficient to resist buckling and distribute loads. Codes provide specific formulas based on the slenderness of the web panel.
        *   **Stiffener Spacing:** The spacing of stiffeners is generally limited by the critical buckling wavelength of the web.

10. **Check Connections:** Ensure welded or bolted connections are adequate for the forces they transmit.

### Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
A gantry girder has a span of 15 m and supports a 50 kN crane hook load. The crane trolley weighs 10 kN and has two wheels spaced 3 m apart. The dead load of the gantry girder itself is 1 kN/m. Assuming an impact factor of 25% and a braking force of 10% of the hook load, calculate the maximum vertical bending moment due to the crane's live load and impact.

**Answer 1:**

*   **Hook Load (static):** 50 kN
*   **Trolley Load:** 10 kN
*   **Total Static Crane Load (per wheel if equally distributed, but here we consider the critical load):** Assume the crane load is distributed between two wheels. For maximum moment, the crane is positioned optimally. The hook load is the critical factor.
*   **Impact Factor:** 25% (0.25)
*   **Dynamic Hook Load:** $50 \text{ kN} \times (1 + 0.25) = 62.5 \text{ kN}$

To find the maximum bending moment, we need to position the two wheels (carrying a portion of the load, often taken as the full hook load distributed between the wheels, or specific wheel loads from the crane manufacturer) optimally. For simplicity in this example, let's assume the 50 kN hook load is distributed equally to the two crane wheels, so each wheel carries 25 kN. The trolley weight is part of the self-weight of the crane structure that is always present.

Let the span of the gantry girder be $L = 15$ m.
Let the wheel spacing be $d = 3$ m.
Let the load per wheel be $P = 25$ kN (dynamic load).

The maximum bending moment for two equal loads $P$ spaced $d$ apart on a simply supported beam of span $L$ occurs when the center of the span is between the wheels, and the distance from the center to the nearest wheel is minimized. The magnitude of the maximum moment is generally given by:

$M_{max} = \frac{P}{2} \left( L - \frac{d}{2} \right)$  when the girder center is between the wheels.

A more rigorous approach involves moving the system of loads.
Let's place the left support at x=0 and the right support at x=15.
The wheels are at positions $x_1$ and $x_1 + 3$.
The total downward load is $2P = 50$ kN.
The reactions at the supports are $R_A$ and $R_B$.

For maximum moment, we position the loads such that the center of the span (7.5 m) is between the wheels. Let the distance of the left wheel from the left support be $x$. The right wheel will be at $x+3$.
We consider the case where the center of gravity of the two wheel loads is at the center of the girder. This means:
$\frac{x + (x+3)}{2} = \frac{L}{2}$
$2x + 3 = 15$
$2x = 12$
$x = 6$ m

So, the wheels are at 6 m and 9 m from the left support.
The load on the left wheel is 25 kN. The load on the right wheel is 25 kN.

Moment at mid-span (7.5 m):
Moment due to left wheel = $25 \text{ kN} \times (7.5 - 6) = 25 \times 1.5 = 37.5$ kNm.
Moment due to right wheel = $25 \text{ kN} \times (7.5 - 9) = 25 \times (-1.5) = -37.5$ kNm.
This is not correct for maximum moment.

The position for maximum moment is when the center of the girder falls between the two loads. For two loads P, spaced d apart, the max moment is at a distance $x = (L-d)/2$ from the center.
Let's consider the position where the center of the girder (7.5 m) is exactly between the two wheels.
So, the left wheel is at $7.5 - 1.5 = 6$ m.
The right wheel is at $7.5 + 1.5 = 9$ m.

Moment at 7.5 m:
Reaction at left support $R_A$: Total load = 50 kN.
$R_A = \frac{25 \times (15-6) + 25 \times (15-9)}{15} = \frac{25 \times 9 + 25 \times 6}{15} = \frac{225 + 150}{15} = \frac{375}{15} = 25$ kN.
$R_B = 50 - 25 = 25$ kN.

Moment at 7.5 m = $R_A \times 7.5 - 25 \times (7.5 - 6) = 25 \times 7.5 - 25 \times 1.5 = 187.5 - 37.5 = 150$ kNm.

This is the moment at the center. The true maximum moment may occur slightly off-center.
For two loads $P$ spaced $d$ apart, the maximum bending moment is:
$M_{max} = P \left( \frac{L}{2} - \frac{d}{4} \right)$ if $L \ge d$
$M_{max} = 25 \left( \frac{15}{2} - \frac{3}{4} \right) = 25 \left( 7.5 - 0.75 \right) = 25 \times 6.75 = 168.75$ kNm.

This value of 168.75 kNm is the maximum bending moment from one crane.
The question asks for the maximum vertical bending moment due to the crane's live load and impact.
So, the dynamic bending moment is 168.75 kNm.

**Question 2:**
What are the primary reasons for imposing limitations on the vertical deflection of gantry girders?

**Answer 2:**
The primary reasons for imposing limitations on the vertical deflection of gantry girders are:

1.  **Proper Crane Operation:** To ensure that the crane wheels maintain consistent contact with the rails and do not derail or experience jerky movements. Excessive deflection can lead to misalignment of the crane.
2.  **Rail Alignment:** To maintain the intended alignment of the crane rails, which is crucial for smooth movement, reduced wear on wheels and rails, and prevention of undue stress on the crane structure.
3.  **Preventing Damage:** Excessive deflection can cause stress concentrations and fatigue in the gantry girder, crane mechanism, and the rails themselves.
4.  **Serviceability:** To ensure the overall usability and functionality of the industrial facility without visual distortions or operational disruptions.

**Question 3:**
For a plate girder gantry girder, what is the main purpose of providing intermediate transverse stiffeners?

**Answer 3:**
The main purpose of providing intermediate transverse stiffeners in a plate girder gantry girder is to **increase the shear buckling resistance of the web**. When the web slenderness ratio ($h/t_w$) is high, the web alone is susceptible to buckling under shear forces. The transverse stiffeners act to divide the web into smaller panels, thus increasing the effective stiffness of the web and preventing premature shear buckling failure. They also help to distribute shear forces more effectively.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Dynamic Nature of Loads:** Gantry girder design is significantly influenced by dynamic loads (impact, braking) due to crane operation.
*   **Load Combinations are Crucial:** Always consider the most critical combination of loads for each design check (bending, shear, deflection).
*   **Lateral-Torsional Buckling (LTB):** This is a critical failure mode for the top flange of gantry girders and requires careful consideration of bracing conditions.
*   **Deflection Limits are Serviceability Requirements:** They ensure the proper functioning of the crane and the structural integrity under working loads.
*   **Stiffeners:** Essential for the shear capacity of plate girder webs, especially for deeper sections or higher shear stresses. Their stiffness and area are critical design parameters.
*   **Crane Rail Integration:** The crane rail is not just a load but an integral part of the gantry girder's top flange system. Its bearing and local buckling behavior need to be checked.
*   **Code Compliance:** Always adhere to the relevant steel design codes (e.g., IS 800, Eurocodes, AISC) for specific load calculations, material properties, allowable stresses, and design procedures.
