### 1. Building Irregularities

Building irregularities are features that deviate from a symmetrical, uniform, and continuous structural system, both in plan and elevation. These deviations can significantly impact a structure's seismic performance, often leading to amplified stresses and unpredictable behavior.

#### 1.1. Irregularities in Elevation

These occur when there are sudden changes in the stiffness, mass, or strength of the building along its vertical axis.

*   **Soft Storey (Weak Storey):**
    *   **Definition:** A storey with significantly less lateral stiffness compared to the storeys above and below it. This often occurs in ground floors with fewer or more open walls (e.g., parking levels, retail spaces).
    *   **Seismic Impact:** During an earthquake, seismic forces concentrate in the stiffest storeys. However, the soft storey, due to its low stiffness, deforms excessively, attracting a disproportionately large amount of story drift. This can lead to:
        *   **P-Delta Effect:** The overturning moment caused by gravity loads acting on the displaced structure. This effect is amplified in soft storeys.
        *   **Progressive Collapse:** Failure can initiate in the soft storey and propagate upwards.
    *   **Example:** A multi-story building where the ground floor has large openings for a lobby or parking, while upper floors have smaller window openings and more shear walls.
*   **Stiff Storey (Hard Storey):**
    *   **Definition:** A storey with significantly greater lateral stiffness compared to the storeys above and below it.
    *   **Seismic Impact:** These storeys attract larger seismic forces due to their stiffness. However, they can also lead to stress concentrations and potentially brittle failure if not designed adequately.
    *   **Example:** A storey with a significant amount of bracing or shear walls compared to adjacent storeys.
*   **Discontinuous Load-Bearing Walls/Columns:**
    *   **Definition:** Vertical load-bearing elements (walls or columns) that do not extend continuously from the foundation to the roof.
    *   **Seismic Impact:** The continuity of load paths is broken, meaning that the loads cannot be transferred smoothly to the foundation. This can lead to:
        *   **Load Shedding:** Loads are transferred to adjacent, less robust elements, causing overstress.
        *   **Concentrated Stresses:** Stress concentrations occur at the discontinuity, making the structure vulnerable.
    *   **Example:** A column that stops at an intermediate floor, with the load being transferred to a beam and then to columns in the floor below.
*   **Discontinuous In-plane Floor/Roof Framing:**
    *   **Definition:** Floor or roof diaphragms (horizontal slabs) that are not continuous or are interrupted by large openings or breaks in framing.
    *   **Seismic Impact:** The diaphragm is responsible for transferring lateral forces to vertical resisting elements. Discontinuities can:
        *   **Reduce Diaphragm Strength and Stiffness:** Leading to uneven distribution of forces.
        *   **Create Stress Concentrations:** At the edges of openings or discontinuities.
    *   **Example:** A floor slab with a large central atrium or a significant portion removed for services.
*   **Mass Irregularity:**
    *   **Definition:** Significant variations in the mass of adjacent storeys.
    *   **Seismic Impact:** Earthquakes excite structures based on their mass. A heavier storey will attract more inertial force. This can lead to:
        *   **Differential Deformations:** The heavier storey will experience larger displacements and drifts.
        *   **Increased Bending Moments:** In columns supporting the heavier storey.
    *   **Example:** A building with a heavy water tank or a mechanical floor located on an upper level.

#### 1.2. Irregularities in Plan

These occur when there are deviations from symmetrical or regular shapes in the building's footprint.

*   **Re-entrant Corners (L-shaped, T-shaped, U-shaped, etc.):**
    *   **Definition:** Buildings with plan shapes that have inward-facing corners (angles greater than 90 degrees).
    *   **Seismic Impact:** These irregularities create significant torsional effects during an earthquake.
        *   **Torsional Amplification:** The re-entrant corners tend to rotate more than other parts of the structure, leading to amplified drifts and stresses at these locations.
        *   **Stress Concentrations:** Stresses are concentrated at the re-entrant corners.
    *   **Example:** A building with an L-shaped floor plan.
*   **Diaphragm Discontinuity/Weak Diaphragms:**
    *   **Definition:** When a floor slab (diaphragm) has a significant reduction in its width or stiffness, or has large openings, it can act as a diaphragm discontinuity.
    *   **Seismic Impact:**
        *   **Poor Load Transfer:** The diaphragm cannot effectively transfer lateral forces to the vertical resisting elements.
        *   **Localised Deformations:** Can lead to significant deformations within the diaphragm itself.
    *   **Example:** A floor slab with large cutouts for staircases or elevator shafts that are not adequately reinforced or connected.
*   **Out-of-Plane Projections of Diaphragms:**
    *   **Definition:** Portions of the diaphragm that extend beyond the main building structure.
    *   **Seismic Impact:** These projections can experience significant out-of-plane bending and shear, leading to localized damage.
    *   **Example:** Cantilevered balconies that are integral to the floor diaphragm.
*   **Non-parallel Systems:**
    *   **Definition:** When the vertical load-resisting elements (walls, frames) are not parallel to each other or to the principal axes of the building.
    *   **Seismic Impact:** Can lead to complex stress distributions and torsional effects.
    *   **Example:** Diagonal bracing elements that are not oriented symmetrically or parallel to building axes.
*   **Inadequate Lateral Stiffness:**
    *   **Definition:** When the overall lateral stiffness of the building plan is insufficient to resist seismic forces.
    *   **Seismic Impact:** Can lead to large overall displacements and potential collapse.

### 2. Influence of Structural Classification

The way a building is classified based on its lateral load-resisting system significantly influences how it responds to seismic forces and the impact of irregularities.

*   **Load-Bearing Wall Systems:**
    *   **Characteristics:** Lateral forces are primarily resisted by shear walls or masonry walls acting as shear panels.
    *   **Influence of Irregularities:**
        *   **Discontinuous Walls:** Very critical, as they break the primary load path.
        *   **Openings in Walls:** Can reduce shear resistance and stiffness.
        *   **Plan Irregularities:** Can induce significant torsion in shear wall systems.
*   **Moment-Resisting Frame Systems:**
    *   **Characteristics:** Lateral forces are resisted by the bending stiffness of beams and columns.
    *   **Influence of Irregularities:**
        *   **Soft Storeys:** Can lead to large story drifts and potential beam-column joint failures.
        *   **Mass Irregularities:** Can cause significant bending moments in columns.
        *   **Plan Irregularities:** Can induce torsion in the frame, leading to P-Delta effects and larger drifts in peripheral columns.
*   **Dual Systems (Combination of Frames and Shear Walls):**
    *   **Characteristics:** Combines the benefits of both systems, with shear walls providing significant stiffness and frames providing ductility.
    *   **Influence of Irregularities:**
        *   **Interaction between Systems:** Irregularities can disrupt the intended load sharing between frames and walls.
        *   **Torsion:** Can cause differential rotation between the shear wall core and the frame, leading to complex stress distributions.

### 3. Concepts of Seismic Design

Seismic design aims to ensure that a structure can withstand earthquake forces without collapse, protecting life and minimizing damage. Key concepts revolve around understanding how a building behaves under lateral loads.

#### 3.1. Center of Mass (CM)

*   **Definition:** The point where the entire mass of a storey or the building is considered to be concentrated. For a uniform building, it is generally at the geometric center of the mass distribution.
*   **Calculation:** It is the weighted average of the masses of all components of the building, considering their location.
    *   $X_{CM} = \frac{\sum (m_i \cdot x_i)}{\sum m_i}$
    *   $Y_{CM} = \frac{\sum (m_i \cdot y_i)}{\sum m_i}$
    where $m_i$ is the mass of component $i$ and $(x_i, y_i)$ are its coordinates.
*   **Seismic Significance:** The inertia force due to an earthquake acts at the Center of Mass.

#### 3.2. Center of Rigidity (CR) (or Center of Stiffness)

*   **Definition:** The point of application of the resultant of all lateral story forces at which the building will translate without any rotation. It is the geometric center of the locations of the centers of rigidity of all resisting elements (shear walls, frames, etc.) of a storey.
*   **Calculation:** It is the weighted average of the stiffness locations of the resisting elements.
    *   $X_{CR} = \frac{\sum (k_i \cdot x_i)}{\sum k_i}$
    *   $Y_{CR} = \frac{\sum (k_i \cdot y_i)}{\sum k_i}$
    where $k_i$ is the lateral stiffness of resisting element $i$ and $(x_i, y_i)$ are the coordinates of its centroid.
*   **Seismic Significance:** The resultant lateral force acts at the Center of Mass, while the resultant restoring force resisting this motion acts at the Center of Rigidity.

#### 3.3. Torsional Eccentricity

*   **Definition:** The horizontal distance between the Center of Mass (CM) and the Center of Rigidity (CR) of a storey.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Static Torsional Eccentricity:** The inherent eccentricity due to the layout of mass and stiffness.
    *   **Dynamic Torsional Eccentricity:** Occurs when the CM and CR do not coincide due to changes in stiffness distribution under dynamic loading or due to higher mode effects.
*   **Seismic Significance:**
    *   **Torsion:** When the CM and CR do not coincide, a lateral force acting at the CM will induce a moment. This moment causes the building to rotate (twist) about the CR.
    *   **Amplified Story Drifts:** The presence of torsion leads to significantly larger story drifts at the extremities of the building compared to the drifts at the center. This can cause:
        *   **Damage to Structural Elements:** Columns and walls at the edges can experience larger forces and displacements.
        *   **Damage to Non-structural Elements:** Partition walls, facades, and finishes are more susceptible to damage due to amplified drifts.
        *   **Potential for Progressive Collapse:** Especially if the torsional effects are not accounted for.

### 5. Practice Questions with Answers

**Answer 1:**
A **soft storey** is a storey with significantly less lateral stiffness compared to the storeys above and below it. This is a critical seismic design consideration because it tends to attract a disproportionately large amount of story drift during an earthquake. This excessive deformation can lead to the **P-Delta effect** (amplified overturning moments), damage to structural elements (like beam-column joints), and in severe cases, progressive collapse of the building.

**Answer 2:**
If shear walls are located only along the perimeter of a rectangular building, the Center of Rigidity (CR) will likely be located at the geometric center of the building. The Center of Mass (CM) will also be at the geometric center if the mass distribution is uniform. However, if the stiffness is concentrated only at the perimeter, and the mass is more uniformly distributed, the CR might be slightly inward from the geometric center.
**Seismic Consequence:** If the CM and CR do not perfectly coincide (even for a rectangular building with perimeter walls), there will be a **torsional eccentricity**. This eccentricity will induce torsion during an earthquake, leading to amplified story drifts at the corners and edges of the building, potentially causing more damage in these locations.

**Answer 3:**
Three common types of building irregularities in elevation and their impacts:
1.  **Soft Storey:** Leads to concentrated story drifts, P-Delta effects, and potential failure initiation.
2.  **Discontinuous Load-Bearing Walls/Columns:** Breaks load paths, causing stress concentrations and load shedding to less capable elements.
3.  **Mass Irregularity (e.g., heavy upper floor):** Results in larger inertial forces on the heavier storey, leading to differential displacements and increased bending moments in supporting columns.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Center of Mass (CM):** The point where the entire mass of a storey or building is considered concentrated. Inertial forces due to ground acceleration act at the CM.
*   **Center of Rigidity (CR):** The point of application of the resultant of all lateral story forces at which the building will translate without any rotation. It represents the centroid of the lateral stiffness of the resisting elements.
*   **Influence on Torsional Response:** When the CM and CR do not coincide (i.e., there is torsional eccentricity), a lateral force acting at the CM will create a moment about the CR. This moment induces **torsion** (twisting) in the building, causing amplified story drifts at the extremities of the structure.

**Answer 5:**
An L-shaped building will exhibit significantly different seismic behavior compared to a rectangular building of similar area and mass due to its **re-entrant corners**. These corners create **torsional eccentricities**. During an earthquake, the L-shaped building will tend to twist more. This torsion leads to:
*   **Amplified Story Drifts:** The drifts will be larger at the wings of the "L" and especially at the re-entrant corners.
*   **Stress Concentrations:** Higher stresses will be induced in the structural elements located at or near the re-entrant corners.
*   **Uneven Distribution of Lateral Forces:** The lateral forces will not be distributed uniformly throughout the structure, potentially overloading certain elements.
Rectangular buildings, especially if symmetrical and with uniformly distributed stiffness, tend to experience more straightforward translational motion without significant torsional effects.

### 6. Important Points to Remember:

*   **Irregularities are detrimental:** Building irregularities significantly degrade seismic performance by introducing non-uniform distribution of forces and deformations.
*   **Soft storeys are critical:** Always pay close attention to designing against soft storeys, as they are a major cause of building collapse in earthquakes.
*   **Torsion is dangerous:** When CM ≠ CR, torsion occurs. This amplifies drifts and stresses, particularly at the periphery of the structure.
*   **Continuous load paths are essential:** Ensure that vertical load-bearing elements are continuous from foundation to roof.
*   **Diaphragms are crucial:** Floor and roof diaphragms must be rigid and continuous to effectively transfer lateral forces to vertical resisting elements.
*   **Structural classification matters:** The seismic response is heavily dependent on whether the building is framed, wall-bearing, or dual.
*   **Design for ductility:** While avoiding collapse, seismic design also aims for ductility to absorb energy and prevent brittle failure.
