# ADVANCED GEOTECHNICAL INVESTIGATION - Module 1: Planning an Investigation Programme

## Topic: Soil Sampling: Disturbed, Undisturbed, Representative, Non-Representative, Chunk, and Tube Samples

This module focuses on the critical initial step in any geotechnical investigation: **soil sampling**. The quality of the samples obtained directly dictates the accuracy and reliability of subsequent laboratory testing and, therefore, the final design recommendations.

---

### 1. Importance of Soil Sampling in Geotechnical Investigations

*   **Foundation of Geotechnical Engineering:** Soil sampling is the primary method for understanding the subsurface conditions at a project site.
*   **Laboratory Testing:** Samples are brought to the laboratory for tests that determine soil properties like strength, compressibility, permeability, and classification.
*   **Design Basis:** The results of laboratory tests on properly obtained samples form the basis for designing foundations, retaining walls, slopes, and other geotechnical structures.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** Investing in appropriate sampling techniques ensures that the most critical soil parameters are accurately measured, preventing costly over-design or failures due to inadequate understanding.

---

### 2. Types of Soil Samples

Soil samples can be broadly categorized based on the degree of disturbance they experience during the sampling process.

#### 2.1. Disturbed Soil Samples

*   **Definition:** Samples that have undergone significant changes in their **in-situ structure, stress state, and water content** during the sampling process.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Soil fabric and particle arrangement are altered.
    *   Natural moisture content may change.
    *   Effective stresses are often lost.
    *   The soil may be loosened or compacted.
*   **Methods of Obtaining Disturbed Samples:**
    *   **Augers (Hand Augers, Power Augers):** Spiral tools that bore into the ground and bring soil to the surface.
    *   **Bailers:** Cylindrical tools with a flap valve at the bottom, used for excavating soft soils and slurries from boreholes.
    *   **Spilt-Spoon Sampler (SPT Sampler):** A thick-walled tube driven into the soil by a falling hammer. While it provides some indication of soil density (N-value), the sample itself is generally considered disturbed.
    *   **Open-End Drive Samplers:** Tubes driven into the soil to obtain samples.
*   **Uses of Disturbed Samples:**
    *   **Visual Classification:** For identifying soil types (e.g., sand, clay, gravel).
    *   **Moisture Content Determination:** Accurate measurement of natural water content.
    *   **Index Property Tests:** Such as Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit), sieve analysis, specific gravity.
    *   **Chemical Tests:** For assessing the presence of harmful substances (e.g., sulfates, chlorides).
    *   **Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) Tests:** Can be performed on remolded samples to obtain an estimate of shear strength.
*   **Limitations:** Not suitable for tests that rely on the soil's natural structure and stress history.

#### 2.2. Undisturbed Soil Samples

*   **Definition:** Samples that aim to retain the **in-situ structure, stress state, and water content** of the soil as closely as possible.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Preserve the natural soil fabric (e.g., layering, fissures in clays).
    *   Maintain original particle arrangement.
    *   Retain natural moisture content.
    *   Minimize loss of effective stress.
*   **Methods of Obtaining Undisturbed Samples:**
    *   **Thin-Walled Tube Samplers (Shelby Tubes):** Seamless metal tubes with a cutting edge, pushed or driven into the soil with minimal disturbance. The degree of disturbance depends on the area ratio and the driving method.
    *   **Piston Samplers (e.g., Open-Drive Piston Sampler, Stationary Piston Sampler):** These samplers use a piston to reduce the friction between the sample and the tube walls during insertion and withdrawal.
    *   **Block Samples:** Large, carefully excavated blocks of soil, typically from shallow depths or exposed faces (e.g., excavations, tunnel portals). These are the most "undisturbed" samples but are practical only for shallow investigations.
    *   **Freeze Samplers:** The soil around the sampler is frozen using liquid nitrogen before extraction, preserving its structure. This is a specialized technique for very sensitive soils.
*   **Uses of Undisturbed Samples:**
    *   **Strength Tests:** Triaxial compression tests (UU, CU, CD), direct shear tests.
    *   **Consolidation Tests:** To determine compressibility and settlement characteristics.
    *   **Permeability Tests:** To measure the coefficient of permeability.
    *   **Anisotropy Studies:** To investigate variations in soil properties with direction.
    *   **Microstructural Analysis:** Using techniques like Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM).
*   **Limitations:** Obtaining truly "undisturbed" samples is challenging, and some degree of disturbance is always present. The quality is highly dependent on the sampling method, operator skill, and soil type.

### 3. Representativeness of Soil Samples

The goal of any sampling program is to obtain samples that accurately reflect the subsurface conditions of the site.

#### 3.1. Representative Samples

*   **Definition:** Samples that accurately reflect the **average properties and conditions** of the stratum from which they were taken. They are obtained through a systematic and appropriate sampling strategy.
*   **Key Factors for Representativeness:**
    *   **Appropriate Sampling Method:** Selecting the right sampler for the soil type and investigation objective.
    *   **Adequate Sampling Frequency:** Taking samples at sufficient depths and intervals to capture variations within a stratum.
    *   **Careful Handling and Preservation:** Proper labeling, sealing, and transportation to prevent further disturbance or alteration.
    *   **Minimizing Bias:** Avoiding sampling only "easy" locations or skipping zones of anticipated poor soil.
    *   **Understanding Stratigraphy:** Having a preliminary understanding of expected soil layers to guide sampling locations.
*   **Example:** If a clay layer is expected to vary in consistency, obtaining samples from the top, middle, and bottom of that layer, and at multiple locations along its horizontal extent, increases the likelihood of obtaining representative samples.

#### 3.2. Non-Representative Samples

*   **Definition:** Samples that **do not accurately reflect** the average properties and conditions of the stratum from which they were taken.
*   **Causes of Non-Representativeness:**
    *   **Sampling Bias:**
        *   **Selective Sampling:** Only taking samples from easily accessible or seemingly good soil.
        *   **Sampling in Localized Features:** Taking samples from an isolated pocket of soft clay within a generally firm layer, or vice versa.
    *   **Improper Sampling Techniques:**
        *   **Over-driving or under-driving samplers:** Leading to sample disturbance or incomplete recovery.
        *   **Using the wrong sampler for the soil type:** E.g., using a thin-walled tube in a very stiff fissured clay might cause fracturing.
    *   **Poor Sample Preservation:**
        *   **Drying out:** Leading to changes in moisture content and potentially shrinkage.
        *   **Contamination:** Introducing foreign material.
        *   **Damage during transport:** Dropping or rough handling.
    *   **Insufficient Sampling Density:** Taking too few samples to account for spatial variability.
*   **Example:** A single sample taken from a borehole in a variable sand layer might represent a localized zone of fine sand, failing to capture the presence of cobbles or zones of looser sand that are also present.

### 4. Specific Types of Samples Mentioned

#### 4.1. Chunk Samples

*   **Definition:** Generally refers to **disturbed** samples taken in large pieces or blocks, typically from excavations, trial pits, or shallow hand-augered boreholes.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   Can be of significant size (e.g., 100mm x 100mm x 100mm or larger).
    *   Often obtained by hand.
    *   The structure is largely disturbed, but larger pieces may offer some clues about layering.
*   **Uses:**
    *   Visual inspection and classification.
    *   Rough assessment of soil consistency.
    *   May be used for remolded strength tests.
*   **Note:** While the term "chunk" might imply a less disturbed sample than a auger sample, it's generally considered disturbed due to the excavation process.

#### 4.2. Tube Samples

*   **Definition:** Samples obtained using a cylindrical tube, either driven or pushed into the soil. This is a broad category that includes both disturbed and relatively undisturbed samples.
*   **Key Subtypes of Tube Samples:**
    *   **Open-Drive Samplers (e.g., Split-Spoon Sampler):**
        *   **Description:** A thick-walled tube split lengthwise into two halves, with a cutting edge at the bottom and a shoe at the top to hold the liner. Driven by a hammer.
        *   **Disturbance Level:** Considered moderately to highly disturbed.
        *   **Common Use:** Standard Penetration Test (SPT) for obtaining a blow count (N-value) and a disturbed sample for classification.
    *   **Thin-Walled Tube Samplers (Shelby Tubes):**
        *   **Description:** Seamless metal tubes with a sharp cutting edge. Pushed or driven into the soil, often with reduced friction mechanisms.
        *   **Disturbance Level:** Considered the primary method for obtaining relatively undisturbed samples. Disturbance depends on the area ratio (ratio of the tube's cross-sectional area to the area inside the cutting edge). Lower area ratios (e.g., less than 10%) generally yield less disturbed samples.
        *   **Common Use:** Obtaining undisturbed samples for laboratory strength and consolidation tests.
    *   **Piston Samplers:**
        *   **Description:** Employ a piston within the sampling tube. The piston creates a vacuum or pressure differential to aid sample entry and reduce friction during withdrawal.
        *   **Disturbance Level:** Generally considered to produce the least disturbed samples among tube samplers, especially for soft soils.
        *   **Common Use:** Obtaining high-quality undisturbed samples from sensitive clays, silts, and organic soils.

### 5. Key Considerations in Planning Soil Sampling

*   **Project Objectives:** What specific engineering properties need to be determined?
*   **Soil Type and Conditions:** Are the soils cohesive or granular, soft or stiff, saturated or unsaturated?
*   **Depth of Investigation:** How deep do the samples need to be taken to represent the critical soil layers influencing the project?
*   **Budget and Time Constraints:** What sampling methods are feasible within the project's resources?
*   **Site Accessibility:** Can heavy drilling equipment reach the sampling locations?
*   **Regulatory Requirements:** Are there any local standards or guidelines for sampling?

### 6. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary difference between a disturbed and an undisturbed soil sample?

**Answer 1:** The primary difference lies in the preservation of the **in-situ structure, stress state, and water content**. Disturbed samples have undergone significant changes in these aspects during sampling, while undisturbed samples aim to retain them as closely as possible.

**Question 2:** Which type of sample is typically obtained using a Split-Spoon Sampler, and what is its primary purpose?

**Answer 2:** A Split-Spoon Sampler is used to obtain a **disturbed** soil sample. Its primary purpose is to provide a quantitative measure of soil density/stiffness through the Standard Penetration Test (SPT) blow count (N-value), and the recovered sample is used for visual classification and index property tests.

**Question 3:** For what type of laboratory tests would you prioritize obtaining undisturbed samples? Give two examples.

**Answer 3:** Undisturbed samples are crucial for tests that depend on the soil's natural fabric and stress history. Two examples are:
    *   **Triaxial Compression Tests** (to determine shear strength parameters like cohesion and friction angle).
    *   **Consolidation Tests** (to determine compressibility and time-settlement characteristics).

**Question 4:** What are the main reasons why a soil sample might be considered non-representative?

**Answer 4:** A soil sample can be non-representative due to:
    *   **Sampling bias** (e.g., selective sampling).
    *   **Improper sampling techniques** (e.g., using the wrong sampler, over-driving).
    *   **Poor sample handling and preservation** (e.g., drying out, contamination).
    *   **Insufficient sampling density** (not enough samples taken to capture variability).
    *   **Sampling only localized features** rather than the entire stratum.

**Question 5:** You need to investigate the settlement characteristics of a soft clay layer for a major building foundation. Which type of sampling method would be most appropriate for obtaining samples for consolidation testing?

**Answer 5:** For investigating settlement characteristics of soft clay, you would need **undisturbed soil samples**. Methods like **thin-walled tube sampling (Shelby tubes)** or, ideally, **piston samplers** would be most appropriate to preserve the clay's structure and stress history for accurate consolidation testing.

---

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **No Sample is Perfectly Undisturbed:** Recognize that some degree of disturbance is inherent in all sampling methods. The goal is to minimize it to an acceptable level for the intended tests.
*   **Matching Sample Type to Test:** Always select the sample type (disturbed vs. undisturbed) based on the requirements of the specific laboratory tests needed for the project.
*   **Quality Control is Paramount:** Proper training of drilling crews, adherence to established procedures, and careful handling are critical for obtaining reliable samples.
*   **Visual Inspection on Site:** Whenever possible, observe the soil as it is extracted from the borehole or trial pit. This provides valuable real-time information that complements laboratory testing.
*   **Documentation is Key:** Accurate and detailed logging of borehole information, sampling depths, methods used, and any observations made on-site is essential for interpreting laboratory results.
*   **Spatial Variability:** Geotechnical properties of soil can vary significantly horizontally and vertically. A well-planned sampling program accounts for this variability.
