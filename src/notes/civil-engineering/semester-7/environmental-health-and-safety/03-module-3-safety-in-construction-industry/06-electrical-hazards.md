# Environmental Health and Safety: Module 3 - Safety in the Construction Industry

## Topic: Electrical Hazards

---

### **Introduction**

Electrical hazards are a significant concern in the construction industry, posing severe risks of electrocution, burns, fires, and explosions. Understanding these hazards, their causes, and implementing effective control measures is crucial for ensuring the safety of workers on construction sites. This module will delve into the common electrical hazards encountered in construction and the strategies to mitigate them.

### **1. Identifying Common Electrical Hazards on Construction Sites (LO1)**

Construction sites are dynamic environments with a multitude of potential electrical dangers. Some of the most common include:

*   **Overhead Power Lines:**
    *   **Description:** High-voltage lines carrying electricity to and from various locations.
    *   **Risks:** Direct contact with lines (e.g., through cranes, scaffolding, ladders) or indirect contact through conductive materials.
    *   **Example:** A crane operator swinging a load too close to an overhead power line, causing an arc flash or direct contact.
*   **Underground Power Lines:**
    *   **Description:** Buried electrical cables carrying power.
    *   **Risks:** Accidental excavation or digging into buried cables, leading to shock or explosion.
    *   **Example:** A backhoe operator digging a trench and hitting an unmarked underground electrical conduit.
*   **Temporary Wiring and Extension Cords:**
    *   **Description:** Often used for lighting, tools, and temporary power on a site.
    *   **Risks:** Damaged insulation, overloaded circuits, improper connections, exposed wires, tripping hazards, contact with water.
    *   **Example:** An extension cord with frayed insulation being used in a damp area, creating a shock hazard.
*   **Portable Tools and Equipment:**
    *   **Description:** Handheld power tools, generators, lighting fixtures, etc.
    *   **Risks:** Faulty wiring, damaged casings, lack of grounding, improper use.
    *   **Example:** A worker using a power drill with a damaged power cord and no ground connection.
*   **Wet Conditions:**
    *   **Description:** Rain, puddles, leaks, high humidity.
    *   **Risks:** Water significantly reduces the electrical resistance of the body and materials, increasing the severity of shock.
    *   **Example:** Using electrical equipment in the rain without proper protection or GFCI.
*   **Confined Spaces:**
    *   **Description:** Enclosed areas with limited entry/exit, like manholes or tanks.
    *   **Risks:** Increased risk of contact with energized equipment, difficulty in escape, accumulation of flammable vapors.
    *   **Example:** Working with portable lighting inside a wet concrete pit.
*   **Faulty Grounding:**
    *   **Description:** The absence or improper functioning of grounding systems for equipment.
    *   **Risks:** In case of a fault (e.g., a live wire touching the metal casing of a tool), the current will not safely flow to the ground, making the casing live.
    *   **Example:** A portable generator not properly grounded, and its metal frame becoming energized.
*   **Arc Flash and Arc Blast:**
    *   **Description:** A sudden release of electrical energy through the air when a fault occurs, creating an intense heat, light, and pressure wave.
    *   **Risks:** Severe burns, blindness, hearing damage, concussions, and potential explosions.
    *   **Example:** A short circuit in a panelboard causing an arc flash.

### **2. Principles of Electricity and Electrical Hazards (LO2)**

Understanding basic electrical principles is key to grasping how hazards occur.

*   **Voltage (V):** The "pressure" that pushes electric current through a circuit. Measured in Volts (V). Higher voltage generally means higher risk.
*   **Current (I):** The flow of electrical charge. Measured in Amperes (A) or milliamps (mA). It's the current that causes harm.
*   **Resistance (R):** The opposition to the flow of electrical current. Measured in Ohms (Ω). The human body has resistance. Water and wet materials have very low resistance.
*   **Ohm's Law:** States the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance: **V = I × R**. This means for a given voltage, lower resistance allows more current to flow.
*   **Path of Least Resistance:** Electricity will always seek the path of least resistance to the ground. The human body can become this path if there's a voltage difference and a conductive path.
*   **Alternating Current (AC) vs. Direct Current (DC):**
    *   **AC:** Commonly used in construction (e.g., from utility companies). It cycles back and forth. Can cause muscle contractions that prevent letting go.
    *   **DC:** Found in batteries. Generally considered less dangerous than AC at similar voltage levels, but still hazardous.
*   **Grounding:** Provides a safe path for electricity to flow to the earth in case of a fault, preventing metal casings of equipment from becoming energized.
*   **GFCI (Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter):** A safety device that monitors the current flowing out and returning. If it detects an imbalance (meaning current is leaking to ground, possibly through a person), it rapidly shuts off power.

### **3. Effects of Electricity on the Human Body (LO3)**

The severity of an electrical shock depends on the amount of current, the path it takes through the body, the duration of contact, and the body's resistance at the time of contact.

*   **Low Current (1-10 mA):**
    *   **Effect:** Tingling sensation, "let-go" threshold (muscle contraction preventing release of conductor).
*   **Medium Current (10-30 mA):**
    *   **Effect:** Painful shock, inability to let go, respiratory paralysis.
*   **High Current (30-75 mA):**
    *   **Effect:** Ventricular fibrillation (disruption of heart rhythm, often fatal), respiratory arrest.
*   **Very High Current (75 mA and above):**
    *   **Effect:** Severe burns, cardiac arrest, muscle contraction causing bone fractures.
*   **Other Effects:**
    *   **Burns:** Can occur at the point of entry and exit, or internally due to heat generated by the current.
    *   **Falls:** A person experiencing a shock might fall from a height, causing secondary injuries.
    *   **Secondary Injuries:** Such as blunt force trauma from falling, burns, or being thrown by an arc blast.

### **4. Key Control Measures for Preventing Electrical Accidents (LO4)**

A multi-layered approach to safety is essential.

*   **De-energize Circuits Whenever Possible:**
    *   **Description:** Before any work is done on or near electrical equipment, ensure it is de-energized.
    *   **Example:** Turning off the main breaker for a lighting circuit before replacing a fixture.
*   **Use Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters (GFCIs):**
    *   **Description:** GFCIs protect against ground faults by quickly interrupting the circuit.
    *   **Requirement:** Required for all receptacles in damp or wet locations and for most temporary power applications.
    *   **Example:** Using GFCI-protected extension cords when working outdoors or in basements.
*   **Use Double-Insulated Tools:**
    *   **Description:** Tools with two layers of insulation, eliminating the need for grounding. They are marked with a square within a square.
    *   **Example:** Many cordless power tools or tools with plastic casings.
*   **Proper Grounding and Bonding:**
    *   **Description:** Ensure all equipment and conductive parts are properly grounded to provide a safe path for fault current. Bonding connects conductive parts to prevent voltage differences.
    *   **Example:** Using grounded extension cords and ensuring generator frames are properly grounded.
*   **Maintain Safe Distances from Overhead Power Lines:**
    *   **Description:** Establish and strictly adhere to minimum clearance distances from overhead power lines. These distances vary by voltage.
    *   **Example:** A sign indicating a 10-foot clearance zone around an overhead power line.
*   **Barricade and Identify Hazardous Areas:**
    *   **Description:** Use cones, barriers, and warning signs to keep workers away from energized equipment or areas with electrical hazards.
    *   **Example:** Barricading off an electrical panel that is open for maintenance.
*   **Adequate Lighting:**
    *   **Description:** Ensure sufficient lighting to perform tasks safely and identify electrical hazards.
    *   **Example:** Using portable work lights in dimly lit areas where electrical work is being performed.
*   **Keep Work Areas Clean and Dry:**
    *   **Description:** Minimize the presence of water and conductive debris in areas where electrical work is being done.
    *   **Example:** Pumping out water from a trench before installing electrical conduit.
*   **Qualified Personels:**
    *   **Description:** Only trained and qualified personnel should perform electrical work.
    *   **Example:** A licensed electrician performing wiring modifications.

### **5. Importance of Proper Equipment Maintenance and Inspection (LO5)**

Regular maintenance and inspection are crucial for preventing electrical failures and ensuring safety.

*   **Pre-Use Inspections:**
    *   **Description:** Workers should visually inspect all electrical tools and equipment before each use.
    *   **What to check:** Cords for fraying or damage, plugs for bent prongs, casings for cracks, guards in place, proper operation.
*   **Regular Inspections and Testing:**
    *   **Description:** Tools and equipment should undergo periodic inspections and testing by qualified personnel. This includes GFCIs and insulation resistance testing.
    *   **Example:** A scheduled inspection of all power tools in a company's inventory every six months.
*   **Prompt Repair or Replacement:**
    *   **Description:** Any damaged or defective electrical equipment should be immediately removed from service, tagged for repair, or disposed of.
    *   **Example:** A damaged extension cord is immediately tagged "DO NOT USE" and taken for repair or replacement.
*   **Documentation:**
    *   **Description:** Maintain records of inspections, testing, and repairs for all electrical equipment.
    *   **Example:** A logbook for each piece of power equipment detailing its maintenance history.

### **6. Role of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) (LO6)**

PPE acts as a last line of defense when other controls cannot fully eliminate the risk.

*   **Insulated Gloves:**
    *   **Description:** Made of rubber or synthetic materials designed to provide electrical insulation. Rated for specific voltage levels.
    *   **Use:** When working with or near exposed energized conductors. Must be inspected before each use.
    *   **Example:** Electricians wearing Class 0 rubber gloves when working on low-voltage circuits.
*   **Safety Glasses/Face Shields:**
    *   **Description:** Protect eyes and face from arc flash, flying debris, and sparks.
    *   **Use:** Essential when there's a risk of arc flash or flying particles.
    *   **Example:** Wearing a full face shield over safety glasses when working on a live electrical panel.
*   **Insulated Footwear:**
    *   **Description:** Boots with rubber soles that provide an additional barrier against electrical current.
    *   **Use:** In environments with a risk of electrical contact.
    *   **Example:** Wearing electrical hazard (EH) rated boots on a construction site.
*   **Flame-Resistant (FR) Clothing:**
    *   **Description:** Clothing made from materials that do not ignite or melt when exposed to high heat, such as from an arc flash.
    *   **Use:** For tasks with a significant risk of arc flash.
    *   **Example:** Wearing an FR shirt and pants when working in areas with high fault current potential.
*   **Insulated Tools:**
    *   **Description:** Tools with insulated handles designed to protect the user from shock.
    *   **Use:** For working on or near energized circuits when de-energization is not possible.
    *   **Example:** Using insulated screwdrivers or pliers.

### **7. Significance of Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) Procedures (LO7)**

Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) is a critical safety procedure designed to prevent unexpected energization or startup of machinery and equipment during maintenance or servicing.

*   **Purpose:** To ensure that hazardous energy sources are isolated and rendered inoperable before an employee performs any work on the equipment.
*   **Key Components:**
    *   **Lockout:** Physically attaching a lock to an energy-isolating device (e.g., circuit breaker, switch) in the "off" or "safe" position. This prevents the device from being operated.
    *   **Tagout:** Attaching a tag to the lock that clearly indicates the name of the person who applied the lock, the date, and the reason for the lockout.
*   **Steps in LOTO:**
    1.  **Preparation:** Identify all energy sources and the appropriate LOTO devices.
    2.  **Notification:** Inform all affected employees that LOTO procedures will be performed.
    3.  **Shutdown:** Turn off the equipment using its normal stopping procedure.
    4.  **Isolation:** Locate and operate the correct energy-isolating devices (e.g., circuit breakers, valves).
    5.  **Lockout/Tagout:** Apply locks and tags to each energy-isolating device in the "off" or "safe" position.
    6.  **Verification:** Attempt to operate the equipment to ensure it cannot be started. Test all modes of operation.
    7.  **Work:** Perform the maintenance or servicing.
    8.  **Restoration:** Remove LOTO devices, re-energize equipment, and notify affected employees.
*   **"When You Lock It Out, You Tag It Out":** A crucial reminder that a lock should always be accompanied by a tag.
*   **"One Person, One Lock":** Each worker performing maintenance should have their own lock and tag on the equipment.

### **8. Emergency Procedures in Case of Electrical Incidents (LO8)**

Knowing how to respond during an electrical emergency can save lives.

*   **Do NOT Touch the Victim:**
    *   **Description:** If someone is in contact with an energized source, do not touch them. You will also become a victim.
*   **Safely Turn Off Power:**
    *   **Action:** If possible, immediately turn off the power source (e.g., circuit breaker, unplug the device) without touching the victim or any conductive material.
*   **If Power Cannot Be Safely Turned Off:**
    *   **Action:** Use a non-conductive object (e.g., dry wood, rubber, plastic) to push the victim away from the source of electricity.
*   **Call for Emergency Services:**
    *   **Action:** Dial your local emergency number (e.g., 911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 112 in Europe) immediately. Provide details about the situation and the location.
*   **Check for Breathing and Pulse:**
    *   **Action:** Once the victim is clear of the electrical source and it's safe to approach, check for breathing and a pulse.
*   **Administer CPR/First Aid:**
    *   **Action:** If trained, begin Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR) if the victim is not breathing or has no pulse. Use an Automated External Defibrillator (AED) if available and trained to use it.
*   **Do Not Move the Victim Unless Absolutely Necessary:**
    *   **Description:** Moving the victim could worsen injuries, especially if they have fallen from a height or sustained internal trauma.
*   **Keep the Area Safe:**
    *   **Action:** Ensure the area is safe for emergency responders and prevent others from entering the danger zone.

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **Electricity is invisible and silent until it causes harm.** Always assume equipment is energized unless proven otherwise.
*   **Water is a conductor.** Always exercise extreme caution when working with or near electricity in wet or damp conditions.
*   **Overhead power lines are extremely dangerous.** Maintain safe working distances and never assume they are de-energized.
*   **Damaged cords and equipment are immediate hazards.** Never use or handle them.
*   **LOTO is non-negotiable for maintenance and repair work.** It is the most effective way to prevent accidental energization.
*   **Only qualified and authorized personnel should perform electrical work.**
*   **Always wear appropriate PPE for the task and the identified electrical hazards.**
*   **Know your emergency procedures.** Quick and correct action can save lives.

### **Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:** You are working on a construction site and notice a temporary power cord with frayed insulation lying in a puddle of water. What is the immediate danger, and what should you do?

**Question 2:** What is the primary purpose of a Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter (GFCI)?
    a) To prevent overloads
    b) To protect against short circuits
    c) To protect against ground faults by interrupting the circuit
    d) To increase the voltage

**Question 3:** Describe the "let-go" threshold in relation to electrical shock.

**Question 4:** You are operating a boom lift on a construction site. What is the most critical electrical hazard you must be aware of? How should you prevent an accident?

**Question 5:** Explain the difference between "lockout" and "tagout" in the context of LOTO procedures.

---

### **Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**
*   **Immediate Danger:** The frayed insulation exposes the live conductors, and the puddle of water provides a conductive path. This creates a severe risk of electric shock to anyone who touches the cord or the water.
*   **What to do:**
    1.  **Do NOT touch the cord or the water.**
    2.  **Immediately inform your supervisor or foreman** about the hazard.
    3.  **If safe to do so and trained, turn off the power source** for that cord.
    4.  **Barricade the area** to prevent others from approaching the hazard.
    5.  **Ensure the cord is tagged** for repair or replacement and taken out of service.

**Answer 2:**
*   The correct answer is **c) To protect against ground faults by interrupting the circuit.** GFCIs are designed to detect imbalances in current flow, which indicate that electricity is leaking to ground, and quickly shut off the power, preventing shocks.

**Answer 3:**
*   The "let-go" threshold is the amount of electrical current (typically around 10-20 mA) that, when passing through the body, causes involuntary muscle contractions. If the current is low enough to pass through the muscles controlling the hands, the person may be unable to release their grip on the energized conductor.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Most Critical Electrical Hazard:** Overhead power lines.
*   **How to Prevent an Accident:**
    *   **Maintain safe clearance distances:** Always be aware of the proximity of power lines and adhere to established minimum distances (these vary by voltage and are often posted).
    *   **High-visibility flagging:** Use non-conductive flags or markers to highlight the location of overhead power lines.
    *   **Operator training:** Ensure boom lift operators are trained on electrical hazard awareness and safe operating procedures around power lines.
    *   **Spotter:** Use a trained spotter to guide the lift operator, especially when working near power lines.
    *   **De-energization:** If possible, contact the utility company to de-energize the lines before work begins.

**Answer 5:**
*   **Lockout:** This refers to the physical application of a lock to an energy-isolating device (e.g., circuit breaker, valve) to prevent it from being operated or the equipment from being energized. The lock physically secures the device in the "off" or "safe" position.
*   **Tagout:** This involves attaching a tag to the lock. The tag provides important information, such as the name of the person who applied the lock, the date, and the reason for the lockout. It serves as a warning that the equipment is being serviced and should not be operated. Together, they ensure that hazardous energy is controlled and that no one accidentally restarts the equipment while someone is working on it.
