# Environmental Health and Safety - Module 2: Chemical Hazards

## Topic: Radiation Hazards, Types, and Effects of Radiation on the Human Body

---

### Learning Outcomes:

*   **LO1:** Understand the fundamental principles of radiation and its sources.
*   **LO2:** Differentiate between ionizing and non-ionizing radiation.
*   **LO3:** Identify the common types of ionizing radiation and their properties.
*   **LO4:** Explain the biological effects of radiation on the human body, distinguishing between deterministic and stochastic effects.
*   **LO5:** Recognize common sources of radiation exposure in everyday life and occupational settings.
*   **LO6:** Understand basic principles of radiation protection.

### 1. Introduction to Radiation and Its Principles

*   **Definition:** Radiation is the emission of energy from a source in the form of waves or particles.

*   **Sources of Radiation:**
    *   **Natural Sources:**
        *   **Cosmic Radiation:** High-energy particles originating from outer space (stars, supernovae). Intensity varies with altitude and latitude.
        *   **Terrestrial Radiation:** Radioactive isotopes naturally present in the Earth's crust, soil, and rocks (e.g., uranium, thorium, potassium-40).
        *   **Internal Radiation:** Radioactive isotopes naturally present within the human body (e.g., potassium-40, carbon-14).
        *   **Radon Gas:** A naturally occurring radioactive gas emitted from the decay of uranium in the soil. Can accumulate in buildings.
    *   **Artificial (Man-made) Sources:**
        *   **Medical Sources:** X-rays (diagnostic imaging), CT scans, radiation therapy, nuclear medicine (radioisotopes for diagnosis and treatment).
        *   **Industrial Sources:** Nuclear power plants, industrial radiography (non-destructive testing), smoke detectors (Americium-241), certain consumer products.
        *   **Nuclear Weapons:** Fallout from nuclear explosions.

*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Radioactivity:** The spontaneous disintegration of atomic nuclei, accompanied by the emission of radiation.
    *   **Isotopes:** Atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Radioactive isotopes (radioisotopes) are unstable and undergo radioactive decay.
    *   **Half-life:** The time it takes for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay. This is a characteristic property of each radioisotope.

### 2. Types of Radiation: Ionizing vs. Non-ionizing

*   **Ionizing Radiation:**
    *   **Definition:** Radiation with sufficient energy to remove tightly bound electrons from atoms or molecules, creating ions (charged particles). This ionization process can damage biological tissues.
    *   **Mechanism:** The energy of ionizing radiation is high enough to break chemical bonds and disrupt cellular structures.

*   **Non-ionizing Radiation:**
    *   **Definition:** Radiation with lower energy, not sufficient to ionize atoms or molecules. It primarily causes heating of tissues.
    *   **Examples:** Radio waves, microwaves, infrared radiation, visible light, and UV-A/UV-B (though UV-C is ionizing).
    *   **Biological Effects:** Primarily thermal effects (heating) at high intensities. UV-A/UV-B can cause skin damage (sunburn) and increase cancer risk by damaging DNA through non-ionizing mechanisms.

---

### 3. Common Types of Ionizing Radiation and Their Properties

Ionizing radiation is typically categorized based on the particles or electromagnetic waves it comprises.

#### 3.1. Alpha (α) Particles

*   **Nature:** Positively charged particles, consisting of two protons and two neutrons (essentially a helium nucleus).
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Mass:** Relatively heavy.
    *   **Charge:** +2.
    *   **Penetration Power:** Very low. Can be stopped by a sheet of paper, the outer layer of skin, or a few centimeters of air.
    *   **Ionizing Power:** Very high. Due to their charge and mass, they interact strongly with matter, causing significant ionization over a short path.
*   **Hazard:** Primarily an **internal hazard**. If inhaled, ingested, or enter the body through wounds, alpha emitters can deliver a high dose of radiation to nearby cells, causing significant damage.
*   **Example:** Radon gas emits alpha particles.

#### 3.2. Beta (β) Particles

*   **Nature:** Negatively charged particles (electrons) or positively charged particles (positrons) emitted from the nucleus during radioactive decay.
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Mass:** Much lighter than alpha particles.
    *   **Charge:** -1 (for electrons) or +1 (for positrons).
    *   **Penetration Power:** Moderate. Can penetrate a few millimeters of skin, a few meters of air, or a thin sheet of aluminum or plastic.
    *   **Ionizing Power:** Moderate. Less ionizing than alpha particles but more than gamma rays.
*   **Hazard:** Can be both an **external and internal hazard**.
    *   **External:** Can cause skin burns if exposure is high.
    *   **Internal:** If ingested or inhaled, they can irradiate internal organs.
*   **Example:** Tritium (used in self-luminous signs), Carbon-14 (used in radiocarbon dating), Strontium-90 (a byproduct of nuclear fission).

#### 3.3. Gamma (γ) Rays

*   **Nature:** High-energy electromagnetic radiation (photons), similar to X-rays but originating from the nucleus.
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Mass:** No mass.
    *   **Charge:** No charge.
    *   **Penetration Power:** Very high. Can penetrate deep into tissues and materials. Requires dense shielding like thick lead or concrete to significantly reduce their intensity.
    *   **Ionizing Power:** Low. Interact less frequently with matter compared to alpha and beta particles, but their high penetration means they can affect large volumes of tissue.
*   **Hazard:** Primarily an **external hazard** due to their high penetration.
*   **Example:** Used in radiation therapy for cancer treatment, medical imaging.

#### 3.4. X-rays

*   **Nature:** High-energy electromagnetic radiation (photons), similar to gamma rays, but generated by the deceleration of electrons or electron transitions in atoms.
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Mass:** No mass.
    *   **Charge:** No charge.
    *   **Penetration Power:** High, similar to gamma rays, but generally less energetic than gamma rays originating from nuclear decay.
    *   **Ionizing Power:** Low.
*   **Hazard:** Primarily an **external hazard**.
*   **Example:** Diagnostic medical imaging (X-ray machines).

#### 3.5. Neutrons

*   **Nature:** Neutral particles found in the nucleus of an atom.
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Mass:** Similar to protons.
    *   **Charge:** No charge.
    *   **Penetration Power:** High, especially at high energies. Can penetrate deeply.
    *   **Ionizing Power:** Indirect. Neutrons themselves do not directly ionize but cause ionization by interacting with atomic nuclei, which then release charged particles. They can also induce radioactivity in stable materials.
*   **Hazard:** Primarily an **external hazard**. Significant hazard around nuclear reactors or particle accelerators.
*   **Example:** Nuclear fission reactors, particle accelerators.

### 4. Biological Effects of Radiation on the Human Body

The effects of radiation depend on the dose, dose rate, type of radiation, the part of the body exposed, and individual sensitivity.

*   **Interaction with Cells:** Ionizing radiation damages cells by:
    *   **Direct Action:** The radiation directly strikes and damages critical molecules like DNA.
    *   **Indirect Action:** The radiation interacts with water molecules in the cell, creating highly reactive free radicals, which then damage cellular components, especially DNA.

*   **Deterministic Effects (Tissue Reactions):**
    *   **Definition:** Effects that have a threshold dose below which they do not occur. The severity of the effect increases with dose above the threshold.
    *   **Cause:** Caused by the death or malfunction of a large number of cells.
    *   **Latency Period:** Typically have a shorter latency period (hours to weeks).
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Skin erythema (redness/sunburn):** Occurs at doses around 1-3 Gy.
        *   **Hair loss (epilation):** Occurs at doses around 3-5 Gy.
        *   **Cataracts:** Clouding of the lens of the eye, with a threshold of around 5 Gy for single exposures.
        *   **Sterility:** Temporary or permanent.
        *   **Radiation sickness (Acute Radiation Syndrome - ARS):** Occurs after high whole-body exposure (typically >1 Gy), characterized by nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fatigue, and bone marrow suppression.
    *   **Important Point:** Deterministic effects are generally considered to be non-stochastic, meaning they are not probabilistic; if the dose is high enough, the effect will occur.

*   **Stochastic Effects:**
    *   **Definition:** Effects for which the probability of occurrence increases with dose, but the severity is independent of the dose. There is generally no threshold dose for these effects; any dose, however small, carries a risk.
    *   **Cause:** Caused by damage to DNA, leading to mutations that can result in cancer or hereditary effects.
    *   **Latency Period:** Typically have a long latency period (years to decades for cancer, one or two generations for hereditary effects).
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Cancer:** The most significant stochastic effect. Radiation can cause mutations in genes that control cell growth, leading to uncontrolled cell proliferation.
        *   **Hereditary Effects (Genetic Mutations):** Mutations in germ cells (sperm or egg) that can be passed on to offspring.
    *   **Important Point:** The risk from stochastic effects is probabilistic. A higher dose increases the probability of the effect occurring.

*   **Dose-Response Relationship:**
    *   **Linear No-Threshold (LNT) Model:** Generally assumed for stochastic effects. It postulates that the risk from radiation exposure is directly proportional to the dose, and there is no safe level of exposure. This model is used for radiation protection purposes.
    *   **Threshold Model:** Used for deterministic effects.

### 5. Common Sources of Radiation Exposure

*   **Occupational Exposure:**
    *   Healthcare professionals (radiologists, nurses, technicians).
    *   Workers in nuclear power plants.
    *   Industrial radiography technicians.
    *   Airline pilots and flight attendants (due to increased exposure to cosmic radiation at altitude).
    *   Researchers working with radioactive materials.

*   **Public Exposure:**
    *   **Medical Diagnostics:** X-rays, CT scans (major contributor to public dose).
    *   **Natural Background Radiation:** Cosmic, terrestrial, and internal sources.
    *   **Consumer Products:** Smoke detectors, some older luminous watches, granite countertops (emitting low levels of radiation).
    *   **Radon:** In homes, especially basements and poorly ventilated areas.

---

### 6. Principles of Radiation Protection (ALARA)

The fundamental principle of radiation protection is **ALARA: As Low As Reasonably Achievable.** This means keeping radiation doses to workers and the public as low as possible, without exceeding acceptable limits, taking economic and social factors into account.

*   **The Three Pillars of Radiation Protection:**
    1.  **Time:** Minimize the time spent near a radiation source. The total dose received is directly proportional to the time of exposure.
        *   *Example:* If you reduce your time in an area by half, you reduce your dose by half.
    2.  **Distance:** Maximize the distance from a radiation source. Radiation intensity decreases rapidly with increasing distance, following the inverse square law (intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance).
        *   *Example:* If you double your distance from a point source, the radiation intensity you receive drops to one-fourth.
    3.  **Shielding:** Use appropriate materials to absorb or block radiation. The type and thickness of shielding depend on the type and energy of the radiation.
        *   **Alpha:** Paper or skin is sufficient.
        *   **Beta:** Aluminum or plastic.
        *   **Gamma/X-rays:** Lead or concrete.
        *   **Neutrons:** Materials rich in hydrogen (like water or polyethylene) are effective for slowing them down, followed by materials that absorb slow neutrons (like cadmium or boron).

*   **Dose Limits:** Regulatory bodies set dose limits for occupational exposure and public exposure to ensure safety. These limits are based on the ALARA principle and the understanding of radiation's biological effects.

---

### Key Points to Remember:

*   Radiation is energy emitted as waves or particles.
*   Ionizing radiation has enough energy to remove electrons from atoms, causing damage. Non-ionizing radiation does not.
*   Alpha particles are heavy, highly ionizing but have low penetration (internal hazard).
*   Beta particles are lighter, moderately ionizing with moderate penetration (internal and external hazard).
*   Gamma and X-rays are electromagnetic radiation, weakly ionizing but highly penetrating (external hazard).
*   Neutrons are uncharged particles with high penetration, causing ionization indirectly (external hazard).
*   **Deterministic effects** have a threshold and their severity increases with dose (e.g., skin burns, cataracts).
*   **Stochastic effects** have no threshold and their probability increases with dose (e.g., cancer, genetic mutations).
*   The ALARA principle (Time, Distance, Shielding) is crucial for radiation protection.
*   Medical imaging is a significant source of public radiation exposure.

### Practice Questions and Answers:

**Question 1:** Which type of ionizing radiation has the highest ionizing power but the lowest penetrating power?
    a) Gamma rays
    b) Beta particles
    c) Alpha particles
    d) X-rays

**Question 2:** Which of the following is an example of a deterministic effect of radiation?
    a) Cancer
    b) Genetic mutation
    c) Skin redness (erythema)
    d) Birth defects

**Question 3:** The ALARA principle stands for:
    a) Always Limit All Radiation Accurately
    b) As Low As Reasonably Achievable
    c) Avoid Large Radiation Amounts
    d) Allow Low Absorption Rates

**Question 4:** To protect yourself from gamma radiation, the most effective shielding material would be:
    a) Paper
    b) Aluminum foil
    c) Thick concrete or lead
    d) Plastic wrap

**Question 5:** Radon gas is primarily a hazard because it emits which type of radiation?
    a) Gamma rays
    b) X-rays
    c) Beta particles
    d) Alpha particles

---

**Answers:**

1.  **c) Alpha particles** (Alpha particles are heavy and charged, leading to dense ionization over a short path, but they are easily stopped by a thin barrier.)
2.  **c) Skin redness (erythema)** (Deterministic effects have a threshold and their severity increases with dose. Skin redness is a classic example.)
3.  **b) As Low As Reasonably Achievable** (This is the cornerstone of radiation protection.)
4.  **c) Thick concrete or lead** (Gamma radiation is highly penetrating, requiring dense materials like lead or concrete for effective shielding.)
5.  **d) Alpha particles** (Radon is a gas that decays to emit alpha particles, which are particularly hazardous when inhaled and deposited in the lungs.)
