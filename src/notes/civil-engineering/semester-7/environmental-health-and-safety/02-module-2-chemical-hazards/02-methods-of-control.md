# Environmental Health and Safety - Module 2: Chemical Hazards

## Topic: Methods of Control

---

### **Introduction**

This module focuses on the critical aspect of managing chemical hazards: implementing effective control measures. Once chemical hazards are identified and assessed, the next logical step is to implement strategies to eliminate or minimize exposure and risk to human health and the environment. This topic will explore the hierarchy of controls, a systematic approach to selecting the most effective methods for managing chemical hazards.

---

### **Learning Outcomes**

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   Understand and apply the Hierarchy of Controls for managing chemical hazards.
*   Identify and describe various methods of control at each level of the hierarchy.
*   Evaluate the effectiveness and suitability of different control measures for specific chemical hazards.
*   Explain the importance of a combined approach to control chemical hazards.
*   Recognize the role of engineering controls, administrative controls, and Personal Protective Equipment (PPE).

---

### **Key Concepts and Definitions**

*   **Chemical Hazard:** A substance that has the potential to cause harm to human health or the environment.
*   **Risk:** The likelihood that a hazard will cause harm and the severity of that harm.
*   **Control Measure:** An action or system implemented to eliminate or reduce the risk associated with a hazard.
*   **Hierarchy of Controls:** A framework that ranks different types of controls based on their effectiveness in reducing exposure. It is a systematic approach to managing risks, prioritizing the most effective methods first.

---

### **The Hierarchy of Controls**

The Hierarchy of Controls is a widely accepted framework for managing risks. It emphasizes that some control methods are more effective than others. The levels are typically presented in order of decreasing effectiveness:

#### **1. Elimination**

*   **Definition:** The most effective control measure. It involves completely removing the hazardous chemical or process from the workplace or activity.
*   **Description:** This is the ideal scenario, as it removes the hazard entirely, thus eliminating any associated risks.
*   **Examples:**
    *   Replacing a toxic solvent with a less hazardous or non-hazardous alternative (e.g., using water-based cleaners instead of solvent-based ones).
    *   Discontinuing a process that requires the use of a dangerous chemical.
    *   Designing a product that does not require the use of a particular hazardous chemical in its manufacturing.

#### **2. Substitution**

*   **Definition:** Replacing a hazardous chemical or process with a less hazardous one.
*   **Description:** While not as effective as elimination, substitution significantly reduces the risk by lowering the inherent danger of the substance or method.
*   **Examples:**
    *   Using a less volatile solvent with a lower flashpoint instead of a highly flammable one.
    *   Replacing a chemical that causes respiratory sensitization with one that does not.
    *   Using a less toxic cleaning agent.

#### **3. Engineering Controls**

*   **Definition:** Physical changes to the workplace, equipment, or process that isolate people from the hazard or reduce the hazard at its source. These controls are designed to work independently of worker behavior.
*   **Description:** Engineering controls are generally more reliable and effective than administrative controls or PPE because they are built into the system.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Ventilation Systems:**
        *   **Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV):** Captures contaminants at or near the source of generation (e.g., fume hoods, extraction arms over welding stations, dust collectors).
        *   **General Dilution Ventilation:** Introduces fresh air into the workspace to dilute the concentration of airborne contaminants (less effective for highly toxic substances).
    *   **Enclosure/Containment:**
        *   Enclosing a process or machine that releases hazardous fumes or dust (e.g., a fully enclosed mixing vessel with a dedicated ventilation system).
        *   Using glove boxes for handling highly toxic or reactive materials.
    *   **Process Modification:**
        *   Automating hazardous tasks to reduce direct human interaction.
        *   Changing the temperature or pressure of a process to reduce the release of vapors.
    *   **Isolation:**
        *   Physically separating hazardous operations from other work areas (e.g., a separate room for chemical storage or mixing).

#### **4. Administrative Controls**

*   **Definition:** Changes to work practices, procedures, and policies that reduce exposure to hazards. These controls rely on worker behavior and adherence to rules.
*   **Description:** Administrative controls are important but are generally considered less effective than engineering controls as they depend on human compliance, which can be variable.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Work Procedures and Practices:**
        *   Developing and implementing Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for handling specific chemicals.
        *   Implementing good housekeeping practices (e.g., regular cleaning to remove dust and spills).
        *   Minimizing the amount of hazardous chemical stored or used at any one time.
        *   Implementing "permit to work" systems for high-risk activities.
    *   **Training and Education:**
        *   Providing comprehensive training on chemical hazards, safe handling procedures, emergency response, and the correct use of PPE.
        *   Regular safety meetings and toolbox talks.
    *   **Work Scheduling and Rotation:**
        *   Limiting the time workers spend in areas where exposure is high.
        *   Rotating tasks to reduce the duration of exposure for individuals.
    *   **Warning Signs and Labels:**
        *   Clearly labeling all chemical containers with hazard information (e.g., GHS labels).
        *   Posting warning signs in areas where hazardous chemicals are used or stored.
    *   **Exposure Monitoring:**
        *   Regularly monitoring air quality and worker exposure levels to assess the effectiveness of controls.

#### **5. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)**

*   **Definition:** Equipment worn by workers to protect them from specific hazards. This is the last line of defense and should only be used when other control measures cannot adequately reduce the risk.
*   **Description:** PPE is highly dependent on correct selection, proper fit, consistent use, and maintenance. It does not eliminate the hazard, only provides a barrier.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Respiratory Protection:** Respirators (e.g., dust masks, half-face or full-face respirators with appropriate cartridges, supplied-air respirators). The type of respirator and cartridge must be matched to the specific chemical and its concentration.
    *   **Skin Protection:** Gloves (made of materials resistant to the specific chemical, e.g., nitrile, neoprene, butyl rubber), lab coats, aprons, chemical-resistant suits.
    *   **Eye and Face Protection:** Safety glasses, chemical splash goggles, face shields.
    *   **Foot Protection:** Chemical-resistant safety boots.

### **Applying the Hierarchy: Choosing the Right Controls**

The most effective strategy for controlling chemical hazards is to use a combination of controls, starting with the most effective (elimination/substitution) and progressively moving down the hierarchy as needed.

*   **Consider the hazard:** The nature of the chemical (e.g., flammability, toxicity, corrosivity, volatility) will dictate the most appropriate controls.
*   **Consider the exposure:** How are workers exposed? (e.g., inhalation, skin contact, ingestion).
*   **Consider the task:** What is being done with the chemical? (e.g., mixing, dispensing, heating).
*   **Consider feasibility and practicality:** While elimination is ideal, it may not always be possible. Engineering controls can be costly, and administrative controls require ongoing effort.

**Important Principle:** **"Control at Source"** is a key concept. Engineering controls are generally the best at achieving control at the source.

---

### **Integrated Approach to Chemical Hazard Control**

A robust chemical safety program utilizes multiple layers of protection. Relying solely on one type of control is often insufficient.

*   **Example Scenario:** Working with volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in a laboratory.
    1.  **Elimination/Substitution:** Can a less volatile or non-hazardous solvent be used?
    2.  **Engineering Controls:** If substitution isn't possible, use a fume hood (LEV) to capture VOCs at the source. Ensure the fume hood is functioning correctly with adequate airflow.
    3.  **Administrative Controls:** Develop SOPs for solvent handling, ensure proper labeling, train personnel on safe practices, and limit the amount of solvent used at the bench.
    4.  **PPE:** Wear chemical-resistant gloves and safety goggles as a final barrier against potential spills or splashes.

---

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **The Hierarchy of Controls is a guide:** Always strive for controls higher up the hierarchy first.
*   **PPE is the last resort:** It is not a substitute for effective engineering or administrative controls.
*   **Controls must be maintained:** Engineering controls need regular inspection and maintenance. Administrative controls require ongoing reinforcement. PPE needs proper care and replacement.
*   **Worker involvement:** Involve workers in the selection and implementation of control measures, as they have firsthand knowledge of the risks and practical challenges.
*   **Regular review:** Periodically review the effectiveness of control measures and update them as necessary, especially after incidents or changes in processes.
*   **Legislation and Standards:** Be aware of relevant national and international regulations and guidelines pertaining to chemical hazard control.

### **Answers to Practice Questions**

1.  The levels of the Hierarchy of Controls, from most effective to least effective, are:
    *   Elimination
    *   Substitution
    *   Engineering Controls
    *   Administrative Controls
    *   Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

2.  **Elimination:** Stopping the use of a particular cleaning solvent altogether by redesigning the cleaning process.
    **Substitution:** Replacing a highly toxic cleaning solvent with a less toxic one.
    **Engineering Control:** Installing a local exhaust ventilation (LEV) system over a mixing tank to capture hazardous fumes at the source.

3.  PPE is considered the last line of defense because:
    *   It does not eliminate the hazard itself, but rather creates a barrier between the worker and the hazard.
    *   Its effectiveness relies heavily on correct selection, proper fit, consistent use, and maintenance by the worker.
    *   It can be uncomfortable, leading to non-compliance.
    *   It provides no protection if it fails or is not worn correctly.

4.  Two types of administrative controls are:
    *   **Safe Work Procedures/SOPs:** Developing detailed written procedures for the safe handling, storage, and disposal of specific chemicals.
    *   **Training and Education:** Providing comprehensive training to workers on the hazards of chemicals they use, safe work practices, emergency procedures, and the correct use of PPE.
    *   (Other acceptable answers include: limiting exposure time, job rotation, good housekeeping, clear signage and labeling).

5.  To manage the risk of skin irritation from the solvent:
    *   **Elimination/Substitution:**
        *   Can the degreasing process be eliminated entirely?
        *   Can a less irritating solvent (e.g., a water-based degreaser) be substituted?
    *   **Engineering Controls:**
        *   Can the degreasing operation be enclosed and ventilated?
        *   Can automated degreasing equipment be used to minimize manual contact?
    *   **Administrative Controls:**
        *   Develop a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) for safe degreasing, emphasizing minimal contact.
        *   Provide thorough training on the hazards of the solvent and the SOP.
        *   Ensure good housekeeping to clean up spills promptly.
        *   Limit the amount of solvent kept at the workstation.
    *   **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):**
        *   Require workers to wear appropriate chemical-resistant gloves (e.g., nitrile, neoprene) that are impermeable to the specific solvent.
        *   Provide and enforce the use of safety glasses or goggles to protect eyes from splashes.
        *   Ensure availability and use of protective clothing like aprons or lab coats.
