# Environmental Health and Safety: Module 2 - Chemical Hazards

## Topic: Disposal of Radioactive Waste

---

### Introduction

Radioactive waste, also known as nuclear waste, is a byproduct of nuclear activities, including nuclear power generation, medical procedures, industrial applications, and military programs. Due to its inherent radioactivity, it poses significant risks to human health and the environment if not managed and disposed of properly. This topic delves into the principles, methods, and challenges associated with the safe and responsible disposal of radioactive waste.

---

### 1. Understanding Radioactive Waste

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Radioactivity:** The spontaneous emission of radiation (alpha particles, beta particles, gamma rays, neutrons) from unstable atomic nuclei.
*   **Radioactive Material:** Any material containing naturally occurring or artificially produced radionuclides.
*   **Radionuclide:** An atom with an unstable nucleus that undergoes radioactive decay.
*   **Half-life:** The time it takes for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay. This is a crucial factor in determining the longevity of the hazard.

**Types of Radioactive Waste:**

Radioactive waste is typically categorized based on its radioactivity level and physical form:

*   **Low-Level Waste (LLW):**
    *   **Description:** Contains small amounts of radioactivity, often with short half-lives. It is generally not hazardous as radiation *per se*, but its handling and disposal require precautions due to its radioactive content.
    *   **Examples:** Contaminated protective clothing, tools, filters, medical swabs, laboratory glassware.
    *   **Characteristics:** Typically solid, but can be liquid or airborne. Generally has low specific activity (radioactivity per unit mass or volume).

*   **Intermediate-Level Waste (ILW):**
    *   **Description:** Contains higher amounts of radioactivity than LLW and may require shielding. It contains radionuclides with longer half-lives and higher concentrations than LLW.
    *   **Examples:** Reactor components, chemical sludges from processing irradiated fuel, contaminated equipment from nuclear power plants.
    *   **Characteristics:** Can be liquid or solid. Often requires shielding during handling and storage.

*   **High-Level Waste (HLW):**
    *   **Description:** Highly radioactive and generates significant heat due to the decay of radionuclides with long half-lives. This category poses the most significant long-term hazard.
    *   **Examples:** Spent nuclear fuel from nuclear reactors, waste streams from the reprocessing of spent nuclear fuel.
    *   **Characteristics:** Typically liquid (initially) and then solidified into a stable form (e.g., glassification). Requires heavy shielding and sophisticated containment due to intense radioactivity and heat generation.

*   **Transuranic Waste (TRU):**
    *   **Description:** Waste containing alpha-emitting radionuclides with atomic numbers greater than uranium (e.g., plutonium, americium, neptunium). These have very long half-lives and require management for very long periods.
    *   **Examples:** Contaminated tools, protective clothing, equipment and residues from the reprocessing of spent nuclear fuel.
    *   **Characteristics:** Typically solid. The long half-lives are a primary concern for disposal.

---

### 2. Principles of Radioactive Waste Management

Safe disposal of radioactive waste is guided by fundamental principles:

*   **Containment:** Preventing the release of radioactive materials into the environment. This involves multiple barriers.
*   **Isolation:** Separating the waste from the biosphere for the duration of its hazardous life.
*   **Shielding:** Using materials (lead, concrete, water) to absorb radiation and protect personnel.
*   **Dilution and Dispersion:** (Used cautiously for very low-level airborne or liquid releases, with strict regulatory limits). Spreading the radioactivity over a large volume of air or water to reduce concentration. **This is NOT a disposal method for most radioactive waste.**
*   **Decay:** Allowing short-lived radionuclides to decay to harmless levels before disposal.
*   **Time:** The ultimate solution for reducing radioactivity is time (decay).
*   **Minimization:** Reducing the volume and radioactivity of waste generated in the first place through efficient processes and careful practices.

---

### 3. Disposal Methods for Radioactive Waste

The disposal method depends heavily on the waste's characteristics (level of radioactivity, half-life, physical form).

**A. Disposal of Low-Level Waste (LLW):**

*   **Near-Surface Disposal:**
    *   **Description:** Waste is buried in engineered facilities located at or near the Earth's surface. Facilities are designed with multiple barriers (e.g., concrete vaults, liners, caps) to prevent radionuclide migration.
    *   **Key Features:** Sites are chosen for favorable geological and hydrological conditions. Monitored for decades after closure.
    *   **Example:** Engineered trenches and vaults at licensed LLW disposal facilities.

**B. Disposal of Intermediate-Level Waste (ILW):**

*   **Geological Disposal (for some ILW):**
    *   **Description:** Similar to HLW disposal, ILW can be disposed of in deep geological repositories, especially if it contains longer-lived radionuclides.
    *   **Description:** Waste is conditioned (e.g., solidified) and placed in engineered containers, then buried in stable geological formations thousands of meters underground.

**C. Disposal of High-Level Waste (HLW) and Long-Lived Transuranic Waste (TRU):**

*   **Deep Geological Disposal:**
    *   **Description:** This is the internationally accepted long-term solution for HLW and long-lived TRU waste. Waste is conditioned into a stable, solid form (e.g., vitrification for liquid HLW). It is then placed in robust, corrosion-resistant canisters. These canisters are emplaced in underground facilities constructed in stable geological formations (e.g., granite, salt, clay). Multiple engineered and natural barriers are used to isolate the waste from the biosphere for hundreds of thousands of years.
    *   **Key Features:**
        *   **Multi-barrier System:**
            *   **Waste Form:** Solidification (e.g., vitrification) makes it resistant to dissolution.
            *   **Canister:** Corrosion-resistant material (e.g., stainless steel, copper).
            *   **Buffer/Backfill:** Materials like bentonite clay swell when wet, sealing gaps and immobilizing radionuclides.
            *   **Geological Barrier:** The host rock itself provides a stable, low-permeability environment.
        *   **Site Selection:** Crucial, requiring extensive geological and hydrological studies to ensure long-term stability and low groundwater movement.
    *   **Status:** While the concept is agreed upon, the implementation of deep geological repositories for HLW is still in development or early operational stages in most countries (e.g., Yucca Mountain in the US, Onkalo in Finland).

*   **Interim Storage:**
    *   **Description:** HLW is often stored temporarily (for decades or even centuries) before final disposal. This allows short-lived radionuclides to decay, reducing heat generation and radioactivity, and provides time for the development and construction of geological repositories.
    *   **Methods:**
        *   **Wet Storage:** Spent fuel pools, where fuel assemblies are submerged in water for cooling and shielding.
        *   **Dry Storage:** Spent fuel is placed in robust, sealed containers (casks) that provide shielding and cooling through natural air circulation.

**D. Other Disposal/Management Approaches (Less common for HLW/ILW, more for LLW or specific scenarios):**

*   **Transmutation:**
    *   **Description:** A process where long-lived radionuclides are bombarded with neutrons in a reactor or accelerator to convert them into shorter-lived or stable isotopes.
    *   **Status:** Still largely in the research and development phase, not a widespread disposal method.

*   **Disposal in Boreholes:**
    *   **Description:** Waste is placed in very deep, narrow boreholes drilled into stable rock formations.
    *   **Status:** Considered as a potential option but less developed than mined repositories.

### 4. Regulatory Framework and International Guidelines

*   **International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA):** Sets international safety standards and guidelines for radioactive waste management and disposal.
*   **National Regulatory Bodies:** Each country has its own regulatory authority responsible for licensing, overseeing, and enforcing safety standards for radioactive waste disposal (e.g., Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) in the US, Environment Agency in the UK).
*   **Key Regulatory Concerns:**
    *   **Radiation Protection:** Ensuring doses to workers and the public are kept as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA).
    *   **Environmental Protection:** Preventing contamination of land, water, and air.
    *   **Long-Term Safety:** Ensuring the containment and isolation of waste for periods commensurate with its hazard.

---

### 5. Challenges in Radioactive Waste Disposal

*   **Long Timescales:** The immense timeframes over which HLW remains hazardous (hundreds of thousands of years) are unprecedented for societal infrastructure.
*   **Public Acceptance:** Gaining public trust and acceptance for the siting and operation of disposal facilities is a major hurdle.
*   **Technical Complexity:** Developing and operating facilities that can safely contain waste for geological timescales is technically challenging.
*   **Cost:** The development and operation of disposal facilities are extremely expensive.
*   **Transboundary Issues:** Decisions made by one country can affect neighboring countries.
*   **Security:** Ensuring the security of radioactive waste from diversion or sabotage.

### Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **LLW** contains small amounts of radioactivity, often with short half-lives, and is generally not hazardous as radiation *per se*, but requires careful handling. **HLW** is highly radioactive, generates significant heat, and contains radionuclides with long half-lives, posing a substantial long-term hazard requiring robust containment and isolation.
2.  The key principles are containment, isolation, shielding, dilution/dispersion (used cautiously), decay, time, and minimization.
3.  A multi-barrier system in deep geological disposal includes:
    *   **Waste Form:** Solidified waste (e.g., glass).
    *   **Canister:** Corrosion-resistant metal container.
    *   **Buffer/Backfill:** Material like bentonite clay to seal gaps and immobilize radionuclides.
    *   **Geological Barrier:** The stable rock formation itself.
4.  Interim storage is necessary for HLW to allow short-lived radionuclides to decay, reducing heat generation and radioactivity. This also provides time for the development and construction of permanent disposal facilities.
5.  The primary challenge is ensuring the safety and containment of the waste for extremely long timescales (hundreds of thousands of years), a period far exceeding the lifespan of human institutions and infrastructure.
6.  Examples include contaminated protective clothing, tools, laboratory glassware, and medical swabs.
7.  The internationally accepted long-term solution for HLW is **deep geological disposal**.

---

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Time is the ultimate factor:** The longer radioactive waste is isolated, the less radioactive it becomes through natural decay.
*   **Containment is key:** Multiple barriers are essential for safe disposal, especially for HLW.
*   **Site selection is critical:** Geological stability and low groundwater movement are paramount for deep geological repositories.
*   **Public perception matters:** Effective communication and community engagement are vital for acceptance of disposal facilities.
*   **No single "perfect" solution:** Management and disposal strategies are constantly evolving based on scientific understanding and technological advancements.
*   **Different wastes need different solutions:** The disposal method must match the hazard of the waste.
