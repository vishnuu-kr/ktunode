### **1. Necessity and Types of Canal Falls**

#### **1.1 Necessity of Canal Falls:**

Canal falls are essential hydraulic structures constructed in irrigation canals to overcome a sudden drop in the canal bed level. This drop is necessitated by:

*   **Change in topography:** When the natural ground slope is steeper than the permissible canal slope.
*   **Alignment requirements:** To maintain a specific canal slope for efficient water delivery despite changes in ground level.
*   **Crossing obstacles:** To safely pass water over natural depressions or existing structures.

**Consequences of not providing a fall:**

*   **Increased velocity:** If water flows down a steeper slope than designed, its velocity increases.
*   **Scouring and erosion:** High velocities can lead to scouring of the canal bed and banks, causing structural damage and sediment transport.
*   **Undermining of foundations:** Scouring can undermine the foundations of downstream structures.
*   **Damage to the canal lining:** Excessive turbulence and high velocities can damage or dislodge canal lining.

#### **1.2 Types of Canal Falls:**

Canal falls are broadly classified based on their shape and the way they dissipate energy:

*   **Glacis Falls:**
    *   **Orifice Fall:** Water flows through an opening (orifice) in a vertical wall.
    *   **Ogee Fall (Parabolic Fall):** Water flows over a curved crest, smoothly transitioning the flow.
    *   **Rapid Fall:** Water flows over a steeply sloping surface (glacis).
*   **Notch Falls:** Water flows over a notch or weir.
    *   **Trapezoidal Notch Fall:** Water flows over a trapezoidal opening.
    *   **Rectangular Notch Fall:** Water flows over a rectangular opening.
    *   **V-Notch Fall:** Water flows over a V-shaped opening.
*   **Sarda Fall:** A specific type of glacis fall with a prominent glacis.
*   **Baffle Wall Fall:** Incorporates baffle walls to break the energy of falling water.

This module focuses on **Trapezoidal Notch Falls**.

### **2. Hydraulic Design Principles for Trapezoidal Notch Falls**

#### **2.1 Basic Concept:**

A trapezoidal notch fall essentially acts as a weir where the water flows over a trapezoidal opening. The primary function is to reduce the water level by a specific amount (the drop) while ensuring controlled flow and preventing excessive energy dissipation that could cause downstream erosion.

#### **2.2 Key Hydraulic Considerations:**

*   **Energy Dissipation:** The fall creates a head difference, causing water to accelerate and gain kinetic energy. This energy needs to be dissipated safely downstream to prevent erosion. The design aims to control the velocity of the water jet falling from the notch.
*   **Discharge:** The structure must be designed to pass the designed discharge of the canal.
*   **Hydraulic Jump:** In many cases, a hydraulic jump is formed downstream of the fall to dissipate energy. The design of the downstream floor and energy dissipating devices (like impact blocks or uplift blocks) is crucial for this.
*   **Uplift Pressure:** The falling water can create uplift pressure on the floor of the fall. Proper design of the downstream floor and curtain walls is necessary to counter this.
*   **Velocity of Approach:** The velocity of water approaching the notch can influence the effective head and discharge.
*   **Downstream Bed Protection:** A solid downstream apron and protection works (like pitching or riprap) are required to prevent scour.

#### **2.3 Hydraulic Design Steps:**

The design of a trapezoidal notch fall typically involves the following steps:

1.  **Determine the required drop (H_drop):** This is given by the difference in bed levels between the upstream and downstream canals.
2.  **Determine the discharge (Q):** This is the design discharge of the canal.
3.  **Select the type of trapezoidal notch:** Common shapes include a broad-crested weir or a narrower crest.
4.  **Calculate the upstream and downstream water levels:** Based on the canal slopes and discharge.
5.  **Determine the head over the crest (h):** This is the difference between the upstream water level and the crest level of the notch.
6.  **Calculate the crest length (L):** Using the weir formula.
7.  **Design the downstream floor and energy dissipating devices:** To safely handle the falling water.

### **3. Key Design Parameters for a Trapezoidal Notch Fall**

#### **3.1 Components of a Trapezoidal Notch Fall:**

*   **Upstream Approach Channel:** Designed to bring water smoothly to the notch.
*   **Notch Crest:** The trapezoidal opening over which water flows.
*   **Side Walls (Breast Walls):** Contain the water flow upstream of the notch.
*   **Downstream Floor (Apron):** A solid, impermeable floor to prevent scour downstream.
*   **Curtain Walls (Flooring Blocks):** Embedded within the downstream floor to intercept seepage and resist uplift.
*   **Energy Dissipating Devices:** Such as impact blocks or uplift blocks to reduce the destructive force of the falling water jet.
*   **Downstream Cut-off Wall:** Prevents under-seepage.
*   **Downstream Protection:** Pitching or riprap to protect the bed and banks from scour.

#### **3.2 Determining Key Parameters:**

**3.2.1 Discharge and Head over Notch:**

The discharge ($Q$) over a weir is generally given by:

$Q = C_d \times L \times h^{3/2}$

Where:
*   $Q$ = Discharge
*   $C_d$ = Coefficient of discharge
*   $L$ = Length of the weir crest
*   $h$ = Head of water over the crest

For a trapezoidal notch, the effective length ($L$) is determined by the width of the notch at the water surface.

**3.2.2 Coefficient of Discharge ($C_d$):**

The $C_d$ for a trapezoidal notch is typically taken as 1.7 times the coefficient of discharge for a rectangular weir, or more accurately, it depends on the shape and the head. Common values can range from **1.5 to 1.7** for a sharp-crested trapezoidal notch. However, for broad-crested notches, it can be lower.

**3.2.3 Determining Crest Length (L):**

Given the design discharge ($Q$) and an estimated head over the crest ($h$), the crest length ($L$) can be calculated:

$L = \frac{Q}{C_d \times h^{3/2}}$

**3.2.4 Determining Notch Dimensions:**

A typical trapezoidal notch has a bottom width ($b$) and a batter ($x$) for the sides (ratio of horizontal to vertical projection). The width of the notch at the water surface ($L$) is then:

$L = b + 2xh$

This equation is transcendental, meaning $L$ and $h$ depend on each other. Design often involves an iterative process or making reasonable assumptions.

**3.2.5 Design of Downstream Floor (Apron):**

The length of the downstream apron is determined using **Khosla's theory** or other empirical methods to ensure hydraulic stability against uplift and exit gradient.

*   **Exit Gradient:** The hydraulic gradient at the downstream end of the apron should be less than the critical gradient for the soil to prevent uplift and scour.
*   **Length of Apron:** Typically designed as a multiple of the head over the crest ($h$) and the downstream cut-off depth.

**3.2.6 Energy Dissipation:**

The kinetic energy of the falling water needs to be dissipated. This is often achieved by:

*   **Hydraulic Jump:** By designing the downstream floor at a specific level to induce a hydraulic jump. The sequent depth of the jump must be safely contained within the apron.
*   **Impact Blocks/Uplift Blocks:** These blocks are placed on the downstream apron to break the water jet and dissipate energy through turbulence. The size, spacing, and arrangement of these blocks are critical.

**3.2.7 Uplift Pressure Counteraction:**

*   **Curtain Walls:** Vertical impermeable walls are embedded at the upstream and downstream ends of the apron to reduce seepage and uplift pressure.
*   **Length of Impervious Floor:** Based on the hydraulic gradient, it should be long enough to ensure the exit gradient is safe.

### **4. Understanding the Basic Drawing Requirements for a Trapezoidal Notch Fall**

#### **4.1 Purpose of Drawings:**

Drawings are essential for visualizing the structure, communicating design details to construction teams, and ensuring correct execution.

#### **4.2 Key Elements to be Shown in Drawings:**

*   **Plan View:**
    *   Layout of the canal approaching and leaving the fall.
    *   Position and dimensions of the trapezoidal notch.
    *   Shape and extent of the downstream apron.
    *   Location of upstream and downstream cut-off walls.
    *   Location of any energy dissipating devices (baffle blocks, uplift blocks).
    *   Details of downstream pitching/riprap.
    *   Bank protection details.
*   **Longitudinal Section:**
    *   Upstream and downstream bed levels.
    *   Water levels upstream and downstream.
    *   Crest level of the notch.
    *   Slopes of the upstream and downstream glacis (if any).
    *   Depth and extent of cut-off walls.
    *   Thickness and levels of the downstream apron.
    *   Dimensions of energy dissipating blocks.
*   **Cross-Sections:**
    *   Cross-section of the canal upstream and downstream.
    *   Cross-section of the fall showing the notch profile.
    *   Cross-section of the downstream apron with cut-off walls and blocks.
    *   Canal lining details.
*   **Details of Special Features:**
    *   Detailed drawing of the trapezoidal notch crest.
    *   Reinforcement details if the structure is in concrete.
    *   Dimensions and placement of energy dissipating blocks.
    *   Details of expansion joints.

#### **4.3 Conventions and Standards:**

*   Use standard engineering drawing symbols and line types.
*   Clearly label all dimensions, levels, and materials.
*   Include a title block with project name, drawing title, scale, date, and designer's name.
*   A suitable scale should be chosen to clearly represent all features.

### **5. Practice Questions or Exercises with Answers**

#### **Question 1:**

A trapezoidal notch fall is to be designed for a canal carrying a discharge of $0.8 m^3/s$. The upstream water level is $1.50$ m above the canal bed. The crest of the trapezoidal notch is to be set $1.0$ m below the upstream water level. Assume the coefficient of discharge ($C_d$) for the trapezoidal notch is $1.7$.

**Calculate:**
a) The head over the crest ($h$).
b) The length of the crest ($L$).
c) If the notch has a bottom width ($b$) of $0.8$ m and a batter of $1$ horizontal : $1$ vertical, calculate the width of the notch at the water surface.

**Solution:**

**Given:**
*   $Q = 0.8 m^3/s$
*   Upstream water level above bed = $1.50$ m
*   Crest level is $1.0$ m below upstream water level.
*   $C_d = 1.7$

**a) Head over the crest ($h$):**
The head over the crest is the difference between the upstream water level and the crest level. Since the crest is $1.0$ m below the upstream water level, the head over the crest is $1.0$ m.
$h = 1.0$ m

**b) Length of the crest ($L$):**
Using the weir formula: $Q = C_d \times L \times h^{3/2}$
$0.8 = 1.7 \times L \times (1.0)^{3/2}$
$0.8 = 1.7 \times L \times 1.0$
$L = \frac{0.8}{1.7} \approx 0.47$ m

**c) Width of the notch at the water surface:**
For a trapezoidal notch with bottom width ($b$) and batter ($x$ horizontal : $1$ vertical):
Width at water surface ($L$) = $b + 2xh$
Given $b = 0.8$ m and batter $x = 1$ (1 horizontal : 1 vertical).
$L = 0.8 + 2 \times 1 \times 1.0$
$L = 0.8 + 2.0 = 2.8$ m

**Note:** There's a discrepancy here. The calculated crest length ($L \approx 0.47$ m) from the discharge equation is significantly different from the width calculated using the trapezoidal geometry ($L = 2.8$ m). This indicates that the assumed head of $1.0$ m might not be correct if the trapezoidal geometry is fixed. In a real design, one would either:
1.  Fix the trapezoidal geometry and iterate to find the correct head $h$ that satisfies the discharge.
2.  Or, if the head is fixed, adjust the trapezoidal geometry (bottom width and batter) to achieve the required crest length.

For this exercise, we've answered each part independently based on the given information. However, it highlights the need for consistency in design parameters.

#### **Question 2:**

A trapezoidal notch fall is to be designed for a canal with a crest width of $3.0$ m. The maximum head over the crest is observed to be $0.5$ m. If the coefficient of discharge for this notch is $1.65$, what is the discharge that can be passed? If the canal bed drops by $2.0$ m, and the upstream water level is $0.7$ m above the crest, design the length of the downstream impervious apron using a suitable factor of safety for the exit gradient (assume safe exit gradient is $1/5$ for downstream cutoff and $1/3$ for downstream end of apron).

**Solution:**

**Given:**
*   Crest width ($L$) = $3.0$ m
*   Maximum head over the crest ($h$) = $0.5$ m
*   Coefficient of discharge ($C_d$) = $1.65$
*   Drop in canal bed = $2.0$ m
*   Upstream water level above crest = $0.7$ m (This is the head over the crest, consistent with $h=0.5$m if upstream water level was $0.5$m above crest. Let's use $h=0.5$m for discharge calculation as it's the maximum observed head.)

**Discharge Calculation:**
$Q = C_d \times L \times h^{3/2}$
$Q = 1.65 \times 3.0 \times (0.5)^{3/2}$
$Q = 1.65 \times 3.0 \times 0.35355$
$Q \approx 1.75$ $m^3/s$

**Downstream Apron Design:**

The length of the impervious apron is determined to ensure hydraulic stability. This involves considering the uplift pressure and the exit gradient.

Let $L_{apron}$ be the length of the impervious apron downstream of the upstream cutoff.
Let $d$ be the depth of the upstream cutoff wall.
Let $D$ be the depth of the downstream cutoff wall.

According to **Khosla's theory**, the head loss due to seepage under the structure is proportional to the length of the path of seepage. The exit gradient is the gradient of the hydraulic surface at the point where water emerges from the downstream end of the apron.

For a simple weir on a permeable foundation with a downstream apron of length $L_{apron}$ and no downstream cutoff, the exit gradient is typically considered to be the gradient at the end of the apron. The length of the apron is often expressed in terms of the head ($h$).

**Simplified Approach for Apron Length (Empirical):**

A common guideline for the length of the downstream apron ($L_{apron}$) based on the head ($h$) and downstream cutoff ($D$) is often given by empirical formulas or based on accepted design practices.

For a given head ($h$), the total head available for seepage is $h$.
The exit gradient ($G_{exit}$) is the head loss divided by the length of the seepage path at emergence.

A simplified rule of thumb for the length of the downstream apron from the toe of the structure (which is often at the downstream end of the crest) or from the downstream cutoff is often related to the head over the crest.

*   **Upstream Cutoff:** Assume a cutoff at the upstream end of the crest.
*   **Downstream Apron:** Let its length be $L_{apron}$.
*   **Downstream Cutoff:** Assume a cutoff at the end of the apron, with depth $D$.

The uplift pressure at the downstream end of the apron depends on the length of the apron and the depth of the downstream cutoff. The exit gradient is critical.

Let's consider a common design practice for the length of the apron ($L_{apron}$) based on the head ($h$) and the required safety factor for the exit gradient.

**Design using Head (h):**
The length of the apron is typically designed to be a multiple of the head ($h$).
For a simple weir, the length of the downstream apron is often taken as $2h$ to $4h$ from the toe, or more conservatively, considering the head and the material properties.

A more formal approach using Khosla's theory involves calculating the hydraulic gradient along the floor. The length of the apron ($L_{apron}$) is chosen such that the exit gradient is less than the critical gradient.

Let's use an empirical approach based on common guidelines for safe exit gradients.
If the upstream cutoff is at the start of the fall and the downstream apron extends $L_{apron}$ from the toe of the fall.

**If we consider the total head drop across the structure is roughly equal to the head over the crest $h=0.5$ m:**

*   **Safe Exit Gradient ($G_{safe}$):**
    *   For downstream end of apron with no downstream cutoff (worst case): $G_{safe} \approx 1/5$ to $1/6$.
    *   For downstream end of apron with downstream cutoff of depth $D$: $G_{safe}$ can be higher, say $1/3$ to $1/4$.

**Let's assume we want to design the apron to safely dissipate the energy, and the head driving seepage is $h=0.5$m.**

A common empirical relation for the length of the downstream apron ($L_{apron}$) from the upstream edge of the downstream floor to the downstream edge of the downstream floor (i.e., length of the floor) based on head ($h$) is often taken as:

$L_{apron} \approx (3 \text{ to } 4) \times h$

Let's use a factor of 4 for conservative design.
$L_{apron} = 4 \times h = 4 \times 0.5$ m = $2.0$ m.

**Alternatively, if we consider the total drop:**
The total head driving seepage could be considered as the difference in water levels upstream and downstream. If the downstream water level is significantly lower, the head could be larger. However, for a fall, the head over the crest is the primary driver for the falling jet's energy.

**Let's consider a design approach based on the head over the crest ($h$) and safe exit gradient:**

Assume the fall structure itself provides some resistance, and the head over the crest ($h=0.5$ m) is the relevant head for determining the length of the downstream apron from the toe of the fall.

Length of apron ($L_{apron}$) = $k \times h$
Where $k$ is a factor that depends on the soil and the structure. For medium sands, $k$ can range from $3$ to $5$.

Let's use $k=5$ for a safer design.
$L_{apron} = 5 \times 0.5$ m = $2.5$ m.

**Incorporating the provided safe exit gradients:**
If we assume the downstream end of the apron is the point where the water emerges, and the head that has dropped to this point is $h=0.5$m. The length of the apron, $L_{apron}$, would be the seepage path length at the exit.

Exit Gradient = $\frac{h}{L_{apron}}$

We want $\frac{h}{L_{apron}} < G_{safe}$

If $G_{safe} = 1/5$ (for no downstream cutoff), then:
$\frac{0.5}{L_{apron}} < \frac{1}{5}$
$L_{apron} > 5 \times 0.5 = 2.5$ m

If we assume a downstream cutoff exists at the end of the apron, and the safe exit gradient is $1/3$:
$\frac{0.5}{L_{apron}} < \frac{1}{3}$
$L_{apron} > 3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$ m

However, the downstream apron length is measured from the toe of the structure. The structure itself provides some resistance.

A more practical approach for the length of the downstream apron would be to consider it as a multiple of the head ($h$). Let's use a standard factor based on common practices for energy dissipation and scour prevention. A common length for the apron is often $4h$ to $6h$ from the toe of the fall.

Let's consider the length of the apron from the toe of the fall to be $4h$.
$L_{apron} = 4 \times h = 4 \times 0.5$ m = $2.0$ m.

**Considering the provided safe exit gradients for the downstream end of the apron:**

*   If there is NO downstream cutoff at the end of the apron, the length of the apron from the toe should be such that the exit gradient is safe, say $1/5$.
    Length of apron ($L_{apron}$) $\ge \frac{\text{Head}}{\text{Safe Exit Gradient}} = \frac{0.5}{1/5} = 2.5$ m.
*   If there IS a downstream cutoff at the end of the apron, the safe exit gradient can be higher, say $1/3$.
    Length of apron ($L_{apron}$) $\ge \frac{\text{Head}}{\text{Safe Exit Gradient}} = \frac{0.5}{1/3} = 1.5$ m.

In practice, the design also considers energy dissipating blocks, which can influence the effective head acting on the apron. However, without specific details on energy dissipators, we'll use the simpler approach.

A common design practice for the length of the impervious apron ($L_{apron}$) for a weir is to ensure the exit gradient is safe. If we assume the downstream cutoff is at the end of the apron, and we want a safe exit gradient of $1/3$:

$L_{apron} = \frac{h_{total}}{G_{safe}}$

The total head causing seepage might not just be $h$, but it's the difference between upstream and downstream water levels. However, for a fall, the head over the crest is critical for the jet's energy.

A practical design approach for the length of the downstream apron is often empirically determined. A reasonable length for the apron would be about $4$ to $6$ times the head over the crest. Let's choose $4h$ as a minimum.

$L_{apron} = 4 \times 0.5$ m = $2.0$ m.

This length should be sufficient to prevent scour. If we were to consider the safe exit gradient criterion more rigorously, we would also need to account for the upstream cutoff and the downstream cutoff depth.

**Final Answer for Apron Length:** Based on empirical guidelines of $4h$ to $6h$, let's design the apron length to be $2.0$ m. This is consistent with providing a safe exit gradient if a downstream cutoff is assumed.

#### **Question 3:**

What are the primary reasons for constructing canal falls?

**Answer:**
The primary reasons for constructing canal falls are:
1.  **To accommodate changes in topography:** When the natural ground slope is steeper than the permissible canal slope.
2.  **To maintain a desired canal slope:** When the alignment requires a steeper slope than the ground to deliver water efficiently.
3.  **To overcome obstacles:** To safely pass water over natural depressions or existing structures.
4.  **To prevent excessive velocity:** Without a fall, the increased slope would lead to higher velocities, causing scouring and damage to the canal.

### **6. Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Purpose of Falls:** To reduce water level and energy safely.
*   **Trapezoidal Notch Fall:** Water flows over a trapezoidal opening, acting as a weir.
*   **Key Design Parameter:** Discharge ($Q$), head over crest ($h$), crest length ($L$), and coefficient of discharge ($C_d$).
*   **Weir Formula:** $Q = C_d \times L \times h^{3/2}$
*   **Coefficient of Discharge ($C_d$):** Typically around $1.5$ to $1.7$ for sharp-crested trapezoidal notches.
*   **Downstream Apron:** Crucial for preventing scour. Its length is determined by hydraulic stability criteria (Khosla's theory, exit gradient).
*   **Energy Dissipation:** Essential to prevent damage to the downstream channel. This is often achieved through hydraulic jumps or energy dissipating blocks.
*   **Uplift Pressure:** Must be managed through proper apron design and cut-off walls.
*   **Drawings:** Should clearly show all structural components, dimensions, and levels.
*   **Iterative Design:** The design of trapezoidal notches can be iterative due to the relationship between crest width, head, and length.
