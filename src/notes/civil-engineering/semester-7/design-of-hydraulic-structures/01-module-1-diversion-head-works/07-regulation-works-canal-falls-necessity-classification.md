# DESIGN OF HYDRAULIC STRUCTURES - Module 1: Diversion Head Works

## Topic: Regulation Works - Canal Falls (Necessity, Classification)

---

### **Learning Outcomes Covered:**

*   Understand the necessity of providing canal falls in irrigation systems.
*   Classify different types of canal falls.
*   Identify the factors influencing the selection of a particular type of canal fall.

---

### **1. Introduction to Regulation Works in Canals**

Regulation works are essential components of canal systems designed to control and manage the flow of water, ensuring efficient and safe distribution for irrigation and other purposes. Among these, **canal falls** are specifically designed to safely dissipate excess energy when a canal has to be taken across a natural depression or a sudden drop in ground level.

---

### **2. Necessity of Canal Falls**

Canals are typically designed to follow the natural contours of the land to minimize earthwork and maintain a consistent gradient. However, the terrain is not always uniform. When a canal encounters a natural **drop in ground level**, a vertical or significant longitudinal slope, it necessitates the provision of a canal fall.

**Key Reasons for Necessity:**

*   **Maintaining Desired Canal Bed Slope:** Irrigation canals are designed with a specific bed slope (longitudinal slope) to ensure sufficient velocity for self-cleansing and to carry the required discharge. If the natural ground slope is steeper than the permissible canal bed slope, a fall is needed to reduce the overall gradient.
*   **Preventing Excessive Velocity:** If the canal is constructed along a steep natural slope without a fall, the water velocity will increase beyond the permissible limit. This can lead to:
    *   **Scouring of the Canal Bed and Sides:** High velocities can erode the canal lining and bed material, leading to structural damage and increased maintenance costs.
    *   **Damage to Downstream Structures:** Uncontrolled high-velocity flow can damage downstream control structures like regulators, cross-drainage works, and outlets.
    *   **Cavitation:** In extreme cases, high velocities can lead to cavitation, causing severe damage to concrete structures.
*   **Controlling Water Surface Elevation:** Falls help manage the water surface profile within the canal, ensuring water reaches the command area at the desired elevation.
*   **Accommodating Natural Topography:** The primary reason is to bridge natural depressions or sudden drops in the ground profile, allowing the canal to continue its path.

**Example:** Imagine a canal originating from a river diversion headwork. The initial reach might follow a gentle slope, but as it progresses into hilly terrain, the ground level might suddenly drop significantly to cross a ravine. Without a fall, the water would accelerate rapidly, causing erosion. A canal fall is therefore essential to manage this drop.

### **3. Classification of Canal Falls**

Canal falls are primarily classified based on the **shape of the transition** used to change the slope and the **method of energy dissipation**. The most common classifications are:

#### **3.1. Based on the Shape of the Transition:**

This classification focuses on how the water flows from the steeper slope into the steeper chute.

*   **Glacis Falls:**
    *   **Description:** These are the most common type of falls. They consist of a **falling glacis**, which is an inclined surface over which the water flows from the upstream crest to the downstream cistern or energy dissipator. The falling glacis is typically designed with a gentler slope than the steep drop.
    *   **Sub-types:**
        *   **Straight Glacis Falls:** The glacis is a single straight inclined plane. This is simple but may not be the most efficient in energy dissipation.
        *   **Ogee Glacis Falls:** The glacis is shaped like an "S" curve, transitioning from a relatively flat upstream slope to a steeper downstream slope. This shape helps in smooth flow transition and better energy dissipation.
        *   **Sunk Glacis Falls:** The glacis is constructed below the upstream and downstream water levels. This submerged condition enhances energy dissipation and reduces the risk of air entrainment.
    *   **Advantages:** Relatively simple to construct, efficient energy dissipation when designed correctly.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be prone to erosion if not adequately protected, potential for downstream scour.

*   **Notch Falls:**
    *   **Description:** In these falls, the water passes over a **notch** or a **weir-like crest**. The flow is essentially over a sharp edge.
    *   **Advantages:** Simple design, effective for smaller drops.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can cause significant drawdown and aeration, leading to potential cavitation and scour problems for larger falls. Less efficient in energy dissipation compared to glacis falls.

*   **Rapids:**
    *   **Description:** This type of fall involves creating a **long, gently sloping, paved channel** (often with a slope of 1 in 10 or gentler). The increased length and gentle slope spread out the energy dissipation over a larger area, simulating a natural rapid.
    *   **Advantages:** Very effective in dissipating energy gently, minimal risk of scour, can accommodate significant drops.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Requires a considerable length of channel, which might not be feasible in all locations.

#### **3.2. Based on the Method of Energy Dissipation:**

This classification focuses on how the kinetic energy of the falling water is dissipated.

*   **Impact Type Falls:**
    *   **Description:** Energy is dissipated by the impact of the falling water on the downstream protection works (e.g., a solid impact block or a cistern).
    *   **Example:** A **cistern type fall** where water falls into a concrete basin (cistern) and is then guided downstream. The impact and turbulence within the cistern help dissipate energy.

*   **Hydraulic Jump Type Falls:**
    *   **Description:** Energy is dissipated by creating a **hydraulic jump** downstream of the fall. A hydraulic jump is a phenomenon where supercritical flow transitions to subcritical flow, involving a sudden rise in water level and significant energy dissipation through turbulence.
    *   **Example:** A **flumed glacis fall** or a **drop inlet fall** designed to transition the flow to a supercritical state, allowing a hydraulic jump to form in a protected downstream cistern or energy dissipator.

*   **Combined Type Falls:**
    *   **Description:** These falls utilize a combination of impact and hydraulic jump principles for energy dissipation.

**Common Types of Canal Falls (Combining both classifications):**

*   **Sarda Type Fall:**
    *   **Description:** A popular type of glacis fall named after the Sarda Canal. It features a **straight glacis** followed by a **cistern** with a solid concrete floor and a downstream glacis. The impact on the floor and the subsequent flow in the cistern help dissipate energy.
    *   **Key Features:** Straight glacis, solid floor cistern, downstream protection.
    *   **Suitability:** For moderate to large drops.

*   **Baffle Wall Type Fall:**
    *   **Description:** A variation of the glacis fall where **baffle walls** (low concrete walls) are built across the cistern. These walls create additional turbulence and eddies, enhancing energy dissipation.
    *   **Key Features:** Glacis, cistern with baffle walls.
    *   **Suitability:** For efficient energy dissipation with moderate to large drops.

*   **Trapezoidal Notch Fall:**
    *   **Description:** Water flows over a trapezoidal notch. The energy dissipation is primarily due to the turbulence created as the water falls through the notch.
    *   **Key Features:** Trapezoidal notch, often with upstream protection.
    *   **Suitability:** Generally for smaller drops, though modified designs exist.

*   **Hydraulic Jump Type Fall (often referred to as a "drop structure" with a dissipator):**
    *   **Description:** Designed to induce a hydraulic jump in a downstream energy dissipator. The upstream section might be a vertical drop or a steep chute.
    *   **Key Features:** Designed for supercritical flow, formation of hydraulic jump in a dissipator.
    *   **Suitability:** Can be very effective for large drops, requires careful hydraulic design.

### **4. Factors Influencing the Selection of a Canal Fall**

The choice of a particular type of canal fall depends on several engineering and site-specific factors:

*   **Magnitude of the Drop (Head):**
    *   Small drops: Notch falls or simple glacis falls.
    *   Moderate to large drops: Sarda type falls, baffle wall falls, or hydraulic jump type falls.

*   **Discharge:**
    *   Larger discharges require more robust energy dissipation measures to prevent scour.

*   **Geological Conditions and Soil Type:**
    *   If the downstream bed material is erodible, a more effective energy dissipator (like a hydraulic jump with a stilling basin) is needed.
    *   Stable soil conditions might allow for simpler impact-type dissipators.

*   **Available Space:**
    *   Rapids require significant longitudinal space.
    *   Other falls are more compact.

*   **Cost of Construction and Maintenance:**
    *   Simpler designs generally have lower initial costs but may require more maintenance.
    *   Complex energy dissipators might have higher initial costs but offer better long-term performance.

*   **Hydraulic Efficiency:**
    *   The fall must dissipate enough energy to ensure the downstream flow is subcritical and manageable.

*   **Downstream Land Use and Structures:**
    *   If sensitive downstream structures or valuable land are present, maximum protection against scour is paramount.

*   **Upstream and Downstream Water Levels:**
    *   These affect the available head and the design of the energy dissipator.

### **5. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:** Explain the primary necessity of providing canal falls in irrigation canals.

**Answer:** Canal falls are necessary to safely dissipate the excess kinetic energy of water when a canal has to be taken across a sudden drop in ground level. This prevents excessive velocities that can lead to scouring of the canal bed and sides, damage to downstream structures, and cavitation. They also help maintain the desired longitudinal slope of the canal for efficient flow.

**Question 2:** List and briefly describe three common types of canal falls based on the shape of the transition.

**Answer:**
1.  **Glacis Falls:** Feature a falling glacis (inclined plane) over which water flows. Sub-types include straight, ogee, and sunk glacis falls.
2.  **Notch Falls:** Water passes over a notch or a weir-like crest, usually a sharp edge.
3.  **Rapids:** Involve a long, gently sloping, paved channel that spreads energy dissipation over a larger area.

**Question 3:** What is the main principle behind a hydraulic jump type fall?

**Answer:** The main principle is to design the fall such that the water transitions from supercritical flow to subcritical flow in a downstream energy dissipator, creating a hydraulic jump. This phenomenon dissipates a significant amount of energy through intense turbulence.

**Question 4:** Discuss any two factors that influence the selection of a canal fall type.

**Answer:**
1.  **Magnitude of the Drop (Head):** Larger drops require more sophisticated energy dissipation mechanisms like hydraulic jump type falls or baffled cisterns, while smaller drops can be managed with simpler notch or straight glacis falls.
2.  **Geological Conditions:** The erodibility of the downstream soil dictates the type of energy dissipator. Loose, erodible soils necessitate robust dissipators (like stilling basins with baffles or impact blocks) to prevent scour, whereas stable ground might permit simpler designs.

**Question 5 (Practical Application):** You are designing a canal fall for a discharge of 20 cumecs where the ground level drops by 3 meters. The downstream soil is silty and prone to erosion. Which type of fall would you primarily consider and why?

**Answer:** Given the significant drop (3 meters) and erodible soil, a **Sarda Type Fall** or a **Hydraulic Jump Type Fall** with a well-designed stilling basin would be most appropriate.
*   **Sarda Type Fall:** The straight glacis helps transition the flow, and the cistern with a solid floor provides impact and turbulence for energy dissipation. Downstream protection is crucial.
*   **Hydraulic Jump Type Fall:** This is often preferred for larger drops as it can dissipate energy very efficiently. Designing a chute to ensure supercritical flow followed by a properly sized stilling basin with downstream blocks will induce a hydraulic jump, effectively dissipating the energy and preventing scour.

---

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   Canal falls are crucial for **energy dissipation** and preventing **scour** in irrigation canals.
*   They are necessitated by **drops in ground level** that exceed the permissible canal bed slope.
*   **Glacis falls** (Sarda type, baffle wall type) and **hydraulic jump type falls** are common and effective for larger drops.
*   The selection process involves balancing **hydraulic requirements**, **site conditions**, and **economic considerations**.
*   Proper **downstream protection** (stillilng basins, concrete aprons, downstream glacis) is critical for all types of falls.
*   Understanding the transition from **supercritical to subcritical flow** is key to designing effective hydraulic jump dissipators.
