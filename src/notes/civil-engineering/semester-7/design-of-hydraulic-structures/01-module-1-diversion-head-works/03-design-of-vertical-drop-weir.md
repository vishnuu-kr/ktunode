# DESIGN OF HYDRAULIC STRUCTURES - Module 1: Diversion Head Works

## Topic: Design of Vertical Drop Weir

This module focuses on the fundamental principles and design considerations for diversion headworks, which are structures built across a river to divert a portion of its water for various uses like irrigation, water supply, or power generation. We will delve into the design of a specific type of barrage structure: the vertical drop weir.

### 1. Introduction to Diversion Headworks

#### 1.1. Purpose of Diversion Headworks

Diversion headworks are structures built across a river to:

*   **Raise the water level:** This allows water to flow by gravity into the canal without the need for pumping.
*   **Divert water:** A controlled portion of the river flow is diverted into the off-taking channels (canals).
*   **Control flow:** Regulate the amount of water diverted and manage flood discharge.
*   **Store a limited amount of water:** Barrages can provide a small head of storage, but are primarily for diversion.

#### 1.2. Types of Diversion Headworks

*   **Weirs:** Generally solid structures that allow some water to flow over their crest.
*   **Barrages:** Structures with gates that can be raised or lowered to control the water level and discharge.

#### 1.3. Components of a Diversion Headwork

A typical diversion headwork, including a vertical drop weir, comprises the following key components:

*   **Weir/Barrage Proper:** The main obstruction across the river.
    *   **Crest:** The top surface of the weir over which water flows.
    *   **Abutments:** Side walls connecting the weir to the river banks.
    *   **Piers:** Vertical supports dividing the weir into spans.
*   **Under Sluices:** Gates located at the river bed level to scour out silt and sediment accumulation upstream of the weir.
*   **Crest Gates/Sluices:** Gates on the weir crest to control the discharge passing over the weir or through the gates.
*   **Canal Regulator/Head Regulator:** Structure at the head of the off-taking canal to control the flow into the canal.
*   **Afflux Bund/Guide Banks:** Earthen embankments to guide the flow and prevent erosion of the river banks near the structure.
*   **Cut-off Walls (Upstream and Downstream):** Impervious barriers to reduce seepage under the structure.
*   **Filter Media (Downstream):** Layers of granular material to relieve uplift pressure and dissipate energy.

### 2. Vertical Drop Weir: Concept and Function

#### 2.1. Definition

A vertical drop weir is a type of barrage where the downstream face of the weir is nearly vertical. It is designed to raise the water level upstream to facilitate diversion into canals. The excess flood water is typically passed over the crest of the weir.

#### 2.2. Functionality

*   **Water Level Raising:** By obstructing the river flow, it creates a pondage area upstream, raising the water surface.
*   **Diversion:** This raised water level allows water to enter the head regulator of the off-taking canal(s) by gravity.
*   **Flood Discharge:** The weir structure is designed to pass the design flood discharge safely, either by flowing over the crest or through regulated gates.
*   **Silt Control:** Under sluices are crucial for flushing accumulated silt from the upstream reach.

---

### 3. Hydraulic Design Principles of a Vertical Drop Weir

The hydraulic design focuses on ensuring the structure can efficiently pass the design discharge while maintaining stable upstream and downstream water levels and minimizing seepage issues.

#### 3.1. Design Discharge

*   **Maximum Flood Discharge:** The weir must be designed to pass the maximum anticipated flood discharge safely. This is typically determined by hydrological analysis.
*   **Off-taking Canal Discharge:** The discharge required for the off-taking canal(s).

#### 3.2. Pond Level (Full Supply Level - FSL Upstream)

*   **Definition:** The desired water level upstream of the weir to facilitate diversion into the canal.
*   **Determination:** Based on the bed level of the off-taking canal and the required head for flow.

#### 3.3. Crest Level (Crest of Weir)

*   **Definition:** The top elevation of the weir structure.
*   **Relationship with Pond Level:** The crest level is typically fixed below the pond level to allow for some freeboard or is set at the pond level if the weir is designed to act as a spillway.
*   **Freeboard:** The vertical distance between the normal full supply level and the top of the upstream guide banks or the weir crest to prevent overtopping during normal operation.

#### 3.4. Sluice Gate Design (Under Sluices and Crest Gates)

*   **Purpose:**
    *   **Under Sluices:** To scour silt from the upstream forebay and to pass low flood discharges and initial flows.
    *   **Crest Gates:** To control the water level upstream and to pass flood discharges when the river flow exceeds the capacity of the weir crest.
*   **Sizing:** The number and size of gates are determined based on the required discharge capacity, silt flushing requirements, and structural considerations.

#### 3.5. Spillway Capacity

*   **Definition:** The ability of the weir to pass water over its crest.
*   **Design:** The length of the weir is determined to pass a certain proportion of the flood discharge based on the crest level and the upstream water level.

#### 3.6. Energy Dissipation Downstream

*   **Problem:** Water flowing over the weir crest possesses significant kinetic energy. If this energy is not dissipated, it can cause scour downstream of the structure.
*   **Solutions:**
    *   **Hydraulic Jump:** Designing the downstream floor (apron) length and downstream water depth to induce a hydraulic jump, which dissipates energy.
    *   **Energy Dissipators:** Using structures like **baffle walls**, **buckets**, or **stilling basins** downstream of the weir apron to further reduce the velocity and energy of the tailwater.

### 4. Design Steps for a Vertical Drop Weir

The design process involves several iterative steps, considering hydraulic, structural, and seepage aspects.

#### 4.1. Data Collection

*   River discharge data (low, average, and flood discharges).
*   Hydrological analysis for design flood.
*   River bed material characteristics.
*   Upstream and downstream water levels.
*   Canal discharge and bed levels.
*   Topography of the site.

#### 4.2. Layout and Component Sizing

*   **Weir Length:** Determined by the required discharge capacity and hydraulic considerations (e.g., limiting afflux, velocity).
    *   **Formula (simplistic for initial estimation):** $Q_{weir} = L_{weir} \times d \times \sqrt{2g \times H}$ (where $d$ is the coefficient of discharge, $H$ is the head over the weir crest). More complex formulations for flow over a broad-crested weir are used in practice.
*   **Piers and Spans:** Dividing the weir into suitable spans for structural stability and ease of gate operation.
*   **Under Sluice Span:** Typically a significant portion of the weir length to effectively remove silt.
*   **Head Regulator Sizing:** Based on the canal's discharge and designed to maintain the required flow without excessive afflux.

#### 4.3. Crest Level Determination

*   Set based on the required pond level and the need to pass flood discharges.

#### 4.4. Hydraulic Jump Design (Khosla's Theory/Lane's Trapezoidal Profile)

*   **Purpose:** To ensure that the hydraulic jump occurs within the downstream apron, dissipating energy and preventing scour.
*   **Key Concepts:**
    *   **Upstream Froude Number ($F_1$):** Calculated based on the velocity and depth of flow approaching the jump.
    *   **Conjugate Depths ($y_1$ and $y_2$):** The depths of flow before and after the hydraulic jump, related by the momentum equation.
    *   **Length of Jump ($L_j$):** The distance over which the jump occurs.
*   **Design Criteria:**
    *   The jump should occur within the apron.
    *   The apron length is typically designed as $L_j$ or $6 y_2$, whichever is greater, to ensure jump formation.
    *   Downstream cutoff depth is critical to prevent uplift pressure from undermining the apron.

#### 4.5. Seepage Analysis (Bligh's Creep Theory and Khosla's Theory)

*   **Problem:** Seepage of water under the structure can cause uplift pressure, reducing the effective weight of the structure and potentially leading to failure. It can also cause exit gradient issues.
*   **Bligh's Creep Theory:**
    *   **Principle:** The length of seepage is the sum of the horizontal and vertical lengths the water travels under the structure.
    *   **Creeping Length ($L_{creep}$):** $L_{creep} = \text{length of floor} + 2 \times (\text{sum of depths of vertical cut-offs})$.
    *   **Exit Gradient ($G_{exit}$):** The gradient of seepage at the downstream end of the structure.
    *   **Allowed Creeping Length ($C_{allowed}$):** Depends on the nature of the soil.
    *   **Condition:** $L_{creep} > C_{allowed} \times G_{exit}$.
*   **Khosla's Theory:**
    *   **Principle:** Based on the theory of potential flow and conformal mapping. It considers the flow as a two-dimensional seepage problem and calculates uplift pressures and exit gradients at critical points.
    *   **Interference of Cut-offs:** Accounts for the reduction in seepage due to the presence of upstream and downstream cut-off walls.
    *   **Correction Factors:** Used to adjust the seepage characteristics based on the relative depths of the cut-offs and their spacing.
    *   **Exit Gradient:** A critical parameter. For cohesive soils, it should not exceed $1/5$ to $1/7$. For sandy soils, it should be lower, around $1/10$ to $1/12$.

#### 4.6. Uplift Pressure Calculation

*   **Uplift Pressure:** The upward hydrostatic pressure exerted by the seeping water on the underside of the weir floor.
*   **Calculation:** Based on Khosla's theory, the uplift pressure at various points along the floor is determined, and the pressure distribution is established.

#### 4.7. Stability Analysis (Structural Design)

*   **Purpose:** To ensure the structural integrity of the weir under various loading conditions (water pressure, self-weight, uplift pressure).
*   **Checks:**
    *   **Sliding:** The structure should not slide horizontally. The frictional resistance must be greater than the horizontal forces.
    *   **Overturning:** The structure should not overturn about its toe. The restoring moments must be greater than the overturning moments.
    *   **Crushing:** The maximum pressure at the base should not exceed the allowable bearing capacity of the soil.
    *   **Tension:** No tensile stresses should develop at the base.

#### 4.8. Freeboard Calculation

*   **Purpose:** To provide a safety margin against wave action and fluctuations in water level.
*   **Factors:** Wind velocity, fetch, and the height of the structure.

### 5. Key Design Considerations and Important Points

*   **Silt Ejection:** Effective design of under sluices is crucial for preventing silt deposition upstream.
*   **Hydraulic Jump Placement:** Ensure the jump occurs on the downstream apron, not on the natural bed, to prevent downstream scour.
*   **Uplift Pressure Mitigation:** Adequate length and depth of cut-off walls, along with filter media, are essential to reduce uplift and control exit gradient.
*   **Structural Stability:** The foundation design and the overall section of the weir must be stable against sliding, overturning, and crushing.
*   **Energy Dissipation:** Proper design of the downstream apron and energy dissipators is critical for preventing scour.
*   **Gate Operation:** Consideration for the design and operation of gates (under sluices, crest gates) for flow control and maintenance.
*   **Afflux:** The rise in water level upstream of the weir due to obstruction should be within acceptable limits to avoid inundation of valuable land.

### 6. Example Problem

**Problem:** Design a vertical drop weir for a river with a maximum flood discharge of 2000 m³/s. The off-taking canal requires a discharge of 50 m³/s at a bed level of 100.00 m. The river bed is sandy. The average upstream water level is 103.00 m and the downstream water level is 100.00 m. Assume a coefficient of discharge $C_d$ of 2.0 for flow over the weir crest. The permissible exit gradient is $1/7$.

**Solution Outline:**

1.  **Determine Pond Level:** Let's assume the pond level is to be maintained at 103.00 m to ensure flow into the canal.
2.  **Determine Crest Level:** Let the crest level be 102.00 m.
3.  **Calculate Head over Weir:** Head $H = 103.00 m - 102.00 m = 1.00 m$.
4.  **Determine Weir Length:**
    *   Assume a portion of the discharge passes through under sluices and the rest over the weir crest.
    *   Let's assume 1000 m³/s passes over the weir crest.
    *   Using the formula for flow over a broad-crested weir (simplified): $Q = C_d \times L \times H^{3/2}$.
    *   $1000 = 2.0 \times L_{weir} \times (1.00)^{3/2}$
    *   $L_{weir} = 1000 / 2.0 = 500 m$.
5.  **Design Under Sluices:**
    *   Remaining discharge = 2000 m³/s - 1000 m³/s = 1000 m³/s.
    *   These under sluices will be located at a lower level and controlled by gates. Their size and number will be determined based on silt flushing requirements and the permissible velocity at the river bed. Let's assume a total span for under sluices of 100 m.
6.  **Design Downstream Apron and Cut-offs (using Khosla's Theory principles):**
    *   **Calculate flow velocity over weir:** Assume an average depth of flow of 1.0 m over the 500 m weir.
    *   **Determine downstream energy dissipation needs:** Calculate the tailwater depth and the kinetic energy of the flow.
    *   **Design Hydraulic Jump:**
        *   Calculate $y_1$ (depth of flow before jump, based on tailwater depth and weir geometry).
        *   Calculate $y_2$ (conjugate depth) using the formula: $y_2 = \frac{y_1}{2} (\sqrt{1+8F_1^2} - 1)$, where $F_1$ is the upstream Froude number.
        *   Calculate the length of the jump $L_j = 6y_2$.
    *   **Apron Length:** Set apron length $\approx L_j$ or $6y_2$.
    *   **Cut-off Walls:**
        *   Determine the depth of the downstream cut-off ($d_2$) required to keep the exit gradient $\leq 1/7$. This involves using Khosla's theory, considering interference effects of upstream cut-off ($d_1$) and the floor length.
        *   Typically, a downstream cut-off of depth $d_2$ is provided. The upstream cut-off ($d_1$) is usually equal to or greater than $d_2$.
7.  **Uplift Pressure Calculation:** Calculate uplift pressures along the apron based on Khosla's theory.
8.  **Stability Analysis:** Check for sliding, overturning, and crushing at the base, considering self-weight, water pressure, and uplift pressure.
9.  **Freeboard:** Provide adequate freeboard to the guide banks.

**(Note: This is a simplified outline. A detailed design involves iterative calculations and referencing specific design codes and charts based on Khosla's theory.)**

### 8. Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **Purpose of Under Sluices:** Under sluices are designed to pass the low flood discharges and to effectively scour out silt and sediment that accumulates in the upstream pond, preventing it from entering the off-taking canals.
2.  **Hydraulic Jump Importance:** A hydraulic jump is a phenomenon where supercritical flow transitions to subcritical flow, characterized by a sudden rise in water level and significant energy dissipation. In weir design, ensuring the hydraulic jump occurs within the downstream apron is crucial to dissipate the kinetic energy of the water flowing over the weir crest, thereby preventing excessive scour of the downstream riverbed and apron.
3.  **Stability Analysis Checks:** The main checks are:
    *   **Sliding Stability:** Ensuring the structure does not slide horizontally due to unbalanced forces.
    *   **Overturning Stability:** Ensuring the structure does not overturn about its downstream toe.
    *   **Crushing Stability:** Ensuring the maximum pressure at the foundation does not exceed the allowable bearing capacity of the soil.
    *   **Tension Check:** Ensuring no tensile stresses are developed at the foundation.
4.  **Difference between Bligh's and Khosla's Theory:**
    *   **Bligh's Creep Theory:** Considers seepage as flow along a tortuous path under the structure and defines a "creeping length." It uses a simple ratio of creeping length to the head to determine safety against exit gradient. It does not account for the effect of intermediate cut-offs or the curvature of flow lines.
    *   **Khosla's Theory:** Based on the theory of potential flow and conformal mapping, it treats seepage as a two-dimensional flow problem. It accounts for the interference effects of upstream and downstream cut-offs and provides a more accurate estimation of uplift pressures and exit gradients at critical points by using correction factors.
5.  **Permissible Creeping Length Calculation:**
    *   Permissible exit gradient ($G_{exit}$) = $1/7$.
    *   Depth of downstream cutoff ($d_2$) = 3 m.
    *   According to Bligh's theory, the creeping length is related to the exit gradient and the length of flow paths. For simplicity and to provide a safe estimate, we can consider the total path length that the water travels.
    *   However, the question is asking for the *permissible creeping length*. If we assume the exit gradient is critical at the downstream end, and the flow path is generally along the floor and down the cutoff, Bligh's theory states that the total creeping length ($L_{creep}$) must be greater than the product of the permissible creeping length per unit head and the total head.
    *   Bligh's theory typically suggests a maximum permissible creeping length per unit head. For sandy soils, this value is around 9-12.
    *   Let's rephrase the intent: If the downstream cutoff is 3m, and the permissible exit gradient is 1/7, this implies a specific length of impervious floor is required. Bligh's theory implies that the total creeping length must be at least $9 \times \text{Total Head}$ to prevent exit gradient issues.
    *   **Re-interpreting the question:** Perhaps it's asking for the *required* creeping length based on a given head. Let's assume the total head causing seepage is $H$. The permissible creeping length $L_{creep}$ is given by $L_{creep} = C_{allowed} \times H$. The downstream cutoff is provided to increase the creeping length.
    *   **A more direct interpretation:** If the question implies that the downstream cutoff of 3m is designed to meet the $1/7$ exit gradient requirement, we can infer that this depth is sufficient. Bligh's theory would suggest a total creeping length requirement.
    *   **Let's assume the question is asking for the minimum floor length plus the depth of the cutoff, if the total head is considered.** This is not a direct calculation of "permissible creeping length" but rather how the downstream cutoff contributes to the total creeping length.
    *   **If we have a Total Head (H) of, say, 5m, and a downstream cutoff of 3m, and the permissible creeping length per unit head is 9 for sandy soil:**
        *   Required total creeping length = $9 \times 5 = 45$ m.
        *   Length of floor + 2 * depth of upstream cutoff + length of floor + depth of downstream cutoff.
        *   Let's assume a symmetrical design with upstream cutoff $d_1$ and downstream cutoff $d_2$.
        *   Creeping length $L_{creep} = L_{floor} + 2d_1 + d_2$.
        *   If $d_2 = 3m$ is designed for $1/7$ exit gradient, and $L_{floor}$ is the length of the apron, and assuming $d_1 \approx d_2$.
    *   **The question is poorly phrased for a direct calculation of "permissible creeping length" from just the cutoff depth and exit gradient.** It's more about how these parameters relate.
    *   **However, if we reverse the logic of Bligh's Theory:** The permissible creeping length is the *total* path length required to keep the exit gradient safe. For sandy soil, this is typically around 9 times the head. The downstream cutoff contributes to this length.
    *   **Correct approach for the given phrasing:** The question is likely asking how much *additional* creeping length the downstream cutoff of 3m provides, in the context of meeting a 1/7 exit gradient. The downstream cutoff directly contributes its depth to the creeping length. So, if the apron length is $L_p$, the total creeping length would be $L_p + 2 \times d_{up} + d_{down}$. The phrase "permissible creeping length" itself is a property of the soil and head.
    *   **Let's assume the question implies:** Given an exit gradient of 1/7, and a downstream cutoff of 3m, what is the *contribution* of this cutoff to the total creeping length? The contribution is its depth, **3 meters**. This 3 meters, when added to the floor length and upstream cutoff, must collectively satisfy the total required creeping length for safe exit gradient.
