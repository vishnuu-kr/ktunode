## RAILWAY, PORT AND HARBOR ENGINEERING: Module 2: Geometric Design of Track - Gradients

### Topic: Points and Crossings

**Learning Outcome 1:** Understand the necessity of points and crossings in railway track design.

**1. Necessity of Points and Crossings**

Points and crossings are essential components of a railway track that enable trains to move from one track to another. They are fundamental for:

*   **Route Selection:** Allowing trains to choose between different paths, such as diverging onto another line, entering a siding, or proceeding on the main line.
*   **Efficient Operations:** Facilitating the movement of trains to sidings for loading/unloading, shunting, stabling, or bypassing slower trains on the main line.
*   **Safety:** Ensuring controlled and safe passage of trains between interconnected tracks, preventing collisions.
*   **Network Connectivity:** Creating junctions and crossings that link different railway lines, forming a cohesive network.

**Key Concept:** Points and crossings are synonymous with "switches" and "frogs" in railway terminology.

---

**Learning Outcome 2:** Identify and describe the components of a turnout.

**2. Turnout (or Set of Points)**

A turnout is a device incorporated into a track to enable a railway vehicle (train) to be diverted from one track to another.

**2.1 Components of a Turnout:**

A standard turnout consists of the following primary components:

*   **Points (Switch Rails):**
    *   **Leading Switch Rail (Tongue Rail):** A tapered rail, usually 4.877 m to 5.5 m long, that is moved to one side or the other to guide the train. It has a pointed end (nose) that fits snugly against the stock rail.
    *   **Stock Rail (Standing Rail):** The main running rail against which the switch rail is pressed.
    *   **Heel of Switch:** The end of the switch rail where it is attached to the stock rail with a heel block and bolts.
*   **Crossing (or Frog):**
    *   **Crossing Point (or Nose):** The theoretical apex where the two rails of the diverging track cross.
    *   **Heel of Crossing:** The end of the crossing where the two rails of the diverging track are again connected.
*   **Connecting Rails:** Rails that connect the heel of the switch to the toe of the crossing.
*   **Guard Rails:**
    *   **Guard Rail for Main Line:** Positioned to guide the wheels of the train on the main line away from the points.
    *   **Guard Rail for Branch Line:** Positioned to guide the wheels of the train on the diverging (branch) line away from the crossing.
*   **Switch Lock/Mechanism:** Devices to secure the switch rails in their desired position.
*   **Heel Block:** A heavy steel block used to connect the switch rail to the stock rail at the heel.
*   **Chairs/Baseplates:** Used to secure rails to sleepers.

**Diagrammatic Representation (Conceptual):**

Imagine a main line track. A turnout allows a train to leave this main line onto a second track. The "points" are the movable parts that direct the wheels. The "crossing" is where the two tracks physically diverge and the wheels essentially "cross over" an open gap.

**Learning Outcome 3:** Describe the components of a crossing.

**3. Crossings (Frogs)**

A crossing, often referred to as a "frog," is a device that provides a passage for the wheel flanges of a train to pass the intersection of two rails. It allows wheels on one track to cross the path of wheels on another track without derailment.

**3.1 Components of a Crossing:**

*   **Heel End:** The wider end of the crossing where the two rails of the diverging track are brought together.
*   **Toe End (or Nose):** The acute angle or point of the crossing where the rails of the two tracks would theoretically intersect.
*   **Crossing Point:** The theoretical point of intersection of the gauge lines of the two rails.
*   **Crossing Angle (or Frog Angle):** The angle formed by the intersection of the two crossing rails. This angle is crucial for determining the ease of passage and the cant deficiency permissible.
*   **Guard Rail:** A rail placed parallel to the running rail that guides the wheel flanges and prevents them from hitting the nose of the crossing.
*   **Filler Rails:** Short rails used to complete the running surface at the crossing.

**Key Concept:** The "number" of a crossing (e.g., 1 in 12, 1 in 8.5) refers to the ratio of the distance along the centerline of the diverging track to the lateral offset at that point, and is directly related to the tangent of the crossing angle. A higher number indicates a smaller angle and a gentler curve, allowing for higher speeds.

**Learning Outcome 4:** Discuss the design features of a turnout.

**4. Design Features of a Turnout**

The design of a turnout is critical for ensuring safe and efficient operation at speeds. Key design features include:

*   **Turnout Angle (or Switch Angle):** The angle between the axis of the straight track and the axis of the diverging track. This determines the radius of the diverging curve.
*   **Crossing Angle (Frog Angle):** The angle of the crossing, as discussed above.
*   **Camber of Switch Rails:** The slight outward curve given to the switch rails to ensure firm contact with the stock rails and to prevent tilting.
*   **Ballasting and Drainage:** Adequate ballasting and drainage are crucial for the stability and longevity of the turnout, as it is a complex and heavily stressed area.
*   **Alignment:** The smooth transition of alignment between the main line and the diverging track is important for passenger comfort and to minimize lateral forces.
*   **Cant (Superelevation):** The amount of cant applied on the diverging curve is designed to counteract centrifugal force and ensure stability at operational speeds.
*   **Fixing of Switch Rails:** The method of fixing the switch rails (e.g., clips, bolts) is designed to provide flexibility while maintaining secure seating.
*   **Points Mechanism:** The design of the mechanism for operating the switch rails (e.g., manual, powered) affects the speed and reliability of setting the points.
*   **Crossing Type:** The design of the crossing (e.g., fixed, spring, moveable) is selected based on operational requirements and speed.

**Important Point:** Turnouts are designed for specific maximum speeds. Higher speeds require gentler curves, which translates to larger turnout numbers and longer overall layouts.

**Learning Outcome 5:** Classify and explain different types of track junctions.

**5. Types of Track Junctions**

Track junctions are points where two or more tracks converge or diverge. They are essential for network connectivity.

*   **Simple Turnout (or Single Turnout):** The most basic junction, allowing a single line to diverge from another.
*   **Double Turnout:** A configuration where two turnouts are placed back-to-back, allowing movement from the main line to two different diverging tracks, or vice-versa.
*   **Symmetric Turnout:** A turnout where the diverging track has the same curvature as the main track, but in the opposite direction.
*   **Asymmetric Turnout:** A turnout where the diverging track has a different curvature from the main track.
*   **Scissors Crossing (or Crossover):** A arrangement of four sets of points and two crossings that allows trains to cross from one track to another without interfering with traffic on either track. This is used in busy areas like stations and depots.
*   **Diamond Crossing:** A junction where two tracks cross each other at an angle. This is common where a line passes over or under another line without connecting.
*   **Slip Points:** A type of junction where a short connecting line allows trains to move from one track to an adjacent one without the need for a full crossover, often used in station platforms.

**Example:** A simple turnout might be used to divert a train into a siding. A scissors crossing would be found in a busy station throat where multiple platforms need to be accessed efficiently.

**Learning Outcome 6:** Describe the construction of a railway track, including earthwork, plate laying, and ballast laying.

**6. Construction of Railway Track**

The construction of a railway track involves several stages, from preparing the formation to laying the final components.

**6.1 Earthwork and Consolidation:**

*   **Formation:** This is the prepared subgrade on which the track structure is built. It involves excavating or filling to achieve the required levels and profile.
    *   **Cutting:** Removal of earth or rock to create a depression for the track.
    *   **Embankment (Filing):** Depositing and compacting earth or other suitable material to raise the track level.
*   **Consolidation:** This is the process of compacting the soil in the formation to increase its density and bearing capacity, thereby preventing settlement.
    *   **Methods:** Compaction is achieved using rollers (e.g., vibratory rollers, sheepsfoot rollers), tampers, or pneumatic-tired rollers.
    *   **Layers:** Embankments are typically constructed in layers (lifts) of 150-300 mm, with each layer compacted before the next is added.
*   **Compaction Standards:** Compaction is critical and is measured by dry density achieved, often expressed as a percentage of the maximum dry density determined by laboratory tests (e.g., Proctor Test).

**6.2 Plate Laying:**

Plate laying refers to the process of laying the main components of the track structure: rails, sleepers, and fastenings.

*   **Track Components:**
    *   **Rails:** Steel bars that form the running surface for the wheels.
    *   **Sleepers (Ties):** Transverse members that support the rails and transmit the loads to the ballast. They can be made of wood, concrete (prestressed or reinforced), or steel.
    *   **Fastenings:** Devices that connect the rails to the sleepers (e.g., clips, spikes, bolts, rail pads).
*   **Methods of Plate Laying:**
    *   **Sleeper Laying:** Sleepers are placed at prescribed spacing (e.g., 500 mm, 600 mm center-to-center, depending on traffic and sleeper type).
    *   **Rail Laying:** Rails are lifted and placed on top of the sleepers.
    *   **Spacing and Gauging:** Ensuring the correct track gauge (distance between the inner faces of the rails) is maintained. Gauges are checked regularly, especially during the initial stages.
    *   **Ballast Spreading:** Ballast is spread under and around the sleepers to provide support and drainage.
    *   **Plumbing and Lining:** Ensuring the track is level (plumbing) and in the correct alignment (lining).
    *   **Track Laying Machines:** Modern construction often utilizes specialized machines for laying sleepers, rails, and ballast, significantly increasing speed and efficiency.

**6.3 Laying of Ballast:**

Ballast is the granular material placed beneath and around the sleepers. It is a critical component of the track structure.

*   **Purpose of Ballast:**
    *   **Support:** Distributes the load from the sleepers to the formation.
    *   **Drainage:** Allows water to drain away from the track structure, preventing saturation of the subgrade.
    *   **Stability:** Holds the sleepers in position and prevents lateral movement of the track.
    *   **Resilience:** Provides a degree of elasticity to absorb vibrations and impacts.
*   **Types of Ballast:**
    *   **Crushed Stone (Granite, Trap, etc.):** Preferred due to its angularity, durability, and interlocking properties.
    *   **Gravel:** Can be used in less demanding conditions.
    *   **Cinders, Chat, Crushed Slag:** Other materials used historically or in specific regions.
*   **Laying Process:**
    *   **Initial Ballast Layer:** A base layer of ballast is spread on the prepared formation.
    *   **Sleeper Placement:** Sleepers are laid on this initial layer.
    *   **Ballast Shoulder:** Ballast is then filled in between and on the sides of the sleepers to form shoulders.
    *   **Ballast Packing (Tamping):** This is a crucial process where ballast is consolidated under the sleepers using mechanical tampers (e.g., vibratory tampers, tamping machines). This ensures good load distribution and sleeper stability.
    *   **Lifting and Packing:** As the track is laid, it is often lifted using ballast tampers to achieve the correct level and alignment.
    *   **Ballast Section:** The ballast is laid to a specific cross-section profile, including a shoulder and a depth below the sleeper.

**Important Point:** Proper compaction of the formation and effective packing of the ballast are essential for the long-term stability and performance of the railway track.

**Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** What is the primary purpose of a turnout in a railway track?
**Answer:** The primary purpose of a turnout is to enable a train to be diverted from one track to another, allowing for route selection, shunting, siding access, and network connectivity.

**Question 2:** Name the two main components of a turnout.
**Answer:** The two main components of a turnout are the **Points (Switch Rails)** and the **Crossing (Frog)**.

**Question 3:** What is the significance of the "number" of a crossing?
**Answer:** The "number" of a crossing (e.g., 1 in 12) represents the ratio of the distance along the centerline of the diverging track to the lateral offset, and it is directly related to the tangent of the crossing angle. A higher number signifies a smaller angle and a gentler curve, which is suitable for higher speeds.

**Question 4:** List three essential functions of ballast in a railway track.
**Answer:** Three essential functions of ballast are:
1.  Distributing the load from the sleepers to the formation.
2.  Providing drainage for the track structure.
3.  Holding the sleepers in position and preventing lateral movement of the track.

**Question 5:** Explain the difference between earthwork consolidation and ballast packing.
**Answer:**
*   **Earthwork consolidation** refers to the compaction of the soil in the formation (the layer beneath the ballast) to increase its density and bearing capacity, preventing settlement of the entire track structure.
*   **Ballast packing** refers to the consolidation of the ballast material under and around the sleepers to ensure good load distribution from the sleepers to the ballast and, ultimately, to the formation.

*   **The speed of trains on a turnout is limited by the turnout number (crossing angle) and the cant deficiency.**
*   **Good drainage is paramount for the stability of the track structure.**
*   **Proper compaction of the formation and accurate ballast packing are essential for the longevity and performance of the railway track.**
*   **Track junctions are vital for the efficient functioning of any railway network.**
