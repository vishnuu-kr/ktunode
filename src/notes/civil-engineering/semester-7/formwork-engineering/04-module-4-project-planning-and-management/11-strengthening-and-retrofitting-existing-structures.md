# FORMWORK ENGINEERING - MODULE 4: PROJECT PLANNING AND MANAGEMENT

## Topic: Strengthening and Retrofitting Existing Structures

This module focuses on the crucial aspects of strengthening and retrofitting existing structures, which often go hand-in-hand with the application of formwork engineering principles in the repair and enhancement of existing buildings and infrastructure. Understanding these processes is vital for formwork engineers involved in restoration, rehabilitation, and seismic upgrade projects.

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

*   **Understand the purpose and necessity of strengthening and retrofitting existing structures.**
*   **Identify common deficiencies and damage patterns in existing structures.**
*   **Categorize and explain various strengthening and retrofitting techniques.**
*   **Discuss the role of formwork engineering in strengthening and retrofitting operations.**
*   **Outline the key considerations in planning and managing retrofitting projects.**
*   **Recognize the importance of material selection and quality control for retrofitting.**
*   **Evaluate the performance and durability of retrofitted structures.**

### 1. Purpose and Necessity of Strengthening and Retrofitting Existing Structures

#### 1.1 Definitions

*   **Strengthening:** The process of increasing the load-carrying capacity of a structural element or system.
*   **Retrofitting:** The process of modifying an existing structure to improve its performance, safety, or functionality, often in response to new building codes, increased loads, or degradation. This can include strengthening, seismic upgrades, or improving energy efficiency.

#### 1.2 Why Retrofit/Strengthen?

*   **Aging Infrastructure:** Deterioration due to time, environmental exposure, and wear and tear.
*   **Change in Usage:** When a structure is repurposed for a heavier load or different function than originally designed.
*   **Inadequate Original Design:** Structures built to outdated codes or with insufficient seismic resistance.
*   **Damage from Events:** Repairing damage caused by earthquakes, fires, floods, or impact.
*   **Seismic Upgrades:** Enhancing a structure's resilience to earthquake forces to meet current seismic design standards.
*   **Code Compliance:** Bringing older structures up to current building codes for safety and functionality.
*   **Economic Considerations:** Often more cost-effective than demolition and new construction.
*   **Historical Preservation:** Retaining architectural and historical significance while improving safety and performance.

#### 1.3 Key Concepts

*   **Capacity:** The maximum load a structural element can safely carry.
*   **Demand:** The actual loads acting on a structural element.
*   **Performance Level:** The desired response of a structure under specific load conditions (e.g., operational, immediate occupancy, life safety, collapse prevention).
*   **Ductility:** The ability of a material or structure to deform significantly without fracturing, which is crucial for seismic performance.

### 2. Common Deficiencies and Damage Patterns in Existing Structures

Understanding these is critical for determining the appropriate retrofitting strategy.

#### 2.1 Types of Deficiencies

*   **Material Degradation:**
    *   **Concrete:** Spalling, cracking, carbonation, chloride ingress, freeze-thaw damage.
    *   **Steel:** Corrosion (rusting), section loss.
    *   **Masonry:** Cracking, efflorescence, spalling, mortar deterioration.
    *   **Wood:** Rot, insect infestation, decay.
*   **Structural Weaknesses:**
    *   **Insufficient Member Size:** Beams, columns, or slabs that are too small for current load demands.
    *   **Inadequate Connections:** Weak or deteriorated joints between structural elements.
    *   **Lack of Lateral Load Resistance:** Insufficient shear walls, bracing, or diaphragm action for wind or seismic forces.
    *   **Foundation Issues:** Settlement, soil instability, inadequate bearing capacity.
*   **Design Flaws:**
    *   **Poor detailing:** Inadequate reinforcement in concrete, insufficient welding in steel.
    *   **Lack of continuity:** Beams not properly supported or continuous.
*   **Damage from External Factors:**
    *   **Earthquakes:** Cracking, shear failures, buckling of members, pounding effects.
    *   **Fires:** Reduced strength of materials, spalling of concrete.
    *   **Impact Loads:** Localized damage, cracking.

#### 2.2 Visual Inspection and Assessment

*   **Non-Destructive Testing (NDT):**
    *   **Rebound Hammer (Schmidt Hammer):** Estimates concrete compressive strength.
    *   **Ultrasonic Pulse Velocity (UPV):** Assesses concrete quality and detects internal defects.
    *   **Half-Cell Potential:** Detects active corrosion in reinforced concrete.
    *   **Covermeter:** Measures concrete cover over reinforcement.
    *   **Infrared Thermography:** Identifies delamination or moisture ingress.
*   **Destructive Testing (DT):**
    *   **Core Sampling:** Extracting concrete or masonry samples for lab testing (compressive strength, durability).
    *   **Pull-out/Pull-off Tests:** Measure bond strength of materials or anchors.
    *   **Load Tests:** Applying controlled loads to structural elements to assess their behavior.
*   **Documentation:** Thorough recording of observed damage, taking photographs, and creating detailed sketches.

#### 2.3 Example

*   **Deficiency:** A concrete beam shows widespread surface cracking and spalling, revealing corroded reinforcement bars.
*   **Assessment:** Rebound hammer tests indicate reduced concrete strength. Covermeter readings show shallow cover. Half-cell potential tests indicate active corrosion.
*   **Implication:** The concrete cover is compromised, and the steel reinforcement is losing its cross-sectional area due to corrosion, reducing the beam's load-carrying capacity and potentially its ductility.

### 3. Strengthening and Retrofitting Techniques

These techniques often involve adding new materials or modifying existing ones. Formwork plays a critical role in many of these.

#### 3.1 Concrete Structures

*   **Encasement/Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** Adding a layer of new concrete around existing columns, beams, or walls. This increases the cross-sectional area, strength, and stiffness.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Crucial for containing the new concrete pour. Often requires precise detailing to ensure tight fit against the existing structure, preventing leakage and creating a monolithic bond. Special forms may be needed for curved or complex shapes.
    *   **Reinforcement:** Additional rebar or steel mesh is typically placed within the new concrete layer.
    *   **Example:** Wrapping a concrete column with a new concrete layer to increase its compressive strength and shear resistance.
*   **Steel Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** Encasing concrete members with steel plates, angles, or channels, often bonded with epoxy grout or concrete.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Used to hold the steel jackets in place during grouting or to create a cavity for concrete encasement.
    *   **Example:** Using steel plates to reinforce a concrete beam's flexural capacity.
*   **Fiber Reinforced Polymer (FRP) Wrapping:**
    *   **Description:** Applying composite fabrics (carbon fiber, glass fiber, aramid fiber) saturated with epoxy resin onto the surface of concrete members. This significantly enhances tensile strength, flexural capacity, and confinement.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Minimal direct formwork is needed, but temporary supports or diaphragms might be required to maintain the shape of the member during the curing of the FRP.
    *   **Example:** Wrapping a concrete column with carbon fiber fabric to improve its seismic performance by increasing confinement and preventing buckling of longitudinal reinforcement.
*   **Bonded Steel Plates/Strips:**
    *   **Description:** Bonding steel plates to the tensile zones of beams or slabs using high-strength epoxy adhesives.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Not directly used for supporting the plates but may be needed for temporary shoring during installation if the existing structure's capacity is temporarily reduced.
    *   **Example:** Adding steel plates to the soffit of a concrete beam to increase its flexural strength.
*   **Prestressed Concrete Strengthening:**
    *   **Description:** Applying external tendons (steel cables) that are stressed and anchored to the existing structure to induce compressive forces, counteracting tensile stresses under load.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Essential for creating anchorages at the ends of the tendons and for encasing the prestressing system in grout.
    *   **Example:** Adding external post-tensioning to a bridge girder to increase its load capacity.
*   **Grouting and Crack Injection:**
    *   **Description:** Injecting epoxy resins or cementitious grouts into cracks to restore structural integrity and prevent further ingress of water and aggressive agents.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Sealing compound (acting as temporary formwork) is used to contain the injected material.
    *   **Example:** Injecting epoxy into spiderweb cracks in a concrete wall to restore its monolithic behavior.

#### 3.2 Masonry Structures

*   **Grouting:**
    *   **Description:** Injecting cementitious grout into the cores of hollow masonry units or cavities to increase strength and stability.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Used to block openings and prevent grout leakage during the injection process.
    *   **Example:** Grouting the cores of a hollow concrete block wall to improve its shear strength.
*   **Reinforcement and Concrete Jacketing:**
    *   **Description:** Adding reinforcement (rebar, steel mesh) and encasing the masonry with concrete or shotcrete.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Essential for containing the concrete or shotcrete. Forms must be designed to accommodate the masonry texture and provide a smooth surface for bonding.
    *   **Example:** Applying shotcrete to the face of a brick wall to create a composite panel with enhanced shear and flexural strength.
*   **Steel Reinforcement and Diaphragm Construction:**
    *   **Description:** Introducing horizontal reinforcement (e.g., rebar or steel straps) and creating a rigid diaphragm system (e.g., concrete slab, steel deck) to improve lateral load distribution.
    *   **Formwork Role:** For concrete diaphragms, standard formwork is used. For steel decks, temporary supports and form liners might be needed.
    *   **Example:** Adding a concrete topping slab with reinforcement to an existing timber floor to create a diaphragm for seismic resistance.

#### 3.3 Steel Structures

*   **Section Enlargement:**
    *   **Description:** Welding additional steel plates or sections to existing members to increase their cross-sectional area and load capacity.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Not typically required for the strengthening itself but may be used for temporary supports during welding or if encasement in concrete is performed.
    *   **Example:** Adding a flange plate to a steel beam to increase its bending capacity.
*   **Strengthening Connections:**
    *   **Description:** Reinforcing or replacing existing bolted or welded connections.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Generally not applicable, but temporary bracing might be needed.
    *   **Example:** Adding gusset plates to a steel truss joint to improve its strength.
*   **Concrete Encasing:**
    *   **Description:** Encasing steel members in concrete for fire protection or to increase their buckling resistance.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Standard formwork is used to contain the concrete.
    *   **Example:** Encasing a steel column in concrete for fire resistance rating.

#### 3.4 Timber Structures

*   **Reinforcement and Sistering:**
    *   **Description:** Adding new timber members alongside existing ones ("sistering") or reinforcing existing members with steel or FRP.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Minimal, but temporary supports might be needed.
    *   **Example:** Adding a new joist next to a sagging timber joist.
*   **Connection Strengthening:**
    *   **Description:** Replacing old fasteners, adding reinforcing plates, or improving joint details.
    *   **Formwork Role:** Not applicable.
    *   **Example:** Adding metal connectors to reinforce a timber roof truss connection.

### 4. The Role of Formwork Engineering in Strengthening and Retrofitting

Formwork is not just for new construction; it's integral to many retrofitting operations.

#### 4.1 Key Responsibilities of Formwork Engineers

*   **Design of Temporary Structures:**
    *   **Shoring and Reshoring:** Designing systems to support existing elements during modification or to carry temporary loads.
    *   **Scaffolding:** Providing safe access and work platforms.
    *   **Bracing:** Ensuring stability of existing or newly added components.
*   **Design of Permanent Formwork:**
    *   **Concrete Jacketing Forms:** Designing forms that are precisely dimensioned to create the desired added section and ensure a good bond. This includes considering tie-rod systems, sealing, and ease of stripping.
    *   **Grout Forms:** Designing forms for grout injection points and containment.
    *   **FRP Support Systems:** While not traditional formwork, designing temporary cradles or jigs to hold elements in place during FRP application.
    *   **Shotcrete Forms:** Designing forms that can withstand the impact pressure of shotcrete and provide the desired finish.
*   **Material Selection for Formwork:** Choosing appropriate materials based on strength, stiffness, reusability, and compatibility with the retrofitting process (e.g., preventing adhesion with epoxy).
*   **Construction Sequencing and Integration:** Understanding how formwork installation and removal will impact the existing structure and the overall construction schedule.
*   **Safety:** Ensuring that all temporary works (shoring, scaffolding) are designed and erected to the highest safety standards, considering the stability of the existing structure.
*   **Quality Control:** Ensuring that formwork is erected accurately and that concrete pours or material applications are performed correctly within the formwork.

#### 4.2 Specific Formwork Challenges in Retrofitting

*   **Tight Access and Complex Geometry:** Existing structures often have limited space and irregular shapes, requiring custom-designed formwork.
*   **Uncertainty in Existing Structure:** The condition of the existing structure might not be fully known, requiring adaptable formwork solutions.
*   **Minimizing Disruption:** Formwork must be designed for efficient installation and removal to minimize disruption to ongoing operations or occupants.
*   **Bonding to Existing Surfaces:** Forms must be designed to allow for proper surface preparation and ensure intimate contact for bonding with the existing material.
*   **Weight and Load Considerations:** Existing structures may have reduced capacity, meaning temporary loads from formwork and new materials must be carefully managed.

#### 4.3 Example

*   **Project:** Strengthening a damaged concrete bridge girder by encasing it with a new concrete jacket.
*   **Formwork Engineering Role:**
    *   Design of a robust formwork system that can be assembled in sections, accommodating the curved profile of the girder.
    *   Selection of form panels and bracing to withstand the hydrostatic pressure of the fresh concrete.
    *   Detailing of tie-rod locations to avoid conflicts with existing reinforcement and ensure minimal damage to the existing concrete surface.
    *   Planning for ease of form removal to avoid damaging the newly strengthened girder.
    *   Designing temporary shoring to support the bridge deck if the girder's capacity is insufficient during the strengthening operation.

### 5. Planning and Managing Retrofitting Projects

Effective project planning is paramount for the success of retrofitting works.

#### 5.1 Project Planning Stages

*   **Feasibility Study:**
    *   Initial assessment of structural condition.
    *   Identification of potential retrofitting options.
    *   Preliminary cost estimation and risk assessment.
*   **Detailed Investigation and Design:**
    *   Comprehensive site investigation (NDT, DT).
    *   Structural analysis of existing and proposed conditions.
    *   Development of detailed design drawings and specifications for strengthening measures and associated formwork.
*   **Permitting and Approvals:** Obtaining necessary building permits and approvals from regulatory bodies.
*   **Procurement:** Selecting contractors, suppliers, and specialist consultants.
*   **Execution:**
    *   Site preparation and safety management.
    *   Implementation of strengthening measures (including formwork erection).
    *   Quality control and testing.
    *   Monitoring and progress reporting.
*   **Closeout and Handover:** Final inspections, documentation, and handover to the owner.

#### 5.2 Key Management Considerations

*   **Risk Management:** Identifying potential risks (e.g., unexpected damage, contractor delays, material failures) and developing mitigation strategies.
*   **Cost Management:** Estimating, budgeting, and controlling project costs effectively.
*   **Schedule Management:** Developing a realistic construction schedule and monitoring progress.
*   **Safety Management (HSE):** Implementing rigorous safety protocols for working with existing structures and potentially hazardous materials.
*   **Quality Assurance/Quality Control (QA/QC):** Establishing procedures to ensure that materials and workmanship meet specified standards.
*   **Communication:** Maintaining clear and consistent communication among all stakeholders (owner, designers, contractors, authorities).
*   **Change Management:** Establishing a process for managing design or scope changes that may arise during the project.
*   **Stakeholder Engagement:** Especially important for occupied buildings, managing the impact on occupants and ensuring their safety and comfort.

#### 5.3 Example

*   **Project:** Retrofitting an occupied office building for seismic resistance.
*   **Planning Aspect:**
    *   **Phasing:** The retrofitting work will need to be phased to minimize disruption to building occupants. Work might be scheduled after hours or on weekends.
    *   **Containment:** Dust and noise control measures are critical. Temporary hoardings and barriers (acting as rudimentary formwork/enclosures) will be needed around work areas.
    *   **Access:** Designing temporary access platforms (scaffolding) that allow workers to reach all parts of the structure without obstructing tenant access.
    *   **Schedule:** Realistic scheduling considering the need for tenant coordination, potential for existing conditions to impact progress, and the sequence of operations to maintain structural stability.

### 6. Material Selection and Quality Control

The performance of retrofitted structures heavily relies on the quality of materials used and their proper application.

#### 6.1 Material Selection Criteria

*   **Strength and Stiffness:** Must be adequate for the intended retrofitting purpose.
*   **Durability:** Resistance to environmental factors (corrosion, moisture, UV, chemicals).
*   **Compatibility:** Chemical and physical compatibility with existing materials (e.g., coefficient of thermal expansion, potential for galvanic corrosion).
*   **Bonding Characteristics:** Ability to bond effectively to the existing substrate.
*   **Ease of Application:** Suitability for the site conditions and contractor capabilities.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** Balancing performance with budget constraints.
*   **Fire Resistance:** Important for some applications.

#### 6.2 Common Retrofitting Materials

*   **High-Strength Concrete and Grouts:** For jacketing, filling voids, and bonding plates.
*   **Epoxy Adhesives:** For bonding steel plates, FRP, and for crack injection.
*   **FRP Composites:** Carbon fiber, glass fiber, aramid fiber fabrics and their associated resins.
*   **Steel:** Plates, angles, channels, reinforcing bars, prestressing tendons.
*   **Specialty Cements:** For repair mortars and grouts.

#### 6.3 Quality Control Measures

*   **Material Testing:**
    *   **Concrete/Grout:** Compressive strength, setting time, workability, shrinkage.
    *   **Steel:** Yield strength, tensile strength, elongation, chemical composition.
    *   **FRP:** Tensile strength, elastic modulus, fiber content, resin properties.
    *   **Epoxy:** Bond strength, viscosity, cure time, mechanical properties.
*   **Workmanship Inspection:**
    *   **Surface Preparation:** Ensuring the existing surface is clean, sound, and properly profiled for bonding.
    *   **Application Procedures:** Verifying that materials are applied according to manufacturer's instructions and design specifications (e.g., correct mixing ratios, application temperatures, layer thickness, curing conditions).
    *   **Formwork Inspection:** Checking formwork for accuracy, stability, and leak-proofing before concrete pours or material application.
    *   **Reinforcement Placement:** Verifying correct bar sizes, spacing, and cover.
*   **Bond Testing:** Performing pull-off tests to verify the bond strength of applied materials (e.g., steel plates, FRP wraps) to the existing substrate.
*   **Non-Destructive Testing:** As mentioned earlier, used for quality assessment of new materials and their integration.

#### 6.4 Example

*   **Retrofit:** Applying FRP wraps to concrete columns.
*   **Quality Control:**
    *   **Material:** Testing the tensile strength and elastic modulus of the FRP fabric and the epoxy resin.
    *   **Surface Prep:** Inspecting the concrete surface for cleanliness, dryness, and acceptable roughness profile.
    *   **Application:** Witnessing the mixing of the epoxy resin to ensure correct ratios and adequate mixing time. Verifying that the fabric is applied with the correct orientation and tension, and that the specified number of layers are applied.
    *   **Bond:** Conducting pull-off tests on representative areas to confirm the bond strength between the FRP and concrete.

### 7. Performance and Durability of Retrofitted Structures

The ultimate goal is to ensure the structure performs as intended and lasts for its service life.

#### 7.1 Performance Evaluation

*   **Load Capacity:** Verification that the strengthened elements meet the required load-carrying capacity.
*   **Seismic Performance:** Assessment of how the structure will behave under seismic loads, ensuring it meets the targeted performance level (e.g., life safety).
*   **Serviceability:** Ensuring that deflections, vibrations, and crack widths are within acceptable limits.
*   **Durability:** The ability of the retrofitted structure to resist degradation over its intended service life.
*   **Aesthetics:** For visible strengthening measures, ensuring they are aesthetically acceptable.

#### 7.2 Factors Affecting Durability

*   **Quality of Design and Construction:** Proper detailing, material selection, and workmanship are critical.
*   **Environmental Exposure:** Aggressive environments (e.g., marine, industrial) can accelerate degradation.
*   **Maintenance:** Regular inspections and maintenance are essential to detect and address any new issues.
*   **Material Compatibility:** Incompatible materials can lead to premature failure.
*   **Protection of Strengthened Elements:** Measures to protect new materials from damage or degradation.

#### 7.3 Monitoring and Assessment

*   **In-situ Monitoring:** Using sensors (strain gauges, accelerometers, displacement transducers) to monitor the behavior of the retrofitted structure under service loads or during seismic events.
*   **Periodic Inspections:** Regular visual inspections to identify any signs of distress or deterioration.
*   **Load Testing:** Occasionally, load tests might be performed on critical elements to verify their performance.

#### 7.4 Example

*   **Retrofit:** Applying a concrete overlay to a bridge deck to repair spalling and increase load capacity.
*   **Performance:**
    *   **Initial:** Load tests confirm the increased load capacity. Surface roughness is within acceptable limits for traffic.
    *   **Durability:** Over time, monitoring the overlay for cracking, delamination, and bond degradation. Ensuring that the de-icing salts used on the bridge deck do not cause premature deterioration of the overlay or the bond to the existing deck. Regular cleaning and minor repairs help maintain durability.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Thorough Investigation is Key:** Never proceed with retrofitting without a comprehensive understanding of the existing structure's condition and deficiencies.
*   **Formwork is Often Critical:** Don't underestimate the role of formwork in the success of many strengthening and retrofitting techniques. Its design requires specific expertise.
*   **Safety First:** Retrofitting often involves working with complex and potentially unstable existing structures. Rigorous safety planning and execution are essential.
*   **Material Properties Matter:** The performance of the retrofit is directly tied to the correct selection and application of materials.
*   **Integration is Crucial:** Strengthening measures must be integrated with the existing structure and its load-carrying mechanisms effectively.
*   **Documentation is Vital:** Maintain meticulous records of all investigations, designs, materials, and construction processes.

### Practice Questions/Exercises

1.  **Scenario:** You are a formwork engineer tasked with designing formwork for concrete jacketing a series of deteriorated concrete columns in a heritage building. The columns are circular with a diameter of 500mm. The new concrete jacket will increase the diameter to 600mm. What are the key considerations for designing this formwork, and what are two specific challenges you might encounter?
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Key Considerations:**
            *   **Precision:** The formwork must be precisely dimensioned to achieve the desired 600mm diameter and ensure tight fit against the existing column to prevent grout leakage.
            *   **Material Selection:** Durable, reusable formwork material that can withstand repeated use and won't react with the concrete. Plywood, steel, or specialized plastic forms.
            *   **Assembly/Disassembly:** Designing for ease of assembly and stripping without damaging the existing column or the new jacket. Split forms or panel systems.
            *   **Anchorage/Support:** Designing a system to support the weight of the fresh concrete and resist lateral pressure. Tie rods or external banding might be needed.
            *   **Surface Preparation Access:** Allowing for proper cleaning and preparation of the existing column surface before concrete placement.
            *   **Grout Tightness:** Ensuring all joints and connections in the formwork are sealed to prevent leakage of cementitious grout.
        *   **Challenges:**
            *   **Limited Access:** Working in an occupied heritage building likely means restricted space and access, requiring custom formwork solutions.
            *   **Irregular Existing Surface:** The existing column surface might not be perfectly uniform, requiring formwork that can accommodate slight irregularities to maintain a consistent gap for the new concrete.
            *   **Protection of Heritage Features:** Ensuring the formwork and construction activities do not damage any historically significant finishes or features of the existing building.

2.  **Compare and contrast** concrete jacketing and FRP wrapping as strengthening techniques for a concrete beam. Discuss the respective roles of formwork in each technique.
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Concrete Jacketing:**
            *   **Description:** Adding a layer of new concrete (often with additional reinforcement) around the existing beam.
            *   **Purpose:** Increases flexural strength, shear strength, stiffness, and confinement.
            *   **Formwork Role:** **Essential and significant.** Custom formwork is designed to enclose the beam, containing the fresh concrete and ensuring a proper bond with the existing surface. This formwork must be robust, watertight, and precisely dimensioned.
        *   **FRP Wrapping:**
            *   **Description:** Applying composite fabric (e.g., carbon fiber) saturated with epoxy resin to the surface of the beam.
            *   **Purpose:** Significantly enhances tensile strength, flexural capacity, and can improve shear strength and confinement.
            *   **Formwork Role:** **Minimal direct formwork.** Typically, no permanent formwork is needed. Temporary supports or cradles might be used to hold the beam or fabric in place during the curing process. Surface preparation is key, but it doesn't involve enclosing the member with forms.
        *   **Comparison:** Concrete jacketing adds significant mass and section, while FRP is a lightweight, high-strength solution. Concrete jacketing requires extensive formwork; FRP requires minimal to no formwork. Concrete jacketing is generally more suitable for increasing member size and providing bulk resistance, while FRP is excellent for targeted strengthening, especially in confined spaces or where added weight is a concern.

3.  **Identify two common deficiencies** in existing reinforced concrete structures and describe the type of retrofitting technique that would be most suitable for each.
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Deficiency 1: Corroded Reinforcement and Spalled Concrete:**
            *   **Description:** Reinforcement bars within concrete have corroded, causing expansion, cracking, and spalling of the concrete cover. This reduces the effective area of steel and the bond between steel and concrete.
            *   **Suitable Retrofit:** **Concrete Jacketing or Grouting/Patching with Reinforcement.**
                *   **Concrete Jacketing:** After removing loose concrete and cleaning/treating the exposed rebar, a new concrete jacket can be applied, effectively encasing the member, providing new concrete cover, and potentially adding more reinforcement to increase capacity. Formwork is crucial here.
                *   **Grouting/Patching:** If the damage is localized and the structural capacity is only slightly affected, patching with a high-strength repair mortar after cleaning and treating corrosion, and possibly injecting grout into existing cracks, could be sufficient. This requires careful surface preparation and temporary forms for patching.

        *   **Deficiency 2: Insufficient Flexural Capacity of a Beam:**
            *   **Description:** A concrete beam is found to be inadequate to carry current or future anticipated loads due to its original design or a change in usage.
            *   **Suitable Retrofit:** **Bonded Steel Plates or FRP Wrapping.**
                *   **Bonded Steel Plates:** Steel plates are bonded to the tensile zone (soffit) of the beam using high-strength epoxy adhesive. This adds significant tensile capacity. Formwork is not directly used for the plates but might be needed for temporary supports if the beam's capacity is compromised during installation.
                *   **FRP Wrapping:** FRP fabrics are wrapped around the beam's soffit and sides to increase its flexural strength and shear capacity. Minimal formwork is involved, focusing on holding the material during curing.
