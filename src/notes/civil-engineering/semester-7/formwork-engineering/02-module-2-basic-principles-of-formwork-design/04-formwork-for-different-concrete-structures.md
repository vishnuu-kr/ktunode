# Formwork Engineering: Module 2 - Basic Principles of Formwork Design
## Topic: Formwork for Different Concrete Structures

---

### **Introduction**

This module delves into the fundamental principles of formwork design, with a specific focus on understanding the diverse types of formwork required for various concrete structures. Effective formwork is crucial for achieving the desired shape, strength, and surface finish of concrete elements, ensuring structural integrity and construction efficiency.

---

### **Learning Outcomes**

Upon completion of this topic, you will be able to:

*   **Identify and describe the common types of concrete structures that require formwork.**
*   **Explain the specific formwork requirements and considerations for different concrete structural elements.**
*   **Discuss the selection criteria for appropriate formwork systems based on structural type, size, complexity, and site conditions.**
*   **Outline the basic design principles and calculations involved in formwork for simple structural elements.**
*   **Recognize the importance of safety and quality control in formwork construction.**

### **1. Common Types of Concrete Structures Requiring Formwork**

Formwork is essential for almost all cast-in-place concrete structures. Here are some of the most common types:

*   **Foundations:**
    *   **Strip Footings:** Continuous concrete support for walls.
    *   **Pad Footings:** Isolated concrete support for columns.
    *   **Raft Foundations:** A large, monolithic slab covering the entire foundation area.
    *   **Pile Caps:** Connect individual piles to distribute building loads.

*   **Walls:**
    *   **Retaining Walls:** Structures designed to hold back soil or other materials.
    *   **Load-Bearing Walls:** Walls that support vertical loads from floors and roofs.
    *   **Shear Walls:** Walls designed to resist lateral forces (e.g., wind, seismic).

*   **Columns:**
    *   Vertical structural elements supporting beams, slabs, and roofs.
    *   Can be square, rectangular, circular, or polygonal.

*   **Beams:**
    *   Horizontal structural elements supporting slabs and carrying loads to columns or walls.
    *   Can be simple, continuous, or haunched.

*   **Slabs:**
    *   Horizontal structural elements forming floors, roofs, or pavements.
    *   **Solid Slabs:** Solid concrete decks.
    *   **Ribbed Slabs:** Slabs with integral ribs for increased strength and reduced weight.
    *   **Hollow-Core Slabs:** Precast elements with voids to reduce weight.
    *   **Flat Slabs:** Slabs supported directly by columns.

*   **Stairs:**
    *   Elements providing access between different levels.
    *   Involve complex geometries and often require detailed formwork.

*   **Domes and Shell Structures:**
    *   Curved or thin-shell concrete structures, often for roofs or large spans.
    *   Require specialized and often custom-designed formwork.

*   **Bridges:**
    *   Superstructures of bridges, including decks, girders, and abutments, are typically cast-in-place concrete and require extensive formwork.

### **2. Formwork Requirements and Considerations for Different Structural Elements**

The design and type of formwork are heavily influenced by the structural element being cast.

#### **2.1 Formwork for Foundations**

*   **Strip Footings:**
    *   **Requirements:** Simple, often rectangular or trapezoidal. Needs to contain concrete laterally and at the base.
    *   **Considerations:** Excavation side slopes can sometimes act as formwork. Vertical side forms are typically timber or plywood.
    *   **Example:** Wooden planks or boards staked into the ground to form the sides of the footing excavation.

*   **Pad Footings:**
    *   **Requirements:** Typically square or rectangular. Needs to contain concrete in all directions.
    *   **Considerations:** Depth and width of the footing. Access for pouring concrete.
    *   **Example:** Plywood or steel forms erected around the perimeter of the excavated hole.

*   **Raft Foundations:**
    *   **Requirements:** Large area, often requires perimeter containment and potentially internal division forms if there are thickened sections or openings.
    *   **Considerations:** Significant lateral pressure from the concrete. Needs to be robust and level.
    *   **Example:** Heavy-duty timber or steel formwork along the perimeter, with formwork for any upstands or internal walls.

*   **Pile Caps:**
    *   **Requirements:** Contain concrete around multiple pile heads. Needs to accommodate the exact location and dimensions of the piles.
    *   **Considerations:** Precision in placement is critical. Formwork needs to be strong enough to resist the outward pressure of the concrete around the piles.
    *   **Example:** Plywood or steel forms constructed around the pile heads, often with cutouts for the piles.

#### **2.2 Formwork for Walls**

*   **Requirements:** Needs to contain concrete on both faces and at the top. Must withstand significant lateral pressure from fresh concrete.
*   **Considerations:**
    *   **Height and Thickness:** Directly impacts lateral pressure.
    *   **Reinforcement:** Location and density of rebar can affect formwork access and tie placement.
    *   **Surface Finish:** The desired finish dictates the material and construction of the formwork.
    *   **Through Ties:** Essential for connecting opposing form faces and resisting lateral pressure.
    *   **Bracing:** Crucial for stability, especially for tall walls.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Timber Formwork:** Plywood or planks, suitable for smaller projects or complex shapes.
    *   **Steel Formwork:** Modular panels, durable, reusable, suitable for large projects and repetitive work.
    *   **Aluminum Formwork:** Lightweight and strong, good for repetitive tasks.
    *   **Ganged Formwork:** Large, pre-assembled sections of formwork, often used for core walls of high-rise buildings.
*   **Example:** For a concrete retaining wall, plywood sheets are erected on both sides, supported by timber walers and braced externally. Through-ties are used to connect the form faces and resist the concrete pressure.

#### **2.3 Formwork for Columns**

*   **Requirements:** Needs to contain concrete on all faces. Must withstand significant lateral pressure, especially for taller columns.
*   **Considerations:**
    *   **Cross-section Shape:** Square, rectangular, or circular. Circular columns often use specialized curved formwork.
    *   **Height:** Influences lateral pressure and bracing requirements.
    *   **Reinforcement:** The cage of reinforcing bars needs to fit within the formwork.
    *   **Striking Time:** How long the formwork remains in place after pouring.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Timber Formwork:** Plywood or plank forms, often with corner battens.
    *   **Steel Column Forms:** Cylindrical steel forms for round columns, or modular steel panels for rectangular columns.
    *   **Adjustable Steel Column Forms:** Allow for varying column dimensions.
*   **Example:** A square concrete column might be formed using four plywood panels, held together by steel bands or clamps and braced to prevent bowing. Circular columns often use pre-formed steel cylinder molds.

#### **2.4 Formwork for Beams**

*   **Requirements:** Needs to support the concrete load from the beam itself and the slab it supports. Formwork is required for the soffit (bottom) and sides of the beam.
*   **Considerations:**
    *   **Span:** Longer spans require stronger and more robust formwork and support systems (props).
    *   **Depth and Width:** Affects the volume and weight of concrete.
    *   **Slab Support:** The beam formwork must be integrated with the slab formwork.
    *   **Bearing:** The beam formwork must be adequately supported at its ends.
*   **Components:**
    *   **Soffit Board/Panel:** Forms the bottom of the beam.
    *   **Side Forms:** Form the sides of the beam.
    *   **Props/Shoring:** Support the soffit board and resist the downward load.
    *   **Bracing:** Prevents lateral movement of the side forms.
*   **Example:** For a concrete beam supporting a slab, a soffit board is placed at the required beam depth, supported by adjustable props. Side forms are attached to the soffit board and are braced to maintain their position.

#### **2.5 Formwork for Slabs**

*   **Requirements:** Forms the bottom surface of the slab and provides containment for the concrete.
*   **Considerations:**
    *   **Span:** The primary factor determining the type and spacing of support.
    *   **Thickness:** Affects the load on the formwork.
    *   **Load Capacity:** Must support the weight of fresh concrete, reinforcement, workers, and construction equipment.
    *   **Levelness:** The formwork must be perfectly level to achieve the required slab elevation and finish.
    *   **Edge Containment:** Forms are needed for the slab edges.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Timber Slab Formwork:** Plywood sheets supported by joists and props.
    *   **Steel Slab Formwork:** Modular steel panels, often used with adjustable props and a lattice of beams.
    *   **System Formwork (e.g., Aluminum or Composite):** Lightweight, modular systems that are quick to erect and strike.
    *   **Permanent Formwork:** Formwork that remains in place after the concrete has cured (e.g., metal decking).
*   **Example:** For a typical concrete floor slab, plywood sheets are laid on joists supported by adjustable props spaced according to the slab span and load. Timber or plywood forms are used around the perimeter.

#### **2.6 Formwork for Stairs**

*   **Requirements:** Complex geometry involving risers, treads, and potentially landings. Requires precise shaping to achieve the correct dimensions and angles.
*   **Considerations:**
    *   **Angle of Inclination:** Affects the shape of the formwork.
    *   **Tread and Riser Dimensions:** Must be accurately formed.
    *   **Support:** Stair formwork needs robust support to withstand the weight of concrete and workers.
    *   **Ease of Striking:** Formwork should allow for easy removal without damaging the finished stair.
*   **Example:** A common approach involves forming the soffit of the stair with angled timber planks supported by props. The risers are formed by vertical planks attached to the soffit formwork, and the treads are formed by the tops of the riser forms.

### **3. Selection Criteria for Appropriate Formwork Systems**

Choosing the right formwork system is a critical decision in formwork engineering. Key factors include:

*   **Structural Element Type and Geometry:**
    *   Simple shapes might use basic timber.
    *   Complex or curved shapes may require specialized or flexible systems.
    *   Walls and columns have different lateral pressure considerations.

*   **Size and Scale of the Project:**
    *   Small residential projects might use traditional timber.
    *   Large commercial or infrastructure projects often benefit from system formwork for efficiency and reusability.

*   **Repetitiveness of Formwork:**
    *   If the same element is repeated many times (e.g., column formwork), reusable system formwork is cost-effective.

*   **Desired Surface Finish:**
    *   High-quality architectural finishes require smooth, well-maintained formwork surfaces, often achieved with specific panel types (e.g., high-density plywood, steel).

*   **Site Conditions and Access:**
    *   Limited access might favor lighter materials like aluminum or system formwork.
    *   Space constraints can influence the type of props or support systems used.
    *   Ground conditions affect the type of foundation needed for props.

*   **Cost and Budget:**
    *   Initial cost of materials.
    *   Erection and dismantling labor costs.
    *   Reusability and durability.

*   **Availability of Materials and Skilled Labor:**
    *   The availability of specific formwork systems and experienced labor can dictate choices.

*   **Striking and Re-use Requirements:**
    *   Formwork designed for quick striking and multiple re-uses is more economical for large projects.

*   **Safety and Structural Integrity:**
    *   The formwork must be strong enough to withstand all anticipated loads and must be safely erected and dismantled.

### **4. Basic Design Principles and Calculations for Simple Structural Elements**

Formwork design involves calculating the loads it will experience and ensuring it can safely resist them.

#### **4.1 Loads on Formwork**

*   **Dead Loads:**
    *   Weight of the formwork itself.
    *   Weight of the reinforcing steel.
    *   Weight of the wet concrete.

*   **Live Loads:**
    *   Weight of workers.
    *   Weight of construction equipment (e.g., vibrators, buggies).
    *   Impact forces from dropping materials.

*   **Lateral Pressure from Concrete:**
    *   This is the most critical load. It depends on:
        *   **Height of the concrete pour (h):** Higher pours exert more pressure.
        *   **Rate of placement (R):** Faster placement increases pressure.
        *   **Temperature of concrete (T):** Higher temperatures reduce pressure.
        *   **Vibration:** Vibration can liquefy concrete, increasing pressure.
        *   **Type of cement and admixtures:** Can affect setting time and pressure.
        *   **Depth from the top of the pour (y):** Pressure increases with depth.

#### **4.2 Lateral Pressure Calculation (Simplified)**

A commonly used simplified formula for lateral pressure ($P_l$) from concrete on formwork is:

$P_l = w \times y$

Where:
*   $w$ is the unit weight of fresh concrete (typically 24 kN/m³ or 150 lb/ft³).
*   $y$ is the depth of fresh concrete from the top of the pour.

However, this is a simplification. More accurate methods account for the factors mentioned above. A more comprehensive approach considers the maximum lateral pressure as the lesser of:

1.  **Hydrostatic Pressure:** $P_{hydro} = w \times H$ (where H is the total height of the pour)
2.  **Pressure due to Rate of Placement and Temperature:**
    *   This is often calculated using empirical formulas or charts provided by formwork manufacturers, which consider the rate of placement, concrete temperature, and cement type. A common simplified expression for a specific pour height *h* can be:
        $P_{max} = \gamma_c \times h'$ (where $\gamma_c$ is the unit weight of concrete and $h'$ is the effective height that produces the maximum pressure).
        *   For walls, $P_{max}$ can be approximated as the minimum of $24h$ kN/m² or $24 + 4000 \times R/T$ kN/m², where $h$ is the height of the pour, $R$ is the rate of placement in m/hr, and $T$ is the concrete temperature in °C.

**Key Point:** The lateral pressure is not uniform. It's highest at the bottom of the fresh concrete pour and reduces to zero at the top surface of the pour. Formwork must be designed to withstand this pressure.

#### **4.3 Design of Formwork Components**

*   **Soffit Boards/Panels:** Must be strong enough to span between joists or supports without excessive deflection. Deflection limits are often specified for aesthetic reasons.
*   **Joists/Beams:** Support the soffit boards and transfer the load to props. Their size and spacing depend on the span and the load they carry.
*   **Props/Shoring:** Must be strong enough to support the formwork and the concrete load without buckling or yielding. Load capacity of props is usually provided by the manufacturer. The spacing of props depends on the load-carrying capacity of the formwork decking and the total load.
*   **Walers and Braces:** Used to strengthen formwork sides and resist lateral pressure. Their size and spacing depend on the lateral pressure and the strength of the form face.
*   **Through Ties:** Must be strong enough to resist the tension caused by the lateral pressure pushing the form faces apart.

#### **4.4 Example Calculation (Simplified - for understanding principles)**

**Problem:** Design formwork for a concrete wall 3m high, to be poured at a rate of 1.5 m/hr at 20°C. Assume concrete unit weight ($w$) = 24 kN/m³.

**Step 1: Determine Lateral Pressure**
Using the simplified formula as a starting point, and considering a more realistic approach:
Let's use an empirical formula for illustration (actual design would use tables or more complex calculations):
Assume a maximum pressure based on the height of the pour and rate of placement. For a 3m pour:
The pressure generally increases from the top to the bottom. The maximum pressure at the base of the pour is critical.
Let's assume a simplified calculation for maximum pressure:
$P_{max} \approx 24 \times 3 = 72 \text{ kN/m}^2$ (This is a simplified hydrostatic assumption, actual pressure will be less due to setting).

A more accurate empirical approximation for this scenario might yield a maximum pressure closer to 50-60 kN/m² at the base. For this example, let's use a conservative maximum pressure of $P_{max} = 50 \text{ kN/m}^2$ at the base.

**Step 2: Design of Form Face (e.g., Plywood)**
Assume 18mm plywood. We need to determine the spacing of walers.
The plywood needs to span between walers without excessive deflection or stress.
Let's assume a permissible bending stress for plywood is $\sigma_{perm}$.
The load on the plywood between walers is the lateral pressure.
*   If walers are spaced at 'a' meters horizontally and 'b' meters vertically, the load on a section of plywood is $P_{max} \times b$.
*   This load causes bending in the plywood.

**Step 3: Design of Walers**
Walers are horizontal members that hold the form face in position and transfer the lateral load to the braces or ties.
The spacing of walers depends on the strength of the form face and the lateral pressure.
*   For a given lateral pressure and form face material, the spacing of walers can be calculated based on the bending strength of the plywood or timber.
*   Walers are typically timber (e.g., 2x4, 2x6) or steel channels.

**Step 4: Design of Through Ties**
Through ties are installed at the waler level to connect opposite form faces.
The total horizontal force at the waler level needs to be resisted by the ties.
Force on a section of wall form = $P_{max} \times (\text{height of section}) \times (\text{spacing of walers})$.
This force is resisted by the tension in the ties. The strength of the ties and their spacing must be adequate.
*   The number of ties per waler level depends on the lateral pressure and the tie capacity.

**Step 5: Design of Bracing**
Bracing is used to prevent the formwork from overturning or buckling.
Braces are typically angled timbers or steel members that transfer lateral forces to the ground or a stable structure.

**Important Note:** This is a highly simplified overview. Actual formwork design involves detailed calculations for bending, shear, and buckling of all components, often using design software or tables provided by formwork system manufacturers, adhering to relevant building codes and standards (e.g., BS EN 13374, ACI 347).

### **5. Safety and Quality Control in Formwork Construction**

#### **5.1 Safety Aspects**

*   **Load Calculations:** Ensuring formwork is designed for all foreseeable loads.
*   **Material Inspection:** Using sound, undamaged materials for formwork and support.
*   **Erection Procedures:** Following safe erection sequences and ensuring components are properly secured.
*   **Bracing and Stability:** Adequate bracing is crucial to prevent collapse, especially during high pours or windy conditions.
*   **Access and Egress:** Providing safe means for workers to access and exit the formwork.
*   **Striking Procedures:** Safe removal of formwork without causing damage or collapse of the concrete element.
*   **Fall Protection:** Implementing measures to prevent falls from height.
*   **Worker Training:** Ensuring all personnel are trained in safe formwork practices.
*   **Regular Inspections:** Daily inspections of formwork, especially before and during concrete pours.

#### **5.2 Quality Control Aspects**

*   **Material Verification:** Checking that the correct type and quality of formwork materials are used.
*   **Dimensional Accuracy:** Ensuring formwork is set out and erected to the correct dimensions, levels, and plumbness as per drawings.
*   **Surface Finish:** Ensuring the form face is clean, oiled, and free from defects that could mar the concrete surface.
*   **Joints and Sealing:** Proper sealing of joints to prevent leakage of concrete grout.
*   **Tie Placement:** Ensuring through-ties are placed correctly and at the required spacing and elevation.
*   **Striking Time:** Adhering to specified striking times based on concrete strength development.
*   **Inspection of Stripped Work:** Checking the concrete surface finish and dimensions after formwork removal.

**Question 5 (Calculation - Conceptual):**

### **Answers to Practice Questions**

**Answer 1:**
*   **Slabs:** Formwork provides the bottom surface and edge containment for the slab, defining its shape, thickness, and level.
*   **Columns:** Formwork defines the shape and dimensions of the vertical support element and resists the lateral pressure of the concrete.
*   **Beams:** Formwork creates the soffit (bottom) and sides of the beam, supporting the concrete and shaping the beam cross-section.

**Answer 2:**
The main factors influencing lateral pressure are:
1.  **Height of the concrete pour.**
2.  **Rate of concrete placement.**
3.  **Temperature of the concrete.**
4.  **Degree of vibration or agitation.**
5.  **Properties of the cement and admixtures.**

**Answer 3:**
Adequate bracing is crucial for wall formwork to:
*   **Resist lateral pressure:** Prevent the formwork from bowing or collapsing outwards due to the pressure of the fresh concrete.
*   **Maintain plumbness:** Ensure the wall is cast vertically and to the correct alignment.
*   **Prevent overturning:** Resist any external forces like wind that might act on the formwork.

**Answer 4:**
Key considerations for a 5-meter high wall formwork:
*   **Lateral Pressure:** This will be significant due to the height. The formwork and bracing must be robustly designed to withstand this pressure.
*   **Formwork Material:** Heavy-duty systems like steel or Ganged formwork might be necessary for efficiency and strength.
*   **Through Ties:** High-strength ties will be essential and need to be spaced appropriately to resist the outward thrust.
*   **Bracing System:** Robust external bracing will be required to maintain stability and plumbness.
*   **Access for Pouring and Compaction:** Safe platforms for workers to pour and vibrate the concrete.
*   **Striking:** Ease of striking and reusability of the formwork panels.
*   **Reinforcement:** Adequate space and access for placing the reinforcement cage.

**Answer 5:**
The total load per meter run on each waler from that section of formwork is calculated as:
Load = Lateral Pressure × Spacing of Walers
Load = 40 kN/m² × 1.2 m
Load = 48 kN/m

This means each meter of the waler needs to be strong enough to support a uniformly distributed load of 48 kN.

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **Formwork is a temporary structure, but its design and construction are critical for the permanent structure's quality and safety.**
*   **Lateral pressure from concrete is the most significant load formwork must resist.**
*   **The choice of formwork system depends on a variety of factors, including structural type, complexity, scale, site conditions, and economics.**
*   **Safety must be paramount in all aspects of formwork design, erection, and dismantling.**
*   **Accurate alignment, leveling, and plumbness are essential for achieving the desired concrete element dimensions and finish.**
*   **Always refer to relevant codes of practice and manufacturer guidelines for detailed design and construction.**
