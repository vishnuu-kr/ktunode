# Formwork Engineering: Module 2 - Basic Principles of Formwork Design
## Topic: Shoring and Reshoring Practices

---

### 1. Introduction to Shoring and Reshoring

*   **Definition of Shoring:** Shoring is a temporary structural support system used to hold or support a structure during construction or repair. In formwork engineering, it specifically refers to the temporary support for freshly cast concrete elements until they gain sufficient strength to support themselves and any subsequent loads.

*   **Purpose of Shoring:**
    *   To support formwork and wet concrete loads.
    *   To support partially set concrete until it can carry its own weight and imposed loads.
    *   To maintain the correct shape and alignment of concrete elements during and after casting.
    *   To prevent premature failure or collapse of concrete structures due to insufficient strength.

*   **Purpose of Reshoring:** Reshoring is the process of erecting temporary shores to support a concrete structure that has already gained sufficient strength to carry its own load, but is not yet strong enough to carry superimposed loads or loads from subsequent construction stages.

*   **Why Reshoring is Necessary:**
    *   **To ensure adequate strength:** Concrete gains strength over time. The initial formwork and shores are designed for a certain early strength. As construction progresses, additional loads are applied (e.g., subsequent floors). Reshoring ensures the concrete slab or beam can handle these new loads by providing support from below until the concrete itself is sufficiently strong.
    *   **To control deflection:** Reshoring helps to minimize or control deflection in concrete elements, especially in long-span structures, during the construction of upper floors.
    *   **To prevent cracking:** By providing continuous support, reshoring can prevent cracking that might occur due to stresses induced by superimposed loads before the concrete has fully cured and gained strength.
    *   **To maintain construction schedule:** Reshoring allows for the removal of the initial formwork for reuse in other areas while still providing the necessary support for the concrete to cure.

### 2. Types of Shoring

Shoring systems are broadly categorized based on the application and the level of support required.

#### 2.1. Vertical Shoring (Sole Piece Shoring / Post Shores)

*   **Description:** This is the most common type of shoring, consisting of vertical posts that support horizontal beams or planks. These posts are typically adjustable to achieve precise heights.

*   **Components:**
    *   **Vertical Posts (Shoring Props):** Adjustable metal or timber poles. Metal props are more common due to their adjustability and load-carrying capacity.
    *   **Headed Props:** Posts with a head fitting for direct support of beams or joists.
    *   **Base Plates/Mud Sills:** Wide base at the bottom of the post to distribute load over the ground or sub-base.
    *   **Ledgers/Bracing:** Horizontal members connecting vertical posts for stability.

*   **Applications:**
    *   Supporting slabs, beams, and columns in multi-story construction.
    *   Temporary support for existing structures during alterations or repairs.
    *   Can be used for both initial shoring and reshoring.

*   **Key Considerations:**
    *   **Load Capacity:** Must be adequate for the formwork, wet concrete, and superimposed loads.
    *   **Stability:** Bracing is crucial to prevent buckling.
    *   **Adjustability:** Allows for fine-tuning of support heights.

#### 2.2. Horizontal Shoring (Beam Shoring)

*   **Description:** This type of shoring supports horizontal structural members (beams, girders) from below using a framework of horizontal beams and vertical posts.

*   **Components:**
    *   **Main Beams/Joists:** Support the formwork deck.
    *   **Joists:** Spaced members supporting the decking.
    *   **Ledgers:** Horizontal members that support the joists.
    *   **Vertical Posts:** Support the ledgers or main beams.
    *   **Bracing:** For lateral stability.

*   **Applications:**
    *   Supporting large or heavy beams and slabs where vertical posts alone might be insufficient or impractical.
    *   Often used in conjunction with vertical shoring.

#### 2.3.  **System Shoring**

*   **Description:** Prefabricated shoring systems designed for modular assembly. They offer speed of erection, high load-carrying capacity, and a degree of pre-engineering.

*   **Types of System Shoring:**
    *   **Cuplock System:** Features cups at regular intervals on vertical members that lock horizontal and diagonal members in place.
    *   **Ringlock System:** Similar to cuplock but uses a ring with locking pins.
    *   **Frame Shoring (Builder's Shoring):** Consists of pre-fabricated frames connected by cross braces.

*   **Advantages:**
    *   Faster erection and dismantling.
    *   High load capacity.
    *   Reduced site labor.
    *   Greater stability and safety.
    *   Easier to adapt to different configurations.

*   **Applications:**
    *   Universally applicable in modern construction for slabs, beams, columns, and walls.
    *   Often used for larger projects and where efficiency is paramount.

#### 2.4.  **Specialized Shoring**

*   **Cantilever Shoring:** Used when it's not possible to support from the ground for the full height. A part of the structure itself provides support. Less common in typical slab casting.

*   **Truss Shoring:** Employed for long spans where standard beams and posts may not be sufficient. Uses truss structures for enhanced strength.

### 3. Shoring Design Principles

The design of shoring systems must ensure safety and structural integrity.

#### 3.1. Load Calculations

*   **Dead Load:**
    *   Weight of formwork (plywood, timber, steel members).
    *   Weight of wet concrete.
    *   Weight of reinforcement.
    *   Weight of the shoring system itself.

*   **Live Load:**
    *   Weight of workers.
    *   Weight of equipment (vibrators, concrete pumps).
    *   Weight of construction materials stored on the formwork.
    *   Wind loads (if applicable and significant).
    *   Impact loads from dropping materials.

*   **Superimposed Loads:** Loads from subsequent floors or structural elements being cast before the underlying concrete has reached its full design strength.

*   **Load Combinations:** Design must consider various load combinations as per building codes and standards.

#### 3.2. Material Properties

*   **Strength of Timber/Steel:** Use of appropriate grades and species of timber or steel for posts, beams, and bracing.
*   **Compressive Strength:** Crucial for vertical posts.
*   **Bending Strength:** Important for beams and joists.
*   **Modulus of Elasticity:** Affects deflection.

#### 3.3. Stability and Bracing

*   **Lateral Bracing:** Prevents buckling of vertical posts and out-of-plane bending of horizontal members.
*   **Diagonal Bracing:** Essential for overall stability of the shoring framework.
*   **Base Support:** Ensure adequate bearing area for posts to prevent settlement. Use of sole plates or mudsills.

#### 3.4. Deflection Control

*   Design shoring to minimize excessive deflection of the formwork deck under load, which can affect the shape and finish of the concrete element.

#### 3.5. Adjustability and Ease of Erection/Dismantling

*   Use adjustable components (props) for precise height adjustment.
*   Design for efficient and safe erection and dismantling to minimize labor and time.

#### 3.6. Safety Factors

*   Apply appropriate safety factors to material strengths to account for uncertainties in loads, material properties, and workmanship.

### 4. Reshoring Practices

Reshoring is a critical step in multi-story concrete construction.

#### 4.1. When to Reshore

*   After the formwork for a slab or beam is removed, but before the concrete has attained sufficient strength to support the loads from the next construction stage (e.g., the next floor's formwork, wet concrete, or superimposed dead and live loads).

*   **Crucial Factor:** The timing of reshoring is dictated by the concrete's strength development, which is influenced by:
    *   Mix design (cement content, water-cement ratio, admixtures).
    *   Curing conditions (temperature, humidity).
    *   Casting date.

#### 4.2. Placement of Reshores

*   **Location:** Reshores are typically placed directly beneath the points of support on the slab or beam being constructed (e.g., directly under beams, joists, or concentrated loads on the slab).
*   **Spacing:** Spacing of reshores is determined by the load-carrying capacity of the concrete element being supported and the design of the reshoring system.

#### 4.3. Load Transfer from Formwork to Reshores

*   **Initial Stripping:** When the formwork is stripped, the weight of the concrete element is transferred to the original shores or to the newly placed reshores.
*   **Gradual Load Transfer:** The process should ensure a gradual transfer of load. The initial shoring is removed incrementally as the reshores take over the load-bearing function.

#### 4.4. Strength Requirements for Reshores

*   The reshores must be designed to carry the load from the supported concrete element PLUS any superimposed loads expected during the period they remain in place.

#### 4.5. Sequential Removal of Shores and Reshores

*   **Sequence:** The original shores supporting the lowest slabs/beams are removed first after the concrete in the upper floors has gained sufficient strength to carry its own weight and the loads from subsequent construction.
*   **Continuous Support:** Throughout the process, the structure must always have adequate support. The removal of one set of shores should be coordinated with the placement and loading of reshores supporting the structure above.

#### 4.6. Considerations for Reshoring

*   **Concrete Strength Testing:** Compressive strength tests (e.g., cylinder tests) are often performed to determine when it's safe to strip formwork and perform reshoring.
*   **Superimposed Loads:** Accurately estimate the loads that will be applied to the concrete before it can self-support.
*   **Adjustability:** Reshore systems should allow for adjustments to ensure they are snug and effectively transfer loads.

### 5. Safety in Shoring and Reshoring

Safety is paramount in all formwork operations, especially with temporary structures.

*   **Qualified Supervision:** Shoring and reshoring operations should be supervised by experienced personnel.
*   **Detailed Plans:** Shoring and reshoring schemes should be designed by qualified engineers and clearly documented.
*   **Regular Inspections:** Frequent inspection of shoring systems for signs of distress, damage, or instability.
*   **Load Limits:** Never exceed the designed load capacity of any shoring component.
*   **Proper Assembly:** Ensure all components are correctly assembled and secured.
*   **Bracing Integrity:** Maintain the integrity of bracing systems.
*   **Access and Egress:** Provide safe access and egress for workers.
*   **Fall Protection:** Implement appropriate fall protection measures.
*   **Material Handling:** Safe procedures for lifting and placing shoring components.
*   **Environmental Conditions:** Consider the impact of wind, rain, or uneven ground on shoring stability.
*   **Pre-Construction Checks:** Verify that the supporting structure (ground, previously cast slabs) can safely carry the shoring loads.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Temporary Support:** Shoring and reshoring are always temporary.
*   **Safety First:** Design and execution must prioritize safety.
*   **Load Calculation is Crucial:** Accurately determine all loads acting on the shoring system.
*   **Stability is Key:** Adequate bracing is essential to prevent collapse.
*   **Concrete Strength:** Understand concrete strength development for timely reshoring.
*   **System Shoring Advantages:** Prefabricated systems offer speed, efficiency, and often higher safety.
*   **Supervision and Inspection:** Essential for maintaining the integrity of shoring systems.
*   **Gradual Load Transfer:** Reshoring ensures loads are transferred smoothly from formwork shores to reshores.
