# Formwork Engineering: Module 3 - Formwork Safety

---

## Introduction to Formwork Safety

Formwork is a temporary structure used to support wet concrete until it gains sufficient strength to support itself. While essential for construction, it poses significant safety risks if not designed, erected, and dismantled correctly. This module focuses on understanding and mitigating these risks to ensure a safe working environment.

---

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

*   **Understand the inherent hazards associated with formwork systems.**
*   **Identify the key legislative and regulatory requirements for formwork safety.**
*   **Describe the principles of safe formwork design and erection.**
*   **Outline the procedures for safe formwork dismantling.**
*   **Recognize the importance of inspection, maintenance, and supervision in formwork safety.**

## 1. Inherent Hazards Associated with Formwork Systems

Formwork is inherently hazardous due to its temporary nature, the loads it must withstand (wet concrete, construction loads, environmental forces), and the dynamic activities involved in its erection and dismantling.

### 1.1 Types of Hazards

*   **Structural Collapse:**
    *   **Overloading:** Exceeding the design capacity of the formwork due to excessive concrete pour rates, premature loading, or accumulation of materials.
    *   **Inadequate Bracing:** Insufficient support against lateral forces (wind, seismic, construction activity).
    *   **Component Failure:** Weak or damaged formwork materials (plywood, timber, steel components, connectors).
    *   **Improper Assembly:** Loose connections, incorrect component placement, or missing elements.
    *   **Foundation Failure:** Settlement or failure of the base supporting the formwork.
*   **Falls from Height:**
    *   Working on elevated platforms without adequate guardrails, safety nets, or personal fall arrest systems (PFAS).
    *   Unsafe access methods to work areas.
    *   Slippery or unstable work surfaces.
*   **Falling Objects:**
    *   Tools, materials, or formwork components dropping from height onto workers below.
    *   During concrete pouring, there's a risk of splashes or spills.
*   **Entrapment and Crushing:**
    *   Workers becoming trapped between moving formwork sections during erection or dismantling.
    *   Crushing injuries from collapsing formwork or falling materials.
*   **Manual Handling Injuries:**
    *   Lifting and moving heavy formwork components without proper lifting techniques or mechanical aids.
*   **Electrical Hazards:**
    *   Contact with live electrical cables or equipment near formwork.
    *   Damaged electrical leads.
*   **Environmental Hazards:**
    *   Wind: Can exert significant lateral forces, especially on large or tall formwork.
    *   Rain: Can make surfaces slippery and compromise the strength of timber components.
    *   Extreme temperatures: Can affect worker performance and the properties of materials.

### 1.2 Examples

*   **Slab Collapse:** A major cause of fatalities. A common scenario involves a gang form for a large concrete slab failing due to inadequate bracing and insufficient support from props, leading to the collapse of the entire structure and the wet concrete.
*   **Wall Collapse:** During the pouring of a concrete wall, the formwork might not be properly tied or braced, leading to a blowout or complete collapse of the formwork, often resulting in serious injury or death from being hit by concrete or formwork.
*   **Falls during Erection:** A carpenter working at height to erect vertical formwork might fall due to not being tied off, or a loose plank giving way.

## 2. Legislative and Regulatory Requirements for Formwork Safety

Compliance with relevant laws, regulations, and codes of practice is crucial for ensuring formwork safety. These vary by jurisdiction but generally cover design, erection, use, and dismantling.

### 2.1 Key Regulatory Bodies and Standards

*   **Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) - USA:** Sets workplace safety standards, including specific requirements for scaffolding and fall protection, which are directly applicable to formwork.
*   **Health and Safety Executive (HSE) - UK:** Provides guidance and enforces regulations for health and safety in the workplace, including the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations (CDM).
*   **Local Building Codes and Standards:** Often dictate requirements for temporary structures and construction practices.
*   **Manufacturer's Guidelines:** Formwork system manufacturers provide specific instructions for safe assembly, use, and dismantling, which should be adhered to.

### 2.2 Common Regulatory Requirements

*   **Competent Person:** A designated individual with the necessary knowledge, experience, and authority to identify and manage formwork hazards. This person oversees design, erection, inspection, and dismantling.
*   **Formwork Design:**
    *   Formwork must be designed to safely support all applicable loads (dead loads, live loads, lateral loads) with an appropriate factor of safety.
    *   Designs should be reviewed and approved by a qualified engineer.
    *   Design drawings and specifications should be readily available on-site.
*   **Safe Erection and Dismantling Procedures:**
    *   A written plan for erection and dismantling should be developed and followed.
    *   Work should be performed in a safe sequence, ensuring stability at all stages.
    *   Workers must be trained on the specific procedures.
*   **Fall Protection:**
    *   Guardrails, safety nets, or PFAS must be used when working at heights above a specified threshold (e.g., 6 feet in the US).
    *   Guardrails typically consist of a top rail, mid-rail, and toe board.
*   **Inspection:**
    *   Formwork must be inspected before, during, and after concrete placement, and after any event that could affect its stability (e.g., heavy wind, impact).
    *   Inspections should be documented.
*   **Load Management:**
    *   The rate of concrete placement must be controlled to prevent overloading.
    *   Accumulation of materials on formwork must be prevented.
*   **Site Control:**
    *   Exclusion zones should be established below formwork during concrete placement and other hazardous operations.
    *   Barriers and signage should be used to warn personnel of hazards.

## 3. Principles of Safe Formwork Design and Erection

Safe formwork is a result of careful planning, sound design, appropriate material selection, and meticulous execution.

### 3.1 Design Considerations

*   **Load Calculations:**
    *   **Dead Load:** Weight of the formwork itself, reinforcing steel, and any construction loads attached to the formwork.
    *   **Live Load:** Weight of wet concrete, workers, equipment, and construction materials. The pressure exerted by wet concrete is a critical factor, increasing with the height and rate of pour.
    *   **Lateral Loads:** Wind pressure, seismic forces, impact from equipment or vehicles, and thrust from partially set concrete.
    *   **Other Loads:** Vibration, impact from materials.
*   **Factor of Safety:** Designs must incorporate a factor of safety to account for uncertainties in load calculations, material properties, and workmanship.
*   **Material Strength and Properties:**
    *   Timber: Strength, moisture content, presence of knots.
    *   Steel: Yield strength, buckling resistance.
    *   Plywood: Grade, thickness, number of plies, moisture resistance.
    *   Connectors (bolts, clamps, pins): Strength, load capacity.
*   **Bracing and Support:**
    *   Adequate bracing is essential to resist lateral forces and prevent buckling of vertical supports (props, shores).
    *   Waler and tie-rod systems are critical for containing lateral pressure in walls and columns.
    *   Cross-bracing and diagonal bracing are vital for stability.
*   **Foundation/Bearing:**
    *   The base supporting the formwork must be stable and capable of carrying the applied loads without excessive settlement.
    *   Leveled and compacted ground or engineered foundations are often required.
*   **Construction Sequencing:** The erection and dismantling sequence must be planned to maintain the stability of the partially completed structure.

### 3.2 Erection Principles

*   **Qualified Personnel:** Formwork erection should be supervised by a competent person and carried out by trained workers.
*   **Pre-Erection Checks:**
    *   Ensure all components are present and in good condition.
    *   Check that the base is stable and properly prepared.
    *   Verify that the design drawings are available and understood.
*   **Systematic Assembly:**
    *   Follow the design drawings and manufacturer's instructions meticulously.
    *   Erect components in the specified sequence.
    *   Ensure all connections are made securely and correctly.
*   **Bracing and Shoring:**
    *   Install all required bracing as soon as components are in place.
    *   Ensure props and shores are plumb and securely seated.
    *   Use proper jacking procedures for adjusting heights.
*   **Ties and Anchors:**
    *   Install wall ties and anchors at the correct spacing and tension.
    *   Ensure they are properly secured to prevent leakage and failure.
*   **Access and Working Platforms:**
    *   Provide safe access to all working areas.
    *   Install guardrails and toe boards on all elevated platforms.
    *   Ensure work surfaces are stable and free of trip hazards.
*   **Load Control:**
    *   Communicate with the concrete supplier and placing crew to manage the pour rate.
    *   Never exceed the designed pour rate or height.
    *   Avoid overloading formwork with materials or equipment.

### 3.3 Examples

*   **System Formwork:** Pre-fabricated modular systems (e.g., Doka, Peri) are designed for specific load capacities and require precise assembly according to manufacturer instructions. Incorrectly locking panels or using incorrect pins can lead to failure.
*   **Timber Formwork:** For columns, the timber planks should be adequately tied with steel bands or ties, and the formwork should be adequately braced diagonally to prevent it from twisting or collapsing under the pressure of wet concrete.
*   **Slab Formwork:** Using standard timber joists supported by adjustable props. The spacing of joists and props, as well as the thickness of plywood, must be calculated based on the slab thickness and the expected load of wet concrete. Bracing of the props is also crucial to prevent them from buckling.

## 4. Procedures for Safe Formwork Dismantling

Dismantling is as hazardous as erection, if not more so, due to the stored energy in the formwork and the potential for sudden collapse if not executed correctly.

### 4.1 Dismantling Principles

*   **Competent Supervision:** Dismantling must be supervised by a competent person.
*   **Written Plan:** A detailed dismantling plan should be in place, outlining the sequence of operations.
*   **Confirmation of Concrete Strength:** **CRITICAL:** Concrete must have gained sufficient strength to support its own weight and any superimposed loads before formwork is removed. This is often confirmed by testing or engineering judgment based on the concrete mix and curing conditions.
*   **Gradual Removal:**
    *   Remove formwork in a planned sequence, starting with the least supported sections.
    *   Never remove all supporting elements simultaneously.
    *   Maintain adequate support until the concrete is strong enough.
*   **Controlled Release of Loads:**
    *   Slowly release tension from ties and formwork fasteners.
    *   Use appropriate tools to avoid sudden release or damage.
*   **Bracing Removal:** Remove bracing only when it is no longer required for stability.
*   **Fall Protection:** Ensure all workers involved in dismantling are adequately protected from falls.
*   **Falling Object Protection:** Establish exclusion zones below the work area and use toe boards to prevent small items from falling.
*   **Component Handling:**
    *   Carefully detach formwork panels and supporting members.
    *   Use appropriate lifting equipment for heavy components.
    *   Store dismantled components safely and away from traffic areas.
*   **Propping:** Retain supporting props or shores until the concrete has reached the required strength for self-support, as specified by the engineer. These are often referred to as "striking” the formwork.

### 4.2 Examples

*   **Wall Formwork Removal:** After concrete has cured, a crew might remove ties first, then loosen any walers, and then carefully push or pull the form panels away from the hardened concrete, ensuring they have an escape route if the formwork shifts unexpectedly.
*   **Slab Formwork Removal:** For a suspended slab, props are typically removed in a staggered sequence. The props closest to the beams or supporting walls are often removed last, as these elements provide initial support to the slab. It's common practice to leave a few props in place for a period after the main formwork is removed, especially for large spans.

## 5. Inspection, Maintenance, and Supervision

These three elements are continuous and interwoven, forming the backbone of proactive formwork safety management.

### 5.1 Inspection

*   **Pre-Erection Inspection:** Checking all components for damage, warping, or defects before assembly.
*   **During Erection Inspection:** Verifying that assembly follows design and manufacturer's instructions, and that bracing is correctly installed.
*   **Pre-Pour Inspection:** A final check before concrete placement, confirming structural integrity, bracing, ties, and cleanliness. This is a critical hold point.
*   **During Pour Inspection:** Monitoring the formwork for any signs of distress (bulging, sagging, loosening of ties) as concrete is being placed.
*   **Post-Pour Inspection:** Checking for any damage or displacement that may have occurred during the pour.
*   **Periodic Inspections:** Throughout the concrete curing period, especially in adverse weather conditions or if loads are placed on the structure.
*   **Pre-Dismantling Inspection:** Assessing the concrete strength and ensuring the dismantling plan is appropriate.
*   **Record Keeping:** All inspections should be documented, noting any defects found and the corrective actions taken.

### 5.2 Maintenance

*   **Component Repair/Replacement:** Promptly repair or replace any damaged or defective formwork components.
*   **Cleaning:** Keep formwork clean and free from debris, which can compromise its integrity or create trip hazards.
*   **Lubrication:** Lubricate moving parts of system formwork as per manufacturer's recommendations.
*   **Storage:** Store formwork components properly to prevent damage from weather or impact.

### 5.3 Supervision

*   **Competent Supervision:** A competent person must be responsible for overseeing all aspects of formwork construction, use, and dismantling.
*   **Toolbox Talks:** Regular safety briefings (toolbox talks) to inform workers about specific hazards, safe work procedures, and any changes in the formwork plan.
*   **Enforcement:** Ensuring that safety procedures are followed and that workers are not taking shortcuts.
*   **Communication:** Maintaining clear communication channels between designers, supervisors, formworkers, and concrete placing crews.
*   **Training:** Ensuring all personnel involved have received adequate training for their roles.

---

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Formwork is a temporary structure designed to carry significant loads. Treat it with respect.**
*   **Never assume formwork is safe. Always inspect and verify.**
*   **A competent person is crucial for formwork safety management.**
*   **Concrete pressure is dynamic and can be significant, especially during high-pour rates.**
*   **Bracing is as important as the primary support.**
*   **The strength of the concrete before formwork removal is paramount.**
*   **Dismantling requires the same rigor and planning as erection.**
*   **Always refer to design drawings and manufacturer's instructions.**
*   **Effective communication and regular inspections are key to preventing incidents.**

What are the three primary types of loads that formwork must be designed to withstand?
a) Dead load, live load, wind load
b) Dead load, live load, seismic load
c) Dead load, wet concrete pressure, lateral loads
d) Dead load, live load, impact load

Who is responsible for ensuring that formwork is designed safely?
a) The formwork erection crew
b) The concrete supplier
c) A qualified and competent engineer
d) The site foreman

**Question 3:**
Which of the following is a critical factor to consider *before* dismantling formwork for a suspended concrete slab?
a) The weather forecast for the next day
b) The color of the formwork panels
c) That the concrete has gained sufficient strength to support itself
d) Whether the formwork looks aesthetically pleasing

**Question 4:**
What are the key components of a fall protection guardrail system?
a) Top rail and toe board
b) Top rail, mid-rail, and toe board
c) Mid-rail and mid-rail
d) Toe board and safety harness

**Question 5:**
Describe two common hazards associated with the erection of formwork and how they can be mitigated.

---

## Answers

**Answer 1:**
c) Dead load, wet concrete pressure, lateral loads.
*   **Dead Load:** Weight of the formwork itself, rebar, etc.
*   **Wet Concrete Pressure:** The pressure exerted by the fluid concrete, which increases with pour height and rate.
*   **Lateral Loads:** Wind, seismic forces, impact, thrusts.

**Answer 2:**
c) A qualified and competent engineer. While others are involved in the process, the ultimate responsibility for the design's integrity lies with a qualified engineer.

**Answer 3:**
c) That the concrete has gained sufficient strength to support itself. This is crucial to prevent premature collapse.

**Answer 4:**
b) Top rail, mid-rail, and toe board. These components, when installed at specific heights, create a barrier to prevent workers from falling.

**Answer 5:**
Two common hazards and their mitigation:

1.  **Hazard:** **Falls from Height** during erection.
    **Mitigation:**
    *   Use of personal fall arrest systems (PFAS) such as harnesses and lanyards.
    *   Installation of guardrail systems around elevated work platforms.
    *   Ensuring safe access methods (e.g., secure ladders, scaffolding).
    *   Proper training on working at height.

2.  **Hazard:** **Structural Collapse** due to overloading or inadequate bracing.
    **Mitigation:**
    *   Ensure formwork is designed by a qualified engineer to withstand all expected loads.
    *   Strict adherence to the design specifications for bracing and support.
    *   Competent person to inspect formwork before, during, and after concrete placement.
    *   Control of concrete pour rate to avoid exceeding the designed capacity.
    *   Never store excessive materials on formwork.
