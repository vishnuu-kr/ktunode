# ENGINEERING WORKSHOP - MODULE 3: FOUNDRY

## Topic: Pattern Making

Welcome, everyone, to our session on Pattern Making, a crucial aspect of the Foundry module. Today, we're diving deep into what patterns are, why they're so important, and how they are made. Understanding pattern making is fundamental to the entire foundry process, and it directly ties into several of our course outcomes.

### 1. Introduction to Pattern Making

Think about it: how do we create a metal object, say a complex engine component or even a decorative piece, through the foundry process? We can't just pour molten metal into thin air and expect it to form a specific shape, can we? That's where patterns come in.

**What is a Pattern?**

Simply put, a **pattern** is a replica or a near-replica of the final casting, but with some modifications to account for the casting process itself. It's the blueprint that guides the creation of the mold cavity, which will eventually be filled with molten metal.

**Why are Patterns Essential in Foundry? (CO1, CO2)**

Patterns are the very first step in creating a casting. Without them, there's no mold, and without a mold, there's no casting. Their importance can be summarized as follows:

*   **Forming the Mold Cavity:** The primary function of a pattern is to create the desired shape of the mold cavity. As you can imagine, this is essential for producing the final casting in the required shape.
*   **Ensuring Dimensional Accuracy:** While patterns are not exact replicas, they are made to specific dimensions that, after accounting for shrinkage and machining allowances, will result in the final casting of the correct size.
*   **Dictating Surface Finish:** The surface of the pattern influences the surface finish of the mold cavity, and consequently, the surface finish of the final casting. A smoother pattern generally leads to a smoother casting.
*   **Facilitating Core Setting (if applicable):** For hollow castings or those with internal features, patterns are designed to accommodate cores, which are used to form these internal surfaces.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO1 (Identify workshop operations and instruments):** Understanding pattern making helps us identify the operations and instruments used in a pattern shop, which is a specialized area within a foundry. We'll talk about specific tools and materials later.
*   **CO2 (Understand appropriate tools and instruments):** To make a pattern, we need specific tools. Knowing what a pattern is helps us understand *why* we need certain types of woodworking or metalworking tools for its creation.

### 2. Key Concepts in Pattern Making

Before we get into the "how," let's clarify some essential terms and considerations that influence pattern design and construction.

#### 2.1. Pattern Allowances

This is a critical area where patterns differ from the final casting. If we made a pattern exactly the same size as the final desired product, our casting would be too small! Why? Because most metals shrink as they cool down from their molten state. Also, castings often need further machining to achieve precise dimensions and a smooth surface. So, we build these allowances directly into the pattern.

*   **Shrinkage Allowance (or Contraction Allowance):** This is the most important allowance. Metals expand when heated and contract when cooled. Molten metal poured into the mold is hotter than the mold itself, and then the solidified metal cools down to room temperature. This cooling process causes contraction. Different metals contract by different amounts. Patternmakers use **shrinkage rules** (which are essentially measuring scales that are longer than standard rules by the expected shrinkage amount) to ensure the pattern is oversized by the correct percentage. For example, if a metal shrinks by 1.25% on cooling, a 1000mm pattern would be made to 1012.5mm. As Khanna P.N. mentions in his "Indian Practical Civil Engineering Handbook," understanding material properties, including thermal expansion and contraction, is vital for accurate construction.
*   **Machining Allowance (or Finish Allowance):** Castings often have rough surfaces and may not meet tight dimensional tolerances. Machining allowance is added to surfaces that will be machined later to provide extra material for this process. The amount of allowance depends on the casting method, the metal being cast, and the required surface finish and accuracy. Arora S.P and Bindra S.P's "Building Construction" might not directly discuss foundry allowances, but the principle of adding material for finishing is common in construction too, like leaving plaster thickness.
*   **Draft Allowance (or Taper Allowance):** Imagine trying to pull a shape out of a box filled with sand. If the sides are perfectly vertical, it would be very difficult, and the mold might break. Draft allowance is a slight taper or slope given to all vertical surfaces of the pattern. This taper allows the pattern to be easily removed from the mold without damaging the mold cavity. This is analogous to how mold release agents work in concrete casting or how building components are designed with slight bevels for easier assembly.
*   **Distortion Allowance:** Some castings, due to their shape or internal stresses developed during cooling, are prone to distortion. If a component is long and thin, or has complex internal structures, it might warp. Patternmakers might intentionally introduce slight counter-distortions into the pattern to counteract this tendency in the final casting. This is a more advanced concept, often relying on experience.
*   **Rattler Allowance:** This is a less common allowance used for very large castings where the weight of the molten metal can cause the mold walls to bulge outwards. A slight inward taper (opposite of draft) might be added to resist this bulging.

#### 2.2. Parting Line and Parting Surface

The parting line is the line where the two halves of a split mold meet. The **parting surface** is the surface of the pattern that forms this line.

*   **Why is it important?** The location of the parting line significantly affects:
    *   **Ease of pattern removal:** A well-chosen parting line minimizes the draft required.
    *   **Mold strength:** It influences how the mold halves are supported.
    *   **Casting quality:** It can affect the amount of excess metal (flash) that might appear at the joint.

Think of a sandcastle mold. The top part you lift off is one half of the mold, and the sand left behind is the other. The line where they meet is the parting line. A good patternmaker will choose a parting line that is easy to separate the mold halves and also creates a good surface for the final casting.

#### 2.3. Core Prints

For castings with internal cavities or holes, we use **cores**. Cores are separate inserts made of sand (or other materials) that are placed into the mold cavity to form these internal features.

*   **What are Core Prints?** Core prints are extensions of the pattern that form recesses or seats in the mold. These recesses are shaped to accurately support the core during the pouring of molten metal. The core print essentially provides a place for the core to rest securely, ensuring it stays in position and doesn't shift. Without proper core prints, the core might sag or move, resulting in a casting with incorrect internal dimensions or even a blown-out cavity.

### 3. Materials Used for Patterns

The choice of material for a pattern depends on several factors, including:

*   **Number of castings required:** For a few castings, wood might be fine. For thousands, metal or plastic is more durable.
*   **Complexity of the casting:** Intricate shapes might require more stable materials.
*   **Accuracy and surface finish required:** Some materials lend themselves to finer finishes.
*   **Cost:** Wood is generally cheaper than metal.

As S.C. Rangwala's "Engineering Materials" would extensively cover, material properties are key. Here are the common ones:

*   **Wood:** This is the most common material for patterns, especially for jobbing work (one-off or small batches).
    *   **Types:** Pine, Walnut, Mahogany, Teak are often used. Pine is cheap and easy to work but prone to warping. Teak and Mahogany are denser, more stable, and durable but more expensive.
    *   **Advantages:** Easy to machine, readily available, relatively inexpensive.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Prone to warping and swelling due to moisture, susceptible to abrasion and damage, may not give very fine surface finish.
    *   **Treatment:** Wood patterns are usually sealed with lacquer or varnish to protect them from moisture and to provide a smoother surface.
*   **Metal:** Used for making a large number of identical castings where durability and accuracy are paramount.
    *   **Types:** Aluminium, Brass, Cast Iron, Bronze.
    *   **Advantages:** Durable, dimensionally stable, can achieve fine surface finish, resistant to abrasion.
    *   **Disadvantages:** More expensive, harder to machine, heavier.
*   **Plastic and Resins:** Increasingly used, especially for complex shapes and 3D printed patterns.
    *   **Types:** Epoxy resins, Polyester resins, various polymers.
    *   **Advantages:** Can be cast or molded into very complex shapes, good dimensional stability, can be lightweight, good surface finish.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Can be brittle, cost can be high for specialized materials.
*   **Plaster and Cement:** Sometimes used for specific applications, especially for temporary patterns or in specialized molding processes.
*   **Wax:** Primarily used for **Investment Casting** (also known as lost-wax casting), where the wax pattern is melted out of the mold.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO4 (Examine the quality of common materials):** By understanding what materials are used for patterns, we can relate this to their properties – hardness, density, machinability, resistance to moisture – which directly impacts the quality of the pattern and, subsequently, the casting.
*   **CO5 (Conduct market study):** Knowing these materials allows us to understand what is available in the market for pattern making.

### 4. Types of Patterns

Patterns are classified based on their construction and how they are used in the molding process. This classification helps us understand the design considerations for different casting requirements.

#### 4.1. Based on Construction

*   **Single-Piece Pattern (or Solid Pattern):** The simplest type. It's a single solid piece without any loose parts.
    *   **When used:** For simple shapes where the pattern can be easily withdrawn from the mold without damaging the sand.
    *   **Limitations:** Requires significant draft allowance, and the parting line is usually at the center.
    *   **Example:** A simple ball or a basic block.
*   **Two-Piece Pattern:** Consists of two halves joined together.
    *   **When used:** For shapes that cannot be withdrawn easily from the mold if made as a single piece.
    *   **How it works:** The pattern is made in two halves, each corresponding to one half of the mold. The halves are usually dowelled together. The parting line is at the joint of the two halves.
    *   **Example:** A simple pulley.
*   **Multi-Piece Pattern:** Consists of more than two pieces.
    *   **When used:** For very complex shapes where even a two-piece pattern would require excessive draft or would be difficult to assemble and handle. The pattern is broken down into several pieces that are assembled and then withdrawn sequentially.
    *   **Example:** A complex engine block casting pattern.
*   **Gated Pattern:** This is a two-piece or multi-piece pattern that has built-in channels (gates) for the molten metal to enter the mold cavity.
    *   **When used:** To save time and effort in creating the gating system separately in the mold. The gates are part of the pattern itself.
    *   **Example:** A pattern for a small gear with integrated gates.
*   **Match Plate Pattern:** This is a specialized type of pattern, usually made of metal or a durable composite, where both halves of the pattern and the gating system are mounted on opposite sides of a single plate.
    *   **When used:** For mass production where high accuracy and speed are required. It's used on **flaskless molding machines**.
    *   **How it works:** The plate is placed between the cope (top half) and drag (bottom half) of the mold flask. The mold is squeezed around the plate, forming both halves of the cavity simultaneously.
    *   **Example:** Patterns for automotive parts produced in large quantities.

#### 4.2. Based on Use

*   **Loose Piece Pattern:** This type of pattern has one or more loose pieces attached to it.
    *   **When used:** For complex shapes where a part of the pattern cannot be withdrawn directly from the mold. The loose piece is removed from the mold first, and then the main pattern is withdrawn.
    *   **Example:** A casting with an undercut feature. A small boss or lug might be attached as a loose piece.
*   **Skeleton Pattern (or Frame Pattern):** This pattern is made of thin strips of wood forming a framework of the casting.
    *   **When used:** For large, bulky castings where a solid pattern would be too heavy, expensive, and difficult to handle.
    *   **How it works:** The frame creates the outer shape, and the core (if any) forms the internal shape. The space between the frame and the core is filled with cheap, loose sand or gravel.
    *   **Example:** Patterns for large, hollow castings like flywheels or some types of bells.
*   **Follow Board:** This is not a pattern itself, but an accessory used with loose patterns. It's a board with a recess shaped to fit the loose part of the pattern.
    *   **When used:** To support the loose piece of the pattern during the first ramming of the sand, ensuring it stays in place and the mold is formed correctly.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO3 (Apply various tools, practices and methods):** Understanding these different types of patterns shows how different molding *practices* and *methods* necessitate specific pattern designs and construction techniques. The tools and practices used for a match plate pattern are very different from those for a skeleton pattern.

### 5. Tools Used in Pattern Making

Pattern making is essentially a form of joinery and shaping, often using woodworking tools, but also requiring precision measurement and finishing. K.C. John's "Mechanical Workshop Practice" and S.K. Hajra Choudhury's "Elements of Workshop Technology" would provide detailed descriptions of these.

Here are some key tools you'd find in a pattern shop:

*   **Measuring and Marking Tools:**
    *   **Steel Rule:** For basic measurements.
    *   **Measuring Tape:** For larger dimensions.
    *   **Vernier Caliper and Micrometer:** For precise measurements of dimensions and allowances.
    *   **Marking Gauge:** To mark lines parallel to an edge, essential for draft and shrinkage allowances.
    *   **Scriber:** For marking lines on wood or metal.
    *   **Try Square and Bevel Protractor:** For checking squareness and angles.
    *   **Shrinkage Rule:** As discussed earlier, this is a specialized measuring tool.
*   **Cutting Tools:**
    *   **Saws:** Hand saws (cross-cut, rip), Bench saws, Band saws, Jig saws (for curved cuts). Band saws are excellent for cutting curves and taper allowances.
    *   **Chisels:** Wood chisels of various sizes and shapes for paring and shaping.
    *   **Files and Rasps:** For shaping, smoothing, and removing material. Rasps are coarser and used for rough shaping, while files provide a smoother finish.
    *   **Spokeshave:** A hand tool used for smoothing and shaping curved wooden surfaces.
*   **Shaping and Finishing Tools:**
    *   **Planes:** Block planes, Jack planes, Smoothing planes for flattening and smoothing surfaces.
    *   **Wood Lathe:** Used for turning cylindrical or conical parts of a pattern.
    *   **Drilling Tools:** Hand drills, Bench drills, Drills bits for making holes for dowels or for core prints.
    *   **Sandpaper and Sanding Blocks/Machines:** For achieving the desired surface finish.
*   **Assembly Tools:**
    *   **Hammers:** For driving nails or dowels.
    *   **Screwdrivers:** For screws used in assembly.
    *   **Clamps:** Various types (bar clamps, pipe clamps, C-clamps) to hold pieces together while glue dries.
    *   **Doweling Jig:** For accurately drilling holes for dowels to ensure proper alignment of pattern parts.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO1 (Identify workshop operations and instruments):** All these tools are instruments used in the operation of pattern making.
*   **CO2 (Understand appropriate tools):** Knowing the different types of patterns and the allowances required helps us select the *appropriate* tool for a specific task. For instance, a spokeshave is appropriate for shaping a curved draft, while a band saw is used for cutting out the basic shape.
*   **CO3 (Apply various tools, practices and methods):** This is where you'll be tested – can you select the right tool for the job and use it correctly to create a pattern with the necessary allowances?

### 6. Pattern Making Process (A General Overview)

Let's walk through a simplified process for making a wooden pattern, say, a two-piece pattern for a simple flange.

1.  **Study the Drawing:** Understand the final casting dimensions, required tolerances, and any special features.
2.  **Determine Pattern Allowances:** Calculate shrinkage, machining, and draft allowances based on the drawing and foundry practices.
3.  **Select Material:** Choose appropriate wood based on size, complexity, and durability needed.
4.  **Prepare the Material:** Cut the wood to approximate size, ensuring grain direction is considered for stability.
5.  **Rough Shaping:** Using saws and potentially a lathe, bring the wood close to the desired dimensions, incorporating allowances like shrinkage and draft.
6.  **Machining and Finishing:** Use planes, spokeshaves, and chisels to achieve the precise dimensions and smooth surfaces. Pay close attention to the draft allowance on all vertical surfaces.
7.  **Parting Surface Creation:** Ensure the parting surface is flat and smooth. If it's a two-piece pattern, the meeting surfaces must be perfectly matched.
8.  **Adding Features:** Attach loose pieces (if any) or core prints using glue and dowels. Ensure strong and accurate joining.
9.  **Assembly:** If it's a multi-piece pattern, assemble all components securely, often using dowels and glue for alignment and strength.
10. **Surface Treatment:** Apply one or more coats of good quality sanding sealer or lacquer. This seals the wood, provides a smooth finish, and protects it from moisture. A final coat of bright color (like red or blue) is often applied to distinguish the pattern from the sand mold.
11. **Inspection:** Check the pattern for accuracy of dimensions, smoothness of surfaces, and correctness of all allowances.

**Relating to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO3 (Apply various tools, measuring devices, practices and different methods):** This entire process is about applying tools and practices. Each step requires specific methods and the correct application of instruments.

### 7. Common Pitfalls in Pattern Making

*   **Incorrect Allowance Calculation:** Not applying shrinkage or machining allowance, or applying them incorrectly, is a major source of error. Always double-check your calculations and the correct shrinkage rule for the metal being cast.
*   **Insufficient Draft:** Making vertical surfaces too steep leads to difficulty in pattern removal, damaging the mold and resulting in poor castings.
*   **Poorly Located Parting Line:** This can increase draft requirements, make the mold difficult to handle, or lead to flash.
*   **Rough Surfaces:** A rough pattern leads to a rough mold and a rough casting, which might require more machining.
*   **Loose Joints or Warped Parts:** Poorly constructed patterns will not produce accurate molds. Use glue and dowels effectively.

**Remember this:** A good pattern is the foundation of a good casting. Investing time and care in pattern making saves a lot of trouble downstream.

### Summary and Key Takeaways

Pattern making is an art and a science. It bridges the gap between the designer's drawing and the final metal product in the foundry. Key aspects to remember are:

*   **Patterns are not exact replicas; they include allowances.**
*   **Shrinkage, machining, and draft allowances are crucial.**
*   **The choice of material depends on the production volume and complexity.**
*   **Different pattern types are suited for different casting needs.**
*   **Precision and care are essential at every step.**

By understanding these concepts, you'll gain a solid appreciation for the foundry process and how critical pattern making is to achieving quality castings.

## Sample Questions with Answers

**Q1. What is the primary purpose of a pattern in the foundry?**

**Answer:** The primary purpose of a pattern is to create a cavity in the molding sand that has the desired shape of the final casting. This cavity is then filled with molten metal.

**Reasoning:** This question tests the fundamental understanding of the role of a pattern, directly relating to its definition and purpose.

**Q2. Explain why shrinkage allowance is necessary for a pattern.**

**Answer:** Shrinkage allowance is necessary because most metals contract (shrink) as they cool from their molten state to room temperature. If a pattern were made to the exact final dimensions of the casting, the resulting casting would be too small. The shrinkage allowance is added to the pattern dimensions to compensate for this contraction, ensuring the final casting is of the correct size.

**Reasoning:** This question probes understanding of a critical pattern allowance. The answer needs to explain the "why" behind the allowance.

**Q3. A pattern maker is making a pattern for a component to be cast in Aluminium. The final casting is required to be 250 mm long. If the shrinkage allowance for Aluminium is 1.25%, what should be the length of the pattern?**

**Answer:**
Length of the pattern = Actual casting length + Shrinkage allowance
Shrinkage allowance = 1.25% of 250 mm
Shrinkage allowance = (1.25 / 100) * 250 mm
Shrinkage allowance = 0.0125 * 250 mm
Shrinkage allowance = 3.125 mm

Length of the pattern = 250 mm + 3.125 mm = 253.125 mm

**Reasoning:** This is a practical, calculation-based question that tests the application of the shrinkage allowance concept. It's a common type of exam question.

**Q4. What is the difference between a loose piece pattern and a skeleton pattern? (CO1, CO2)**

**Answer:**
*   **Loose Piece Pattern:** This pattern has one or more small, detachable parts that are attached to the main pattern. These loose pieces are removed from the mold *before* the main pattern is withdrawn, to facilitate the removal of complex shapes or undercuts from the mold cavity.
*   **Skeleton Pattern:** This pattern is a frame-like structure made of wooden strips that form the outline of the casting. It's used for large, bulky castings to save material and weight. The space within the skeleton is often filled with loose sand or gravel, and a core forms the internal shape.

**Reasoning:** This question requires distinguishing between two types of patterns based on their construction and application, linking to CO1 (identifying workshop operations/patterns) and CO2 (understanding appropriate tools/practices for different patterns).

**Q5. Why is draft allowance essential in pattern making? Give an example of a tool used to create draft. (CO3)**

**Answer:** Draft allowance is essential to ensure that the pattern can be easily withdrawn from the mold cavity without damaging the delicate sand walls of the mold. It is a slight taper or slope given to all vertical surfaces of the pattern. Without adequate draft, the friction between the pattern and the sand would cause the mold to break, leading to a faulty casting.
A tool like a **spokeshave** or a **bench plane** set at a slight angle can be used to create draft on a wooden pattern. Alternatively, when cutting on a **band saw**, the blade can be tilted to achieve the required taper.

**Reasoning:** This question asks for the reason behind an allowance and an example of a tool application, directly addressing CO3 (applying tools and practices).
