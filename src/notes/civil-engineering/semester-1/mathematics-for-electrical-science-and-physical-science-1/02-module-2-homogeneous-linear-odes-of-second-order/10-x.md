# Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order - Topic: $ke^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving deeper into the fascinating world of second-order homogeneous linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with constant coefficients. We've already seen how equations with real, distinct roots and real, repeated roots behave. Now, we're going to tackle the case that often shows up in systems exhibiting oscillatory behavior, like electrical circuits with inductors and capacitors, or mechanical systems with springs and dampers. This case involves **complex conjugate roots**, and our focus today is on understanding solutions of the form $ke^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$.

Think about a simple RLC circuit. When you switch it on, the current doesn't just jump to a steady state. It often oscillates, perhaps with some damping. These oscillations, and how they decay over time, are precisely what solutions involving sines and cosines, multiplied by an exponential decay or growth factor, describe. That exponential factor $e^{\alpha x}$ tells us whether the oscillations are growing (if $\alpha > 0$), decaying (if $\alpha < 0$), or remaining constant (if $\alpha = 0$). The $\cos(\omega x)$ term, along with its sine counterpart, describes the oscillatory nature itself, with $\omega$ determining the frequency of these oscillations. The constant $k$ simply scales the amplitude of this response.

This topic is crucial because it directly addresses **Course Outcome 2 (CO2): Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equations with constant coefficients**. Specifically, we are focusing on a particular form of the solution that arises from a specific type of characteristic equation root. Understanding this will equip you to model and analyze many real-world physical phenomena.

Let's start by recalling how we find the general solution to a homogeneous linear ODE of the second order with constant coefficients:

$ay'' + by' + cy = 0$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants, and $a \neq 0$.

We assume a solution of the form $y = e^{mx}$. Substituting this into the ODE, we get the **characteristic equation**:

$am^2 + bm + c = 0$

The roots of this quadratic equation, $m_1$ and $m_2$, dictate the form of our general solution.

---

## The Case of Complex Conjugate Roots

What happens when the discriminant of the characteristic equation, $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$, is **negative**? This means we have no real roots, but rather a pair of complex conjugate roots. Let's express these roots in the standard form:

$m = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

If $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, we can write $b^2 - 4ac = -(4ac - b^2)$. Let $4ac - b^2 = \beta^2$, where $\beta = \sqrt{4ac - b^2}$. Then, the roots become:

$m = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{-\beta^2}}{2a} = \frac{-b \pm i\beta}{2a}$

So, our complex conjugate roots are:

$m_1 = -\frac{b}{2a} + i\frac{\beta}{2a}$
$m_2 = -\frac{b}{2a} - i\frac{\beta}{2a}$

Let's simplify this by introducing new constants: $\alpha = -\frac{b}{2a}$ and $\omega = \frac{\beta}{2a} = \frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}$.

Now, our roots are of the form:

$m_1 = \alpha + i\omega$
$m_2 = \alpha - i\omega$

When we have these complex roots, the solutions we initially derived from $y = e^{mx}$ would be $y_1 = e^{(\alpha + i\omega)x}$ and $y_2 = e^{(\alpha - i\omega)x}$. These are complex-valued functions. In many physical applications, we are interested in real-valued solutions.

### From Complex Exponentials to Real Sines and Cosines: Euler's Formula

This is where a beautiful piece of mathematics comes in handy: **Euler's Formula**. As introduced in many calculus texts, like Anton's "Calculus" (12th ed.), Euler's formula states:

$e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i\sin(\theta)$

Using this, we can rewrite our complex solutions:

$y_1 = e^{(\alpha + i\omega)x} = e^{\alpha x}e^{i\omega x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\omega x) + i\sin(\omega x))$
$y_2 = e^{(\alpha - i\omega)x} = e^{\alpha x}e^{-i\omega x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(-\omega x) + i\sin(-\omega x))$

Since $\cos(-\theta) = \cos(\theta)$ and $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin(\theta)$, we have:

$y_2 = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\omega x) - i\sin(\omega x))$

Now we have two complex-valued solutions. A fundamental theorem in the theory of linear ODEs states that if $y_1$ and $y_2$ are solutions, then any linear combination $c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ is also a solution. We can use this property to construct two *real-valued* and *linearly independent* solutions.

Let's try forming two linear combinations:

1.  **Sum of $y_1$ and $y_2$:**
    $y_{1} + y_{2} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\omega x) + i\sin(\omega x)) + e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\omega x) - i\sin(\omega x))$
    $y_{1} + y_{2} = 2e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega x)$

2.  **Difference of $y_1$ and $y_2$ (divided by $2i$ to keep it real):**
    $\frac{y_{1} - y_{2}}{2i} = \frac{e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\omega x) + i\sin(\omega x)) - e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\omega x) - i\sin(\omega x))}{2i}$
    $\frac{y_{1} - y_{2}}{2i} = \frac{e^{\alpha x}(2i\sin(\omega x))}{2i} = e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$

So, our two linearly independent real-valued solutions are:

$y_{A}(x) = e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega x)$
$y_{B}(x) = e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$

### The General Solution

The general solution to the ODE $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ when the characteristic equation has complex conjugate roots $m = \alpha \pm i\omega$ is a linear combination of these two solutions:

$y(x) = C_1 e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega x) + C_2 e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$

Or, more compactly, we can factor out the exponential term:

$y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(C_1 \cos(\omega x) + C_2 \sin(\omega x))$

Here, $C_1$ and $C_2$ are arbitrary constants determined by initial or boundary conditions.

**Remember this:** When you encounter complex conjugate roots $\alpha \pm i\omega$ for the characteristic equation, the form of the real-valued solutions will involve $e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega x)$ and $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$.

---

## Understanding the Components: $\alpha$ and $\omega$

Let's break down what $\alpha$ and $\omega$ mean physically.

*   **$\alpha$ (The Decay/Growth Factor):** This is the real part of the complex roots.
    *   If $\alpha < 0$, the term $e^{\alpha x}$ represents **exponential decay**. This means the oscillations will gradually die out over time. Think of a pendulum with friction – it eventually stops swinging. This is common in damped systems.
    *   If $\alpha > 0$, the term $e^{\alpha x}$ represents **exponential growth**. The oscillations will become larger and larger. This is often indicative of an unstable system, like a microphone too close to a speaker (feedback).
    *   If $\alpha = 0$ (which happens when $b=0$), there's no exponential decay or growth. The oscillations would theoretically continue forever with constant amplitude. This is called **undamped oscillation**.

*   **$\omega$ (The Angular Frequency):** This is the imaginary part of the complex roots (multiplied by $i$). It determines how rapidly the oscillations occur. A larger $\omega$ means more oscillations in a given interval of $x$.
    *   The term $\cos(\omega x)$ oscillates between +1 and -1.
    *   The period of oscillation is $T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}$.
    *   The frequency is $f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}$.

So, the general solution $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(C_1 \cos(\omega x) + C_2 \sin(\omega x))$ describes **damped oscillations** if $\alpha < 0$, **growing oscillations** if $\alpha > 0$, and **undamped oscillations** if $\alpha = 0$.

## Examples to Illustrate

Let's work through a few examples to solidify our understanding.

### Example 1: Purely Oscillatory System (No Damping)

**Problem:** Solve the ODE $y'' + 4y = 0$.

**Analysis:**
This is a homogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients. The characteristic equation is:
$m^2 + 4 = 0$

Solving for $m$:
$m^2 = -4$
$m = \pm \sqrt{-4} = \pm 2i$

Here, our roots are purely imaginary, $m = 0 \pm 2i$. This fits the form $\alpha \pm i\omega$ where $\alpha = 0$ and $\omega = 2$.

**Solution:**
Since $\alpha = 0$, there is no exponential decay or growth. The solutions are of the form $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$.
The general solution is:
$y(x) = e^{0x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))$
$y(x) = C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x)$

**Relatable Analogy:** Imagine a perfectly frictionless pendulum. Once you push it, it would swing back and forth forever with the same amplitude. This ODE describes such an idealized system.

### Example 2: Damped Oscillations

**Problem:** Solve the ODE $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$.

**Analysis:**
The characteristic equation is:
$m^2 + 2m + 5 = 0$

Using the quadratic formula $m = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$:
$m = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(5)}}{2(1)}$
$m = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2}$
$m = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2}$
$m = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2}$
$m = -1 \pm 2i$

The roots are $m_1 = -1 + 2i$ and $m_2 = -1 - 2i$. This is of the form $\alpha \pm i\omega$, where $\alpha = -1$ and $\omega = 2$.

**Solution:**
With $\alpha = -1$, we expect exponential decay. The general solution is:
$y(x) = e^{-1x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))$
$y(x) = e^{-x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))$

**Relatable Analogy:** Consider a pendulum with a bit of air resistance or friction in its pivot. It will swing, but each swing will be slightly smaller than the last. The $e^{-x}$ term makes the amplitude of the oscillations decrease over time. The $\cos(2x)$ and $\sin(2x)$ terms describe the back-and-forth motion.

### Example 3: Growing Oscillations (Unstable System)

**Problem:** Solve the ODE $y'' - 2y' + 2y = 0$.

**Analysis:**
The characteristic equation is:
$m^2 - 2m + 2 = 0$

Using the quadratic formula:
$m = \frac{-(-2) \pm \sqrt{(-2)^2 - 4(1)(2)}}{2(1)}$
$m = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 8}}{2}$
$m = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{-4}}{2}$
$m = \frac{2 \pm 2i}{2}$
$m = 1 \pm i$

The roots are $m_1 = 1 + i$ and $m_2 = 1 - i$. This is of the form $\alpha \pm i\omega$, where $\alpha = 1$ and $\omega = 1$.

**Solution:**
With $\alpha = 1$, we expect exponential growth in the amplitude of oscillations. The general solution is:
$y(x) = e^{1x}(C_1 \cos(1x) + C_2 \sin(1x))$
$y(x) = e^{x}(C_1 \cos(x) + C_2 \sin(x))$

**Relatable Analogy:** Think about trying to balance a pencil on its tip. If you nudge it slightly, gravity will cause it to fall further away from the vertical, increasing its deviation exponentially. If you could somehow make it wobble while falling, that would be analogous to growing oscillations. In electronics, this can happen with positive feedback loops.

## Finding the Constants $C_1$ and $C_2$

To find the specific solution for a given problem, you'll usually be provided with initial conditions. For a second-order ODE, these are typically the value of $y$ and its derivative $y'$ at a specific point, say $x=0$. For instance:

$y(0) = y_0$
$y'(0) = v_0$

Let's use Example 2 ($y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$) and add some initial conditions: $y(0) = 1$ and $y'(0) = 0$.

Our general solution is $y(x) = e^{-x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))$.

**Step 1: Apply the first initial condition, $y(0) = 1$.**
$y(0) = e^{-0}(C_1 \cos(0) + C_2 \sin(0))$
$1 = 1(C_1 \cdot 1 + C_2 \cdot 0)$
$1 = C_1$

So, $C_1 = 1$. Our solution now looks like $y(x) = e^{-x}(\cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))$.

**Step 2: Find the derivative $y'(x)$.**
We need to use the product rule and chain rule.
$y'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}[e^{-x}(\cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))]$
$y'(x) = (-e^{-x})(\cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x)) + e^{-x}(-2\sin(2x) + 2C_2 \cos(2x))$

**Step 3: Apply the second initial condition, $y'(0) = 0$.**
$y'(0) = (-e^{-0})(\cos(0) + C_2 \sin(0)) + e^{-0}(-2\sin(0) + 2C_2 \cos(0))$
$0 = (-1)(1 + C_2 \cdot 0) + 1(-2 \cdot 0 + 2C_2 \cdot 1)$
$0 = -1(1) + 1(2C_2)$
$0 = -1 + 2C_2$
$1 = 2C_2$
$C_2 = \frac{1}{2}$

**The Specific Solution:**
Substituting $C_1 = 1$ and $C_2 = \frac{1}{2}$ back into the general solution:

$y(x) = e^{-x}(\cos(2x) + \frac{1}{2} \sin(2x))$

This equation describes a system that starts at $y=1$ with zero initial velocity, and then oscillates with decreasing amplitude due to the $e^{-x}$ factor.

## Connection to Course Outcomes and Further Study

This topic directly supports **CO2** by providing the method to solve a specific case of homogeneous linear ODEs. The ability to identify complex conjugate roots and transform them into real-valued sinusoidal solutions is a core skill.

For those of you looking ahead or wanting to connect this to other areas, this form of solution is foundational for:

*   **Signals and Systems (Haykin & Van Veen):** Understanding the response of LCR circuits, mechanical vibrations, and control systems. The concepts of damping ratio and natural frequency are directly related to $\alpha$ and $\omega$.
*   **Laplace Transforms (CO3):** While we solved this using the characteristic equation, Laplace transforms provide an alternative, powerful method to solve these ODEs, especially when initial conditions are given. The complex roots of the characteristic equation correspond to poles of the system's transfer function in the Laplace domain.
*   **Taylor Series (CO4):** The exponential and trigonometric functions themselves can be represented by their Taylor series, offering another perspective on the behavior of these solutions, particularly for small values of $x$.

### Important Points to Remember for Exams:

1.  **Identify the characteristic equation:** Always start by writing down $am^2 + bm + c = 0$.
2.  **Calculate the discriminant:** $b^2 - 4ac$. If it's negative, you're in the complex roots case.
3.  **Find the roots:** Use the quadratic formula to get $m = \alpha \pm i\omega$.
4.  **Form the general solution:** $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(C_1 \cos(\omega x) + C_2 \sin(\omega x))$.
5.  **Apply initial conditions:** Be careful with differentiation (product rule!) and algebraic manipulation when solving for $C_1$ and $C_2$.

**Common Pitfall:** Students sometimes forget to include the $e^{\alpha x}$ term when $\alpha \neq 0$, or they might swap $\alpha$ and $\omega$ when writing down the solution. Double-check your roots and their corresponding solution forms!

## Sample Questions and Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
For the ODE $y'' - 6y' + 13y = 0$, what is the form of the general solution?

**Answer:**
First, write the characteristic equation: $m^2 - 6m + 13 = 0$.
Calculate the discriminant: $\Delta = (-6)^2 - 4(1)(13) = 36 - 52 = -16$.
Since $\Delta < 0$, we have complex conjugate roots.
The roots are $m = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{6 \pm 4i}{2} = 3 \pm 2i$.
So, $\alpha = 3$ and $\omega = 2$.
The general solution is of the form $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(C_1 \cos(\omega x) + C_2 \sin(\omega x))$.
Therefore, the general solution is $y(x) = e^{3x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))$. This describes growing oscillations because $\alpha = 3 > 0$.

**Question 2 (Application with Initial Conditions):**
Solve the ODE $y'' + 9y = 0$ with initial conditions $y(0) = 2$ and $y'(0) = -6$.

**Answer:**
The characteristic equation is $m^2 + 9 = 0$.
The roots are $m^2 = -9$, so $m = \pm \sqrt{-9} = \pm 3i$.
Here, $\alpha = 0$ and $\omega = 3$.
The general solution is $y(x) = e^{0x}(C_1 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x)) = C_1 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x)$.

Now, apply initial conditions:
1.  $y(0) = 2$:
    $2 = C_1 \cos(0) + C_2 \sin(0)$
    $2 = C_1(1) + C_2(0)$
    $C_1 = 2$.
    So, $y(x) = 2 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x)$.

2.  Find $y'(x)$:
    $y'(x) = -6 \sin(3x) + 3C_2 \cos(3x)$.

3.  $y'(0) = -6$:
    $-6 = -6 \sin(0) + 3C_2 \cos(0)$
    $-6 = -6(0) + 3C_2(1)$
    $-6 = 3C_2$
    $C_2 = -2$.

The specific solution is $y(x) = 2 \cos(3x) - 2 \sin(3x)$. This represents undamped oscillations with a specific amplitude and phase determined by the initial conditions.

**Question 3 (Identifying Behavior):**
What type of behavior (damped, growing, or undamped oscillations) does the solution to $y'' + 2y' + 2y = 0$ exhibit?

**Answer:**
The characteristic equation is $m^2 + 2m + 2 = 0$.
The roots are $m = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(2)}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 8}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-4}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm 2i}{2} = -1 \pm i$.
Here, $\alpha = -1$ and $\omega = 1$.
Since $\alpha = -1$ is negative, the solution exhibits **damped oscillations**. The amplitude of the oscillations will decrease exponentially over time, described by the $e^{-x}$ factor in the general solution $y(x) = e^{-x}(C_1 \cos(x) + C_2 \sin(x))$.
