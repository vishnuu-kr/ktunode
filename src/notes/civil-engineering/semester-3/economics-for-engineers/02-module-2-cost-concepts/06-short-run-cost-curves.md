### 1. Introduction to Short-Run Costs

In economics, the **short run** is a period during which at least one factor of production is fixed. In a typical production process, capital (e.g., factory size, machinery) is often fixed in the short run, while labor and raw materials can be varied.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Total Cost (TC):** The total expenditure incurred by a firm to produce a given quantity of output.
*   **Fixed Costs (FC):** Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run. These are unavoidable costs even if production is zero.
*   **Variable Costs (VC):** Costs that vary directly with the level of output. If output increases, variable costs increase; if output decreases, variable costs decrease.

---

### 2. Breakdown of Short-Run Cost Components

#### 2.1. Total Cost (TC)

**Definition:** The sum of all costs, both fixed and variable, incurred in producing a given output.

**Formula:**
$TC = FC + VC$

**Example:** A manufacturing plant has fixed costs of $10,000 per month (rent, insurance, depreciation). If the variable cost of producing 100 units is $5,000, the total cost is $10,000 + $5,000 = $15,000.

#### 2.2. Fixed Cost (FC)

**Definition:** Costs that remain constant regardless of the output level.

**Characteristics:**
*   Incurred even at zero output.
*   Examples: Rent, salaries of permanent staff, insurance premiums, depreciation of machinery.

**Graphical Representation:**
A horizontal line, indicating that FC is constant at all output levels.

#### 2.3. Variable Cost (VC)

**Definition:** Costs that change directly with the level of output.

**Characteristics:**
*   Zero at zero output.
*   Increases as output increases.
*   Initially, VC increases at a decreasing rate (due to increasing efficiency), then at an increasing rate (due to diminishing marginal returns).
*   Examples: Raw materials, direct labor wages, energy costs for production.

**Graphical Representation:**
An upward-sloping curve that starts from the origin. Its shape reflects the law of diminishing marginal returns.

---

### 3. Short-Run Average Cost Curves

These curves represent costs per unit of output.

#### 3.1. Average Fixed Cost (AFC)

**Definition:** Fixed cost per unit of output.

**Formula:**
$AFC = FC / Q$ (where Q is the quantity of output)

**Characteristics:**
*   Always declines as output increases.
*   Approaches zero as output becomes very large, but never actually reaches zero.
*   Due to spreading fixed costs over a larger number of units.

**Graphical Representation:**
A downward-sloping curve that gets progressively flatter.

**Example:** If FC = $10,000:
*   At Q = 100 units, AFC = $10,000 / 100 = $100 per unit.
*   At Q = 1,000 units, AFC = $10,000 / 1,000 = $10 per unit.

#### 3.2. Average Variable Cost (AVC)

**Definition:** Variable cost per unit of output.

**Formula:**
$AVC = VC / Q$

**Characteristics:**
*   Typically U-shaped.
*   Initially declines as output increases due to increasing efficiency and specialization (spreading of fixed variable inputs).
*   Eventually rises as output increases due to diminishing marginal returns (overcrowding, strain on fixed capital).

**Graphical Representation:**
A U-shaped curve.

**Example:** Assume VC for producing Q units is given by $VC = 5Q + 0.1Q^2$.
*   At Q = 10 units, VC = $5(10) + 0.1(10)^2 = 50 + 10 = $60. AVC = $60 / 10 = $6 per unit.
*   At Q = 20 units, VC = $5(20) + 0.1(20)^2 = 100 + 40 = $140. AVC = $140 / 20 = $7 per unit.

#### 3.3. Average Total Cost (ATC)

**Definition:** Total cost per unit of output.

**Formula:**
$ATC = TC / Q$
$ATC = (FC + VC) / Q$
$ATC = AFC + AVC$

**Characteristics:**
*   Also U-shaped.
*   The vertical distance between ATC and AVC represents AFC, which narrows as output increases.
*   The minimum point of ATC occurs at a higher output level than the minimum point of AVC.

**Graphical Representation:**
A U-shaped curve, above the AVC curve.

**Example:** Using the previous examples (FC = $10,000, VC = $5Q + 0.1Q^2$):
*   At Q = 100 units:
    *   TC = $10,000 + (5(100) + 0.1(100)^2) = 10,000 + (500 + 1,000) = $11,500$.
    *   ATC = $11,500 / 100 = $115 per unit.
    *   Check: AFC = $10,000 / 100 = $100. AVC = ($500 + 1000) / 100 = $15. ATC = $100 + $15 = $115.

### 4. Short-Run Marginal Cost Curve

#### 4.1. Marginal Cost (MC)

**Definition:** The change in total cost resulting from producing one additional unit of output. It represents the cost of producing the last unit.

**Formula:**
$MC = \Delta TC / \Delta Q$
(Where $\Delta TC$ is the change in total cost and $\Delta Q$ is the change in quantity)

If costs are continuous, MC is the derivative of TC with respect to Q:
$MC = d(TC) / dQ$

**Characteristics:**
*   Typically U-shaped, but its minimum occurs at a lower output level than AVC and ATC.
*   MC initially falls as output increases because of increasing efficiency.
*   MC eventually rises due to diminishing marginal returns, as the cost of producing each additional unit starts to increase.
*   MC intersects AVC and ATC at their respective minimum points.

**Graphical Representation:**
A U-shaped curve.

**Example:** If TC changes from $15,000 for 100 units to $15,200 for 101 units:
*   MC = ($15,200 - $15,000) / (101 - 100) = $200 / 1 = $200.

Let's use the previous VC formula ($VC = 5Q + 0.1Q^2$) and assume FC = $10,000$.
$TC = 10,000 + 5Q + 0.1Q^2$
$MC = d(TC)/dQ = 0 + 5 + 2(0.1)Q = 5 + 0.2Q$

*   At Q = 10 units, MC = $5 + 0.2(10) = 5 + 2 = $7 per unit.
*   At Q = 20 units, MC = $5 + 0.2(20) = 5 + 4 = $9 per unit.

---

### 5. Relationships Between Short-Run Cost Curves

*   **MC and AVC:**
    *   When MC < AVC, AVC is falling.
    *   When MC > AVC, AVC is rising.
    *   MC intersects AVC at AVC's minimum point.
*   **MC and ATC:**
    *   When MC < ATC, ATC is falling.
    *   When MC > ATC, ATC is rising.
    *   MC intersects ATC at ATC's minimum point.
*   **AFC:** Always falls.
*   **ATC vs. AVC:** ATC is always above AVC, and the gap between them (AFC) narrows as output increases. ATC is minimized at a higher output than AVC.

---

### 6. Graphical Representation of Short-Run Cost Curves

**(Imagine a graph with Quantity (Q) on the horizontal axis and Cost ($) on the vertical axis)**

*   **FC:** Horizontal line.
*   **VC:** Upward sloping, starts at the origin, initially less steep than MC, then steeper.
*   **TC:** Upward sloping, starts at the level of FC (at Q=0), parallel to VC curve.
*   **AFC:** Downward sloping, convex to the origin.
*   **AVC:** U-shaped, reaches minimum at a lower Q than ATC.
*   **ATC:** U-shaped, always above AVC.
*   **MC:** U-shaped, intersects AVC and ATC at their minimum points. MC is steeper than AVC and ATC.

**Key Insight:** The shape of the variable and total cost curves, and thus the average and marginal cost curves, is determined by the **Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns**. Initially, adding more variable inputs (like labor) to fixed inputs (like machinery) leads to increasing returns (lower costs per unit). Eventually, however, adding more variable inputs leads to diminishing returns (higher costs per unit) as the fixed inputs become overcrowded or overutilized.

---

### 7. Applications in Engineering

Understanding short-run cost curves is crucial for engineers in various decision-making processes:

*   **Production Planning:** Determining the optimal output level to minimize average costs or meet demand efficiently.
*   **Capacity Utilization:** Evaluating how to best use existing fixed capacity.
*   **Cost-Benefit Analysis:** Assessing the cost implications of changes in production volume.
*   **Make-or-Buy Decisions:** Comparing the cost of producing a component in-house versus purchasing it from an external supplier.
*   **Process Improvement:** Identifying areas where variable costs can be reduced.
*   **Equipment Investment:** While this primarily relates to long-run decisions, understanding short-run cost behavior informs the economic justification for capital investments.

**Example:** An engineer managing a manufacturing plant needs to decide whether to increase production to meet a surge in demand. By analyzing the short-run cost curves, the engineer can determine:
*   If producing more units will push AVC and ATC to levels that make the increased production unprofitable.
*   The marginal cost of producing the additional units to ensure they are covered by the selling price.
*   The impact on overall profitability by understanding how fixed costs are spread over a larger output.

### 8. Practice Questions & Exercises

**Question 1:**
A firm has total fixed costs of $5,000. Its total variable costs at an output of 50 units are $10,000.
a) What is the total cost at an output of 50 units?
b) What is the average fixed cost at an output of 50 units?
c) What is the average variable cost at an output of 50 units?
d) What is the average total cost at an output of 50 units?

**Question 2:**
Suppose the total cost function for a firm is given by $TC = 100 + 10Q + 0.5Q^2$, where TC is total cost and Q is the quantity of output.
a) Identify the fixed cost, variable cost function, and the components of the variable cost function.
b) Derive the marginal cost (MC) function.
c) Calculate MC, AVC, and ATC when Q = 20 units.

**Question 3:**
Explain why the Marginal Cost (MC) curve intersects both the Average Variable Cost (AVC) and Average Total Cost (ATC) curves at their minimum points.

---

### 9. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
a) Total Cost (TC) = Fixed Cost (FC) + Variable Cost (VC)
TC = $5,000 + $10,000 = **$15,000**

b) Average Fixed Cost (AFC) = FC / Q
AFC = $5,000 / 50 = **$100** per unit

c) Average Variable Cost (AVC) = VC / Q
AVC = $10,000 / 50 = **$200** per unit

d) Average Total Cost (ATC) = TC / Q = AFC + AVC
ATC = $100 + $200 = **$300** per unit
Alternatively, ATC = $15,000 / 50 = $300 per unit

**Answer 2:**
a)
*   Fixed Cost (FC) = **$100** (the constant term in the TC function)
*   Variable Cost Function (VC) = **$10Q + 0.5Q^2$**
    *   The $10Q$ term represents variable costs that increase linearly with output.
    *   The $0.5Q^2$ term represents variable costs that increase with the square of output, reflecting diminishing marginal returns.

b) Marginal Cost (MC) is the derivative of TC with respect to Q:
$MC = d(TC)/dQ = d(100 + 10Q + 0.5Q^2)/dQ$
$MC = 0 + 10 + 2(0.5)Q$
$MC = **10 + Q$**

c) When Q = 20 units:
*   $MC = 10 + 20 = **$30**
*   $VC = 10(20) + 0.5(20)^2 = 200 + 0.5(400) = 200 + 200 = $400$
    $AVC = VC / Q = $400 / 20 = **$20**
*   $TC = 100 + 400 = $500$ (or $TC = 100 + 10(20) + 0.5(20)^2 = 100 + 200 + 200 = 500$)
    $ATC = TC / Q = $500 / 20 = **$25**
    (Check: ATC = AFC + AVC. AFC = $100 / 20 = $5. ATC = $5 + $20 = $25$. Correct!)

**Answer 3:**
The MC curve intersects the AVC and ATC curves at their minimum points because of the mathematical relationship between marginal and average values:

*   **When MC < AVC:** Adding an extra unit costs less than the current average variable cost. This pulls the average down. Therefore, AVC is falling.
*   **When MC > AVC:** Adding an extra unit costs more than the current average variable cost. This pulls the average up. Therefore, AVC is rising.
*   **At the minimum AVC:** The cost of the extra unit (MC) is exactly equal to the current average variable cost. This is the only point where the average is neither rising nor falling, hence it's the minimum.

The same logic applies to the relationship between MC and ATC. MC represents the cost of the "last unit," and ATC represents the average cost of "all units." MC will pull the average up or down until it reaches the point where the marginal cost equals the average cost, which is the minimum point for the average.

---

### 10. Important Points to Remember

*   **Short Run vs. Long Run:** The key differentiator is the presence of at least one fixed input in the short run.
*   **Fixed Costs are Irrelevant to Marginal Decisions:** In the short run, firms should focus on variable costs and marginal costs when making decisions about increasing or decreasing output. Fixed costs are sunk in the short run.
*   **Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns:** This is the fundamental principle driving the U-shapes of AVC, ATC, and MC curves.
*   **MC as the Driver:** MC dictates the changes in ATC and AVC.
*   **Graphical Intersections are Key:** MC intersects AVC and ATC at their lowest points. ATC is always above AVC. AFC is always declining.
