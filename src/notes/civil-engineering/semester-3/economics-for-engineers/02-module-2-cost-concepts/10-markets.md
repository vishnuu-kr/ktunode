# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS: Module 2 - Cost Concepts

## Topic: Markets

This module introduces fundamental market concepts crucial for engineers to understand the economic environment in which their projects and decisions operate. Understanding how prices are determined, how supply and demand interact, and the different market structures will enable engineers to make more informed and economically sound decisions.

*   **LO2: Illustrate the demand and supply curves and their graphical representation.**

### **1. Demand and Supply: The Foundation of Markets**

#### **1.1 Demand**

*   **Definition:** Demand refers to the **quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices during a specific period.** It's not just the desire for a product, but also the ability to pay for it.

*   **Law of Demand:** This fundamental law states that, **ceteris paribus (all other factors remaining constant), as the price of a good or service increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa.**

    *   **Why?**
        *   **Substitution Effect:** As the price of a good rises, consumers tend to switch to cheaper substitute goods.
        *   **Income Effect:** A higher price reduces the purchasing power of consumers, leading them to buy less.

*   **Demand Schedule:** A table that lists the quantities demanded of a good at different prices.

*   **Demand Curve:** A graphical representation of the demand schedule. It is typically downward-sloping, illustrating the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

    *   **Axes:**
        *   Vertical Axis: Price (P)
        *   Horizontal Axis: Quantity Demanded (Qd)

*   **Movement ALONG the Demand Curve vs. Shift of the Demand Curve:**
    *   **Movement Along:** A change in quantity demanded due to a change in the **price of the good itself.** This is represented by a movement from one point to another on the existing demand curve.
    *   **Shift Of:** A change in demand due to a change in a **non-price determinant of demand.** This is represented by a new demand curve shifting either to the right (increase in demand) or to the left (decrease in demand).

#### **1.2 Supply**

*   **Definition:** Supply refers to the **quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at various prices during a specific period.**

*   **Law of Supply:** This law states that, **ceteris paribus, as the price of a good or service increases, the quantity supplied increases, and vice versa.**

    *   **Why?** Higher prices incentivize producers to produce and sell more, as it generally leads to higher profits.

*   **Supply Schedule:** A table that lists the quantities supplied of a good at different prices.

*   **Supply Curve:** A graphical representation of the supply schedule. It is typically upward-sloping, illustrating the direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.

    *   **Axes:**
        *   Vertical Axis: Price (P)
        *   Horizontal Axis: Quantity Supplied (Qs)

*   **Movement ALONG the Supply Curve vs. Shift of the Supply Curve:**
    *   **Movement Along:** A change in quantity supplied due to a change in the **price of the good itself.** This is represented by a movement from one point to another on the existing supply curve.
    *   **Shift Of:** A change in supply due to a change in a **non-price determinant of supply.** This is represented by a new supply curve shifting either to the right (increase in supply) or to the left (decrease in supply).

### **2. Determinants of Demand and Supply (Factors Causing Shifts)**

#### **2.1 Determinants of Demand (Non-Price Factors)**

These factors, when they change, cause the entire demand curve to shift.

*   **Income:**
    *   **Normal Goods:** Demand increases as income rises (e.g., cars, vacations).
    *   **Inferior Goods:** Demand decreases as income rises (e.g., generic brands, public transportation).
*   **Prices of Related Goods:**
    *   **Substitutes:** Goods that can be used in place of each other. If the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good increases (e.g., if coffee prices rise, demand for tea might increase).
    *   **Complements:** Goods that are used together. If the price of a complement rises, demand for the original good decreases (e.g., if gasoline prices rise, demand for SUVs might decrease).
*   **Tastes and Preferences:** Changes in consumer preferences (e.g., due to advertising, trends, or health concerns) affect demand.
*   **Expectations:** Consumers' expectations about future prices or income can influence current demand (e.g., expecting a sale might decrease current demand).
*   **Number of Buyers:** An increase in the number of consumers in the market leads to an increase in demand.
*   **Demographics:** Changes in population size, age distribution, etc., can impact demand for certain goods and services.

#### **2.2 Determinants of Supply (Non-Price Factors)**

These factors, when they change, cause the entire supply curve to shift.

*   **Input Prices (Cost of Production):** An increase in the cost of raw materials, labor, or energy leads to a decrease in supply (shifts left).
*   **Technology:** Advancements in technology usually reduce production costs and increase supply (shifts right).
*   **Government Policies:**
    *   **Taxes:** An increase in taxes on production decreases supply (shifts left).
    *   **Subsidies:** A subsidy on production increases supply (shifts right).
    *   **Regulations:** Stricter regulations can increase production costs and decrease supply (shifts left).
*   **Expectations:** Producers' expectations about future prices can influence current supply decisions (e.g., expecting higher prices in the future might lead to withholding current supply).
*   **Number of Sellers:** An increase in the number of producers in the market leads to an increase in supply.
*   **Prices of Other Goods Produced by the Same Firm:** If a firm can produce other goods that are more profitable, it may shift resources away from producing the current good, decreasing its supply.

### **3. Market Equilibrium**

*   **Definition:** Market equilibrium occurs at the price where the **quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied.** This is the point where the demand and supply curves intersect.

*   **Equilibrium Price ($P^*$)**: The price at which Qd = Qs. Also known as the market-clearing price.
*   **Equilibrium Quantity ($Q^*$)**: The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price.

#### **3.1 Graphical Representation of Equilibrium**

*   The intersection of the downward-sloping demand curve and the upward-sloping supply curve determines the equilibrium price and quantity.

#### **3.2 Disequilibrium: Surplus and Shortage**

*   **Surplus (Excess Supply):** Occurs when the price is **above** the equilibrium price ($P > P^*$). At this higher price, Qs > Qd. Producers have unsold inventory and will likely lower prices to sell it, moving the market towards equilibrium.

*   **Shortage (Excess Demand):** Occurs when the price is **below** the equilibrium price ($P < P^*$). At this lower price, Qd > Qs. Consumers are willing to buy more than is available, leading to competition among buyers and putting upward pressure on prices, moving the market towards equilibrium.

### **4. Analyzing Changes in Equilibrium**

Changes in the determinants of demand or supply will cause the respective curves to shift, leading to a new equilibrium price and quantity.

*   **Increase in Demand (Shift Right):**
    *   **Effect:** Leads to a **higher equilibrium price** ($P^* \uparrow$) and a **higher equilibrium quantity** ($Q^* \uparrow$).
    *   **Example:** An increase in consumer income for a normal good.

*   **Decrease in Demand (Shift Left):**
    *   **Effect:** Leads to a **lower equilibrium price** ($P^* \downarrow$) and a **lower equilibrium quantity** ($Q^* \downarrow$).
    *   **Example:** A negative health report about a particular food.

*   **Increase in Supply (Shift Right):**
    *   **Effect:** Leads to a **lower equilibrium price** ($P^* \downarrow$) and a **higher equilibrium quantity** ($Q^* \uparrow$).
    *   **Example:** Technological improvement in production.

*   **Decrease in Supply (Shift Left):**
    *   **Effect:** Leads to a **higher equilibrium price** ($P^* \uparrow$) and a **lower equilibrium quantity** ($Q^* \downarrow$).
    *   **Example:** Increase in the cost of raw materials.

#### **4.1 Simultaneous Shifts**

When both demand and supply shift, the effect on price and quantity depends on the magnitude and direction of each shift.

*   **Example:** If demand increases and supply decreases, the equilibrium price will definitely rise, but the change in equilibrium quantity is ambiguous. It depends on whether the demand shift is larger than the supply shift.

### **5. Market Structures**

Market structure refers to the characteristics of a market, including the number of firms, the nature of the product, and the ease of entry and exit. These structures significantly influence pricing decisions, output levels, and profitability.

#### **5.1 Perfect Competition**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Many buyers and sellers:** No single buyer or seller can influence the market price.
    *   **Homogeneous (identical) products:** Products are perfect substitutes.
    *   **Free entry and exit:** Firms can easily enter or leave the market.
    *   **Perfect information:** Buyers and sellers have complete knowledge of prices and quality.
*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   Firms are **price takers**. They must accept the market price.
    *   Focus on **cost minimization** to maximize profit.
    *   Innovation is driven by the need to reduce costs or improve product quality slightly to gain a competitive edge, but radical innovation might not be rewarded as much if competitors can easily imitate.
    *   Example: Agricultural markets (e.g., wheat, corn, under ideal assumptions).

#### **5.2 Monopoly**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Single seller:** One firm controls the entire market supply.
    *   **Unique product with no close substitutes:** Consumers have no alternatives.
    *   **High barriers to entry:** Difficult for new firms to enter (e.g., patents, control of resources, government regulations).
*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   The firm is a **price maker**. It can set the price for its product.
    *   Focus on **product differentiation** and **exclusive technologies** (often protected by patents or R&D).
    *   Engineers may be involved in developing and protecting proprietary technologies.
    *   Pricing decisions are crucial to maximize profit, considering the demand curve.
    *   Example: A pharmaceutical company with a patent for a life-saving drug; a utility company with a natural monopoly.

#### **5.3 Monopolistic Competition**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Many buyers and sellers:** Similar to perfect competition.
    *   **Differentiated products:** Products are similar but not identical (e.g., branding, quality, features). This gives firms some degree of market power.
    *   **Relatively free entry and exit:** Barriers to entry are low.
*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   Focus on **product design, features, and branding** to differentiate from competitors.
    *   **Marketing and advertising** are important.
    *   Engineers play a key role in developing new features and improving product quality to attract customers.
    *   Pricing is less constrained than in perfect competition but more constrained than in monopoly.
    *   Example: Restaurants, clothing stores, software applications.

#### **5.4 Oligopoly**

*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Few dominant sellers:** A small number of firms control a large portion of the market.
    *   **Products can be identical or differentiated.**
    *   **Significant barriers to entry:** High costs or established brand loyalty make entry difficult.
    *   **Interdependence:** Firms' decisions are highly interdependent; one firm's actions significantly affect others.
*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   **Strategic decision-making** is critical, considering competitors' likely reactions.
    *   **Collusion** (forming cartels) to fix prices or output is possible but often illegal.
    *   **Non-price competition** (advertising, product development) is common.
    *   Engineers might be involved in developing new technologies or product features that give a firm a temporary advantage, anticipating competitor responses.
    *   Example: Automobile industry, airline industry, telecommunications.

### **6. Elasticity**

Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one variable to a change in another variable.

#### **6.1 Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)**

*   **Definition:** Measures how much the **quantity demanded** of a good changes in response to a **one percent change in its price.**

*   **Formula:**
    $PED = \frac{\% \Delta Qd}{\% \Delta P}$

*   **Interpretation:**
    *   **Elastic Demand (|PED| > 1):** Quantity demanded is highly responsive to price changes. A small price change leads to a large change in quantity demanded. (e.g., luxury goods, goods with many substitutes).
    *   **Inelastic Demand (|PED| < 1):** Quantity demanded is not very responsive to price changes. A large price change leads to a small change in quantity demanded. (e.g., necessities, goods with few substitutes, addictive goods).
    *   **Unit Elastic Demand (|PED| = 1):** The percentage change in quantity demanded is equal to the percentage change in price.
    *   **Perfectly Elastic Demand (PED = -∞):** Consumers will buy an infinite amount at a specific price but nothing at a higher price.
    *   **Perfectly Inelastic Demand (PED = 0):** Quantity demanded does not change regardless of price.

*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   Understanding PED helps in **forecasting sales** and revenue changes based on price adjustments.
    *   For products with elastic demand, price increases can lead to significant revenue decreases. Engineers might focus on cost reduction to maintain profitability.
    *   For products with inelastic demand, price increases can lead to revenue increases. Engineers might focus on quality and features that make the product more essential.

#### **6.2 Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)**

*   **Definition:** Measures how much the **quantity supplied** of a good changes in response to a **one percent change in its price.**

*   **Formula:**
    $PES = \frac{\% \Delta Qs}{\% \Delta P}$

*   **Interpretation:**
    *   **Elastic Supply (PES > 1):** Producers are responsive to price changes.
    *   **Inelastic Supply (PES < 1):** Producers are not very responsive to price changes.
    *   **Unit Elastic Supply (PES = 1):** Percentage change in quantity supplied equals percentage change in price.

*   **Determinants of PES:**
    *   **Time Horizon:** Supply is generally more elastic in the long run than in the short run, as producers have more time to adjust production levels.
    *   **Availability of Inputs:** If inputs are readily available, supply is more elastic.
    *   **Flexibility of Production Process:** If production can be easily scaled up or down, supply is more elastic.

*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   Understanding PES is important for **capacity planning and production scheduling**.
    *   If supply is inelastic, sudden increases in demand can lead to significant price spikes. Engineers might focus on improving production flexibility.

#### **6.3 Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (CPED)**

*   **Definition:** Measures how the **quantity demanded** of one good changes in response to a **one percent change in the price of another good.**

*   **Formula:**
    $CPED = \frac{\% \Delta Qd_A}{\% \Delta P_B}$

*   **Interpretation:**
    *   **Substitutes (CPED > 0):** If the price of good B increases, the demand for good A increases (e.g., Coke and Pepsi).
    *   **Complements (CPED < 0):** If the price of good B increases, the demand for good A decreases (e.g., cars and gasoline).
    *   **Unrelated Goods (CPED = 0):** The price of good B has no effect on the demand for good A.

*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   Helps understand how **competitors' pricing strategies** or the pricing of **complementary products** can impact the demand for a firm's own products.
    *   Engineers designing product bundles might consider the CPED of different products.

#### **6.4 Income Elasticity of Demand (IED)**

*   **Definition:** Measures how the **quantity demanded** of a good changes in response to a **one percent change in consumers' income.**

*   **Formula:**
    $IED = \frac{\% \Delta Qd}{\% \Delta Income}$

*   **Interpretation:**
    *   **Normal Good (IED > 0):** Demand increases as income increases.
    *   **Inferior Good (IED < 0):** Demand decreases as income increases.

*   **Implications for Engineers:**
    *   Helps forecast demand based on **macroeconomic trends and income growth**.
    *   Crucial for planning production and marketing strategies for products expected to be consumed by specific income groups.

### **Key Concepts to Remember**

*   **Ceteris Paribus:** A crucial assumption meaning "all other things being equal."
*   **Law of Demand:** Inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.
*   **Law of Supply:** Direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.
*   **Equilibrium:** Where demand equals supply, resulting in the market-clearing price and quantity.
*   **Surplus:** Price above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.
*   **Shortage:** Price below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
*   **Shifts vs. Movements:** Distinguish between changes in quantity demanded/supplied (movements along the curve due to price change) and changes in demand/supply (shifts of the curve due to non-price factors).
*   **Market Structures:** Perfect Competition, Monopoly, Monopolistic Competition, Oligopoly – each has unique implications for pricing, output, and innovation.
*   **Elasticity:** Measures responsiveness. PED, PES, CPED, and IED are vital for economic analysis and decision-making.

### **Practice Questions & Exercises**

**Section 1: Demand and Supply**

1.  **Question:** If the price of gasoline increases, what is likely to happen to the demand for SUVs? Explain your reasoning using the concept of complementary goods.
    **Answer:** The demand for SUVs is likely to decrease. SUVs are often complementary to gasoline, as they consume a significant amount of fuel. When the price of gasoline rises, the cost of operating an SUV increases, making it less attractive to consumers. This would cause a leftward shift in the demand curve for SUVs.

2.  **Question:** Suppose a heatwave causes a surge in demand for ice cream. Describe what happens in the ice cream market. Will there be a surplus or a shortage at the original equilibrium price? How will the market adjust to a new equilibrium?
    **Answer:** A surge in demand will cause the demand curve for ice cream to shift to the right. At the original equilibrium price, there will be a **shortage** because the quantity demanded will now exceed the quantity supplied. As consumers compete for the limited supply, the price of ice cream will rise. This higher price will incentivize producers to supply more (movement up the supply curve) and discourage some consumers from buying as much (movement up the demand curve). The market will adjust to a new equilibrium with a higher price and a higher quantity of ice cream sold.

**Section 2: Elasticity**

3.  **Question:** A company is considering raising the price of its new widget. Market research indicates that the price elasticity of demand for widgets is -0.5. Is demand for widgets elastic or inelastic? If the company raises the price, what will happen to its total revenue?
    **Answer:** The price elasticity of demand is -0.5. Since the absolute value of PED is less than 1 (|-0.5| < 1), demand for widgets is **inelastic**. If the company raises the price, total revenue will **increase**. With inelastic demand, a price increase leads to a proportionally smaller decrease in quantity demanded, resulting in higher total revenue.

4.  **Question:** The price of coffee beans increases significantly due to bad weather in producing countries. What is likely to happen to the price elasticity of supply for coffee in the short run compared to the long run? Explain why.
    **Answer:** The price elasticity of supply for coffee is likely to be **inelastic in the short run** and **more elastic in the long run**.
    *   **Short Run:** In the short run, coffee farmers have limited ability to increase production in response to higher prices. Factors like the time it takes for coffee plants to mature and existing harvesting capacity limit how quickly supply can respond.
    *   **Long Run:** In the long run, farmers have more time to plant new coffee trees, invest in better equipment, and adjust their production methods. This increased flexibility allows for a larger response to price changes, making supply more elastic.

**Section 3: Market Structures**

5.  **Question:** A firm operates in a market with many competitors, selling a product that is very similar but not identical to its rivals' products. Entry into the market is relatively easy. Which market structure does this describe, and what is the primary focus for engineers in such a market?
    **Answer:** This describes **monopolistic competition**. The primary focus for engineers in this market structure is on **product differentiation**. This can involve developing new features, improving quality, focusing on design, or contributing to branding and marketing efforts to make the product stand out from competitors and attract customers.

**Section 4: Practice Problem**

6.  **Scenario:** Consider the market for smartphones.
    *   **Initial Equilibrium:** Assume the initial equilibrium price is \$500 and the equilibrium quantity is 10 million units.
    *   **Event 1:** A new technological breakthrough makes production cheaper and faster.
    *   **Event 2:** Consumer income rises, and smartphones are considered normal goods.
    *   **Analyze:** What will happen to the equilibrium price and quantity after each event, assuming the other factor remains constant? Then, analyze what might happen if both events occur simultaneously.

    **Answer:**

    *   **Event 1 (Cheaper Production - Increase in Supply):** The supply curve will shift to the right. This will lead to a **lower equilibrium price** and a **higher equilibrium quantity**.
    *   **Event 2 (Higher Income - Increase in Demand):** The demand curve will shift to the right. This will lead to a **higher equilibrium price** and a **higher equilibrium quantity**.

    *   **Simultaneous Events (Increase in Supply and Increase in Demand):**
        *   The effect on **equilibrium quantity** will be **unambiguously higher** because both events increase quantity demanded/supplied.
        *   The effect on **equilibrium price** is **ambiguous**. The increase in supply pushes prices down, while the increase in demand pushes prices up. The final change in price depends on the magnitude of the shifts. If the increase in demand is larger than the increase in supply, the price will rise. If the increase in supply is larger than the increase in demand, the price will fall. If they are of equal magnitude (in terms of their price impact), the price might remain unchanged.

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **Understanding demand and supply is fundamental to all economic analysis.**
*   **Changes in non-price factors are what cause curves to shift**, leading to new market equilibria.
*   **Market structure matters greatly.** Engineers need to be aware of the competitive landscape when making decisions.
*   **Elasticity is a powerful tool for forecasting** the impact of price changes on revenue and the impact of economic changes on quantities.
*   **Engineers' roles extend beyond technical design; they are often involved in cost management, product development, and strategic planning within market constraints.**
