# Surveying & Geomatics - Module 1: Introduction to Surveying

## Topic: Methods of Orientation (by Compass and by Back Sighting)

---

### 1. Introduction to Orientation

**Definition:** Orientation in surveying refers to the process of establishing the direction of survey lines relative to a known direction or datum. It's crucial for correctly placing survey points on a map or plan and for ensuring the accuracy and consistency of the entire survey.

**Why is Orientation Important?**

*   **Establishing Reference:** To fix the position of a survey traverse or network on a map or in relation to known features.
*   **Accuracy:** To ensure that all measurements are taken with respect to a consistent direction, preventing systematic errors.
*   **Re-establishment:** To enable the re-establishment of survey lines if they are lost or disturbed.
*   **Understanding Relationships:** To understand the spatial relationships between different points and lines in a survey.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Meridian:** An imaginary line passing through the geographic North and South poles, used as a reference for direction.
*   **True Meridian:** The line connecting the geographic North and South poles.
*   **Magnetic Meridian:** The direction indicated by the magnetic needle of a compass.
*   **Arbitrary Meridian:** A meridian established for a local survey, often parallel to a significant feature like a road or railway.
*   **Bearing:** The horizontal angle measured clockwise from a meridian (North or South) to a survey line.
*   **Azimuth:** The horizontal angle measured clockwise from a meridian (usually North) to a survey line, ranging from 0° to 360°.
*   **Back Bearing:** The bearing of a line from its second point to its first point, which is always 180° different from the forward bearing.

### 2. Methods of Orientation

There are several methods to orient a survey, but this topic focuses on two fundamental techniques: **Orientation by Compass** and **Orientation by Back Sighting**.

#### 2.1. Orientation by Compass

This method utilizes a magnetic compass to establish a reference direction.

**2.1.1. Principle:**
The magnetic needle of a compass aligns itself with the Earth's magnetic field, pointing towards magnetic north. By taking readings of magnetic bearings of known lines or by establishing the direction of a magnetic meridian, the entire survey can be oriented.

**2.1.2. Key Components and Instruments:**

*   **Magnetic Compass:**
    *   **Graduated Circle (or Limb):** Marked with degrees from 0° to 360° or N-E-S-W quadrants.
    *   **Magnetic Needle:** A magnetized pointer that rotates freely on a pivot.
    *   **Sight Vanes (or Alidades):** Used for sighting points.
    *   **Mirror:** To read the bearing on the graduated circle.
*   **Object:** A well-defined point or feature whose direction from the compass station is to be determined.

**2.1.3. Procedures:**

**a) Orientation using a Known Bearing (e.g., Magnetic North):**

1.  **Set up the Compass:** Place the compass accurately over a known point (station).
2.  **Level the Compass:** Ensure the compass is perfectly horizontal.
3.  **Allow Needle to Settle:** Let the magnetic needle come to rest.
4.  **Rotate the Compass Box:** Rotate the graduated circle (or compass box) until the 'N' or 0° mark aligns with the magnetic needle. This establishes the magnetic meridian through the station.
5.  **Sight the Object:** While holding the compass box stationary, look through the sight vanes and sight the next point (object).
6.  **Read the Bearing:** Read the bearing of the line of sight on the graduated circle. This is the magnetic bearing of the line from the current station to the sighted object.

**Example:**
If you are at station A, and you sight station B, and after aligning the compass box with magnetic north, you read 45° on the graduated circle, then the magnetic bearing of AB is N 45° E.

**b) Orientation of a Known Line (Magnetic Traverse):**

If you already know the magnetic bearing of a previous line (e.g., the line from the previous station to the current station), you can use this to orient the compass to establish subsequent bearings.

1.  **Set up the Compass:** Place the compass at the current station.
2.  **Level the Compass:** Ensure it is horizontal.
3.  **Known Back Bearing:** Assume the bearing of the line from the previous station (say, station X) to the current station (station Y) is N 30° E. This means the back bearing from Y to X is S 30° W (or 180° + 30° = 210°).
4.  **Align with Back Bearing:** Rotate the compass box so that the back bearing (S 30° W) on the graduated circle aligns with the magnetic needle.
5.  **Sight the Next Object:** Sight the next station (station Z).
6.  **Read the Forward Bearing:** The reading on the graduated circle at the forward sight vane will be the magnetic bearing of the line YZ.

**Important Note on Magnetic Deviation:**
Magnetic meridians are not always the same as true meridians due to magnetic declination (the angle between magnetic north and true north). For accurate mapping, this declination must be accounted for. However, for relative orientation and establishing directions within a survey, magnetic bearings are often sufficient.

**2.1.4. Advantages of Compass Orientation:**

*   **Simplicity:** Relatively easy to understand and perform.
*   **Speed:** Can be quicker for initial reconnaissance or simple traverses.
*   **No Prior Known Lines Needed:** Can be used to establish the initial direction of a survey.

**2.1.5. Disadvantages of Compass Orientation:**

*   **Magnetic Interference:** Affected by local magnetic anomalies (iron deposits, steel structures, electrical currents).
*   **Accuracy Limitations:** Generally less accurate than other methods, especially for long lines or precise work.
*   **Declination Issues:** Requires correction for magnetic declination if true bearings are needed.

#### 2.2. Orientation by Back Sighting

This method relies on having a previously established and known line within the survey to orient the instrument for subsequent observations. It's a fundamental technique in traversing.

**2.2.1. Principle:**
When traversing, after moving from station A to station B and taking a forward sight to B, the instrument at B can be rotated by 180° to face back towards A. By aligning the instrument's line of sight with the line AB (using the previously established bearing or direction), the instrument is oriented. Subsequent sights taken from B to other stations will then have their bearings correctly referenced to the established meridian.

**2.2.2. Key Concepts and Instruments:**

*   **Total Station/Theodolite:** Modern instruments for measuring horizontal and vertical angles.
*   **Prism/Reflector:** Used to reflect the laser beam from the total station back to the instrument.
*   **Known Line:** A survey line whose direction (azimuth or bearing) has already been determined and is often referenced to a known meridian or coordinate system.
*   **Back Bearing/Back Azimuth:** The bearing or azimuth of a line when viewed from the second point to the first point. If the forward bearing of AB is $\beta$, the back bearing of BA is $\beta \pm 180°$.

**2.2.3. Procedures:**

**a) Traversing using known Forward Bearings:**

1.  **Set up at Station A:** Set up the total station/theodolite over station A.
2.  **Sight Station B:** Sight station B (using a prism or by direct observation for a theodolite) and measure the horizontal angle and distance. Record the forward bearing of AB.
3.  **Move to Station B:** Move the instrument to station B and set it up accurately.
4.  **Back Sight to Station A:**
    *   **Theodolite:** Rotate the telescope 180° (vertically) to face towards A. Sight station A. Adjust the lower plate (or use a set zero function) so that the reading on the horizontal circle corresponds to the back bearing of AB (e.g., if AB bearing was N 45° E, the back bearing BA is S 45° W or 225°). This step orients the instrument.
    *   **Total Station:** Sight station A (usually a dedicated back sight or with a prism). The instrument is typically programmed to automatically calculate the back azimuth based on the previously established line. If the known bearing of AB is already in the system, sighting A from B will re-orient the instrument to that established direction.
5.  **Sight Station C:** With the instrument oriented, sight station C and measure the horizontal angle and distance. The instrument directly displays the bearing of BC relative to the established meridian.

**b) Orientation when the Meridian is Unknown, but a Known Line Exists:**

If you don't have a direct magnetic north reading, but you know the bearing of a line (e.g., line PQ) which is part of your survey or a known datum, you can use this to orient your instrument.

1.  **Set up at Station A:** Set up the total station/theodolite over station A.
2.  **Sight Station B:** Measure the bearing/azimuth of AB and record it.
3.  **Move to Station B:** Set up the instrument at station B.
4.  **Orient to AB:** Sight station A. Ensure the instrument's horizontal circle reads the back bearing/azimuth of AB. This aligns the instrument's reference direction with the established direction of AB.
5.  **Sight Station C:** Sight station C and measure the bearing/azimuth of BC. This bearing is now relative to the same meridian as AB.

**Example:**
Suppose you are traversing and have established the azimuth of line AB as 70°. You move to station B.
1. Set up at B.
2. Sight A.
3. Set the horizontal circle reading to 70° + 180° = 250° (back azimuth of AB).
4. Now sight station C. The reading you get from the instrument will be the azimuth of BC, correctly oriented relative to the same reference direction that gave AB an azimuth of 70°.

**2.2.4. Advantages of Back Sighting Orientation:**

*   **High Accuracy:** Significantly more accurate than compass orientation, as it relies on precise angle measurements.
*   **Consistency:** Maintains directional consistency throughout a traverse.
*   **Independent of Magnetic Fields:** Not affected by local magnetic anomalies.
*   **Foundation for Georeferencing:** Essential for establishing local coordinate systems that can later be tied to national or global systems.

**2.2.5. Disadvantages of Back Sighting Orientation:**

*   **Requires a Known Line:** Cannot be used for the very first line of a survey unless a known bearing is provided.
*   **Instrumental Errors:** Susceptible to instrumental errors if not properly calibrated or used.
*   **More Complex Setup:** Requires careful setup and precise sighting.

### 3. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
What is the primary purpose of orienting a survey?
a) To measure the elevation of points.
b) To establish the direction of survey lines relative to a known reference.
c) To calculate the area of a land parcel.
d) To determine the slope of the ground.

**Question 2:**
Which of the following is a potential source of error when using a magnetic compass for orientation?
a) Poor leveling of the instrument.
b) Local magnetic anomalies.
c) Incorrect back sighting.
d) Unstable instrument setup.

**Question 3:**
If the magnetic bearing of a line AB is N 50° E, what is the magnetic back bearing of line BA?
a) N 50° W
b) S 50° E
c) S 50° W
d) N 40° W

**Question 4:**
A surveyor is using a total station. After setting up at station P and sighting station Q, the instrument records the azimuth of PQ as 120°. The surveyor then moves to station Q. To orient the instrument at Q for subsequent measurements, what should the surveyor do with the back sight to P?
a) Align the instrument to read 120° on the horizontal circle.
b) Align the instrument to read 120° + 180° = 300° on the horizontal circle.
c) Sight P and then sight R, ignoring the back sight.
d) Ignore station P and directly sight station R.

**Question 5:**
Explain the difference between orientation by compass and orientation by back sighting in terms of accuracy and reliance on external factors.

---

### 4. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
b) To establish the direction of survey lines relative to a known reference.

**Answer 2:**
b) Local magnetic anomalies.

**Answer 3:**
c) S 50° W
*Explanation: The back bearing is always 180° different from the forward bearing. If the forward bearing is N 50° E, then 180° away in the opposite direction is S 50° W.*

**Answer 4:**
b) Align the instrument to read 120° + 180° = 300° on the horizontal circle.
*Explanation: The back azimuth of PQ is the azimuth of QP, which is obtained by adding 180° to the azimuth of PQ. This sets the instrument's orientation relative to the established direction of PQ.*

**Answer 5:**
*   **Accuracy:**
    *   **Compass Orientation:** Generally less accurate. It is affected by magnetic declination and local magnetic disturbances.
    *   **Back Sighting Orientation:** Highly accurate. It relies on precise angular measurements from a previously established line and is independent of magnetic fields.
*   **Reliance on External Factors:**
    *   **Compass Orientation:** Relies on the Earth's magnetic field and requires careful consideration of magnetic declination and potential magnetic interference.
    *   **Back Sighting Orientation:** Relies on the accuracy of the initial survey line and the precision of the surveying instrument. Once a line is established, it provides a stable, internal reference for subsequent measurements.

---

### 5. Important Points to Remember

*   **Orientation is fundamental:** It provides the directional control for all survey measurements.
*   **Meridians are key:** Whether magnetic, true, or arbitrary, a meridian acts as the reference line for bearings and azimuths.
*   **Compass orientation is convenient but less precise:** Be aware of its limitations due to magnetic interference.
*   **Back sighting is the preferred method for accurate traversing:** It ensures directional consistency and avoids magnetic influences.
*   **Back bearing/azimuth is always 180° from the forward bearing/azimuth.**
*   **Always check and double-check your orientation:** Errors in orientation can propagate throughout the entire survey.
*   **Modern instruments (Total Stations) often automate back-sighting and orientation:** However, understanding the underlying principles is crucial for troubleshooting and interpreting results.
