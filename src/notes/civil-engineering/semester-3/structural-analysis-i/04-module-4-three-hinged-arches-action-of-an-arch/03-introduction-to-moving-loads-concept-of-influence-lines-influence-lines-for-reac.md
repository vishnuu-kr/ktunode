# Structural Analysis - I: Module 4 - Three Hinged Arches (Action of an Arch)

## Topic: Introduction to Moving Loads & Influence Lines

This module delves into the behavior of structures subjected to loads that change their position. We will explore the powerful concept of influence lines, which are essential tools for understanding how varying load positions affect internal forces and reactions.

---

### 1. Introduction to Moving Loads

**Concept:**
*   **Moving Loads:** Loads that are not fixed in position but can traverse across a structure. Examples include vehicles on bridges, cranes on gantry girders, or even the weight of a train.
*   **Significance:** The effect of a moving load on a structure (reactions, shear forces, bending moments) depends on its position. To design a structure to withstand the worst possible effects, we need to determine the critical positions of these moving loads.

---

### 2. Concept of Influence Lines

**Definition:**
*   An **Influence Line (IL)** is a diagram that shows the variation of a particular function (reaction, shear force, bending moment, etc.) at a fixed point in a structure as a unit load moves across the structure.
*   The ordinates of the influence line represent the value of the function at that fixed point when a unit load is placed at the corresponding position on the structure.

**Key Principles:**
*   **Unit Load:** An influence line is generated by moving a load of **unity (1)** across the span.
*   **Fixed Point:** The influence line is plotted for a specific point in the structure.
*   **Varied Position:** The influence line shows how the function at that fixed point changes as the unit load's position changes.

**Applications:**
*   **Determining Critical Load Positions:** Influence lines quickly identify where a moving load (or multiple loads) will produce the maximum or minimum value of a specific internal force or reaction.
*   **Analyzing Multiple Concentrated Loads:** They simplify the analysis of multiple moving loads by allowing us to calculate the total effect by summing the contributions of each load at its respective position on the influence line.
*   **Analyzing Uniformly Distributed Loads:** They help determine the maximum effect of a UDL, especially when its length is variable or less than the span.

---

### 3. Influence Lines for Simply Supported Beams

A simply supported beam is a beam supported at its ends, allowing free rotation but preventing vertical displacement.

#### 3.1. Influence Lines for Reactions

**Reactions:** The upward forces exerted by the supports to maintain equilibrium.

**Procedure:**
1.  Assume a unit load moving from one end (A) to the other (B).
2.  At each position of the unit load, determine the vertical reaction at the support for which the IL is being drawn.
3.  Plot these reaction values against the position of the unit load.

**Example: Simply Supported Beam of Span L**

Let the beam be supported at A and B, with span L. We want to draw the IL for the vertical reaction at support A ($R_A$).

*   **Load at A (x=0):** $R_A = 1$ (unit load is on support A, so it contributes fully to $R_A$).
*   **Load at B (x=L):** $R_A = 0$ (unit load is on support B, so it contributes zero to $R_A$).
*   **Load at a distance 'x' from A:** Using equilibrium, $\Sigma M_B = 0$, we get $R_A \times L - 1 \times (L-x) = 0$, so $R_A = \frac{L-x}{L} = 1 - \frac{x}{L}$.

**Shape of IL for $R_A$:**
*   Starts at 1.0 directly above support A.
*   Linearly decreases to 0.0 directly above support B.
*   The ordinate at any point 'x' represents the value of $R_A$ when a unit load is at 'x'.

**Shape of IL for $R_B$:**
*   Symmetrical to the IL for $R_A$.
*   Starts at 0.0 above support A and linearly increases to 1.0 above support B.
*   The ordinate at any point 'x' represents the value of $R_B$ when a unit load is at 'x'.

**Calculating Reaction for a Moving Load:**
If a system of moving loads is applied, the total reaction at a support is the sum of the products of each load and the corresponding ordinate of the influence line at its position.

$$R_A = \sum (W_i \times y_{R_A, i})$$
where $W_i$ is the magnitude of the $i$-th load, and $y_{R_A, i}$ is the ordinate of the IL for $R_A$ at the position of $W_i$.

#### 3.2. Influence Lines for Shear Force

**Shear Force (V):** The algebraic sum of the vertical forces to the left or right of a section.

**Convention:**
*   Shear is positive if it tends to rotate the section clockwise.
*   When drawing IL for shear at section C, we consider a unit load to the left of C and a unit load to the right of C.

**Procedure:**
1.  Consider a section at a fixed point C.
2.  Move a unit load from one end to the other.
3.  Calculate the shear force at section C for each position of the unit load.
    *   When the unit load is to the **left** of C: $V_C = R_A - 1$.
    *   When the unit load is to the **right** of C: $V_C = R_A$.
4.  Plot these shear values against the position of the unit load.

**Example: Simply Supported Beam of Span L, Section C at distance 'a' from A**

*   **IL for Shear at C (looking from left):**
    *   Unit load between A and C (0 to 'a'): $V_C = R_A$. IL for $R_A$ is $1 - x/L$. So, $V_C = 1 - x/L$.
    *   At C (x=a): $V_C = R_A$ (just to the left of C). $V_C = 1 - a/L$.
    *   Unit load between C and B ('a' to L): $V_C = R_A - 1$. IL for $R_A$ is $1 - x/L$. So, $V_C = (1 - x/L) - 1 = -x/L$.
    *   At B (x=L): $V_C = R_A - 1 = 0 - 1 = -1$.

**Shape of IL for $V_C$:**
*   Starts at 0 above support A.
*   Linearly increases to $1 - a/L$ at section C (from the left).
*   There is a sudden drop of 1.0 at section C.
*   Linearly decreases from $-a/L$ above section C to $-1.0$ above support B.
*   The ordinate at any point 'x' represents the shear at C when a unit load is at 'x'.

**Calculating Shear for a Moving Load:**
$$V_C = \sum (W_i \times y_{V_C, i})$$
where $y_{V_C, i}$ is the ordinate of the IL for $V_C$ at the position of $W_i$.

#### 3.3. Influence Lines for Bending Moment

**Bending Moment (M):** The internal moment at a section due to external forces.

**Procedure:**
1.  Consider a section at a fixed point C.
2.  Move a unit load from one end to the other.
3.  Calculate the bending moment at section C for each position of the unit load.
4.  Plot these moment values against the position of the unit load.

**Example: Simply Supported Beam of Span L, Section C at distance 'a' from A**

*   **Load between A and C (0 to 'a'):** The bending moment at C is due to the unit load. $M_C = 1 \times (a)$.
*   **Load between C and B ('a' to L):** The bending moment at C is due to the reaction $R_A$. $M_C = R_A \times a$. We know $R_A = \frac{L-x}{L}$ when the load is at 'x'. So, $M_C = \frac{L-x}{L} \times a$.
*   At A (x=0): $M_C = 0$ (load is not on the beam to cause moment at C).
*   At C (x=a): $M_C = 1 \times a$.
*   At B (x=L): $M_C = R_A \times a = \frac{L-L}{L} \times a = 0$.

**Shape of IL for $M_C$:**
*   Starts at 0 above support A.
*   Linearly increases to 'a' at section C.
*   Linearly decreases from 'a' at section C to 0 above support B.
*   The shape is a triangle with the peak ordinate 'a' at section C.

**Calculating Bending Moment for a Moving Load:**
$$M_C = \sum (W_i \times y_{M_C, i})$$
where $y_{M_C, i}$ is the ordinate of the IL for $M_C$ at the position of $W_i$.

### 4. Influence Lines for Overhanging Beams

An overhanging beam is a beam with one or both ends extending beyond the supports.

**Key Differences from Simply Supported Beams:**
*   **Negative Ordinates:** Influence lines for overhanging beams can have negative ordinates, indicating that a load in that region causes a moment or shear of opposite sign compared to a load within the main span.
*   **Load in the Overhang:** A load placed in the overhang can significantly affect the forces and moments at points within the main span.

**Procedure (General):**
The procedure for drawing influence lines for reactions, shear forces, and bending moments is the same as for simply supported beams, but the calculations will account for the overhang. We use equilibrium equations and the principle of superposition.

**Example: Beam with a Single Overhang**

Consider a beam supported at A and B, with an overhang from B to C. Let span AB = L and overhang BC = l.

**IL for Reaction $R_A$:**
*   Unit load at A (x=0): $R_A = 1$.
*   Unit load at B (x=L): $R_A = 0$.
*   Unit load at C (x=L+l): $\Sigma M_B = 0 \Rightarrow R_A \times L - 1 \times l = 0 \Rightarrow R_A = l/L$.
*   For a load at 'x' between B and C: $R_A \times L - 1 \times (x-L) = 0 \Rightarrow R_A = (x-L)/L$.

**Shape of IL for $R_A$:**
*   Starts at 1.0 above A.
*   Linearly decreases to 0.0 above B.
*   Linearly increases to $l/L$ above C.

**IL for Reaction $R_B$:**
*   Unit load at A (x=0): $R_B = 0$.
*   Unit load at B (x=L): $R_B = 1$.
*   Unit load at C (x=L+l): $\Sigma M_A = 0 \Rightarrow R_B \times L - 1 \times (L+l) = 0 \Rightarrow R_B = (L+l)/L = 1 + l/L$.
*   For a load at 'x' between A and B: $R_B = 1 - R_A = 1 - (1-x/L) = x/L$.
*   For a load at 'x' between B and C: $R_B = 1 - R_A = 1 - (x-L)/L$.

**Shape of IL for $R_B$:**
*   Starts at 0.0 above A.
*   Linearly increases to 1.0 above B.
*   Linearly increases further to $(1+l/L)$ above C.

**IL for Shear at a section C' within the overhang (distance 'y' from C):**
*   Unit load between A and B: $V_{C'} = R_A$.
*   Unit load between B and C: $V_{C'} = R_A - R_B$.
*   Unit load in the overhang, to the right of C': $V_{C'} = R_A - R_B - 1$.

**IL for Bending Moment at a section C' within the overhang (distance 'y' from C):**
*   The bending moment at C' depends on the forces and moments to its left or right.
*   For a load between A and B: $M_{C'} = R_A \times (L+y)$.
*   For a load between B and C: $M_{C'} = R_A \times (L+y) - R_B \times (L+y-L)$.
*   For a load in the overhang, to the right of C': $M_{C'} = R_A \times (L+y) - R_B \times (L+y-L) - 1 \times (y)$.

**General Shape for Bending Moment in Overhangs:**
*   IL for bending moment at a section in the overhang typically has a parabolic shape if the loads causing it are distributed, or a combination of linear segments if the loads are concentrated.
*   A key feature is that a load in the overhang can cause a *negative* moment at a support.

### 5. Analysis for Different Types of Moving Loads

#### 5.1. Single Concentrated Load

*   **Procedure:** Place the unit load at various positions on the IL for the desired function (reaction, shear, moment). The ordinate of the IL at that position gives the value of the function for a unit load. Multiply this ordinate by the magnitude of the actual concentrated load.
*   **Maximum Effect:** To find the maximum positive or negative effect, identify the highest positive or lowest negative ordinate on the IL and multiply it by the load magnitude.

#### 5.2. Several Concentrated Loads

*   **Procedure:** This is where ILs are most powerful.
    1.  Draw the IL for the function (e.g., $R_A$).
    2.  Position the system of loads on the structure such that the desired effect is maximized. This generally involves trial and error or specific rules related to the IL shape.
    3.  For each load $W_i$ in the system, find its position on the IL and determine the corresponding ordinate $y_i$.
    4.  The total effect is the sum of the contributions of each load:
        $$Effect = \sum (W_i \times y_i)$$

*   **Rule for Maximum:** For a series of concentrated loads, to maximize a function (like bending moment), position the series such that the resultant of the loads is at the point of maximum ordinate of the IL, or distribute the loads around the maximum ordinate.

#### 5.3. Uniformly Distributed Load (UDL)

*   **Shorter than the Span (length 'a' < Span L):**
    *   **Procedure:** To find the maximum effect of a UDL of intensity 'w' and length 'a', position the UDL such that it covers the portion of the IL that gives the maximum "area" weighted by the load intensity.
    *   **Maximum Effect:** The total effect is the integral of $(w \times y)$ over the length of the UDL. This is equivalent to $w \times (\text{Area under the IL covered by the UDL})$.
    *   **Placement for Maximum:** Position the UDL to cover the region of the IL with the largest ordinates. For a simply supported beam and maximum bending moment, this is often centered on the maximum ordinate. For maximum shear at an end, it's placed to cover the IL from the support.

*   **Longer than the Span (length 'a' > Span L):**
    *   **Procedure:** When the UDL is longer than the span, it means the entire span will always be covered by the UDL.
    *   **Maximum Effect:** The effect at any point is simply $w \times (\text{Area under the entire IL})$.
    *   **Placement for Maximum:** The UDL will be placed to cover the entire span, and the IL area will directly give the maximum effect.

### 6. Conditions for Maximum Bending Moment and Shear Force

These conditions are derived by observing the ILs and considering how different load types affect them.

#### 6.1. Maximum Shear Force

*   **For a Single Concentrated Load (W):** The maximum shear occurs when the load is positioned directly over the section for which the IL is drawn, or at the nearest support if the section is not at the end. This corresponds to the maximum ordinate of the IL for shear at that section.
*   **For Several Concentrated Loads:** The maximum shear occurs when the leading load of the series is placed at the section (or support) that corresponds to the maximum positive ordinate, or when the trailing load is placed at the section that corresponds to the maximum negative ordinate. It's often a trial-and-error process aligning the loads with significant ordinates.
*   **For a UDL (shorter than span):** The maximum shear at a section occurs when the UDL is placed to cover the region of the IL that yields the maximum positive area. For shear at an end support, the UDL is placed to cover the IL from the support.
*   **For a UDL (longer than span):** The maximum shear is $w \times (\text{Area under the entire IL})$.

#### 6.2. Maximum Bending Moment

*   **For a Single Concentrated Load (W):** The maximum bending moment occurs when the load is positioned at the point of the maximum ordinate of the IL for bending moment at that section.
*   **For Several Concentrated Loads:** The maximum bending moment usually occurs when the system of loads is positioned such that one of the loads is at the location of the maximum ordinate of the IL, and the resultant of the loads is also strategically placed. A common strategy is to place the load that is closest to the resultant of the system at the point of maximum IL ordinate.
*   **For a UDL (shorter than span):** The maximum bending moment occurs when the UDL is positioned to cover the region of the IL that yields the maximum positive area. For a simply supported beam, this often means placing the UDL symmetrically about the point of maximum IL ordinate.
*   **For a UDL (longer than span):** The maximum bending moment is $w \times (\text{Area under the entire IL})$.

---

### **Important Points to Remember**

*   **Influence lines are for moving loads.** For a fixed load, you simply calculate the force/moment directly.
*   **The ordinate of an IL represents the value of the function when a UNIT load is at that point.**
*   **For multiple loads, the total effect is the sum of the products of each load and the corresponding IL ordinate.**
*   **The shape of the IL is characteristic of the structure and the function being analyzed.** For example, ILs for shear are often discontinuous, while ILs for bending moment are usually continuous but may have kinks.
*   **For maximum effects, analyze the ordinates (or areas under the IL) corresponding to the critical positions of the moving loads.**
*   **Overhanging beams can have negative ordinates in their influence lines.**
*   **The Merriman's rule or equivalent graphical methods can be used to determine the optimal positioning of multiple concentrated loads for maximum effect.**

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Q1. Simply Supported Beam:**
A simply supported beam has a span of 10m. Draw the ILs for:
a) Vertical reaction at the left support.
b) Shear force at a section 3m from the left support.
c) Bending moment at a section 4m from the left support.
Calculate the values of these functions when a concentrated load of 20 kN moves over the beam.

**Answer Q1:**
*   **IL for $R_A$:** A triangle with base from 0 to 10m, height 1.0 above the left support (x=0).
    *   For 20 kN at x=2m: $R_A = 20 \times (1 - 2/10) = 20 \times 0.8 = 16$ kN.
    *   For 20 kN at x=7m: $R_A = 20 \times (1 - 7/10) = 20 \times 0.3 = 6$ kN.
*   **IL for $V_C$ (at x=3m):** A shape starting at 0, rising linearly to $1 - 3/10 = 0.7$ at x=3m, then dropping to $-3/10 = -0.3$ at x=3m, and linearly decreasing to -1.0 at x=10m.
    *   For 20 kN at x=2m (left of C): $V_C = 20 \times (1 - 2/10) = 20 \times 0.8 = 16$ kN.
    *   For 20 kN at x=4m (right of C): $V_C = 20 \times (-4/10) = 20 \times (-0.4) = -8$ kN.
*   **IL for $M_C$ (at x=4m):** A triangle with base from 0 to 10m, peak height 4m at x=4m.
    *   For 20 kN at x=2m: $M_C = 20 \times (2) = 40$ kNm.
    *   For 20 kN at x=7m: $M_C = 20 \times (1 - 7/10) \times 4 = 20 \times 0.3 \times 4 = 24$ kNm.

**Q2. Overhanging Beam:**
A beam of span 6m is supported at points A and B, 4m apart. The beam overhangs by 2m on the right side (B to C). Draw the IL for:
a) Reaction at A.
b) Reaction at B.
c) Bending moment at support B.

**Answer Q2:**
*   **IL for $R_A$:** Triangle from x=0 to x=4m, height 1.0 at x=0, 0 at x=4m. From x=4m to x=6m (overhang), a line rising from 0 at x=4m to $2/4 = 0.5$ at x=6m.
*   **IL for $R_B$:** Triangle from x=0 to x=4m, height 0 at x=0, 1.0 at x=4m. From x=4m to x=6m, a line rising from 1.0 at x=4m to $(4+2)/4 = 6/4 = 1.5$ at x=6m.
*   **IL for $M_B$:** Section at B (x=4m).
    *   Load from 0 to 4m: $M_B = -R_A \times 4$. $R_A = 1 - x/4$. So $M_B = -(1-x/4) \times 4 = x - 4$.
    *   Load from 4m to 6m: $M_B = -1 \times (x-4)$.
    *   Shape: Linearly decreases from 0 at x=0 to -4 at x=4m. Then linearly decreases from -4 at x=4m to $-1 \times (6-4) = -2$ at x=6m. The maximum negative moment at support B is 4.0 kNm when the load is at B.

**Q3. UDL Analysis:**
A UDL of intensity 10 kN/m and length 4m moves over a simply supported beam of span 8m. Calculate the maximum bending moment.

**Answer Q3:**
*   To find the maximum bending moment, we need to find the position of the UDL that covers the portion of the IL for bending moment with the maximum area. The IL for bending moment at mid-span (4m from support) is a triangle with base 8m and peak height 4m.
*   To maximize the moment, the 4m UDL should be centered over the peak of the IL (at mid-span).
*   The portion of the IL covered by the UDL will be from x=2m to x=6m.
*   The IL ordinate at x=2m is 2m. The IL ordinate at x=6m is 2m. The IL ordinate at x=4m is 4m.
*   The area under the IL covered by the UDL can be calculated as the area of a trapezoid: $\frac{1}{2} \times (2+4) \times 2 + \frac{1}{2} \times (4+2) \times 2 = 6 + 6 = 12 \text{ m}^2$.
*   Alternatively, it's the area of a central triangle (base 4m, height 2m) plus two trapezoids (base 2m, heights 2m and 4m). This calculation is complex.
*   **Simpler approach:** Place the UDL symmetrically about the point of maximum bending moment for a simply supported beam, which is at mid-span. The IL for moment at mid-span is a triangle with apex at mid-span. The UDL (4m) will cover from 2m to 6m.
    *   The IL ordinate at 2m is 2m.
    *   The IL ordinate at 6m is 2m.
    *   The IL ordinate at 4m is 4m.
    *   The area under the IL for the UDL (x=2 to x=6) is: Area = (Area of rectangle from 2 to 6 with height 2) + (Area of triangle from 2 to 4 with height 2) + (Area of triangle from 4 to 6 with height 2) = (4m * 2m) + (1/2 * 2m * 2m) + (1/2 * 2m * 2m) = 8 + 2 + 2 = 12 m^2.
*   Maximum Bending Moment = UDL Intensity $\times$ Area under IL = 10 kN/m $\times$ 12 m$^2$ = 120 kNm.
