# SURVEY LAB: Module 1 - Conventional Surveying

This module introduces the fundamental principles and practices of **Conventional Surveying**, a cornerstone of the surveying profession. We will explore the traditional methods and instruments used to measure and record the physical features of the Earth's surface.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

*   **Understand the basic principles of conventional surveying.**
*   **Identify and explain the function of common conventional surveying instruments.**
*   **Perform basic field procedures for distance, angle, and elevation measurements.**
*   **Apply basic mathematical techniques for calculating survey data.**
*   **Understand the concepts of accuracy, precision, and sources of error in conventional surveying.**
*   **Recognize the role of conventional surveying in various applications.**

## 1. Introduction to Conventional Surveying

### 1.1 Definition and Purpose

*   **Conventional Surveying:** Refers to the traditional methods of surveying that rely on direct physical measurements using instruments that record angles and distances. These methods are typically manual or semi-automated, contrasting with modern electronic and satellite-based techniques.
*   **Purpose:**
    *   To determine the relative positions of points on the Earth's surface.
    *   To measure distances, angles, and elevations.
    *   To create maps, plans, and records of land features.
    *   To establish boundaries and control points for construction projects.
    *   To gather data for land administration and management.

### 1.2 Basic Principles

*   **Triangulation:** A method of surveying where points are located by forming triangles. The principle is that if you know one side of a triangle and two angles, you can calculate all other sides and angles.
    *   **Base Line:** A precisely measured line, forming the starting point for triangulation.
    *   **Angles:** Measured using instruments like theodolites.
*   **Trilateration:** Similar to triangulation but relies on measuring all three sides of triangles.
*   **Traversing:** A series of connected straight lines, the lengths and directions of which are measured and computed. The survey progresses from a known point to an unknown point.
    *   **Open Traverse:** A traverse that does not close back to the starting point or a known point.
    *   **Closed Traverse:** A traverse that forms a closed loop, returning to the starting point. This allows for checking the accuracy of measurements.
*   **Levelling:** The process of determining the vertical elevation of points relative to a reference datum (e.g., mean sea level).

## 2. Conventional Surveying Instruments

### 2.1 Measuring Distance

#### 2.1.1 Measuring Tapes (Band Chains)

*   **Description:** Flexible steel or fiberglass tapes marked with graduations (feet, meters, links).
*   **Types:**
    *   **Steel Tapes:** Durable and accurate, but can stretch under tension and are affected by temperature.
    *   **Invar Tapes:** Made of an alloy with a low coefficient of thermal expansion, ideal for precise measurements.
    *   **Fiberglass Tapes:** Lighter, flexible, and resistant to corrosion, but less accurate than steel tapes for high-precision work.
*   **Usage:** Used for shorter distances, setting out lines, and measuring details.
*   **Important Considerations:**
    *   Maintain consistent tension (pull) when measuring.
    *   Account for sag (droop) of the tape, especially for longer distances.
    *   Consider temperature effects on tape length.
    *   Ensure the tape is held horizontal or corrected for slope.

#### 2.1.2 Subtense Bar

*   **Description:** A bar of known length (typically 2 meters) with targets at each end, used with a theodolite.
*   **Principle:** Measures horizontal distances indirectly by measuring the angle subtended by the known length of the bar at the observing station.
*   **Formula (simplified):** Distance = (Length of Subtense Bar) / (Tangent of Subtense Angle)
*   **Usage:** Useful for measuring distances across inaccessible terrain or water bodies.

---

### 2.2 Measuring Angles

#### 2.2.1 Compass

*   **Description:** An instrument that indicates direction relative to the Earth's magnetic poles.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Magnetic Compass:** Uses a magnetized needle that aligns with the Earth's magnetic field.
    *   **Prismatic Compass:** Incorporates a prism to allow for more accurate sighting and reading of bearings simultaneously.
*   **Measurements:**
    *   **Bearing:** The angle between a magnetic meridian and a line, measured clockwise from North (0° to 360°).
    *   **Azimuth:** Similar to bearing but always measured clockwise from a reference meridian.
*   **Limitations:** Susceptible to magnetic disturbances (local attraction) from iron or steel objects.
*   **Usage:** Primarily for reconnaissance and approximate directional control.

#### 2.2.2 Theodolite

*   **Description:** A precision optical instrument for measuring horizontal and vertical angles. It consists of a telescope mounted on a horizontal and vertical axis.
*   **Key Components:**
    *   **Telescope:** Used for sighting points.
    *   **Horizontal Circle:** Marked with degrees, minutes, and seconds for measuring horizontal angles.
    *   **Vertical Circle:** Marked with degrees, minutes, and seconds for measuring vertical angles (zenith angles or altitudes/depressions).
    *   **Vernier/Micrometer:** Used to read the angles on the circles with high precision.
    *   **Leveling Screws:** Used to level the instrument.
    *   **Plumb Bob:** Used to center the instrument directly over a ground point.
*   **Measurements:**
    *   **Horizontal Angle:** Angle between two points measured in the horizontal plane.
    *   **Vertical Angle:** Angle between the horizontal and a line of sight to a point.
*   **Usage:** Essential for precise angle measurements in traversing, triangulation, and setting out.

#### 2.2.3 Sextant

*   **Description:** An instrument used for measuring the angle between two objects, primarily used in celestial navigation but also in terrestrial surveying.
*   **Principle:** Uses mirrors to bring images of two objects into coincidence.
*   **Usage:** Less common in modern ground surveying but historically significant.

### 2.3 Measuring Elevations

#### 2.3.1 Levelling Instrument (Dumpy Level, Tilting Level, Automatic Level)

*   **Description:** An instrument used to establish horizontal lines of sight for the purpose of determining differences in elevation.
*   **Key Components:**
    *   **Telescope:** For sighting.
    *   **Bubble Tube (Level Vial):** To ensure the line of sight is horizontal.
    *   **Levelling Screws:** To adjust the instrument to a horizontal position.
*   **Usage:** Used in conjunction with a **Levelling Staff**.

#### 2.3.2 Levelling Staff (Rod)

*   **Description:** A graduated rod, typically made of wood or fiberglass, used to measure vertical distances from the levelling instrument.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Single-Piece Staff:** For shorter distances.
    *   **Folding Staff:** More portable for longer distances.
*   **Usage:** Held vertically by a **Stavesperson** at the points where elevations are to be determined.

#### 2.3.3 Levelling Process (Differential Levelling)

*   **Objective:** To determine the difference in elevation between two points.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  **Set up the Levelling Instrument:** Place the instrument on a tripod, level it, and ensure it is stable.
    2.  **Backsight (BS):** The stavesperson holds the levelling staff vertically on a point of known elevation (known as a Turning Point - TP or Bench Mark - BM). The surveyor sights the staff with the levelling instrument and records the reading (BS). This reading is from the instrument's horizontal line of sight down to the staff.
    3.  **Foresight (FS):** The levelling instrument is moved to a new position, and the stavesperson holds the staff on a new point (either the target point or another TP). The surveyor sights the staff and records the reading (FS). This reading is from the instrument's horizontal line of sight down to the staff.
    4.  **Calculations:**
        *   **Elevation of new point = Elevation of known point + Backsight (BS) - Foresight (FS)** (This is a simplified explanation; more commonly: Elevation of HI = Elevation of known point + BS; Elevation of new point = Elevation of HI - FS)
        *   **HI (Height of Instrument):** The elevation of the horizontal line of sight from the instrument.
*   **Example:**
    *   Known Point A Elevation: 100.00 m
    *   Backsight (BS) reading on Staff at A: 1.50 m
    *   Height of Instrument (HI): 100.00 m + 1.50 m = 101.50 m
    *   Foresight (FS) reading on Staff at Point B: 2.20 m
    *   Elevation of Point B: 101.50 m - 2.20 m = 99.30 m
    *   Difference in Elevation (B - A): 99.30 m - 100.00 m = -0.70 m

#### 2.3.4 Bench Marks (BM)

*   **Definition:** A point of established or determined elevation, used as a reference for levelling.
*   **Types:** Permanent (e.g., concrete pillars) or temporary (e.g., a nail on a building).

## 3. Field Procedures and Calculations

### 3.1 Traversing

*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Start from a known point or establish a starting point.
    2.  Measure the bearing or azimuth and distance of the first leg of the traverse.
    3.  Move to the next station, set up the theodolite, and measure the interior angles and forward bearings/azimuths of the traverse legs.
    4.  Repeat for all legs of the traverse.
    5.  For a closed traverse, the last leg should connect back to the starting point, allowing for checks.
*   **Data Recorded:**
    *   Station occupied.
    *   Horizontal angles (e.g., interior angles).
    *   Vertical angles.
    *   Telescope face left (FL) and face right (FR) readings (to minimize instrumental errors).
    *   Distances (measured directly or calculated using trigonometry).
    *   Magnetic bearings/azimuths (for checking).

### 3.2 Calculating Traverse Data

*   **Balancing Angles:** In a closed traverse, the sum of measured interior angles should equal the theoretical sum ( (n-2) * 180° ), where n is the number of sides. Adjustments are made to the measured angles.
*   **Calculating Bearings/Azimuths:** The bearing/azimuth of each subsequent leg is calculated based on the bearing/azimuth of the previous leg and the measured interior angle.
*   **Calculating Coordinates (Latitude and Departure):**
    *   **Latitude (L):** The north-south component of a traverse leg.  $L = Distance * cos(Bearing/Azimuth)$
    *   **Departure (D):** The east-west component of a traverse leg. $D = Distance * sin(Bearing/Azimuth)$
    *   Bearings/Azimuths are converted to angles relative to the North axis (0° to 90°).
*   **Checks for Closure:**
    *   **Angle Check:** Sum of interior angles.
    *   **Bowditch Rule (for closed traverses):** Adjustments are applied to latitudes and departures to ensure the total departure and total latitude sum to zero, signifying a closed loop.

### 3.3 Calculating Area from Traverse Data

*   **Double Meridian Distance (DMD) Method:** A common method for calculating the area of a closed traverse using the latitudes and departures of each leg.
*   **Shoelace Formula:** Another method that uses the coordinates of the vertices of the polygon.

### 3.4 Levelling Calculations

*   **Rise and Fall Method:** Records both backsight (BS) and foresight (FS) readings. Differences in elevation are calculated as "rise" (if BS > FS) or "fall" (if BS < FS).
    *   Sum of BS - Sum of FS = Sum of Rise - Sum of Fall = Total Difference in Elevation
*   **Height of Instrument (HI) Method:** Calculates the elevation of the horizontal line of sight (HI) for each instrument setup.
    *   Elevation of new point = HI - FS

## 4. Accuracy, Precision, and Errors

### 4.1 Accuracy vs. Precision

*   **Accuracy:** How close a measurement is to the true value.
    *   *Example:* If the true distance is 100.00 m, a measurement of 100.05 m is more accurate than 100.50 m.
*   **Precision:** The degree of closeness between repeated measurements of the same quantity.
    *   *Example:* If measurements are 100.05 m, 100.06 m, and 100.04 m, these are precise. If they are 100.05 m, 100.50 m, and 101.00 m, they are not precise.

### 4.2 Sources of Error

*   **Systematic Errors:** Errors that are constant in magnitude and direction for a given set of conditions. They can be predicted and corrected for.
    *   **Instrumental Errors:** Imperfections in instruments (e.g., uncalibrated scales, warped telescope).
    *   **Environmental Errors:** Effects of temperature, wind, gravity, and refraction.
    *   **Personal Errors (can be systematic):** Consistent mistakes made by the surveyor (e.g., parallax error due to improper eye positioning).
*   **Random Errors:** Errors that vary in magnitude and direction due to unpredictable causes. They cannot be predicted or corrected for but can be minimized through careful observation and statistical analysis.
    *   **Natural causes:** Fluctuations in temperature, air pressure, or wind.
    *   **Human limitations:** Slight variations in aligning sights, reading scales, or holding instruments.

### 4.3 Error Propagation and Control

*   **Rules of Error Propagation:** Mathematical rules used to determine how errors in individual measurements affect the final computed result.
*   **Minimizing Errors:**
    *   **Instrument Calibration:** Ensure instruments are in good working order and calibrated.
    *   **Proper Field Procedures:** Follow established techniques for setting up, sighting, and reading instruments.
    *   **Redundant Measurements:** Taking extra measurements to verify results.
    *   **Using Appropriate Instruments:** Selecting instruments suitable for the required level of accuracy.
    *   **Field Checks:** Performing checks and re-observations during fieldwork.

## 5. Applications of Conventional Surveying

*   **Cadastral Surveying:** Establishing and re-establishing property boundaries.
*   **Topographical Surveying:** Mapping the natural and man-made features of the land, including relief.
*   **Engineering Surveying:** Providing data for the design and construction of infrastructure projects (roads, bridges, buildings, tunnels).
*   **Control Surveying:** Establishing a network of precise points (horizontal and vertical control) for large-scale mapping and engineering projects.
*   **Mine Surveying:** Surveying underground and above-ground mine operations.
*   **Hydrographic Surveying:** Mapping underwater features and depths.

---

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Accuracy vs. Precision:** Understand the difference and strive for both.
*   **Instrument Care:** Handle instruments with care; they are precision tools.
*   **Thorough Field Notes:** Record all data clearly and systematically. Mistakes in notes are difficult to rectify.
*   **Redundancy:** Make redundant measurements and perform checks whenever possible.
*   **Systematic Errors:** Identify and correct for systematic errors.
*   **Random Errors:** Minimize random errors through careful observation.
*   **Understanding Principles:** Always understand the underlying principles of the measurements you are taking.
*   **Application Context:** The required accuracy and methods depend on the project's purpose.

## Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
Conventional surveying refers to traditional methods of surveying that rely on direct physical measurements using instruments that record angles and distances. Its primary purposes include determining the relative positions of points, measuring distances, angles, and elevations, creating maps and plans, establishing boundaries, and gathering data for land administration.

**Answer 2:**
Triangulation is a surveying method where points are located by forming triangles. The principle is that if one side and two angles of a triangle are known, all other sides and angles can be calculated. This allows surveyors to extend their control network over large areas, even across inaccessible terrain, by establishing a baseline and then measuring angles to create a network of interconnected triangles.

**Answer 3:**
A magnetic compass uses a magnetized needle that aligns with the Earth's magnetic field. A prismatic compass is an improved version that incorporates a prism, allowing for simultaneous sighting and reading of the bearing, leading to greater accuracy. A major limitation of magnetic compasses is their susceptibility to **local attraction**, where nearby ferrous materials (iron, steel) can deflect the magnetic needle, causing inaccurate readings.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Height of Instrument (HI):**
    HI = Elevation of BM A + BS
    HI = 55.250 m + 1.485 m = **56.735 m**

*   **Elevation of Point B:**
    Elevation of Point B = HI - FS
    Elevation of Point B = 56.735 m - 0.950 m = **55.785 m**

**Answer 5:**
*   **Accuracy** refers to how close a measurement is to the true value. For example, measuring a distance as 10.05 m when the true distance is 10.00 m is more accurate than measuring it as 10.20 m.
*   **Precision** refers to the degree of closeness between repeated measurements of the same quantity. For example, taking measurements of 10.05 m, 10.06 m, and 10.04 m indicates high precision, even if the true value is slightly different.

**Answer 6:**
*   **Systematic Errors:**
    1.  **Temperature Expansion/Contraction of Measuring Tapes:** A steel tape will expand in heat and contract in cold, leading to a systematic error in distance measurement.
    2.  **Collimation Error in a Theodolite:** If the line of sight of the telescope is not perfectly perpendicular to the horizontal axis, it introduces a systematic error in angle measurements.
*   **Random Errors:**
    1.  **Parallax Error:** Due to slight variations in the surveyor's eye position when reading a scale.
    2.  **Wind Swaying the Levelling Staff:** Unpredictable movement of the staff caused by wind can lead to random errors in levelling readings.

**Answer 7:**
A traverse is a series of connected straight lines, the lengths and directions of which are measured and computed.
*   **Open Traverse:** A traverse that does not close back to its starting point or a known point. Its accuracy is harder to check.
*   **Closed Traverse:** A traverse that forms a loop, returning to the starting point or connecting two known points. This closure allows for verification of measurements and error adjustment.

**Answer 8:**
*   **Error:** True Distance - Measured Distance = 50.00 m - 50.02 m = **-0.02 m** (or 0.02 m error).
*   This is an example of **accuracy**. The measurement has an error relative to the true value. If you repeated the measurement and consistently got values around 50.02 m, that would be precision.
