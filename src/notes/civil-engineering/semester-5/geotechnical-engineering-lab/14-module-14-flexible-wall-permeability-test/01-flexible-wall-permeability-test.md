## Module 14: Flexible Wall Permeability Test

### Topic: Flexible Wall Permeability Test

---

### 1. Introduction to Permeability

*   **Definition:** Permeability is a measure of a soil's ability to allow water to flow through it. It is a fundamental property in geotechnical engineering, influencing various aspects like seepage, consolidation, and slope stability.
*   **Importance:**
    *   **Seepage Analysis:** Understanding how water flows through soil is crucial for designing foundations, retaining walls, and dams to prevent excessive pore water pressures.
    *   **Consolidation:** The rate at which a soil consolidates under load is directly related to its permeability.
    *   **Dewatering:** Permeability dictates the effectiveness of dewatering operations for excavations.
    *   **Groundwater Flow:** It helps in predicting groundwater movement and its impact on structures.
*   **Factors Affecting Permeability:**
    *   **Particle Size:** Larger particles generally lead to higher permeability (e.g., gravels > sands > silts > clays).
    *   **Particle Shape:** Angular particles create more tortuous flow paths, reducing permeability compared to rounded particles.
    *   **Void Ratio (Porosity):** Higher void ratios generally result in higher permeability.
    *   **Soil Structure:** The arrangement of soil particles (e.g., dispersed vs. flocculated in clays) significantly impacts permeability.
    *   **Degree of Saturation:** Permeability is typically considered for saturated soils.
    *   **Temperature of Water:** Higher water temperatures reduce viscosity, leading to higher permeability.

---

### 2. Permeability Tests: Overview

*   **Types of Permeability Tests:**
    *   **Laboratory Tests:**
        *   **Constant Head Permeability Test:** Suitable for granular soils (sands and gravels).
        *   **Falling Head Permeability Test:** Suitable for fine-grained soils (silts and clays).
        *   **Flexible Wall Permeability Test (Triaxial Permeability Test):** Primarily used for cohesive soils, allowing for controlled stress conditions.
    *   **Field Tests:**
        *   **Pumping Tests:** Used to determine aquifer properties.
        *   **Borehole Tests:** Various methods to assess permeability in situ.

### 3. Flexible Wall Permeability Test (Triaxial Permeability Test)

*   **Learning Outcome Addressed:** Understanding the principles and procedures of the flexible wall permeability test.

*   **Key Concept:** This test is performed using a triaxial cell, where the soil sample is confined by hydrostatic pressure (cell pressure) and subjected to a hydraulic gradient across its length. The term "flexible wall" refers to the rubber membrane that encloses the soil sample, allowing it to deform under applied stresses without the leakage that can occur with rigid-wall permeameters.

*   **Objective:** To determine the coefficient of permeability ($k$) of cohesive soils under controlled and realistic stress conditions.

*   **Principle:** Darcy's Law governs the flow of water through porous media. In this test, we measure the volume of water flowing through the sample over a specific time under a known hydraulic gradient and confining pressure.

    *   **Darcy's Law:** $Q = k \times i \times A \times t$
        *   $Q$: Volume of water flowing (e.g., $cm^3$)
        *   $k$: Coefficient of permeability (e.g., $cm/sec$)
        *   $i$: Hydraulic gradient ($h/L$)
            *   $h$: Head loss across the sample (difference in water levels)
            *   $L$: Length of the flow path (length of the sample)
        *   $A$: Cross-sectional area of the sample (e.g., $cm^2$)
        *   $t$: Time of flow (e.g., $sec$)

*   **Apparatus:**
    *   **Triaxial Cell:** A cylindrical chamber capable of holding pressurized water.
    *   **Soil Specimen:** Typically a cylindrical sample, usually trimmed from a larger block or consolidated sample.
    *   **Rubber Membrane:** A flexible, impermeable membrane that encases the soil specimen.
    *   **End Caps/Porous Stones:** Placed at the top and bottom of the specimen to allow water to enter and exit the sample while preventing soil particles from escaping. These are usually saturated before use.
    *   **Pressure Transducers/Manometers:** To measure the pore water pressure at the inlet and outlet ends of the specimen.
    *   **Volume Measuring Device:** A burette, graduated cylinder, or flow meter to measure the volume of water flowing through the sample.
    *   **Water Supply:** For applying backpressure and maintaining flow.
    *   **Backpressure System:** To saturate the sample and ensure it is fully consolidated.
    *   **De-aired Water:** Essential to prevent air bubbles from interfering with flow measurements.

*   **Procedure:**

    1.  **Specimen Preparation:**
        *   Prepare a cylindrical soil specimen of specified dimensions.
        *   Trim any irregularities from the sample.
        *   Measure the initial dimensions (diameter, height) and weight of the specimen.
        *   Enclose the specimen in a rubber membrane and seal it at the ends using O-rings.
        *   Place porous stones on the top and bottom of the specimen.

    2.  **Assembly in Triaxial Cell:**
        *   Carefully place the specimen assembly into the triaxial cell.
        *   Ensure the porous stones are in contact with the inlet and outlet ports of the cell.

    3.  **Saturation (Backpressure Saturation):**
        *   Apply a low confining pressure (cell pressure) to the sample.
        *   Apply backpressure to the specimen from both ends. This process saturates the soil pores with de-aired water and dissolves any entrapped air.
        *   Continue applying backpressure until the Skempton's pore pressure parameter 'B' is close to 1 (typically $B \ge 0.95$), indicating full saturation. This is verified by observing the pore pressure response to changes in cell pressure.

    4.  **Consolidation (if applicable):**
        *   If the test needs to be performed at a specific confining pressure representative of field conditions, consolidate the sample under that pressure for a sufficient period.

    5.  **Permeability Measurement:**
        *   Apply the desired **confining pressure (cell pressure)**.
        *   Establish a **hydraulic gradient** across the specimen by applying a constant difference in water pressure between the top and bottom ends. This can be done by:
            *   **Constant Head Method:** Connecting a reservoir to the inlet and outlet with a fixed difference in water levels.
            *   **Falling Head Method (less common in flexible wall):** Using a burette with a known volume and measuring the time it takes for the water level to fall.
        *   Ensure the flow is from the bottom to the top or top to bottom, as specified by the test standard.
        *   **Measure the volume of water ($Q$) that flows through the specimen over a measured time interval ($t$).**
        *   Record the inlet and outlet pore water pressures to verify the applied head difference.

    6.  **Repeat Measurements:**
        *   Repeat the flow measurements for several time intervals to ensure steady-state flow has been achieved.
        *   If different hydraulic gradients are to be tested, adjust the head difference and repeat the measurements.

    7.  **Final Measurements:**
        *   After the test, recover the specimen and measure its final dimensions and weight to determine the void ratio and degree of saturation.

*   **Calculations:**

    1.  **Hydraulic Gradient ($i$):**
        *   $i = \frac{h_1 - h_2}{L_{specimen}}$
            *   $h_1$: Head at the inlet end (e.g., pressure head)
            *   $h_2$: Head at the outlet end (e.g., pressure head)
            *   $L_{specimen}$: Length of the soil specimen.

    2.  **Cross-sectional Area ($A$):**
        *   $A = \frac{\pi D^2}{4}$
            *   $D$: Diameter of the soil specimen.

    3.  **Coefficient of Permeability ($k$):**
        *   **Using Darcy's Law directly (for steady flow):**
            *   $k = \frac{Q \times L}{i \times A \times t} = \frac{Q \times L}{(h_1 - h_2) \times A \times t}$
        *   **For falling head (if applicable in a variation):**
            *   $k = \frac{aL}{At} \ln\left(\frac{h_1}{h_2}\right)$
                *   $a$: Area of the standpipe or burette.
                *   $A$: Cross-sectional area of the specimen.
                *   $L$: Length of the specimen.
                *   $t$: Time for head to fall from $h_1$ to $h_2$.

    4.  **Correction for Viscosity:**
        *   The calculated $k$ is usually at the temperature of the test. To compare with standard values or for different temperatures, it needs to be corrected for the viscosity of water.
        *   $k_{20} = k_T \times \frac{\mu_T}{\mu_{20}}$
            *   $k_{20}$: Permeability at $20^\circ C$.
            *   $k_T$: Permeability at the test temperature $T$.
            *   $\mu_T$: Viscosity of water at temperature $T$.
            *   $\mu_{20}$: Viscosity of water at $20^\circ C$.
        *   Viscosity of water decreases with increasing temperature. Values for viscosity can be found in standard tables.

*   **Advantages of Flexible Wall Permeability Test:**
    *   **Realistic Stress Conditions:** Allows the soil specimen to experience confining stresses that are more representative of in-situ conditions.
    *   **Uniform Saturation:** Backpressure saturation ensures complete saturation, crucial for accurate permeability measurements.
    *   **Reduced Boundary Effects:** The flexible membrane minimizes leakage at the boundaries, unlike some rigid-wall permeameters.
    *   **Suitable for Cohesive Soils:** Specifically designed for fine-grained soils where other tests might be less reliable.
    *   **Can Measure Anisotropy:** By orienting the sample differently relative to the flow direction, the anisotropic nature of permeability can be investigated.

*   **Disadvantages of Flexible Wall Permeability Test:**
    *   **Time-Consuming:** Requires careful specimen preparation, saturation, and potentially consolidation.
    *   **Requires Specialized Equipment:** Triaxial cell and associated pressure/volume measuring devices are needed.
    *   **Potential for Membrane Issues:** Tears or faulty seals in the membrane can lead to erroneous results.
    *   **More Complex Calculations:** Requires understanding of pore pressure and correction for viscosity.

### 4. Factors Affecting Test Results

*   **Specimen Saturation:** Incomplete saturation can lead to air blockage, reducing the measured permeability.
*   **Specimen Preparation:** Damage to the soil structure during trimming or handling can alter permeability.
*   **Porous Stone Condition:** Clogged or dry porous stones can impede flow and introduce errors.
*   **Leakage:** Faulty membrane seals or connections can cause water loss.
*   **Temperature Fluctuations:** Changes in water temperature affect viscosity, thus affecting the calculated $k$.
*   **Air Entrapment:** Air bubbles within the specimen or in the flow path can significantly reduce permeability.
*   **Pore Pressure Measurement Accuracy:** Inaccurate measurement of inlet and outlet pressures leads to errors in the hydraulic gradient.
*   **Steady-State Flow:** Not waiting for steady-state flow before taking measurements can lead to incorrect $k$ values.

---

### 5. Interpretation of Results

*   **Coefficient of Permeability ($k$):** The primary output of the test.
*   **Range of $k$ Values:**
    *   Gravels: $10^{-1}$ to $10^1$ cm/s
    *   Sands: $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$ cm/s
    *   Silts: $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-7}$ cm/s
    *   Clays: $< 10^{-7}$ cm/s
*   **Anisotropy:** If the test is performed with flow parallel and perpendicular to the natural depositional direction, the ratio of these permeabilities indicates the degree of anisotropy.
*   **Relationship with Void Ratio:** Permeability generally increases with an increase in void ratio for a given soil type.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A flexible wall permeability test was conducted on a clay specimen. The specimen dimensions were: diameter = 38 mm, length = 76 mm. During the test, a volume of 45 $cm^3$ of water flowed through the specimen in 10 minutes. The inlet pressure head was 50 cm and the outlet pressure head was 20 cm (relative to the bottom of the specimen). The test was conducted at a temperature of $22^\circ C$.
Calculate the coefficient of permeability ($k$) at $22^\circ C$. Assume the viscosity of water at $22^\circ C$ is $0.957 \times 10^{-3} Pa \cdot s$ and at $20^\circ C$ is $1.002 \times 10^{-3} Pa \cdot s$.

**Answer 1:**

*   **Given:**
    *   Diameter ($D$) = 38 mm = 3.8 cm
    *   Length ($L$) = 76 mm = 7.6 cm
    *   Volume of flow ($Q$) = 45 $cm^3$
    *   Time ($t$) = 10 minutes = 600 seconds
    *   Inlet head ($h_1$) = 50 cm
    *   Outlet head ($h_2$) = 20 cm
    *   Test temperature ($T$) = $22^\circ C$
    *   $\mu_{22} = 0.957 \times 10^{-3} Pa \cdot s$
    *   $\mu_{20} = 1.002 \times 10^{-3} Pa \cdot s$

*   **Calculations:**
    1.  **Cross-sectional Area ($A$):**
        $A = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (3.8 \text{ cm})^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \times 14.44 \text{ cm}^2}{4} \approx 11.31 \text{ cm}^2$

    2.  **Head Loss ($h_1 - h_2$):**
        $h_1 - h_2 = 50 \text{ cm} - 20 \text{ cm} = 30 \text{ cm}$

    3.  **Hydraulic Gradient ($i$):**
        $i = \frac{h_1 - h_2}{L} = \frac{30 \text{ cm}}{7.6 \text{ cm}} \approx 3.95$

    4.  **Coefficient of Permeability ($k$) at $22^\circ C$:**
        $k = \frac{Q \times L}{i \times A \times t} = \frac{45 \text{ cm}^3 \times 7.6 \text{ cm}}{3.95 \times 11.31 \text{ cm}^2 \times 600 \text{ s}}$
        $k = \frac{342 \text{ cm}^2}{26753.1 \text{ cm}^3/s} \approx 0.0128 \text{ cm/s}$

    5.  **Corrected Permeability ($k_{20}$):**
        $k_{20} = k_{22} \times \frac{\mu_{22}}{\mu_{20}} = 0.0128 \text{ cm/s} \times \frac{0.957 \times 10^{-3} Pa \cdot s}{1.002 \times 10^{-3} Pa \cdot s}$
        $k_{20} = 0.0128 \text{ cm/s} \times 0.955 \approx 0.0122 \text{ cm/s}$

    **Therefore, the coefficient of permeability at $22^\circ C$ is approximately $0.0128 \text{ cm/s}$, and the corrected permeability at $20^\circ C$ is approximately $0.0122 \text{ cm/s}$.**

**Question 2:**
List at least three advantages of using the flexible wall permeability test compared to the constant head permeability test for cohesive soils.

**Answer 2:**
1.  **Realistic Stress Conditions:** The flexible wall test allows for the application of confining pressure, mimicking in-situ stress conditions, which is crucial for cohesive soils that are sensitive to stress. The constant head test usually applies a relatively low confining pressure.
2.  **Improved Saturation:** The backpressure saturation process in the flexible wall test ensures complete saturation of fine-grained soils, which is difficult to achieve with other methods and critical for accurate permeability measurements.
3.  **Reduced Boundary Leakage:** The flexible membrane effectively seals the specimen, minimizing leakage at the boundaries between the soil and the end caps/porous stones, which can be a significant issue in rigid-wall permeameters with fine-grained soils.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Saturation is Key:** Ensure the soil sample is fully saturated before starting permeability measurements. Use backpressure saturation.
*   **De-aired Water:** Always use de-aired water to prevent air entrapment.
*   **Steady-State Flow:** Wait for the flow rate to stabilize before taking measurements.
*   **Temperature Correction:** Always correct the permeability value to a standard temperature (usually $20^\circ C$) for comparison.
*   **Specimen Integrity:** Handle the specimen carefully during preparation and assembly to avoid disturbing its structure.
*   **Porous Stones:** Ensure porous stones are saturated and clean before use.
*   **Confining Pressure:** The chosen confining pressure should be representative of the in-situ conditions you are trying to model.
*   **Hydraulic Gradient:** Use a gradient that is sufficient to cause measurable flow but not so high that it causes soil erosion or non-Darcy flow.
