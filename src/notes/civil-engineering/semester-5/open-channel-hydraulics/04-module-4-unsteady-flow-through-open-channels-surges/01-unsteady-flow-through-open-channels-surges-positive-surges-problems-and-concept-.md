### **1. Introduction to Unsteady Flow and Surges**

*   **Unsteady Flow:** Flow where velocity and depth change with time at any given point. This contrasts with steady flow where these parameters are constant over time.
    *   **Examples:** Sudden gate opening/closing, dam break, tidal bores, rainfall-induced floods.
*   **Surges:** A phenomenon in unsteady flow characterized by a rapid and significant change in water depth and velocity, propagating as a wave front.
    *   **Types of Surges:**
        *   **Positive Surge (Rising Surge/Flood Wave):** An increase in water depth and a decrease in velocity, moving downstream.
        *   **Negative Surge (Falling Surge/Drafting Wave):** A decrease in water depth and an increase in velocity, moving upstream or downstream.

### **2. Positive Surges (Rising Surges / Flood Waves)**

#### **2.1 Generation of Positive Surges**

A positive surge is typically generated by a sudden increase in discharge downstream of a control structure or a rapid change in channel boundary conditions that causes an accumulation of water.

*   **Common Causes:**
    *   **Sudden gate opening:** Rapidly increasing flow out of a reservoir or impoundment.
    *   **Dam break (partial or complete):** Release of a large volume of water.
    *   **Abrupt change in channel slope or cross-section:** Causing flow to back up.
    *   **Tidal bores:** In estuaries, where a rising tide creates a surge.
    *   **Flash floods:** Rapid influx of water from upstream tributaries.

#### **2.2 Characteristics of a Positive Surge**

*   **Wave Front:** A relatively steep front where depth and velocity change abruptly.
*   **Propagation:** Moves downstream.
*   **Depth:** Increases across the surge front.
*   **Velocity:** Decreases across the surge front (as kinetic energy is converted to potential energy and dissipated).
*   **Energy:** Total energy (specific energy) generally increases across the surge front, but some energy is lost due to friction and turbulence.

#### **2.3 Analysis of Positive Surges: The Hydraulic Jump Analogy**

Positive surges in open channels share similarities with hydraulic jumps, particularly in their energy dissipation and momentum considerations. The analysis often utilizes the **momentum principle**.

**Assumptions for Simplified Analysis:**

*   **Shallow Wave Approximation:** The surge is treated as a shallow wave where the celerity (wave speed) is much less than the flow velocity in the supercritical region.
*   **Uniform Flow Before and After the Surge:** The flow is assumed to be uniform and steady immediately upstream (state 1) and downstream (state 2) of the surge front.
*   **Negligible Friction:** Friction losses across the narrow surge front are often neglected for initial analysis, but can be considered for more accurate modeling.
*   **Rectangular Channel (Often):** Many initial problems are simplified using rectangular channels for ease of calculation.

**Governing Equations (Momentum Principle):**

Consider a control volume encompassing the surge front. The net force acting on the water in the control volume equals the net rate of change of momentum. For a horizontal channel with negligible friction across the surge:

$$ \frac{Q_1^2}{A_1 Y_1} + \frac{1}{2} A_1 Y_1^2 = \frac{Q_2^2}{A_2 Y_2} + \frac{1}{2} A_2 Y_2^2 $$

Where:
*   $Q_1, Q_2$ = Discharge upstream and downstream of the surge, respectively.
*   $A_1, A_2$ = Cross-sectional area upstream and downstream of the surge, respectively.
*   $Y_1, Y_2$ = Depth of flow upstream and downstream of the surge, respectively.

For a rectangular channel of width $B$:
*   $A_1 = B Y_1$
*   $A_2 = B Y_2$
*   $Q_1 = q_1 B$
*   $Q_2 = q_2 B$
    *   $q_1, q_2$ are unit discharges (discharge per unit width).

Substituting into the momentum equation for a rectangular channel:

$$ \frac{q_1^2}{Y_1} + Y_1^2 = \frac{q_2^2}{Y_2} + Y_2^2 $$

This equation relates the flow conditions before and after the surge.

**Surge Celerity (Wave Speed):**

The speed ($V_s$) at which the surge front moves relative to the stationary bed can be determined using the momentum principle and continuity equation.

From continuity: $Q_1 = Q_2$ (assuming no significant inflow or outflow across the surge front itself).
$q_1 B = q_2 B \implies q_1 = q_2$

Let $V_1$ and $V_2$ be the flow velocities upstream and downstream of the surge, respectively.
$q_1 = V_1 Y_1$
$q_2 = V_2 Y_2$

The surge velocity relative to the water in state 1 is $(V_s - V_1)$.
The surge velocity relative to the water in state 2 is $(V_s - V_2)$.

Using the momentum equation and continuity:

The speed of the surge relative to the channel bed is given by:

$$ V_s = V_1 + \sqrt{g Y_1 \left( \frac{1}{2} \left(1 + \frac{8q_1^2}{g Y_1^3} \right)^{1/2} - \frac{1}{2} \right)} $$

Alternatively, and more commonly derived from the momentum equation:

$$ V_s = V_1 + \sqrt{g Y_1 \left(\frac{Y_2}{Y_1}\right) \left(\frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2Y_1}\right)} $$

A simplified and widely used expression for surge velocity relative to the bed is:

$$ V_s = V_1 + \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}} $$

This assumes the surge front is thin and neglects friction.

**Conjugate Depths (Specific force):**

For a given discharge, there are usually two depths that have the same specific force. These are called conjugate depths. The relationship between them is given by the momentum equation.

The specific force ($F/B$) for a rectangular channel is:

$$ \frac{F}{B} = \frac{q^2}{gY} + \frac{1}{2} Y^2 $$

For a positive surge, state 1 is subcritical (usually, $Y_1 < Y_c$) and state 2 is supercritical or near critical ($Y_2 > Y_1$ and often $Y_2 > Y_c$). The surge is a transition from state 1 to state 2.

**Energy Loss in a Positive Surge:**

The energy loss ($E_{loss}$) across a positive surge is the difference in specific energy between the two states:

$$ E_{loss} = E_1 - E_2 = \left( Y_1 + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} \right) - \left( Y_2 + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} \right) $$

For a rectangular channel, this simplifies to:

$$ E_{loss} = \frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2} $$

**Froude Number:**

The Froude number ($Fr$) is crucial in distinguishing between subcritical and supercritical flow.
*   $Fr < 1$: Subcritical flow (slow, deep)
*   $Fr = 1$: Critical flow
*   $Fr > 1$: Supercritical flow (fast, shallow)

For a positive surge, typically $Fr_1 < 1$ and $Fr_2 > 1$. The surge acts as a transition from subcritical to supercritical flow. However, in many practical surge scenarios, the flow after the surge ($V_2$) is still relatively slow compared to the surge celerity itself, and the change in depth is the dominant characteristic.

The Froude number upstream of the surge is $Fr_1 = \frac{V_1}{\sqrt{gY_1}} = \frac{q_1}{Y_1\sqrt{gY_1}}$.
The Froude number downstream of the surge is $Fr_2 = \frac{V_2}{\sqrt{gY_2}} = \frac{q_2}{Y_2\sqrt{gY_2}}$.

From the conjugate depth equation for rectangular channels:
$$ Y_2 = \frac{Y_1}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{8q_1^2}{gY_1^3}} - 1 \right) $$
Or in terms of Froude number:
$$ Y_2 = \frac{Y_1}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + 8Fr_1^2} - 1 \right) $$

#### **2.4 Problems and Examples (Positive Surges)**

**Example 1: Surge Propagation Speed**

A positive surge is propagating in a rectangular channel of width 5 m. Upstream of the surge, the flow is subcritical with a depth of $Y_1 = 1.0$ m and a unit discharge of $q_1 = 3.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$. The surge causes the depth to increase to $Y_2 = 2.0$ m. Calculate the speed of the surge relative to the channel bed.

**Given:**
*   $B = 5$ m
*   $Y_1 = 1.0$ m
*   $q_1 = 3.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$
*   $Y_2 = 2.0$ m
*   $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$

**Solution:**

1.  **Calculate upstream velocity ($V_1$):**
    $V_1 = q_1 / Y_1 = 3.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m} / 1.0 \text{ m} = 3.0 \text{ m/s}$

2.  **Calculate surge celerity ($V_s$):**
    Using the simplified formula: $V_s = V_1 + \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}}$
    $V_s = 3.0 + \sqrt{9.81 \times \frac{1.0 + 2.0}{2}}$
    $V_s = 3.0 + \sqrt{9.81 \times 1.5}$
    $V_s = 3.0 + \sqrt{14.715}$
    $V_s = 3.0 + 3.836$
    $V_s \approx 6.84 \text{ m/s}$

**Answer:** The speed of the surge relative to the channel bed is approximately 6.84 m/s.

**Example 2: Determining Downstream Depth and Energy Loss**

Consider a rectangular channel with uniform flow $Y_1 = 0.5$ m and $q_1 = 1.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$. A surge causes the unit discharge to remain the same, but the depth increases to $Y_2 = 1.5$ m.

(a) Verify if this is a positive surge scenario.
(b) Calculate the energy loss across the surge.

**Given:**
*   $Y_1 = 0.5$ m
*   $q_1 = 1.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$
*   $Y_2 = 1.5$ m
*   $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$

**Solution:**

(a) **Verify positive surge:**
    *   Upstream depth $Y_1 = 0.5$ m.
    *   Upstream velocity $V_1 = q_1 / Y_1 = 1.0 / 0.5 = 2.0 \text{ m/s}$.
    *   Upstream Froude number $Fr_1 = V_1 / \sqrt{g Y_1} = 2.0 / \sqrt{9.81 \times 0.5} = 2.0 / \sqrt{4.905} = 2.0 / 2.215 \approx 0.90$. Since $Fr_1 < 1$, the upstream flow is subcritical.
    *   Downstream depth $Y_2 = 1.5$ m, which is greater than $Y_1$. This indicates a rising water level, characteristic of a positive surge.
    *   Downstream velocity $V_2 = q_1 / Y_2 = 1.0 / 1.5 \approx 0.667 \text{ m/s}$.
    *   Downstream Froude number $Fr_2 = V_2 / \sqrt{g Y_2} = 0.667 / \sqrt{9.81 \times 1.5} = 0.667 / \sqrt{14.715} = 0.667 / 3.836 \approx 0.17$.
    *   **Correction:** My initial assumption of $Y_2 > Y_c$ for positive surge may be misleading in some contexts. The key is the increase in depth and propagation. However, if we were to check the conjugate depth for $q_1=1.0$ and $Y_1=0.5$:
        $Y_2' = \frac{0.5}{2} (\sqrt{1 + 8(1.0^2 / (9.81 \times 0.5^3))} - 1)$
        $Y_2' = 0.25 (\sqrt{1 + 8 / (9.81 \times 0.125)} - 1)$
        $Y_2' = 0.25 (\sqrt{1 + 8 / 1.226} - 1)$
        $Y_2' = 0.25 (\sqrt{1 + 6.525} - 1) = 0.25 (\sqrt{7.525} - 1) = 0.25 (2.743 - 1) = 0.25 \times 1.743 \approx 0.436$ m.
        This shows that if the flow were to jump from $Y_1=0.5$ (subcritical) to a state with the same specific force, the other conjugate depth would be $0.436$ m, which is smaller. The scenario given ($Y_2=1.5$m) is not a direct hydraulic jump from $Y_1=0.5$m for the same discharge. The problem statement implies a surge that *causes* the depth to increase to $Y_2=1.5$m, likely due to an external factor increasing the total downstream volume. Let's proceed with the given $Y_2$.

    *   The problem states the depth *increases* to $Y_2 = 1.5$ m. This increase in depth from $Y_1 = 0.5$ m signifies a positive surge.

(b) **Calculate energy loss:**
    Using the formula for energy loss in a rectangular channel:
    $E_{loss} = \frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2}$
    $E_{loss} = \frac{(1.5 - 0.5)^3}{4 \times 0.5 \times 1.5}$
    $E_{loss} = \frac{(1.0)^3}{3.0}$
    $E_{loss} = \frac{1.0}{3.0} \approx 0.333 \text{ m}$

**Answer:**
(a) The scenario describes a positive surge as the water depth increases from 0.5 m to 1.5 m.
(b) The energy loss across the surge is approximately 0.333 m.

### **3. Concept of Negative Surges (Falling Surges / Drafting Waves)**

#### **3.1 Generation of Negative Surges**

A negative surge is generated by a sudden decrease in downstream discharge or a rapid reduction in the inflow to a channel section.

*   **Common Causes:**
    *   **Sudden gate closure:** Rapidly reducing flow from a reservoir.
    *   **Rapid draining of a tank or reservoir.**
    *   **Downstream structure failure** (e.g., breach in a levee at a lower elevation).
    *   **Tidal ebb flow** in estuaries.

#### **3.2 Characteristics of a Negative Surge**

*   **Wave Front:** A disturbance that causes a rapid drop in water depth.
*   **Propagation:** Can propagate upstream or downstream depending on the initial conditions and the nature of the disturbance. Often considered to propagate upstream in scenarios like gate closure to release stored water.
*   **Depth:** Decreases across the surge front.
*   **Velocity:** Increases across the surge front.
*   **Energy:** Specific energy generally decreases across a negative surge due to dissipation.

#### **3.3 Analysis of Negative Surges**

The analysis of negative surges is conceptually similar to positive surges, utilizing the momentum and continuity principles. However, the flow conditions (depth and velocity) change in the opposite direction.

*   **Upstream state (1):** Higher depth, lower velocity.
*   **Downstream state (2):** Lower depth, higher velocity.

The momentum equation for a rectangular channel still holds:
$$ \frac{q_1^2}{Y_1} + Y_1^2 = \frac{q_2^2}{Y_2} + Y_2^2 $$

However, for a negative surge, $Y_1 > Y_2$.

**Conjugate Depths:**
For a given discharge, the same conjugate depth relationship applies. If state 1 is subcritical ($Fr_1 < 1$), and the surge causes a decrease in depth, state 2 will be even more subcritical or potentially transition to supercritical if the velocity increase is significant enough and $Y_2$ is small.

The relationship between conjugate depths $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ for a given unit discharge $q$ is:
$$ Y_2 = \frac{Y_1}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{8q^2}{gY_1^3}} - 1 \right) $$
or
$$ Y_1 = \frac{Y_2}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{8q^2}{gY_2^3}} - 1 \right) $$

**Surge Celerity (Negative Surge):**

The speed of a negative surge relative to the bed can be approximated. If it's propagating upstream, the relative speed of the wave to the water upstream is $(V_1 - V_s)$.

The speed of the negative surge relative to the bed is often given by:
$$ V_s = V_1 - \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}} $$
Note the negative sign, indicating propagation against the flow direction or a slower downstream propagation. If this value is positive, it propagates downstream. If it's negative, it means it propagates upstream relative to the initial flow $V_1$.

**Energy Loss:**
Energy loss occurs in both types of surges due to friction and turbulence.

$$ E_{loss} = \left( Y_1 + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} \right) - \left( Y_2 + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} \right) $$
For a negative surge, $Y_1 > Y_2$ and $V_1 < V_2$. The specific energy generally decreases.

#### **3.4 Examples and Applications of Negative Surges**

*   **Reservoir Release:** When a gate at the outlet of a reservoir is partially closed, the water level in the reservoir rises, and a negative surge propagates upstream, signaling the reduced flow.
*   **Tidal Ebb:** As the tide recedes in an estuary, a falling water level and increasing flow velocity occur, which can be conceptualized as a negative surge.
*   **Channel Drainage:** Rapidly draining a channel section can lead to a falling water surface that propagates as a negative surge.

**Conceptual Problem:**

Imagine a wide, slow-moving river with a uniform depth of 3 m and a velocity of 0.5 m/s. Suddenly, a large sluice gate downstream is closed, causing the flow to be stopped entirely in that section. What would happen to the water surface upstream of the gate closure?

**Analysis:**
*   The closure of the gate creates a "blockage" for the downstream flow.
*   Water upstream of the blockage will start to pile up.
*   This piling up causes the water depth to increase, leading to a positive surge propagating downstream from the gate.
*   However, if we consider the instant of gate closure and the effect on the water body immediately behind the closed gate, the flow is forced to stop abruptly. This cessation of flow and subsequent rise in level is often modeled with complex unsteady flow equations.

Let's rephrase for a negative surge:
Imagine a wide, slow-moving river with a uniform depth of 3 m and a velocity of 0.5 m/s. Suppose a large volume of water is suddenly released from an upstream reservoir, causing the water level in the river to drop rapidly by 1 meter over a short period. What happens to the water surface?

**Analysis:**
*   This represents a significant decrease in water depth.
*   The velocity would tend to increase as the water "drafts" from upstream.
*   This phenomenon is characteristic of a negative surge. The rapid drop in water level signifies the passage of the surge front, where depth decreases and velocity increases.

**Example 3: Negative Surge Conjugate Depths**

A negative surge occurs in a rectangular channel where the upstream conditions are $Y_1 = 2.0$ m and $q_1 = 5.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$. This surge causes the depth to decrease to $Y_2 = 1.0$ m.

(a) Calculate the unit discharge in state 2.
(b) Calculate the energy loss across the surge.
(c) Determine the approximate speed of the negative surge relative to the bed, assuming it propagates upstream.

**Given:**
*   $Y_1 = 2.0$ m
*   $q_1 = 5.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$
*   $Y_2 = 1.0$ m
*   $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$

**Solution:**

(a) **Calculate unit discharge ($q_2$):**
    From the momentum equation for a rectangular channel:
    $\frac{q_1^2}{Y_1} + Y_1^2 = \frac{q_2^2}{Y_2} + Y_2^2$
    $\frac{5.0^2}{2.0} + 2.0^2 = \frac{q_2^2}{1.0} + 1.0^2$
    $\frac{25}{2.0} + 4.0 = q_2^2 + 1.0$
    $12.5 + 4.0 = q_2^2 + 1.0$
    $16.5 = q_2^2 + 1.0$
    $q_2^2 = 15.5$
    $q_2 = \sqrt{15.5} \approx 3.937 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$

    **Note:** For a negative surge, we generally expect $q_2 < q_1$. This result is unexpected. Let's re-examine the problem premise or the assumption that $q_1=q_2$ for a surge. In a true surge where there's no external inflow/outflow across the surge front itself, continuity ($q_1=q_2$) should hold. The fact that $q_2$ calculated is less than $q_1$ suggests that the conditions $Y_1, q_1, Y_2$ might not satisfy the momentum equation for a simple surge scenario or there's an external factor at play.

    Let's assume the problem implies $q_1 = q_2$ for the surge analysis, which is standard unless otherwise specified. Then, the problem setup might be flawed or it represents a specific scenario where discharge *does* change significantly across the wave. If we *must* use the momentum equation to find $q_2$ given $Y_1, Y_2$, then the above calculation is correct for *a* transition between these states, but not necessarily a surge where discharge is conserved.

    **Let's assume continuity holds ($q_1 = q_2$) for surge analysis, and recalculate $Y_2$ if needed, or use the given $Y_2$ and see if the problem is consistent.**

    Let's re-verify the conjugate depths for $q_1=5.0$ m³/s/m and $Y_1=2.0$ m.
    $Y_2' = \frac{Y_1}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{8q_1^2}{gY_1^3}} - 1 \right)$
    $Y_2' = \frac{2.0}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{8 \times 5.0^2}{9.81 \times 2.0^3}} - 1 \right)$
    $Y_2' = 1.0 \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{8 \times 25}{9.81 \times 8}} - 1 \right)$
    $Y_2' = \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{200}{78.48}} - 1 \right)$
    $Y_2' = \left( \sqrt{1 + 2.548} - 1 \right)$
    $Y_2' = \left( \sqrt{3.548} - 1 \right)$
    $Y_2' = 1.884 - 1 = 0.884$ m.

    So, if $Y_1=2.0$m and $q_1=5.0$ m³/s/m, the conjugate depth with the same discharge would be $Y_2' = 0.884$m, not $1.0$m.
    This means the problem as stated doesn't represent a simple surge where discharge is conserved between state 1 and state 2 using the standard conjugate depth relation.
    **However, we will proceed with the given values and the goal is to analyze the *transition* from state 1 to state 2 as described.**

    Let's assume the problem intends to ask for the change in state, not necessarily a pure surge with conserved discharge. If we MUST find $q_2$ given $Y_1, Y_2$ and the momentum equation holding:
    From (a), $q_2 \approx 3.937 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$. This suggests a decrease in discharge across the wave. This could happen if, for example, the wave front itself is leaky or if the upstream process of wave generation leads to a reduction in flow.

    **For the purpose of this study guide, we will assume the standard surge model where continuity holds, and if there's a discrepancy in the problem statement, we will highlight it and proceed with the most common interpretation (conserved discharge).**

    **Let's re-frame Example 3 with consistent values:**

    **Revised Example 3:**
    A negative surge occurs in a rectangular channel where the upstream conditions are $Y_1 = 2.0$ m and $q_1 = 5.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$. Calculate the downstream depth ($Y_2$) and energy loss across the surge, assuming continuity ($q_1 = q_2$) holds.

    **Given:**
    *   $Y_1 = 2.0$ m
    *   $q_1 = 5.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$
    *   $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$

    **Solution:**

    (a) **Calculate downstream depth ($Y_2$):**
        Using the conjugate depth equation:
        $Y_2 = \frac{Y_1}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + \frac{8q_1^2}{gY_1^3}} - 1 \right)$
        $Y_2 = 0.884$ m (as calculated above).

        Since $Y_2 < Y_1$, this is a valid negative surge scenario.

    (b) **Calculate energy loss:**
        First, find $V_1$ and $V_2$:
        $V_1 = q_1 / Y_1 = 5.0 / 2.0 = 2.5 \text{ m/s}$
        $V_2 = q_1 / Y_2 = 5.0 / 0.884 \approx 5.656 \text{ m/s}$

        Specific energy $E_1 = Y_1 + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} = 2.0 + \frac{2.5^2}{2 \times 9.81} = 2.0 + \frac{6.25}{19.62} = 2.0 + 0.319 = 2.319$ m.
        Specific energy $E_2 = Y_2 + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} = 0.884 + \frac{5.656^2}{2 \times 9.81} = 0.884 + \frac{31.99}{19.62} = 0.884 + 1.631 = 2.515$ m.

        **Correction:** $E_2 > E_1$? This is unusual for a negative surge where energy dissipation is expected. Let's recheck the formulas.
        The energy loss formula for rectangular channels is:
        $E_{loss} = \frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2}$
        $E_{loss} = \frac{(0.884 - 2.0)^3}{4 \times 2.0 \times 0.884}$
        $E_{loss} = \frac{(-1.116)^3}{7.072}$
        $E_{loss} = \frac{-1.391}{7.072} \approx -0.197$ m.
        A negative energy loss implies energy *gain*, which is physically impossible due to dissipation. This indicates that the simplified energy loss formula might not be directly applicable or the derived $Y_2$ leads to an inconsistency.

        **Let's use the specific energy difference:**
        $E_{loss} = E_1 - E_2 = 2.319 - 2.515 = -0.196$ m.
        This negative value still points to a potential issue with the problem's setup or the direct application of these simplified surge equations in this specific context.

        **Important Consideration:** The simplified surge equations (based on momentum and continuity across a thin front) assume a rapid transition. For very slow or gradual changes, or when friction is dominant, these might not accurately predict energy changes. The formula $E_{loss} = \frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2}$ is derived from the difference in specific energy when conjugate depths satisfy the momentum equation, and it inherently assumes energy dissipation. If the calculation results in negative loss, it might mean the given $Y_1, Y_2$ pair doesn't represent a typical dissipative surge or the model assumptions are stretched.

        **For exam purposes, if a problem gives $Y_1, Y_2, q_1$ and asks for energy loss, use the $E_1 - E_2$ method, but be aware of potential inconsistencies if the loss is negative.** The formula for energy loss is most reliably applied when the conjugate depths are calculated from a known state.

    (c) **Approximate surge speed:**
        Using the simplified formula for negative surge celerity (propagating upstream):
        $V_s = V_1 - \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}}$
        $V_s = 2.5 - \sqrt{9.81 \times \frac{2.0 + 0.884}{2}}$
        $V_s = 2.5 - \sqrt{9.81 \times \frac{2.884}{2}}$
        $V_s = 2.5 - \sqrt{9.81 \times 1.442}$
        $V_s = 2.5 - \sqrt{14.146}$
        $V_s = 2.5 - 3.763$
        $V_s \approx -1.263 \text{ m/s}$.

        The negative sign indicates that the surge is propagating *upstream* relative to the channel bed, with a speed of approximately 1.263 m/s.

### **4. Key Points to Remember**

*   **Surges are unsteady flow phenomena** characterized by rapid changes in depth and velocity.
*   **Positive surges** increase depth and decrease velocity, propagating downstream.
*   **Negative surges** decrease depth and increase velocity, potentially propagating upstream or downstream.
*   **Momentum and continuity equations** are fundamental for analyzing surges.
*   **Conjugate depths** relate flow conditions before and after a hydraulic jump or surge.
*   **Surge celerity ($V_s$)** is the speed of the surge front relative to the stationary bed.
    *   For positive surges: $V_s \approx V_1 + \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}}$
    *   For negative surges (propagating upstream): $V_s \approx V_1 - \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}}$
*   **Energy loss** occurs across surges due to dissipation. The formula $E_{loss} = \frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2}$ is often used for rectangular channels when $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ are conjugate depths.
*   **Froude number** helps identify flow regimes (subcritical $Fr<1$, supercritical $Fr>1$). Positive surges often transition from subcritical to supercritical or a higher Froude number state.
*   **Problem interpretation is key:** Always check if the given values are consistent with standard surge principles (e.g., conserved discharge for simple surge analysis).

### **5. Practice Questions**

**Question 1:**
A positive surge is propagating in a rectangular channel with a width of 10 m. Upstream of the surge, the flow depth is $Y_1 = 1.2$ m and the unit discharge is $q_1 = 4.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$. The surge causes the depth to increase to $Y_2 = 2.5$ m.
(a) Calculate the upstream velocity ($V_1$).
(b) Calculate the speed of the surge relative to the channel bed.
(c) Calculate the energy loss across the surge.

**Answer to Question 1:**
(a) $V_1 = q_1 / Y_1 = 4.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m} / 1.2 \text{ m} \approx 3.33 \text{ m/s}$
(b) $V_s = V_1 + \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}} = 3.33 + \sqrt{9.81 \times \frac{1.2+2.5}{2}} = 3.33 + \sqrt{9.81 \times 1.85} = 3.33 + \sqrt{18.1485} = 3.33 + 4.26 = 7.59 \text{ m/s}$
(c) $E_{loss} = \frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2} = \frac{(2.5 - 1.2)^3}{4 \times 1.2 \times 2.5} = \frac{(1.3)^3}{12} = \frac{2.197}{12} \approx 0.183 \text{ m}$

**Question 2:**
A negative surge occurs in a wide rectangular channel. Upstream conditions are $Y_1 = 4.0$ m and $q_1 = 10.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$. The downstream depth after the surge is $Y_2 = 2.0$ m. Assuming continuity ($q_1 = q_2$):
(a) Calculate the downstream unit discharge ($q_2$).
(b) Calculate the upstream velocity ($V_1$) and downstream velocity ($V_2$).
(c) Calculate the energy loss across the surge.
(d) Determine the approximate speed of the surge relative to the channel bed, assuming it propagates upstream.

**Answer to Question 2:**
(a) Assuming continuity, $q_2 = q_1 = 10.0 \text{ m}^3/\text{s/m}$.
(b) $V_1 = q_1 / Y_1 = 10.0 / 4.0 = 2.5 \text{ m/s}$
    $V_2 = q_2 / Y_2 = 10.0 / 2.0 = 5.0 \text{ m/s}$
(c) $E_{loss} = \frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2} = \frac{(2.0 - 4.0)^3}{4 \times 4.0 \times 2.0} = \frac{(-2.0)^3}{32} = \frac{-8.0}{32} = -0.25 \text{ m}$.
    *(As noted previously, a negative energy loss indicates potential inconsistency or limitations of the simplified formula. Using $E_1-E_2$ would yield a similar result.)*
    $E_1 = 4.0 + \frac{2.5^2}{2 \times 9.81} = 4.0 + 0.319 = 4.319$ m
    $E_2 = 2.0 + \frac{5.0^2}{2 \times 9.81} = 2.0 + 1.274 = 3.274$ m
    $E_{loss} = E_1 - E_2 = 4.319 - 3.274 = 1.045$ m.
    **Correction:** The energy loss formula $\frac{(Y_2 - Y_1)^3}{4 Y_1 Y_2}$ is only valid when $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ are conjugate depths satisfying the momentum equation with conserved discharge. Since $Y_1=4.0$ and $Y_2=2.0$ with $q=10.0$ does not satisfy the conjugate depth equation (we calculated $Y_2 \approx 2.83$m for $Y_1=4.0$ and $q=10.0$), we *must* use the specific energy difference method. Therefore, the energy loss is 1.045 m.
(d) $V_s = V_1 - \sqrt{g \frac{Y_1+Y_2}{2}} = 2.5 - \sqrt{9.81 \times \frac{4.0+2.0}{2}} = 2.5 - \sqrt{9.81 \times 3.0} = 2.5 - \sqrt{29.43} = 2.5 - 5.43 = -2.93 \text{ m/s}$.
    The surge propagates upstream at approximately 2.93 m/s.
