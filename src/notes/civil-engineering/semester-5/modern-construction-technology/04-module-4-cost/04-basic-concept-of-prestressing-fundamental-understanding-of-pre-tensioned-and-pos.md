# Modern Construction Technology - Module 4: Cost

## Topic: Basic Concepts of Prestressing

### Introduction to Prestressing

Prestressing is a technique used in concrete construction to introduce internal compressive stresses into the concrete *before* it is subjected to external service loads. This is done to counteract the tensile stresses that would otherwise develop in the concrete under these loads, thereby improving the structural performance and efficiency of concrete members.

**Key Concept:** The fundamental idea is to put the concrete in a state of compression where it is inherently strong, effectively cancelling out the tensile stresses caused by bending due to applied loads.

**Why Prestressing?**

*   **Increased Strength and Stiffness:** Prestressing allows concrete members to carry higher loads and exhibit less deflection.
*   **Reduced Cracking:** By maintaining compression, cracking under service loads is significantly reduced or eliminated.
*   **Economical Use of Materials:** Enables the use of thinner sections and less concrete and steel for the same load-carrying capacity compared to traditional reinforced concrete.
*   **Longer Spans:** Allows for the construction of longer spans for bridges, beams, and slabs.
*   **Improved Durability:** Reduced cracking leads to better protection of steel reinforcement from corrosion.

### Types of Prestressing

Prestressing can be broadly categorized based on when the prestressing force is applied relative to the casting of the concrete.

---

### 1. Pre-tensioned Construction

**Definition:** In pre-tensioned construction, the high-strength steel wires or strands are stretched (tensioned) *before* the concrete is cast. The concrete is then poured around these tensioned tendons. Once the concrete has gained sufficient strength, the tension in the steel is released, and the force is transferred to the concrete through bond action between the concrete and the steel.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Tensioning:** Achieved using hydraulic jacks or other tensioning devices that grip the steel tendons.
*   **Anchorage:** The tensioning is usually done between anchorages or abutments, or by gripping the steel against a structural member.
*   **Bond Transfer:** The prestressing force is transferred to the concrete by the bond between the concrete and the steel. This requires adequate concrete strength and sufficient length for the bond to develop.
*   **Casting:** Concrete is poured into formwork that encloses the tensioned tendons.
*   **Cutting:** After curing and achieving sufficient strength, the tendons are cut at the ends, releasing the tension.
*   **Applications:** Pre-tensioned members are typically manufactured in factories (precast concrete) and then transported to the construction site. Common applications include:
    *   Beams (e.g., for bridges, buildings)
    *   Slabs
    *   Poles and piles
    *   Textile reinforcement (e.g., in railway sleepers)

**Mechanism of Force Transfer (Pre-tensioned):**

1.  **Tensioning of Tendons:** Steel tendons are gripped and stretched to a predetermined force.
2.  **Casting Concrete:** Concrete is poured around the tensioned tendons.
3.  **Curing and Hardening:** The concrete cures and gains strength.
4.  **Release of Tension:** The external tensioning device is released. The stretched tendons attempt to contract, but their ends are anchored by the hardened concrete. This anchorage grips the steel, and the resulting pulling force on the concrete creates a compressive stress within it.

**Diagrammatic Representation (Conceptual):**

```
      +---------------------+
      |                     |
      |   Concrete Member   |
      |                     |
      +---------------------+
           | | | | | | |
           v v v v v v v  (Tensioned Tendons)
```

**Advantages of Pre-tensioning:**

*   Simpler casting process for mass production.
*   Good bond transfer due to direct contact.
*   Generally more economical for factory production.

**Disadvantages of Pre-tensioning:**

*   Limited to factory production (precast).
*   Requires strong anchorages to hold the tension.
*   End zone stresses can be complex and require special attention.
*   Losses due to elastic shortening of concrete.

---

### 2. Post-tensioned Construction

**Definition:** In post-tensioned construction, the high-strength steel tendons are placed in ducts or sheaths (usually plastic or metal tubes) within the concrete formwork *before* the concrete is cast. After the concrete has hardened and gained sufficient strength, the tendons are threaded through the ducts and tensioned. The force is then transferred to the concrete through mechanical anchorage devices at the ends of the tendons.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Ducts/Sheaths:** Tubes or channels are placed within the formwork to house the tendons.
*   **Tensioning:** Achieved using hydraulic jacks that bear against the hardened concrete structure itself.
*   **Anchorage:** Specialized devices (e.g., wedges, cones) are used to grip the tendons and anchor them against the concrete.
*   **Grouting (Optional but common):** After tensioning, the ducts are often filled with grout (a cementitious mixture) to:
    *   Bond the tendons to the concrete for better load transfer.
    *   Protect the steel from corrosion.
    *   Improve fire resistance.
*   **Applications:** Post-tensioning can be applied to both precast and cast-in-situ concrete. It's often used for:
    *   Bridges (especially longer spans and segmental construction)
    *   Buildings (slabs on grade, flat slabs, beams, walls)
    *   Water tanks and containment structures
    *   Nuclear power plants

**Mechanism of Force Transfer (Post-tensioned):**

1.  **Placing Ducts and Reinforcement:** Ducts and conventional reinforcement are placed in the formwork.
2.  **Casting Concrete:** Concrete is poured, surrounding the ducts.
3.  **Curing and Hardening:** Concrete cures and gains strength.
4.  **Tensioning Tendons:** Tendons are inserted into the ducts. Hydraulic jacks are used to grip the tendons at one end and pull them, stretching them against a reaction (usually the concrete member itself).
5.  **Anchoring Tendons:** Anchorage devices are fitted at the ends of the tendons to hold them in their tensioned state.
6.  **Grouting (if applicable):** Ducts are filled with grout.

**Diagrammatic Representation (Conceptual):**

```
      +---------------------+
      |                     |
      |   Concrete Member   |
      |   [--------] <---- Duct
      |   [--------] <---- Duct
      |                     |
      +---------------------+
           ^ ^ ^ ^ ^ ^ ^  (Tendons threaded through ducts, tensioned)
```

**Advantages of Post-tensioning:**

*   Can be used for both precast and cast-in-situ construction.
*   Allows for longer spans and more complex shapes.
*   Reduced loss of prestress due to friction and wobble.
*   Allows for adjustments to the prestressing force.

**Disadvantages of Post-tensioning:**

*   More complex process, requiring specialized equipment and skilled labor.
*   Anchorage zones require careful design to handle concentrated stresses.
*   Potential for friction losses along the tendon path.
*   Grouting adds an extra step and cost.

### Key Differences: Pre-tensioned vs. Post-tensioned

| Feature           | Pre-tensioned Construction                                | Post-tensioned Construction                                 |
| :---------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tensioning Time** | Before concrete casting                                   | After concrete hardening                                    |
| **Force Transfer** | Bond between steel and concrete                           | Mechanical anchorages                                       |
| **Ducts/Sheaths** | Not required                                              | Required to house tendons                                   |
| **Production**    | Primarily factory-made (precast)                          | Factory-made (precast) or site-cast (cast-in-situ)          |
| **Flexibility**   | Less flexible; suitable for standard shapes               | More flexible; suitable for various shapes and spans        |
| **Equipment**     | Tensioning beds, anchorages for beds                      | Hydraulic jacks, anchorages, grout pumps, ducts             |
| **End Zones**     | Bond development is critical; potential for end-splitting | Anchorage zones require specific design for stress transfer |
| **Cost**          | Generally lower for standard precast elements           | Can be higher due to complexity and equipment               |

### Loss of Prestress

The initial prestressing force applied to the tendons is not fully maintained in the concrete due to various factors. These losses reduce the effective prestress.

**Types of Losses (Important to understand for effective prestressing):**

1.  **Elastic Shortening of Concrete:**
    *   **Pre-tensioned:** When the tendons are released, the concrete shortens elastically under the instantaneous compressive stress. This elastic shortening causes the tendons to elongate, losing some of their tension.
    *   **Post-tensioned:** If multiple tendons are tensioned sequentially, the concrete shortens elastically under the stress from each tendon. This loss is usually less significant than in pre-tensioned members.

2.  **Creep of Concrete:**
    *   Over time, concrete continues to deform under sustained stress (creep). This deformation causes the concrete to shorten, which in turn leads to a loss of tension in the prestressing steel.

3.  **Shrinkage of Concrete:**
    *   As concrete dries and hardens, it shrinks. This shrinkage causes the concrete to become shorter, pulling on the prestressing steel and reducing its tension. This loss is more significant in pre-tensioned members where the bond is direct.

4.  **Relaxation of Steel:**
    *   High-tensile steel wires or strands, when held at a constant strain for extended periods, exhibit a reduction in stress over time. This phenomenon is called relaxation.

5.  **Friction (Primarily in Post-tensioning):**
    *   **Curvature Effect:** Due to the curvature of the tendons in post-tensioned members (e.g., draped tendons), there is a loss of force as the tendon slides against the duct.
    *   **Wobble Effect:** Variations in the alignment of the ducts along the length of the member can cause a frictional loss as the tendon is tensioned.

6.  **Anchorage Slip (Primarily in Post-tensioning):**
    *   When the tensioning jack is released, the anchorages (wedges) may slip slightly, causing a reduction in the effective prestressing force.

**Important Note:** Understanding and quantifying these losses is crucial for accurately calculating the effective prestress and designing safe and efficient prestressed concrete structures.

### Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:**
Briefly explain the fundamental principle of prestressing concrete.

**Answer 1:**
Prestressing involves introducing internal compressive stresses into concrete before it is subjected to external service loads. This is done to counteract the tensile stresses that would otherwise develop under load, thereby improving the concrete's ability to resist bending and cracking.

**Question 2:**
Describe the process of pre-tensioned construction and how the prestressing force is transferred to the concrete.

**Answer 2:**
In pre-tensioned construction, high-strength steel tendons are tensioned *before* concrete casting. Concrete is then poured around these tensioned tendons. Once the concrete hardens sufficiently, the tension in the steel is released. The prestressing force is transferred to the concrete through the **bond** that develops between the steel and the concrete.

**Question 3:**
Describe the process of post-tensioned construction and how the prestressing force is transferred to the concrete.

**Answer 3:**
In post-tensioned construction, tendons are placed in ducts within the formwork *before* concrete casting. After the concrete hardens, tendons are threaded through the ducts and tensioned using hydraulic jacks. The force is then transferred to the concrete via **mechanical anchorages** at the ends of the tendons. Ducts are often grouted afterwards.

**Question 4:**
List three key differences between pre-tensioned and post-tensioned construction.

**Answer 4:**
Three key differences are:
1.  **Tensioning Time:** Pre-tensioned = before casting; Post-tensioned = after hardening.
2.  **Force Transfer:** Pre-tensioned = bond; Post-tensioned = mechanical anchorages.
3.  **Ducts:** Pre-tensioned = not required; Post-tensioned = required.

**Question 5:**
What is the primary reason for grouting the ducts in post-tensioned construction?

**Answer 5:**
The primary reasons for grouting are:
*   To bond the tendons to the concrete, improving load transfer and reducing losses.
*   To protect the steel tendons from corrosion.
*   To improve fire resistance.

**Question 6:**
Identify two major sources of prestress loss in concrete members.

**Answer 6:**
Two major sources of prestress loss are:
*   Elastic shortening of concrete (especially in pre-tensioned)
*   Creep of concrete
*   Shrinkage of concrete
*   Relaxation of steel
*   Friction (in post-tensioned)
*   Anchorage slip (in post-tensioned)
*(Any two of these are acceptable)*

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Prestressing is about creating internal compression to resist tensile stresses.**
*   **Pre-tensioning relies on bond transfer of force; post-tensioning relies on mechanical anchorages.**
*   **Pre-tensioning is typically for factory-made precast elements.**
*   **Post-tensioning offers more flexibility for site-cast elements and longer spans.**
*   **Losses of prestress are inevitable and must be accounted for in design.**
*   **The choice between pre-tensioning and post-tensioning depends on factors like span, production method, economics, and structural requirements.**
