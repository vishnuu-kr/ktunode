# TRANSPORTATION ENGINEERING: TUNNEL ENGINEERING

This module introduces the fundamental aspects of tunnel engineering, focusing on tunnel cross-sections, surveying techniques for alignment, and methods for transferring horizontal and vertical control into the tunnel.

## 1. Tunnel Sections

A tunnel section refers to the shape and dimensions of the excavated tunnel. The choice of tunnel section is dictated by various factors, including the purpose of the tunnel, geological conditions, construction method, and economic considerations.

### 1.1 Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Cross-Sectional Area:** The area of the tunnel opening perpendicular to its longitudinal axis. This directly influences the volume of material to be excavated and the capacity of the tunnel.
*   **Shape of the Tunnel:** The geometric form of the tunnel's cross-section.
*   **Lining:** The structural material installed within the excavated tunnel to provide support, prevent collapse, and protect against environmental factors.
*   **Excavation Method:** The technique used to remove the rock or soil to create the tunnel opening.

### 1.2 Common Tunnel Section Shapes

The shape of a tunnel section is crucial for structural stability, ease of excavation, and clearance for the intended use.

*   **Circular Sections:**
    *   **Description:** A perfect circle.
    *   **Advantages:**
        *   **Hydrostatically sound:** Distributes internal and external pressures evenly.
        *   **Structurally efficient:** Offers maximum strength for a given cross-sectional area, making it ideal for soft ground or areas with high external pressure.
        *   **Uniform lining thickness:** Simplifies construction and reduces material waste.
        *   **Ideal for TBM (Tunnel Boring Machine) excavation:** TBMs naturally create circular openings.
    *   **Disadvantages:**
        *   Less efficient for space utilization for road or rail traffic compared to other shapes.
        *   Requires more excavation volume than other shapes for the same functional width.
    *   **Examples:** Sub-surface metro tunnels, many large-diameter water supply tunnels, tunnels constructed with TBMs.

*   **Horseshoe Sections (or Arch-Culvert Sections):**
    *   **Description:** Characterized by a rounded arch at the top and a relatively flat or slightly sloped invert (bottom) section.
    *   **Advantages:**
        *   **Good compromise:** Combines structural efficiency of the arch with better clearance for road or rail traffic.
        *   **Efficient excavation:** Can be excavated using drill-and-blast or roadheader methods.
        *   **More practical for road/rail:** Provides better headroom and shoulder space.
    *   **Disadvantages:**
        *   Requires more complex lining construction than a circular tunnel.
        *   Invert section can be prone to structural issues if not properly designed and constructed.
    *   **Examples:** Many road tunnels, railway tunnels, and older tunnel constructions.

*   **Rectangular or Box Sections:**
    *   **Description:** Primarily used for underground structures like subways or underpasses where the depth is limited and the primary concern is space utilization.
    *   **Advantages:**
        *   **Maximum usable space:** Efficient for accommodating multiple tracks or wide roadways.
        *   **Relatively simple construction:** Can be built using cut-and-cover methods.
    *   **Disadvantages:**
        *   **Structurally inefficient:** Corners concentrate stresses, making them weaker against external pressures.
        *   Requires substantial internal bracing or thicker walls.
        *   Not suitable for deep tunnels or areas with significant ground pressure.
    *   **Examples:** Sub-surface metro stations, underpasses, culverts.

*   **Segmental or D-Shaped Sections:**
    *   **Description:** Often used in soft ground tunneling with TBMs. Consists of precast concrete segments that form a curved tunnel lining.
    *   **Advantages:**
        *   **Rapid construction:** Segments are installed as the TBM advances.
        *   **Structural integrity:** Provides a robust lining.
    *   **Disadvantages:**
        *   Limited flexibility in diameter.
        *   Cost of precast segments.
    *   **Examples:** Modern urban subway tunnels constructed with TBMs.

### 1.3 Factors Influencing Section Choice

*   **Geology and Ground Conditions:**
    *   **Hard Rock:** Can often accommodate more varied shapes, including less regular ones.
    *   **Soft Ground/Unstable Soil:** Favors circular or near-circular shapes for maximum stability and uniform pressure distribution.
*   **Intended Use:**
    *   **Roads/Railways:** Require specific clearance envelopes for vehicles, tracks, and overhead equipment. Horseshoe or modified circular shapes are common.
    *   **Water Conveyance:** Circular sections are often preferred due to hydraulic efficiency and pressure resistance.
    *   **Pedestrian Tunnels:** Can have simpler shapes depending on width requirements.
*   **Construction Method:**
    *   **TBM:** Naturally produces circular tunnels.
    *   **Drill-and-Blast/Roadheader:** Can create a wider range of shapes, including horseshoe.
    *   **Cut-and-Cover:** Typically results in rectangular or box sections.
*   **Lining Material and Design:** The structural capacity of the lining material and the chosen support system influence the required shape.
*   **Cost:** Excavation volume, lining material, and construction time all contribute to the overall cost, influencing the choice of section.
*   **Excavation Support Requirements:** The need for immediate support during excavation can dictate the shape.

### 1.4 Practice Question

**Question:** A tunnel is planned for a new high-speed railway line through stable, hard rock. The primary considerations are maximizing train speed and ensuring structural integrity. Which tunnel section would likely be the most suitable, and why?

**Answer:** A **circular or slightly modified horseshoe section** would likely be the most suitable.
*   **Circular:** Offers excellent structural integrity against external pressures (though less critical in stable rock) and is efficient for TBM excavation if that method is chosen. However, it might require more excavation volume and a less optimized clearance for the train compared to a horseshoe.
*   **Horseshoe:** Provides a good balance between structural efficiency (due to the arch) and optimal clearance for the railway, allowing for better headroom and potential service areas. It is also amenable to drill-and-blast or roadheader excavation.

**Important Point to Remember:** The choice of tunnel section is a multifaceted decision involving a trade-off between structural requirements, functional needs, construction methods, and cost.

## 2. Tunnel Surveying - Alignment

Tunnel surveying is critical for accurately establishing the tunnel's intended path and guiding its construction. This involves determining the horizontal and vertical position of the tunnel in relation to established ground control points.

### 2.1 Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Alignment:** The horizontal and vertical path of the tunnel.
*   **Ground Control:** Reference points established on the surface (horizontal and vertical) used to control the tunnel alignment.
*   **Advance Control:** Surveying points established *within* the tunnel to guide excavation.
*   **Face:** The advancing end of the tunnel excavation.
*   **Bench Marks (BMs):** Points of known elevation used for vertical control.
*   **Control Points:** Points with known horizontal (x, y) and vertical (z) coordinates.
*   **Tunnel Coordinate System:** A local coordinate system established for the tunnel to simplify calculations and operations.

### 2.2 Establishing Tunnel Alignment

1.  **Surface Control Establishment:**
    *   **Horizontal Control:** Precise surveys (e.g., triangulation, traversing, GNSS) are used to establish a network of control points on the surface, tied to national or regional geodetic networks.
    *   **Vertical Control:** Leveling surveys are conducted to establish reliable benchmarks along the tunnel route.

2.  **Route Reconnaissance and Preliminary Alignment:**
    *   Geological surveys, topographical mapping, and engineering studies inform the preliminary alignment of the tunnel.

3.  **Transferring Surface Control to Tunnel Entrances:**
    *   **Shaft Transfer:** If shafts are used for access, specialized methods are employed to transfer surface control down the shaft. This can involve:
        *   **Plumbing (Wire or Laser):** Precisely suspending wires or lasers down the shaft and observing their position relative to surface control.
        *   **Gyroscopic Theodolites:** Used to establish accurate bearings in the absence of direct line-of-sight.
    *   **Portal Transfer:** For tunnels with portals (entrances) at the surface, direct observation from surface control points is used.

4.  **Establishing Advance Control (Tunnel Traverse):**
    *   A network of control points (e.g., tunnel hubs or bolts) is established along the tunnel walls or roof at regular intervals.
    *   **Traversing:** A closed traverse is run from the established tunnel entrance control point into the tunnel, measuring angles and distances to these advance control points.
    *   **Theodolites and EDM (Electronic Distance Measurement) equipment:** Modern total stations are essential for accurate angle and distance measurements.
    *   **Precise leveling:** Used to establish the vertical control (grade) along the tunnel.

5.  **Monitoring and Adjustment:**
    *   The tunnel traverse is continuously monitored and adjusted for accumulated errors.
    *   Regular checks against the intended alignment are performed.

### 2.3 Tunnel Alignment Data

*   **Horizontal Alignment:** Specified by a series of straight lines (tangents) and curves (circular, parabolic).
*   **Vertical Alignment (Grade):** Specified by a series of straight lines (grades) and vertical curves (parabolic) to ensure smooth transitions in elevation.

### 2.4 Practice Question

**Question:** During the construction of a tunnel, the survey team has established a precise traverse from the portal. They need to set out a curve of 500m radius with a deflection angle of 15 degrees. What are the key elements they need to calculate and measure to guide the excavation?

**Answer:** The survey team needs to calculate and measure:
*   **Tangent Length (T):** The distance from the Point of Intersection (PI) of the tangents to the beginning (BC) and end (EC) of the curve. $T = R \tan(\Delta/2)$, where R is the radius and $\Delta$ is the deflection angle.
*   **Length of the Curve (L):** The distance along the arc of the curve from BC to EC. $L = R \times (\Delta \times \pi/180)$, where $\Delta$ is in radians.
*   **Chainage of BC and EC:** The cumulative distance along the tunnel alignment where the curve begins and ends.
*   **Setting Out Points:** Points along the curve at regular intervals (e.g., every 10m or 20m) with their offsets and bearings to guide the excavation face. This involves calculating chord lengths and deflection angles for setting out the curve using theodolite and tape/EDM.

**Important Point to Remember:** Accuracy in tunnel surveying is paramount to avoid costly deviations, structural problems, and safety hazards.

## 3. Transferring Centre Grade into Tunnel

This process involves accurately transferring the calculated horizontal position (centerline) and vertical position (grade) from the surface control points into the tunnel excavation face.

### 3.1 Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Centerline:** The horizontal projection of the tunnel's longitudinal axis.
*   **Grade Line:** The vertical profile of the tunnel's longitudinal axis.
*   **Survey Marks:** Points established within the tunnel to represent the centerline and grade.
*   **Offset:** The perpendicular distance from a survey mark to the desired centerline or grade line.
*   **Drop (or Fall):** The vertical distance from a horizontal line to a point on the grade line.
*   **Hubs/Bolts:** Steel pins or bolts embedded in the tunnel roof or walls to serve as survey reference points.

### 3.2 Transferring Centerline (Horizontal Control)

1.  **Establish Horizontal Control Points:**
    *   From the established advance control points in the tunnel, a series of reference points are set out along the tunnel walls. These are typically "hubs" or bolts driven into the rock or concrete lining.
    *   The **centerline** of the tunnel is then established relative to these hubs.

2.  **Methods for Transferring Centerline:**
    *   **Theodolite and Tape/EDM:**
        *   The surveyor sets up the theodolite over a known hub.
        *   The target (prism or reflective surface) is moved along the tunnel wall, and precise angles and distances are measured.
        *   The desired centerline is projected by setting out points with specific offsets from the wall hubs.
        *   **Key technique:** Establishing a baseline along the tunnel side and then "dropping" lines perpendicular to this baseline to define the centerline.
    *   **Laser Alignment Systems:**
        *   A laser beam is projected from a stable mount at the tunnel portal or a shaft.
        *   The laser beam defines a straight line, effectively projecting the centerline.
        *   The excavation crew uses the laser beam as a reference to maintain the desired position.
        *   **Advantages:** Can provide a continuous reference line.
        *   **Challenges:** Susceptible to blockage by debris, dust, and vibrations; requires precise initial setup.

3.  **Setting Out Points on the Face:**
    *   Points representing the desired tunnel excavation limits (e.g., top, bottom, sides) are marked on the tunnel face based on the established centerline and grade.
    *   This is often done using:
        *   **Plumb bobs or weighted strings:** To transfer horizontal positions vertically.
        *   **Levels or tilting levels:** To transfer vertical positions horizontally.
        *   **Temporary survey stakes or marks:** Painted directly on the rock or sprayed onto the face.

### 3.3 Transferring Grade (Vertical Control)

1.  **Establish Vertical Control Points:**
    *   From established benchmarks at the tunnel entrance or shaft, precise leveling is carried out to establish vertical control points (hubs) within the tunnel.

2.  **Methods for Transferring Grade:**
    *   **Precise Leveling:**
        *   A precise level is set up at a known elevation.
        *   A leveling staff is held at survey marks (hubs) along the tunnel walls or roof.
        *   This allows the surveyor to determine the actual elevation of these hubs and therefore establish the grade.
    *   **Zenith or Nadir Plumbing (for Shafts):**
        *   **Zenith Plumbing:** A precise theodolite is used at the surface to sight a plumb line (wire or laser) precisely above a ground control point. A sensitive level is then used to transfer this vertical line down the shaft.
        *   **Nadir Plumbing:** The process is reversed, with the theodolite at the bottom of the shaft sighting upwards to a plumb line.
        *   These plumb lines (or projected laser lines) are then used as references to establish the tunnel's centerline and grade.
    *   **Vertical Curves:** If the tunnel alignment includes vertical curves, these are calculated and the corresponding grades are set out at intervals along the curve.

3.  **Setting Out Grade on the Face:**
    *   The target grade for the excavation is marked on the face.
    *   This is often done by setting out points at the desired elevation relative to the surveyed hubs.
    *   For example, if a hub is at a known elevation and the desired tunnel invert is 3 meters below the hub's elevation, a mark is made 3 meters vertically below that hub.

### 3.4 Maintaining Control

*   **Regular Checks:** Surveyors must continuously check and re-establish control points as excavation progresses.
*   **Error Accumulation:** Traverses are subject to error accumulation, so frequent re-sectioning and checks are vital.
*   **Communication:** Close communication between surveyors and excavation crews is essential.

### 3.5 Practice Question

**Question:** A tunnel is being excavated using a roadheader. The surveyor has established a hub on the tunnel roof at the correct chainage and offset, with a known elevation. The excavation crew needs to know where to cut the tunnel floor (invert) relative to this hub. If the designed tunnel invert is 5.2 meters below the centerline, and the centerline is 0.8 meters below the hub on the roof, what is the vertical distance from the hub to the desired excavation floor?

**Answer:**
*   Distance from hub to centerline = 0.8 meters (downwards)
*   Distance from centerline to tunnel invert = 5.2 meters (downwards)
*   Total vertical distance from hub to tunnel invert = 0.8 m + 5.2 m = **6.0 meters** (downwards from the hub).

The surveyor would mark a point 6.0 meters vertically below the established roof hub to guide the roadheader operator on the desired floor level.

**Important Point to Remember:** The accuracy of transferring centerline and grade directly impacts the excavation's positional accuracy, ensuring it stays within design limits and avoids costly over-break or under-break.

## Summary of Key Takeaways

*   **Tunnel Sections:** Circular sections are structurally efficient and ideal for TBMs, while horseshoe sections offer better clearance for road/rail. Rectangular sections are used for shallow structures. The choice depends on geology, use, construction, and cost.
*   **Tunnel Surveying - Alignment:** Establishing and maintaining precise horizontal and vertical control on the surface and transferring it accurately into the tunnel is fundamental. This involves precise traversing, leveling, and specialized techniques for shafts.
*   **Transferring Centre Grade:** This is the practical application of survey data. The centerline and grade are transferred to the excavation face using methods like theodolite and tape/EDM, or laser systems, ensuring the tunnel is excavated along its designed path. Accurate marking of excavation limits is crucial for the drilling and blasting or roadheader operations.
