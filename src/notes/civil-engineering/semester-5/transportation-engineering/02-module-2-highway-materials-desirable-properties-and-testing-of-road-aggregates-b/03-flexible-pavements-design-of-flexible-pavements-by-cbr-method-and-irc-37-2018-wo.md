## 1. Desirable Properties and Testing of Road Aggregates

Aggregates form the backbone of flexible pavements, providing strength and stability. They are broadly classified into coarse aggregates, fine aggregates, and filler materials.

### 1.1 Desirable Properties of Road Aggregates:

*   **Strength:** Ability to resist crushing under loads.
    *   **Importance:** Prevents premature failure of the pavement due to traffic loads.
*   **Hardness:** Resistance to abrasion and weathering.
    *   **Importance:** Prevents excessive wear and tear, especially in wearing courses.
*   **Toughness:** Resistance to impact or shock.
    *   **Importance:** Important for aggregates subjected to impact forces from heavy traffic.
*   **Shape:** Angular, cubical, or flaky/elongated.
    *   **Importance:** Angular and cubical aggregates provide better interlocking and stability. Flaky and elongated aggregates are undesirable due to poor interlocking and potential for breakage.
*   **Texture:** Surface roughness.
    *   **Importance:** Rough textured aggregates provide better adhesion with the binder.
*   **Size and Gradation:** Distribution of aggregate particles by size.
    *   **Importance:** Proper gradation ensures dense packing, minimizing voids and maximizing strength.
*   **Cleanliness:** Absence of deleterious materials like clay, silt, organic matter.
    *   **Importance:** Deleterious materials reduce the strength and durability of the pavement.
*   **Soundness:** Resistance to weathering and disintegration.
    *   **Importance:** Prevents degradation of aggregates due to freezing and thawing cycles or chemical reactions.

### 1.2 Testing of Road Aggregates:

The following tests are crucial for evaluating the suitability of aggregates:

#### a) Aggregate Crushing Value (ACV) Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the resistance to crushing under a gradually applied compressive load.
*   **Principle:** A sample of aggregate is subjected to a compressive load in a testing machine. The percentage of material passing a 2.36 mm sieve after crushing is the aggregate crushing value.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  A cylindrical mold is filled with oven-dried aggregate of specific size range.
    2.  A plunger is placed on top of the aggregate.
    3.  The load is applied at a controlled rate (10-15 tonnes/minute) up to 40 tonnes for normal aggregates and 20 tonnes for soft aggregates.
    4.  The load is maintained for 10 minutes.
    5.  The crushed material is sieved.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ ACV (\%) = \frac{W_2}{W_1} \times 100 $$
    Where:
    *   $W_1$ = weight of oven-dried sample
    *   $W_2$ = weight of fraction passing 2.36 mm sieve after crushing
*   **Desirable Values (IRC):**
    *   Wearing course: Max 30%
    *   Base course: Max 35%
    *   Sub-base course: Max 40%

#### b) Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the resistance to impact or shock loading.
*   **Principle:** A sample of aggregate is subjected to repeated impacts by a hammer of a specific weight falling from a specific height. The percentage of material passing a 2.36 mm sieve after 15 blows is the aggregate impact value.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  A cylindrical mold is filled with oven-dried aggregate of specific size range.
    2.  A hammer of 13.5-14.0 kg falls from a height of 38 cm.
    3.  15 blows are applied.
    4.  The crushed material is sieved.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ AIV (\%) = \frac{W_2}{W_1} \times 100 $$
    Where:
    *   $W_1$ = weight of oven-dried sample
    *   $W_2$ = weight of fraction passing 2.36 mm sieve after impact
*   **Desirable Values (IRC):**
    *   Wearing course: Max 30%
    *   Base course: Max 35%
    *   Sub-base course: Max 40%

#### c) Los Angeles Abrasion Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the resistance to abrasion and crushing.
*   **Principle:** Aggregates are placed in a rotating steel cylinder along with a specified number of steel balls. The rotation causes the aggregates to be abraded and crushed. The percentage of material passing a 1.70 mm sieve after 500 revolutions is the Los Angeles Abrasion Value.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  A specific weight of aggregate and steel balls are placed in the Los Angeles abrasion machine.
    2.  The machine is rotated at a speed of 30-33 rpm for 500 revolutions.
    3.  The material is sieved.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ LAAV (\%) = \frac{W_2}{W_1} \times 100 $$
    Where:
    *   $W_1$ = weight of oven-dried sample
    *   $W_2$ = weight of fraction passing 1.70 mm sieve after rotation
*   **Desirable Values (IRC):**
    *   Wearing course: Max 30%
    *   Base course: Max 35%
    *   Sub-base course: Max 40%

#### d) Attrition Value Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the resistance to wear by friction.
*   **Principle:** Aggregates are placed in a rotating cylinder with steel balls. The rotation causes attrition (wear) due to friction. The percentage of fine material produced is the attrition value.
*   **Desirable Values (IRC):** Max 35%

#### e) Water Absorption Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the amount of water absorbed by aggregates.
*   **Principle:** Aggregates are saturated with water and their weights are measured in dry, saturated surface dry (SSD), and wet conditions.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ Water Absorption (\%) = \frac{W_{SSD} - W_{dry}}{W_{dry}} \times 100 $$
*   **Importance:** High absorption indicates porous aggregates, which can lead to frost damage and reduced durability.

#### f) Flakiness and Elongation Index Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the percentage of flaky (thickness < 0.6 times average dimension) and elongated (length > 1.8 times average dimension) particles.
*   **Principle:** Aggregates are passed through sieves with slots and templates to separate flaky and elongated particles.
*   **Desirable Values (IRC):**
    *   Flakiness Index: Max 15%
    *   Elongation Index: Max 15%

#### g) Specific Gravity and Bulk Density Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the density of aggregates.
*   **Principle:** Measures the weight of a unit volume of aggregate, including voids.
*   **Importance:** Important for mix design calculations and stability analysis.

## 2. Desirable Properties and Testing of Bituminous Materials

Bituminous materials (bitumen, asphalt cement, cutbacks, emulsions) act as binders in flexible pavements, holding aggregates together and providing waterproofing.

### 2.1 Desirable Properties of Bituminous Materials:

*   **Ductility:** Ability to stretch without breaking.
    *   **Importance:** Ensures cohesion and prevents cracking under traffic loads.
*   **Viscosity:** Resistance to flow at different temperatures.
    *   **Importance:** Affects workability during construction and performance under varying temperatures.
*   **Adhesion (Affinity):** Ability to stick to aggregate surfaces.
    *   **Importance:** Crucial for the durability of the asphalt mix.
*   **Cohesion:** Internal resistance to deformation.
    *   **Importance:** Contributes to the strength of the bituminous layer.
*   **Temperature Susceptibility:** The degree to which viscosity changes with temperature.
    *   **Importance:** Binders with low temperature susceptibility are preferred for a wider range of operating temperatures.
*   **Stripping Resistance:** Resistance to separation from aggregate surfaces due to the presence of water.
    *   **Importance:** Prevents premature pavement failure.
*   **Durability:** Resistance to aging and weathering.
    *   **Importance:** Ensures long-term performance.

### 2.2 Testing of Bituminous Materials:

The following tests are commonly performed:

#### a) Penetration Test:

*   **Purpose:** To measure the hardness or consistency of bitumen.
*   **Principle:** A standard needle is allowed to penetrate a sample of bitumen under a standard load for 5 seconds at a standard temperature (25°C). The depth of penetration is measured in tenths of a millimeter.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  A sample of bitumen is placed in a container and allowed to reach the standard temperature.
    2.  A standard needle is loaded with a standard weight.
    3.  The needle is allowed to penetrate the bitumen for 5 seconds.
    4.  The depth of penetration is measured.
*   **Classification:** Bitumen is graded based on its penetration value (e.g., 60/70 bitumen means penetration range between 60 and 70 at 25°C).

#### b) Viscosity Test (Kinematic and Dynamic):

*   **Purpose:** To measure the resistance to flow.
*   **Principle:**
    *   **Kinematic Viscosity:** Measured by timing the flow of a fixed volume of bitumen through a capillary tube under gravity at a specific temperature. Units are Stokes (cm²/s) or Centistokes (cSt).
    *   **Dynamic Viscosity:** Measured by timing the flow of bitumen through a capillary tube under a constant pressure. Units are Poises (P) or Centipoises (cP).
*   **Importance:** Crucial for determining the appropriate grade of bitumen for mixing and compaction temperatures.

#### c) Softening Point Test (Ring and Ball):

*   **Purpose:** To determine the temperature at which bitumen becomes soft and starts to flow.
*   **Principle:** A steel ball is placed on a disc of bitumen supported in a ring. The assembly is heated in a water bath at a controlled rate. The softening point is the temperature at which the ball touches a plate placed at the bottom of the ring.
*   **Importance:** Indicates the tendency of bitumen to become fluid at high temperatures. Higher softening point is desirable for warmer climates.

#### d) Ductility Test:

*   **Purpose:** To measure the ability of bitumen to stretch without breaking.
*   **Principle:** A briquette of bitumen is pulled apart at a slow, constant speed at a specific temperature (25°C). The distance the bitumen can stretch before breaking is its ductility.
*   **Units:** Centimeters.
*   **Importance:** Indicates the cohesive property of the binder.

#### e) Flash and Fire Point Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the temperatures at which bitumen vapors ignite and sustain combustion.
*   **Principle:** A sample of bitumen is heated at a controlled rate, and a flame is applied periodically.
    *   **Flash Point:** The lowest temperature at which the vapor ignites momentarily.
    *   **Fire Point:** The lowest temperature at which the vapor ignites and burns continuously.
*   **Importance:** Safety during handling and transportation. Higher flash and fire points are desirable to prevent ignition during heating.

#### f) Solubility Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the amount of bitumen soluble in a specific solvent (e.g., Trichloroethylene).
*   **Principle:** Bitumen is dissolved in the solvent, and the insoluble residue is weighed.
*   **Importance:** Indicates the presence of impurities or non-bituminous matter. Pure bitumen is completely soluble.

#### g) Stripping Test (Water Susceptibility Test):

*   **Purpose:** To evaluate the affinity of bitumen to aggregate in the presence of water.
*   **Principle:** Aggregates coated with bitumen are immersed in water for a period. The percentage of aggregate surface that remains coated is assessed.
*   **Importance:** Crucial for preventing stripping and ensuring pavement durability.

## 3. Properties and Testing of Subgrade Soil

The subgrade is the foundation layer upon which the pavement structure is built. Its strength and stability are paramount.

### 3.1 Desirable Properties of Subgrade Soil:

*   **High Bearing Capacity:** Ability to support the pavement structure and traffic loads without excessive deformation.
*   **Good Drainage:** Ability to drain water effectively to prevent saturation and loss of strength.
*   **Low Plasticity:** Minimize volume changes due to moisture variations.
*   **Resistance to Swelling and Shrinkage:** Important for expansive soils.
*   **Good Compaction Characteristics:** Allows for efficient densification during construction.

### 3.2 Testing of Subgrade Soil:

#### a) California Bearing Ratio (CBR) Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the strength and supporting capacity of the subgrade soil. This is the most widely used method for flexible pavement design.
*   **Principle:** A standard piston is forced into a compacted soil sample at a standard rate. The load required to achieve a specific penetration (2.5 mm and 5.0 mm) is compared to the load required for a standard crushed rock sample.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  The soil sample is compacted in a CBR mold at its optimum moisture content and maximum dry density (or specified compaction effort).
    2.  The compacted sample is soaked in water for a specified period (usually 96 hours) to simulate field moisture conditions.
    3.  A standard piston (diameter 50 mm for general tests) is pushed into the soaked sample at a rate of 1.27 mm/min.
    4.  Loads at 2.5 mm and 5.0 mm penetration are recorded.
*   **CBR Calculation:**
    $$ CBR (\%) = \frac{Load \ at \ specified \ penetration}{Standard \ Load \ at \ specified \ penetration} \times 100 $$
    *   **Standard Loads:**
        *   For 2.5 mm penetration: 1370 kg
        *   For 5.0 mm penetration: 2055 kg
    *   If the load at 5.0 mm is greater than at 2.5 mm, the CBR at 5.0 mm is used, and the CBR at 2.5 mm is adjusted by multiplying it by a factor (typically 1.15).
*   **Desirable CBR Values:** Higher CBR values indicate stronger soil. For subgrades, CBR values typically range from 2% to 10% or more, depending on the pavement design requirements.

#### b) Plasticity Index (PI) Test:

*   **Purpose:** To determine the plasticity of the soil.
*   **Principle:** Performed using the Atterberg limits tests (Liquid Limit and Plastic Limit).
    *   **Liquid Limit (LL):** The moisture content at which soil transitions from a plastic to a liquid state.
    *   **Plastic Limit (PL):** The moisture content at which soil transitions from a plastic to a semi-solid state.
    *   **Plasticity Index (PI) = LL - PL**
*   **Importance:** Low PI soils (non-plastic or slightly plastic) are generally preferred for subgrades as they exhibit less volume change with moisture fluctuations. Expansive clays have high PI.

#### c) Compaction Tests (Proctor Test):

*   **Purpose:** To determine the optimum moisture content (OMC) and maximum dry density (MDD) for a given soil.
*   **Principle:** Soil is compacted in layers with a standard hammer at different moisture contents. The dry density is calculated for each moisture content, and a plot of dry density vs. moisture content is used to determine OMC and MDD.
*   **Importance:** Essential for achieving the required strength and stability of the subgrade layer through proper compaction.

#### d) Gradation Analysis (Sieve Analysis and Hydrometer Analysis):

*   **Purpose:** To determine the particle size distribution of the soil.
*   **Principle:** Soil is sieved through a series of sieves to determine the proportion of coarse-grained material, and a hydrometer test is used for fine-grained material.
*   **Importance:** Influences drainage, compaction, and strength characteristics.

## 4. Flexible Pavement Design by CBR Method

The CBR method is an empirical design method based on the in-situ strength of the subgrade as indicated by the CBR value. It aims to determine the required thickness of each pavement layer to prevent excessive deformation under traffic loading.

### 4.1 Principles of CBR Design:

The design involves calculating the required structural number (SN) and then selecting appropriate layer thicknesses and materials that satisfy this SN. The load is expressed in terms of Equivalent Single Wheel Load (ESWL) or cumulative number of repetitions of standard axle loads (N).

The fundamental concept is that each pavement layer has a specific structural value (Structural Coefficient, 'a') and thickness ('d'). The cumulative structural capacity is expressed as the structural number (SN).

$$ SN = a_1 d_1 + a_2 d_2 + a_3 d_3 + ... $$

Where:
*   $SN$ = Structural Number
*   $a_1, a_2, a_3$ = Structural coefficients of the respective layers (wearing course, base course, sub-base course)
*   $d_1, d_2, d_3$ = Thicknesses of the respective layers in inches (note: historically, the CBR method often uses inches for layer thicknesses in its original charts and tables).

### 4.2 Design Procedure using CBR Method:

1.  **Determine Traffic Load:**
    *   Estimate the **Annual Average Daily Traffic (AADT)**.
    *   Determine the **Vehicle Damage Factor (VDF)**, which represents the damage caused by a commercial vehicle compared to a standard axle load.
    *   Calculate the **design traffic in terms of million standard axle loads (msa)** over the design life of the pavement.
        $$ N = \frac{365 \times (1+r)^n - 1}{r} \times AADT \times VDF $$
        Where:
        *   $N$ = cumulative number of standard axle loads (8160 kg)
        *   $r$ = annual growth rate of traffic
        *   $n$ = design life of the pavement (years)

2.  **Determine Subgrade CBR:**
    *   Conduct CBR tests on the subgrade soil.
    *   If the subgrade is not uniform, the lowest CBR value is typically used, or an average value if proper stabilization is planned.

3.  **Determine Required Structural Number (SN):**
    *   Using the design traffic (N) and subgrade CBR, refer to **CBR Design Charts** (available in standard textbooks or codes) to find the required SN. These charts are typically based on empirical relationships derived from extensive field testing.

4.  **Select Pavement Layers and Material Properties:**
    *   Choose the types of materials for each pavement layer (e.g., asphalt concrete for wearing course, crushed stone for base, granular material for sub-base).
    *   Determine the **structural coefficients ($a_i$)** for each material type and its intended position in the pavement structure. These coefficients depend on the material's quality and its relationship with the subgrade. For example, IRC codes provide typical 'a' values.

5.  **Calculate Layer Thicknesses:**
    *   Based on the required SN, select initial trial thicknesses for each layer ($d_1, d_2, d_3$, etc.).
    *   Calculate the total SN for the trial design: $SN = a_1 d_1 + a_2 d_2 + a_3 d_3 + ...$
    *   Adjust layer thicknesses until the calculated SN meets or exceeds the required SN.
    *   **Constraint:** The thickness of each layer is usually governed by minimum or maximum limits specified in codes to ensure adequate performance and constructability.

### 4.3 Worked-Out Problem (CBR Method):

**Problem:** Design a flexible pavement for a national highway with the following data:
*   Initial traffic (AADT) = 1500 commercial vehicles per day
*   Annual growth rate (r) = 7%
*   Design life (n) = 15 years
*   Vehicle Damage Factor (VDF) = 4.0
*   Subgrade CBR = 5%
*   Assume the following structural coefficients:
    *   Wearing Course (Asphalt Concrete): $a_1 = 0.40$
    *   Base Course (Crushed Stone): $a_2 = 0.25$
    *   Sub-base Course (Gravel): $a_3 = 0.15$

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Calculate Design Traffic (N)**

$$ N = \frac{365 \times [(1+0.07)^{15} - 1]}{0.07} \times 1500 \times 4.0 $$
$$ N = \frac{365 \times [2.759 - 1]}{0.07} \times 6000 $$
$$ N = \frac{365 \times 1.759}{0.07} \times 6000 $$
$$ N = 91763 \times 6000 $$
$$ N \approx 550.58 \times 10^6 \text{ standard axle loads} $$

**Step 2: Determine Required Structural Number (SN)**

Using the CBR design charts (referencing standard IRC or Asphalt Institute charts), for a subgrade CBR of 5% and a design traffic of 550.58 msa, the required Structural Number (SN) is approximately **5.5**.

**(Note:** The exact SN value will depend on the specific chart used. This is an illustrative value.)

**Step 3: Select Layer Thicknesses**

We need to select $d_1, d_2, d_3$ such that $0.40 d_1 + 0.25 d_2 + 0.15 d_3 \ge 5.5$.
Assume minimum layer thicknesses based on typical highway construction practices:
*   Wearing Course ($d_1$): Minimum 40 mm (approx. 1.5 inches)
*   Base Course ($d_2$): Minimum 125 mm (approx. 5 inches)
*   Sub-base Course ($d_3$): Minimum 150 mm (approx. 6 inches)

Let's try some trial thicknesses (using inches for compatibility with traditional CBR charts):

**Trial 1:**
*   $d_1$ = 1.5 inches (Asphalt Concrete)
*   $d_2$ = 5.0 inches (Crushed Stone Base)
*   $d_3$ = 7.0 inches (Gravel Sub-base)

$$ SN = (0.40 \times 1.5) + (0.25 \times 5.0) + (0.15 \times 7.0) $$
$$ SN = 0.60 + 1.25 + 1.05 = 2.90 $$
This SN (2.90) is far less than the required SN (5.5). We need significantly thicker layers.

**Trial 2:**
*   $d_1$ = 2.0 inches (Asphalt Concrete)
*   $d_2$ = 8.0 inches (Crushed Stone Base)
*   $d_3$ = 12.0 inches (Gravel Sub-base)

$$ SN = (0.40 \times 2.0) + (0.25 \times 8.0) + (0.15 \times 12.0) $$
$$ SN = 0.80 + 2.00 + 1.80 = 4.60 $$
Still less than 5.5.

**Trial 3:**
*   $d_1$ = 2.5 inches (Asphalt Concrete)
*   $d_2$ = 10.0 inches (Crushed Stone Base)
*   $d_3$ = 14.0 inches (Gravel Sub-base)

$$ SN = (0.40 \times 2.5) + (0.25 \times 10.0) + (0.15 \times 14.0) $$
$$ SN = 1.00 + 2.50 + 2.10 = 5.60 $$
This SN (5.60) is greater than the required SN (5.5).

**Therefore, the designed pavement structure is:**
*   Wearing Course (Asphalt Concrete): 2.5 inches (approx. 63 mm)
*   Base Course (Crushed Stone): 10.0 inches (approx. 250 mm)
*   Sub-base Course (Gravel): 14.0 inches (approx. 350 mm)

**(Note:** In metric units, the structural coefficients and SN values are different. If working directly in metric, the approach remains the same, but the values will differ.)

## 5. Design of Flexible Pavements by IRC:37-2018

IRC:37-2018 is the Indian Roads Congress code of practice for the design of flexible pavements. It provides a comprehensive methodology that incorporates modern design principles and accounts for various factors.

### 5.1 Key Concepts and Principles of IRC:37-2018:

*   **Mechanistic-Empirical Approach:** IRC:37-2018 uses a mechanistic-empirical approach, combining principles of mechanics (stress and strain analysis) with empirical relationships derived from field data and laboratory testing.
*   **Traffic Loading (Vehicle Load Spectrum - VLS):** Instead of a single cumulative number of standard axles, IRC:37-2018 uses a **Vehicle Load Spectrum (VLS)**. This accounts for the distribution of different axle loads and their repetitions over the design life.
*   **Material Characterization:** Requires detailed material characterization, including:
    *   **Subgrade:** CBR value.
    *   **Granular Materials (Sub-base, Base):** CBR value or resilient modulus ($M_r$).
    *   **Bituminous Layers:** Resilient modulus ($M_r$) and fatigue/rutting models.
*   **Performance Criteria:** Designs are based on limiting distresses such as:
    *   **Fatigue Cracking:** Longitudinal cracking due to repeated tensile strain in the bottom of the bituminous layers.
    *   **Rutting:** Permanent deformation accumulating in the pavement layers, leading to depressions in the wheel path.
*   **Layered Elastic Theory:** The stress and strain within the pavement layers are analyzed using layered elastic theory.
*   **Resilient Modulus ($M_r$):** This is a key parameter for characterizing the stiffness of unbound granular materials and the fatigue behavior of bituminous layers. It represents the recoverable strain under repeated loading.
*   **Design Life:** The pavement is designed for a specified period, typically 20 years for National Highways and State Highways.
*   **Traffic Growth:** Annual growth rate of traffic is considered.

### 5.2 Design Procedure using IRC:37-2018:

1.  **Determine Design Traffic (Vehicle Load Spectrum - VLS):**
    *   Estimate **Average Annual Daily Truck Traffic (AADTT)**.
    *   Obtain the distribution of **axle loads** from traffic surveys.
    *   Determine the **annual growth rate of truck traffic**.
    *   Calculate the **number of repetitions of each axle load category** over the design life (e.g., 20 years). This forms the VLS.
    *   Convert all axle loads to **equivalent standard axle loads (ESALs)** using an equivalency factor. The IRC code specifies procedures for this.

2.  **Characterize Subgrade:**
    *   Determine the **CBR value of the subgrade**. The design is generally based on the soaked CBR value.

3.  **Select Pavement Materials and Properties:**
    *   Choose appropriate materials for each layer (e.g., asphalt concrete, modified bitumen asphalt, crushed stone, recycled materials).
    *   Determine the **resilient modulus ($M_r$)** of each material. For bituminous layers, this is typically obtained from laboratory tests (e.g., Repeated Load Triaxial Test). For granular layers, it can be estimated based on CBR or other properties.
    *   Determine the **structural coefficients** for bituminous layers based on their $M_r$ values and fatigue/rutting models.

4.  **Analyze Stresses and Strains:**
    *   Use **layered elastic analysis software** to determine the stresses and strains at critical locations within the pavement structure under the applied axle loads from the VLS.
    *   The critical locations are typically:
        *   Tensile strain at the bottom of the bituminous layer (for fatigue cracking).
        *   Vertical compressive strain at the top of the subgrade (for rutting).
        *   Vertical compressive strain at the top of the base layer (for rutting).

5.  **Apply Performance Criteria:**
    *   **Fatigue Cracking:** Compare the calculated tensile strain ($\epsilon_t$) at the bottom of the bituminous layer with the allowable tensile strain ($\epsilon_{ta}$) from the fatigue model of the specific bituminous mix. The number of repetitions allowed for a given strain level is determined by a fatigue equation.
        $$ N_{allowable} = k_f (\frac{\epsilon_{ta}}{\epsilon_t})^m $$
        Where $k_f$ and $m$ are fatigue parameters for the mix. The total number of repetitions of each axle load from the VLS should be less than or equal to the allowable repetitions for the calculated strain.
    *   **Rutting:** Compare the calculated vertical compressive strain ($\epsilon_c$) at the top of the subgrade with the allowable vertical strain ($\epsilon_{ca}$) from the rutting model. The number of repetitions allowed for a given strain level is determined by a rutting equation.
        $$ N_{allowable} = k_r (\frac{\epsilon_{ca}}{\epsilon_c})^m $$
        Where $k_r$ and $m$ are rutting parameters. The total number of repetitions of each axle load from the VLS should be less than or equal to the allowable repetitions.

6.  **Determine Layer Thicknesses:**
    *   Iteratively adjust the thicknesses of the pavement layers until both fatigue and rutting criteria are satisfied for the entire VLS. This is typically done using design software that automates the layered elastic analysis and performance checks.

### 5.3 Worked-Out Problem (IRC:37-2018 - Simplified Approach):

**Problem:** Design a flexible pavement for a major district road with the following data:
*   AADTT = 500 trucks per day
*   Annual growth rate (r) = 6%
*   Design life (n) = 10 years
*   Subgrade CBR = 7%
*   Consider a simplified design using IRC:37-2018 where the design chart is directly linked to cumulative ESALs and subgrade CBR. Assume the cumulative ESALs for the design life are 70 msa.

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Calculate Design Traffic (Cumulative ESALs)**

Given:
*   AADTT = 500
*   r = 6%
*   n = 10 years
*   Assume Average number of axles per truck = 2
*   Assume average number of wheels per axle = 4 (though design is usually based on axle load)
*   Assume Traffic Load Equivalency Factor (LEF) for the typical axle load distribution is 1.0 (for simplification, a more detailed analysis would use VLS).

$$ N_{ESALs} = \frac{365 \times [(1+0.06)^{10} - 1]}{0.06} \times 500 \times 2 \times 1.0 $$
$$ N_{ESALs} = \frac{365 \times [1.791 - 1]}{0.06} \times 1000 $$
$$ N_{ESALs} = \frac{365 \times 0.791}{0.06} \times 1000 $$
$$ N_{ESALs} = 4817 \times 1000 $$
$$ N_{ESALs} \approx 4.82 \text{ msa} $$

**Wait!** The problem statement provides 70 msa directly. This indicates that the VLS and LEF calculations have already been performed to arrive at this figure, which is common in practical applications when using design charts. Let's proceed with the given 70 msa.

**Step 2: Determine Pavement Structure using IRC:37-2018 Design Charts**

According to IRC:37-2018, for a subgrade CBR of 7% and a design traffic of 70 msa, the required pavement structure is typically found using design charts. These charts provide combinations of layer thicknesses and materials.

**Question 3 (Subgrade):**
A subgrade soil sample is compacted in a CBR mold and tested. The loads recorded at penetrations of 2.5 mm and 5.0 mm are 800 kg and 1200 kg respectively. Calculate the CBR value of the subgrade.

**Answer 3:**
*   Load at 2.5 mm = 800 kg
*   Load at 5.0 mm = 1200 kg

Standard load at 2.5 mm = 1370 kg
Standard load at 5.0 mm = 2055 kg

CBR at 2.5 mm = $(800 / 1370) \times 100 = 58.4\%$
CBR at 5.0 mm = $(1200 / 2055) \times 100 = 58.4\%$

Since the load at 5.0 mm is greater than the load at 2.5 mm, we should use the CBR at 5.0 mm, and the CBR at 2.5 mm should be adjusted. However, in this case, both are the same.
If CBR at 5.0 mm was higher, we would use it. If CBR at 2.5 mm was higher, we would use it, and possibly adjust the 5.0 mm value.

In this specific case, both values are equal, and it's very high. Let's re-examine the standard loads.
The problem implies a very strong subgrade if these loads are achieved with a standard CBR setup.
Let's assume the standard loads are correctly applied.
Since the load at 5.0 mm (1200 kg) is greater than the load at 2.5 mm (800 kg), we should use the 5.0 mm penetration value.
CBR at 5.0 mm = (1200 / 2055) * 100 = 58.4%
According to standard practice, if the CBR at 5.0 mm is greater than the CBR at 2.5 mm, the CBR at 2.5 mm should be adjusted by multiplying it by 1.15.
Adjusted CBR at 2.5 mm = 58.4% * 1.15 = 67.16%.
Since the CBR at 5.0 mm (58.4%) is lower than the adjusted CBR at 2.5 mm (67.16%), the design CBR value is **58.4%**.

**Important Note:** In real-world subgrade testing, a CBR of this magnitude (58.4%) is exceptionally high. It usually indicates a very strong material like a dense-graded base course rather than a natural subgrade. This might be a hypothetical problem.

**Question 4 (CBR Design):**
A pavement needs to be designed for a cumulative traffic of 100 msa on a subgrade with a CBR of 4%. If the structural coefficients for the wearing course, base course, and sub-base course are 0.42, 0.24, and 0.14 respectively, and assuming layer thicknesses are 1.5 inches, 6 inches, and 10 inches, calculate the total structural number and determine if it's adequate.

**Answer 4:**
*   Required SN for 100 msa and CBR 4% (from charts) is typically around 5.0 to 5.2.
*   Given layer thicknesses:
    *   $d_1$ (wearing course) = 1.5 inches
    *   $d_2$ (base course) = 6 inches
    *   $d_3$ (sub-base course) = 10 inches
*   Given structural coefficients:
    *   $a_1 = 0.42$
    *   $a_2 = 0.24$
    *   $a_3 = 0.14$

Calculated SN = $(a_1 \times d_1) + (a_2 \times d_2) + (a_3 \times d_3)$
Calculated SN = $(0.42 \times 1.5) + (0.24 \times 6) + (0.14 \times 10)$
Calculated SN = $0.63 + 1.44 + 1.40 = 3.47$

Since the calculated SN (3.47) is less than the typically required SN (around 5.0-5.2) for the given traffic and subgrade conditions, this pavement structure is **not adequate**. Thicker layers would be required.

*   **Material Properties:** Understanding the structural coefficients and resilient moduli of different pavement materials is key to analysis and design.
