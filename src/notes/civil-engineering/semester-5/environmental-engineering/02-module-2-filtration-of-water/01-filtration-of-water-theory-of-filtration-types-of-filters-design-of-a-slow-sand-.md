## 1. Theory of Filtration

Filtration is a physical process used to remove suspended solids and microorganisms from water by passing it through a porous medium. The effectiveness of filtration depends on several mechanisms that work together.

### 1.1 Primary Mechanisms of Filtration

*   **Straining/Sieving:** Larger suspended particles are physically blocked by the pores of the filter medium, preventing them from passing through. This is the most straightforward mechanism.
    *   **Example:** Gravel layers in a filter bed effectively strain out larger debris.
*   **Sedimentation:** As water flows through the filter bed, the velocity decreases within the voids, allowing gravity to settle out some suspended particles.
*   **Impaction/Interception:** Suspended particles flowing in the water stream come into contact with the filter medium surface due to their trajectory and get stuck.
*   **Adsorption/Surface Attraction:** Particles, especially colloidal ones, are attracted to the surface of the filter medium particles due to electrostatic forces or Van der Waals forces and adhere to them.
*   **Biological Activity (Schmutzdecke - for Slow Sand Filters):** A biological layer forms on the surface of the filter sand in slow sand filters. This layer, composed of bacteria, algae, protozoa, and other microorganisms, plays a vital role in removing pathogens through biological and biochemical processes.

### 1.2 Factors Affecting Filtration Efficiency

*   **Size and Nature of Suspended Solids:** Smaller, lighter, and colloidal particles are harder to remove.
*   **Type and Size of Filter Medium:** The pore size and surface area of the filter medium are crucial.
*   **Flow Rate:** Higher flow rates can reduce the time for particles to interact with the filter medium, decreasing efficiency.
*   **Depth of Filter Bed:** A deeper bed generally offers more opportunities for particle removal.
*   **Accumulation of Solids (Clogging):** As solids accumulate, they form a "cake" or clog the filter pores, increasing resistance to flow and reducing efficiency if not managed.
*   **Water Temperature:** Affects viscosity and biological activity.

## 2. Types of Filters

Water filters can be broadly classified based on their operating characteristics, filter media, and application.

### 2.1 Based on Operating Characteristics

*   **Slow Sand Filters (SSF):** Operate at low filtration rates (0.1-0.4 m³/m²/hr or 0.03-0.11 L/m²/s). They rely on a biological layer (schmutzdecke) for effective pathogen removal. They are effective for removing bacteria, protozoa, and some viruses, as well as turbidity.
*   **Rapid Sand Filters (RSF):** Operate at higher filtration rates (5-15 m³/m²/hr or 1.4-4.2 L/m²/s). They typically require pre-treatment (coagulation and sedimentation) to remove finer particles before filtration. They are primarily used for turbidity removal and are less effective in removing bacteria compared to SSFs without pre-treatment.

### 2.2 Based on Filter Media

*   **Single-Medium Filters:** Use a single type of filter material (e.g., sand only).
*   **Dual-Media Filters:** Use two layers of different filter media (e.g., anthracite over sand). This offers better depth filtration and longer filter runs.
*   **Multi-Media Filters:** Use three or more layers of different filter media (e.g., gravel, coarse sand, fine sand, garnet).

### 2.3 Other Types of Filters

*   **Pressure Filters:** Operate under pressure, forcing water through the filter medium. Used in smaller plants or for specific industrial applications.
*   **Vacuum Filters:** Water is drawn through the filter medium by a vacuum. Less common in municipal water treatment.
*   **Diaphragm Filters:** Use a permeable membrane or diaphragm as the filter medium.
*   **Bag Filters:** Water passes through a porous bag.
*   **Cartridge Filters:** Use replaceable filter cartridges with various filter media.
*   **Membrane Filters (Microfiltration, Ultrafiltration, Nanofiltration, Reverse Osmosis):** These are advanced filtration techniques using membranes with very small pore sizes for highly efficient removal of even dissolved substances and ions.

## 3. Slow Sand Filters (SSF)

### 3.1 Working Principle

Slow sand filters are biological filters that achieve high-quality effluent by passing raw or pre-treated water slowly through a bed of fine sand. The key to their effectiveness is the development of a biological layer known as the **schmutzdecke** on the surface of the sand bed.

1.  **Initial Stage (Ripening):** When a new SSF is put into operation, it takes a period (typically 1-4 weeks) for the schmutzdecke to develop. During this period, effluent quality is inferior, and the water is usually bypassed.
2.  **Operational Stage:** Once the schmutzdecke is established, it consists of a complex community of microorganisms. These organisms remove suspended solids and pathogens through:
    *   **Physical Straining:** by the sand grains and the biofilm itself.
    *   **Adsorption:** onto the surface of the sand and biofilm.
    *   **Biological Metabolism:** Microorganisms consume organic matter and pathogens, breaking them down.
3.  **Filter Bed:** Below the schmutzdecke is a clean sand bed that provides support and further filtration.
4.  **Underdrain System:** Collects the filtered water.

### 3.2 Components of a Slow Sand Filter

*   **Filter Tank:** Usually rectangular, made of concrete.
*   **Filter Sand:** The main filtering medium, typically with an effective size of 0.15-0.35 mm and a uniformity coefficient of less than 3. The depth of the sand bed is usually 0.6 to 1.2 meters.
*   **Support Gravel:** Layers of graded gravel at the bottom to support the sand bed and prevent the sand from entering the underdrain system. The gravel layers are typically 0.15 to 0.75 meters deep, with the coarsest at the bottom.
*   **Underdrain System:** Perforated pipes or a porous false floor to collect filtered water and distribute it uniformly.
*   **Inlet and Outlet Works:** To control the inflow of raw water and the outflow of filtered water. The outlet is usually regulated by a weir or valve to maintain a constant water level above the sand bed.
*   **Overflow Pipe:** To prevent flooding.

### 3.3 Operation and Maintenance

*   **Filtration Rate:** Low, typically 0.1-0.4 m³/m²/hr (or m/hr).
*   **Cleaning (Harrowing):** When the head loss exceeds a certain limit (e.g., 1 meter) or the filtration rate drops significantly, the filter needs cleaning. This involves draining the filter, scraping off the top layer of sand (including the schmutzdecke), and replacing it with clean sand or washing the scraped sand.
*   **Longevity of Sand:** The sand bed gradually gets depleted over time, requiring periodic replenishment.

### 3.4 Advantages of SSF

*   High-quality effluent, particularly effective in removing bacteria and viruses.
*   Simple to operate and maintain.
*   Low energy consumption (gravity-fed).
*   Does not require chemicals for operation (except during initial setup and maintenance).

### 3.5 Disadvantages of SSF

*   Requires a large land area due to low filtration rates.
*   Slow to start up (ripening period).
*   Less effective in removing turbidity if the raw water is highly turbid.
*   Cleaning is labor-intensive.
*   Not suitable for very cold climates where biological activity is reduced.

### 3.6 Design of a Slow Sand Filter

**Design Parameters:**

*   **Filtration Rate (v):** Typically 0.1 to 0.4 m/hr.
*   **Effective Size of Sand ($d_{10}$):** 0.15 to 0.35 mm.
*   **Uniformity Coefficient (UC):** $\frac{d_{60}}{d_{10}} < 3$.
*   **Depth of Sand Bed (H_sand):** 0.6 to 1.2 m.
*   **Depth of Gravel Support (H_gravel):** 0.15 to 0.75 m (graded).
*   **Freeboard:** 0.5 to 1.0 m.
*   **Water Depth above Sand:** 1.0 to 1.5 m.
*   **Filter Area (A):** Determined by the required flow rate (Q) and the filtration rate (v): $A = \frac{Q}{v}$.

**Design Steps:**

1.  **Determine Required Filter Area:** Calculate the total flow rate (Q) required by the treatment plant. Select an appropriate filtration rate (v) from the typical range (e.g., 0.2 m/hr).
    $A = \frac{Q}{v}$
2.  **Determine Dimensions of the Filter Bed:** Decide on the shape (usually rectangular) and dimensions of the filter bed(s). Multiple beds are often used for redundancy and continuous operation.
3.  **Select Sand Properties:** Choose the effective size ($d_{10}$) and uniformity coefficient (UC) of the sand based on design standards.
4.  **Determine Depth of Sand Bed:** Specify the depth of the sand layer (e.g., 1.0 m).
5.  **Design the Gravel Support System:**
    *   The gravel should be graded, with the coarsest at the bottom and finest at the top, directly below the sand.
    *   Typical layering:
        *   Bottom layer: 25-40 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
        *   Second layer: 12-25 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
        *   Third layer: 6-12 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
        *   Top layer: 3-6 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
    *   Total gravel depth is usually 0.3 to 0.75 m.
6.  **Design the Underdrain System:**
    *   Usually consists of a main collector pipe and lateral perforated pipes spaced evenly.
    *   The spacing of laterals depends on the filter width and the size of the laterals.
7.  **Determine Water Depth:** Specify the depth of water to be maintained above the sand bed (e.g., 1.2 m).
8.  **Provide Freeboard:** Ensure adequate freeboard above the normal water level to prevent overflow (e.g., 0.75 m).
9.  **Inlet and Outlet Structures:** Design inlet arrangements to avoid scouring the sand bed (e.g., splash plates) and outlet structures to control the water level (weir).

**Example Design Calculation for SSF:**

**Problem:** Design a slow sand filter to treat a flow of 5 MLD (Million Liters per Day). Assume a filtration rate of 0.2 m/hr. Use sand with an effective size of 0.25 mm and a uniformity coefficient of 2.5. The depth of the sand bed is 1.0 m.

**Solution:**

1.  **Convert flow to m³/hr:**
    $Q = 5 \text{ MLD} = 5 \times 10^6 \text{ L/day} = \frac{5 \times 10^6}{24} \text{ m³/hr} \approx 208.33 \text{ m³/hr}$

2.  **Calculate required filter area:**
    $v = 0.2 \text{ m/hr}$
    $A = \frac{Q}{v} = \frac{208.33 \text{ m³/hr}}{0.2 \text{ m/hr}} = 1041.65 \text{ m²}$

3.  **Determine filter bed dimensions:**
    Let's assume the filter beds are rectangular. We can use multiple beds for flexibility. Suppose we design for two equal beds.
    Area per bed = $1041.65 / 2 = 520.83 \text{ m²}$
    Let the width of each bed be 10 m.
    Length of each bed = $520.83 \text{ m²} / 10 \text{ m} = 52.08 \text{ m}$
    So, we can have two filter beds, each measuring $10 \text{ m} \times 52.08 \text{ m}$.

4.  **Sand Properties:**
    Effective size ($d_{10}$) = 0.25 mm
    Uniformity Coefficient (UC) = 2.5 (This is acceptable as UC < 3).

5.  **Depth of Sand Bed:**
    $H_{\text{sand}} = 1.0 \text{ m}$ (Given)

6.  **Gravel Support System:**
    Provide graded gravel layers as described earlier, totaling approximately 0.5 m depth.

7.  **Underdrain System:**
    Design a system of perforated laterals spaced appropriately within the width of the beds.

8.  **Water Depth:**
    Let's assume a water depth of 1.2 m above the sand.

9.  **Freeboard:**
    Provide at least 0.75 m freeboard.

**Summary of Design:**

*   Number of filter beds: 2
*   Dimensions of each bed: $10 \text{ m} \times 52.08 \text{ m}$
*   Total filter area: $1041.65 \text{ m²}$
*   Sand bed depth: 1.0 m
*   Gravel support depth: ~0.5 m
*   Water depth above sand: 1.2 m
*   Freeboard: ~0.75 m
*   Filtration rate: 0.2 m/hr
*   Sand: $d_{10}$ = 0.25 mm, UC = 2.5

## 4. Rapid Sand Filters (RSF)

### 4.1 Working Principle

Rapid sand filters operate at significantly higher flow rates than slow sand filters. They primarily rely on physical filtration mechanisms and are most effective when the raw water is pre-treated, typically by coagulation, flocculation, and sedimentation.

1.  **Pre-treatment:** Coagulants (like alum or ferric chloride) are added to destabilize suspended particles, causing them to aggregate into larger flocs. Flocculation further encourages floc growth. Sedimentation allows larger flocs to settle out, reducing the load on the filter.
2.  **Filtration:** Water flows through a bed of coarser sand (compared to SSF) at a high rate. The primary removal mechanisms are straining, interception, and adsorption.
3.  **Clogging and Head Loss:** As filtration proceeds, impurities accumulate in the upper layers of the sand bed, forming a "filter cake" and increasing the resistance to flow (head loss).
4.  **Backwashing:** When the head loss reaches a predetermined limit (typically 2-3 meters) or the filtration rate drops significantly, the filter bed must be cleaned. This is done by **backwashing**, where clean water (and sometimes air scour) is pumped upwards through the filter bed at a high velocity to fluidize the sand grains and wash away the accumulated impurities.

### 4.2 Components of a Rapid Sand Filter

*   **Filter Tank:** Usually concrete, rectangular or circular.
*   **Filter Media:**
    *   **Top Layer:** Anthracite (effective size 0.8-1.2 mm) – for depth filtration.
    *   **Middle Layer:** Sand (effective size 0.45-0.7 mm) – primary filtering medium.
    *   **Bottom Layer:** Gravel (graded, 2-30 mm) – support layers for the sand and to prevent sand from entering the underdrain.
*   **Underdrain System:** Collects filtered water and distributes backwash water and air. It can be a false floor (nozzles or porous plates) or a collection manifold with perforated laterals.
*   **Wash Water Troughs:** Placed at the top to collect and carry away the dirty wash water during backwashing.
*   **Inlet and Outlet Works:** For raw water inlet, filtered water outlet, backwash water inlet, and waste wash water outlet.
*   **Air Scour System (Optional but common):** For enhanced cleaning during backwashing.

### 4.3 Operation and Maintenance

*   **Filtration Rate:** High, typically 5 to 15 m³/m²/hr (or m/hr).
*   **Backwashing:** Performed regularly (e.g., daily or every few days) depending on raw water quality and head loss. It involves a sequence of air scour (if used) followed by water wash.
*   **Filter Runs:** The period between backwashes, typically 24-72 hours.
*   **Loss of Sand:** Some sand is lost during each backwash, requiring periodic replenishment.

### 4.4 Advantages of RSF

*   High filtration rates, requiring less land area.
*   Effective in removing turbidity and suspended solids, especially after pre-treatment.
*   Can handle variations in raw water quality better than SSF.
*   Automated backwashing is possible.

### 4.5 Disadvantages of RSF

*   Requires pre-treatment (coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation).
*   Less effective in removing bacteria and viruses compared to SSF without proper pre-treatment.
*   More complex operation and maintenance, requiring skilled personnel.
*   Higher energy consumption for backwashing.
*   Requires chemicals for pre-treatment.

### 4.6 Design of a Rapid Sand Filter

**Design Parameters:**

*   **Filtration Rate (v):** Typically 5 to 15 m/hr.
*   **Sand Effective Size ($d_{10}$):** 0.45 to 0.7 mm.
*   **Sand Uniformity Coefficient (UC):** $\frac{d_{60}}{d_{10}} < 1.7$.
*   **Anthracite Effective Size ($d_{10}$):** 0.8 to 1.2 mm.
*   **Anthracite Uniformity Coefficient (UC):** $\frac{d_{60}}{d_{10}} < 1.5$.
*   **Depth of Sand Bed:** 0.6 to 0.75 m.
*   **Depth of Anthracite Layer:** 0.15 to 0.3 m.
*   **Depth of Gravel Support:** 0.15 to 0.75 m (graded).
*   **Freeboard:** 0.5 to 1.0 m.
*   **Water Depth above Media:** 1.0 to 1.5 m.
*   **Backwash Rate:** 35 to 60 m/hr (depending on temperature and media size).
*   **Air Scour Rate (if used):** 40 to 70 m/hr.

**Design Steps:**

1.  **Determine Required Filter Area:** Calculate the total flow rate (Q) required. Select an appropriate filtration rate (v) from the typical range (e.g., 10 m/hr).
    $A = \frac{Q}{v}$
2.  **Determine Dimensions of Filter Beds:**
    *   RSF are often designed as multiple rectangular beds.
    *   The width of beds is typically 5-10 m.
    *   The length can vary significantly.
    *   The total area is divided among several beds for continuous operation and maintenance.
3.  **Select Filter Media Properties:**
    *   Choose effective size and uniformity coefficient for sand and anthracite according to standards.
    *   Depth of anthracite: e.g., 0.25 m.
    *   Depth of sand: e.g., 0.65 m.
    *   Total media depth = Anthracite depth + Sand depth = 0.25 + 0.65 = 0.9 m.
4.  **Design the Gravel Support System:**
    *   Provide graded gravel layers below the sand to support the media and prevent it from entering the underdrain.
    *   Typical layering:
        *   Bottom layer: 25-40 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
        *   Second layer: 12-25 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
        *   Third layer: 6-12 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
        *   Top layer: 3-6 mm gravel (10-15 cm depth)
    *   Total gravel depth is usually 0.3 to 0.75 m.
5.  **Design the Underdrain System:**
    *   Nozzles: Small porous discs or pipes with small orifices, evenly spaced.
    *   Porous Plates: A false floor made of porous material.
    *   Manifold and Laterals: A central manifold with perforated lateral pipes.
    *   The system must ensure uniform distribution of backwash water and air, and efficient collection of filtered water.
6.  **Determine Water Depth:** Specify the depth of water to be maintained above the filter media (e.g., 1.2 m).
7.  **Provide Freeboard:** Ensure adequate freeboard above the normal water level (e.g., 0.75 m).
8.  **Design Wash Water Troughs:** Spaced to efficiently collect dirty wash water during backwashing. The depth and spacing depend on the backwash rate.
9.  **Backwash System Design:**
    *   Calculate the required backwash rate (e.g., 50 m/hr).
    *   Determine the duration of backwash (e.g., 5-10 minutes).
    *   Calculate the total volume of wash water needed per backwash and per day.
    *   Design the backwash pumps and piping.
    *   If air scour is used, determine the air flow rate and design the air blowers and distribution system.

**Example Design Calculation for RSF:**

**Problem:** Design a rapid sand filter to treat a flow of 20 MLD. Assume a filtration rate of 12 m/hr. The filter bed consists of 0.25 m anthracite over 0.65 m sand.

**Solution:**

1.  **Convert flow to m³/hr:**
    $Q = 20 \text{ MLD} = 20 \times 10^6 \text{ L/day} = \frac{20 \times 10^6}{24} \text{ m³/hr} \approx 833.33 \text{ m³/hr}$

2.  **Calculate required filter area:**
    $v = 12 \text{ m/hr}$
    $A = \frac{Q}{v} = \frac{833.33 \text{ m³/hr}}{12 \text{ m/hr}} \approx 69.44 \text{ m²}$

3.  **Determine filter bed dimensions:**
    Let's assume we use 4 filter beds for redundancy.
    Area per bed = $69.44 / 4 = 17.36 \text{ m²}$
    Let the width of each bed be 5 m.
    Length of each bed = $17.36 \text{ m²} / 5 \text{ m} = 3.47 \text{ m}$
    So, we can have 4 filter beds, each measuring $5 \text{ m} \times 3.47 \text{ m}$.

4.  **Filter Media:**
    *   Anthracite: Depth = 0.25 m
    *   Sand: Depth = 0.65 m
    *   Total media depth = 0.9 m

5.  **Gravel Support:**
    Provide graded gravel layers, typically 0.3 to 0.75 m deep.

6.  **Underdrain System:**
    Design an underdrain system (e.g., nozzles or manifold system) to provide uniform backwash.

7.  **Water Depth:**
    Assume a water depth of 1.2 m above the media.

8.  **Freeboard:**
    Provide 0.75 m freeboard.

**Summary of Design:**

*   Number of filter beds: 4
*   Dimensions of each bed: $5 \text{ m} \times 3.47 \text{ m}$
*   Total filter area: $69.44 \text{ m²}$
*   Media depth: 0.25 m anthracite + 0.65 m sand = 0.9 m
*   Gravel support depth: ~0.5 m
*   Water depth above media: 1.2 m
*   Freeboard: 0.75 m
*   Filtration rate: 12 m/hr

## 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary mechanism responsible for pathogen removal in a slow sand filter?
**Answer:** The development and activity of the biological layer known as the "schmutzdecke" on the surface of the sand bed.

**Question 2:** A rapid sand filter is designed to operate at a filtration rate of 10 m/hr. If the required treatment capacity is 1 MLD (Million Liters per Day), what is the minimum filter area required?
**Answer:**
$Q = 1 \text{ MLD} = 1 \times 10^6 \text{ L/day} = \frac{1 \times 10^6}{24} \text{ m³/hr} \approx 41.67 \text{ m³/hr}$
$v = 10 \text{ m/hr}$
$A = \frac{Q}{v} = \frac{41.67 \text{ m³/hr}}{10 \text{ m/hr}} \approx 4.17 \text{ m²}$
The minimum filter area required is approximately 4.17 m².

**Question 3:** List the main advantages of using rapid sand filters over slow sand filters.
**Answer:**
*   Higher filtration rates, requiring less land area.
*   Can handle variations in raw water quality better (with pre-treatment).
*   More effective in removing turbidity and suspended solids (with pre-treatment).
*   Automated backwashing is possible.

**Question 4:** Describe the process of backwashing in a rapid sand filter.
**Answer:** Backwashing is the process of cleaning a rapid sand filter. It involves reversing the flow of water (and sometimes air) upwards through the filter bed at a high velocity. This fluidizes the filter media, loosening and washing away the accumulated debris and filter cake. Dirty wash water is then removed through wash water troughs.

**Question 5:** What is the typical range for the effective size of sand used in a slow sand filter?
**Answer:** 0.15 mm to 0.35 mm.

**Question 6:** If a slow sand filter is designed for a flow of 1 MLD and a filtration rate of 0.2 m/hr, calculate the total filter area required. If the filter bed is to be rectangular with a width of 5 meters, what would be its length?
**Answer:**
$Q = 1 \text{ MLD} \approx 41.67 \text{ m³/hr}$
$v = 0.2 \text{ m/hr}$
$A = \frac{Q}{v} = \frac{41.67 \text{ m³/hr}}{0.2 \text{ m/hr}} = 208.35 \text{ m²}$
Total filter area = 208.35 m².
If width = 5 m, then length = $A / \text{width} = 208.35 \text{ m²} / 5 \text{ m} = 41.67 \text{ m}$.
The filter bed would be approximately 5 m wide and 41.67 m long.

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Filtration** is a physical process to remove suspended solids and microorganisms.
*   Key removal mechanisms include **straining, sedimentation, impaction, and adsorption**.
*   **Slow Sand Filters (SSF)** rely on a biological layer (schmutzdecke) and operate at low rates. They are excellent for pathogen removal.
*   **Rapid Sand Filters (RSF)** operate at high rates and primarily use physical mechanisms, requiring pre-treatment for effective removal of fine particles and pathogens.
*   **Pre-treatment (coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation)** is crucial for RSF performance.
*   **Backwashing** is the essential cleaning process for RSF.
*   **Sand and anthracite** are common filter media, with **graded gravel** used for support.
*   **Effective size and uniformity coefficient** are critical parameters for filter media selection.
*   **Filtration rate, media depth, and freeboard** are key design considerations for both SSF and RSF.
*   **Land area** is a significant factor: SSFs require more land than RSFs.
