### 1. Introduction to Prestressed Concrete

#### 1.1 What is Prestressing?

*   **Definition:** Prestressing is the process of introducing internal compressive stresses into concrete members before they are subjected to external service loads. This is achieved by tensioning high-strength steel tendons (wires, strands, or bars) within or around the concrete.
*   **Purpose:**
    *   To counteract the tensile stresses that would otherwise develop in concrete under service loads.
    *   To improve the load-carrying capacity and performance of concrete structures.
    *   To reduce deflections and cracking.
    *   To enable the use of concrete in tension-dominated structural elements.

#### 1.2 Why Prestress Concrete?

*   **Concrete's Weakness in Tension:** Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. In conventionally reinforced concrete, steel reinforcement carries the tensile stresses, but this often leads to cracking.
*   **Eliminating/Reducing Tensile Stresses:** Prestressing effectively "pre-compresses" the concrete in areas where tensile stresses are expected under load. This shifts the stress distribution, making the concrete behave more efficiently.
*   **Benefits over Reinforced Concrete:**
    *   **Larger Spans:** Enables longer spans for beams and slabs.
    *   **Reduced Member Size:** Allows for shallower beams and thinner slabs for the same load capacity.
    *   **Improved Durability:** Minimizes or eliminates cracking, protecting the steel reinforcement from corrosion and improving service life.
    *   **Better Deflection Control:** Reduces sagging and improves serviceability.
    *   **Aesthetically Pleasing:** Thinner members can lead to more slender and elegant designs.
    *   **Efficient Material Usage:** Maximizes the use of concrete's compressive strength.

#### 1.3 Basic Components of a Prestressed Concrete Member

*   **High-Strength Steel Tendons:** Wires, strands (groups of wires), or bars with very high tensile strength (typically 1500-1800 N/mm²).
*   **Concrete:** Normal strength concrete is used, but its compressive strength is crucial.
*   **Anchorage Devices:** Devices used to grip and anchor the tensioned tendons at the ends of the member (e.g., wedges, plates).
*   **Sheathing/Ducts (for post-tensioning):** Sleeves or tubes that house the tendons, preventing bonding with the concrete initially.
*   **Grouting (for post-tensioning):** Cementitious material injected into the sheathing after tensioning to bond the tendons to the concrete and protect them.

#### 1.4 Historical Context

*   The concept of prestressing dates back to the 19th century, but practical applications gained momentum in the early 20th century.
*   **Pioneering Work:** Eugène Freyssinet (France) is widely recognized as the "father of prestressed concrete." He developed high-strength steel wires and effective anchoring systems, leading to significant advancements in its application.

### 2. Types of Pre-stressing

The classification of prestressing is primarily based on the **timing of prestressing relative to the application of external loads.**

#### 2.1 Pre-tensioning

*   **Definition:** In pre-tensioning, the high-strength steel tendons are tensioned *before* the concrete is cast. The tensioned tendons are held at the ends by external anchorages (e.g., a stressing bed). Once the concrete has gained sufficient strength, it bonds to the tensioned tendons, and the external anchorages are released. The compressive force is then transferred to the concrete through bond and end anchorage.

*   **Process:**
    1.  **Anchorages:** Tendons are stretched between fixed anchorages in a casting bed.
    2.  **Concrete Casting:** Concrete is cast around the tensioned tendons.
    3.  **Hardening:** Concrete hardens and gains sufficient strength.
    4.  **Tendon Release:** External forces holding the tendons are released. The tension in the tendons is transferred to the concrete through bond.

*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Bond Transfer:** Prestress is transferred by bond action between concrete and steel.
    *   **Manufacturing:** Typically carried out in factories or casting yards for precast members (e.g., beams, railway sleepers, poles).
    *   **Tendon Profile:** Tendons are generally straight or slightly draped within the cross-section.
    *   **Stress Transfer:** Tendons must be tensioned at a high enough stress to overcome losses due to elastic shortening of concrete and bond slip.

*   **Examples:**
    *   Precast concrete beams for bridges and buildings.
    *   Railway sleepers.
    *   Utility poles.
    *   Floor slabs.

#### 2.2 Post-tensioning

*   **Definition:** In post-tensioning, the concrete is cast first, and the tendons are tensioned *after* the concrete has hardened and gained sufficient strength. Tendons are usually placed within ducts (sheathing) that are embedded in the concrete during casting. After the concrete has reached its designed strength, the tendons are pulled through the ducts using hydraulic jacks, and then anchored at the ends. The prestress is transferred to the concrete through these anchors and, if grouting is used, through bond after grouting.

*   **Process:**
    1.  **Ducts/Sheathing:** Ducts are placed within the formwork before concrete casting. Tendons are threaded through these ducts.
    2.  **Concrete Casting:** Concrete is cast around the ducts and tendons.
    3.  **Hardening:** Concrete hardens and gains sufficient strength.
    4.  **Tensioning:** Tendons are tensioned using hydraulic jacks anchored against the concrete member.
    5.  **Anchoring:** Wedges or other anchorage devices secure the tensioned tendons at the ends.
    6.  **Grouting (Optional but common):** Ducts are injected with grout, which bonds the tendons to the concrete, protecting them and transferring prestress.

*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Anchorage Transfer:** Prestress is transferred through end anchorages.
    *   **Manufacturing:** Can be done either at a casting yard (precast post-tensioning) or at the construction site (cast-in-situ post-tensioning).
    *   **Tendon Profile:** Tendons can be profiled (curved or draped) to effectively counteract bending moments. This is a major advantage.
    *   **Flexibility:** More flexible in design and application, especially for complex shapes and site construction.

*   **Types of Post-tensioning:**
    *   **Bonded Post-tensioning:** After tensioning, the ducts are grouted, creating a bond between the tendons and the concrete. This provides better load distribution and enhances the structural integrity.
    *   **Unbonded Post-tensioning:** Tendons are typically coated with grease and encased in plastic sheathing. They are not grouted. This allows for more flexibility in individual tendon replacement and can reduce the effects of progressive failure.

*   **Examples:**
    *   Large span bridges (especially box girders).
    *   Building slabs and beams.
    *   Water tanks.
    *   Dams.
    *   Stadium roofs.

#### 2.3 Comparison: Pre-tensioning vs. Post-tensioning

| Feature             | Pre-tensioning                               | Post-tensioning                                   |
| :------------------ | :------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| **Timing of Tension** | Before concrete casting                      | After concrete hardening                          |
| **Transfer of Stress** | By bond action                               | By end anchorages (and grout for bonded)          |
| **Manufacturing**   | Factory/Casting yard (precast)               | Site or casting yard                              |
| **Tendon Profile**  | Generally straight or slightly draped        | Can be curved/draped effectively                  |
| **Ducts/Sheathing** | Not required                                 | Required                                          |
| **Grouting**        | Not applicable                               | Usually required (for bonded)                     |
| **Typical Use**     | Smaller structural elements, mass production | Large span structures, complex shapes, site work |
| **Efficiency**      | Very efficient for mass-produced elements    | Highly versatile and efficient for specific needs |

### 3. Analysis of Members: Basic Principles

#### 3.1 Stress Distribution under Prestressing Force

Consider a rectangular concrete beam subjected to a concentric prestressing force $P$.

*   **Concentric Prestressing:** When the prestressing force is applied along the centroidal axis of the beam, it induces a uniform compressive stress of $P/A$ throughout the cross-section, where $A$ is the cross-sectional area.

    *   **Stress due to $P$ only:** $\sigma = P/A$ (compressive)

#### 3.2 Stress Distribution under Eccentric Prestressing Force

When the prestressing force $P$ is applied eccentrically at a distance $e$ from the centroidal axis, it induces both a uniform compressive stress and a bending stress.

*   **Eccentric Prestressing:**
    *   **Force $P$ acting at centroid:** Induces uniform compressive stress $P/A$.
    *   **Moment $Pe$ created by eccentricity:** Induces bending stresses.
        *   Top fiber: $\pm (Pe \cdot y_{top}) / I$ (tensile/compressive)
        *   Bottom fiber: $\pm (Pe \cdot y_{bottom}) / I$ (tensile/compressive)
        *   Where $I$ is the moment of inertia and $y$ is the distance from the neutral axis.

*   **Combined Stress:** The total stress at any fiber is the sum of the stress due to the axial force and the stress due to the moment.

    *   **Stress at top fiber ($\sigma_{top}$):** $-P/A + (Pe \cdot y_{top}) / I$
    *   **Stress at bottom fiber ($\sigma_{bottom}$):** $-P/A - (Pe \cdot y_{bottom}) / I$

    *   **Note:** The sign convention is important. Here, compressive stress is shown as negative. The eccentricity $e$ is usually taken positive when the force is above the centroid.

    *   **For a rectangular section of width $b$ and depth $h$:**
        *   $A = b \cdot h$
        *   $I = (b \cdot h^3) / 12$
        *   $y_{top} = y_{bottom} = h/2$

    *   **Stress at top fiber:** $-P/A + (Pe \cdot (h/2)) / I = -P/A + (P \cdot e \cdot (h/2)) / (b h^3 / 12) = -P/A + 6Pe / (bh^2)$
    *   **Stress at bottom fiber:** $-P/A - (Pe \cdot (h/2)) / I = -P/A - (P \cdot e \cdot (h/2)) / (b h^3 / 12) = -P/A - 6Pe / (bh^2)$

*   **Resultant Stress Distribution:** The stress distribution due to eccentric prestressing is linear, with the maximum compression at one extreme fiber and tension at the other (or compression across the entire section if the resultant stress is kept within limits).

#### 3.3 Stress Distribution under Prestressing and External Loads

When a prestressed member is subjected to external loads (e.g., bending moments due to service loads), the stresses induced by these loads are superimposed on the stresses due to prestressing.

*   **Load-induced stresses:** Under a sagging moment $M$, the top fibers experience compression and the bottom fibers experience tension.
*   **Total Stress:**
    *   **At top fiber:** $\sigma_{top} = (-P/A + Pe \cdot y_{top}/I) + (\text{stress due to } M \text{ at top})$
    *   **At bottom fiber:** $\sigma_{bottom} = (-P/A - Pe \cdot y_{bottom}/I) + (\text{stress due to } M \text{ at bottom})$

*   **Importance of Prestressing Profile:** By curving the tendons (post-tensioning), the prestressing force can be made to counteract the external bending moments more effectively.
    *   A parabolic profile of tendons can be used to completely balance the effect of a uniformly distributed load (UDL) on a simply supported beam.

#### 3.4 Preliminary Analysis - Concept of Load Balancing

*   **Definition:** Load balancing is a conceptual approach to designing prestressed concrete members. It involves choosing the prestressing force and its profile such that the stresses induced by the prestressing force balance, or partially balance, the stresses induced by external service loads.
*   **How it works:**
    *   The upward prestressing force creates an internal moment that counteracts the downward bending moment from external loads.
    *   By suitably choosing the eccentricity of the tendons, one can make the stresses due to prestressing directly oppose the stresses due to the applied loads.
    *   For example, if a beam is subjected to a UDL, a parabolic tendon profile can be used to provide a self-equilibrating upward moment that perfectly balances the downward moment from the UDL, resulting in zero bending stress in the concrete under service loads.

### 4. Advantages and Disadvantages of Prestressed Concrete

#### 4.1 Advantages (Recap)

*   Higher strength and stiffness.
*   Reduced cracking and deflections.
*   Improved durability and service life.
*   Economical for longer spans and heavier loads.
*   More efficient use of materials.
*   Aesthetically superior due to slender members.

#### 4.2 Disadvantages

*   **Higher Initial Cost:** High-strength steel and specialized equipment increase initial costs.
*   **Requires Skilled Labour:** Prestressing operations demand skilled personnel.
*   **Complex Analysis:** The analysis and design process can be more complex than for reinforced concrete.
*   **Losses of Prestress:** Prestress is reduced over time due to various factors (discussed in later modules), which needs careful consideration.
*   **Anchorage Zones:** The anchorage zones are critical and require special design considerations to prevent crushing or splitting of concrete.

### 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
Differentiate between pre-tensioning and post-tensioning. Which method is generally more suitable for large-span bridges and why?

**Answer 1:**
*   **Pre-tensioning:** Tendons tensioned before concrete casting, prestress transferred by bond. Primarily used for precast elements.
*   **Post-tensioning:** Tendons tensioned after concrete hardening, prestress transferred by end anchorages (and grout for bonded). Can be done on-site or in precast yards, allows for curved tendon profiles.
*   **Large-span bridges:** Post-tensioning is generally more suitable because the ability to use curved tendon profiles allows for more effective counteraction of bending moments over longer spans. It also offers more flexibility for site construction and complex geometry.

---

**Question 2:**
A concrete beam of rectangular section 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep is prestressed by a concentric force of 200 kN. Calculate the stress distribution in the beam due to this prestressing force.

**Answer 2:**
*   Section area, $A = b \times h = 100 \text{ mm} \times 200 \text{ mm} = 20000 \text{ mm}^2 = 0.02 \text{ m}^2$.
*   Prestressing force, $P = 200 \text{ kN}$.
*   Since the force is concentric, it is applied at the centroid of the section.
*   The stress induced is uniform compression throughout the cross-section.
*   Stress, $\sigma = P/A = 200 \text{ kN} / 20000 \text{ mm}^2 = 10 \text{ N/mm}^2$.
*   **Stress Distribution:** The beam experiences a uniform compressive stress of $10 \text{ N/mm}^2$ across its entire cross-section.

---

**Question 3:**
A post-tensioned concrete beam of rectangular section 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep is prestressed by a force of 200 kN applied at an eccentricity of 50 mm from the centroidal axis. Calculate the stress at the top and bottom fibers of the beam at the section where the eccentricity is maximum.

**Answer 3:**
*   Section width, $b = 100$ mm.
*   Section depth, $h = 200$ mm.
*   Area, $A = b \times h = 100 \times 200 = 20000 \text{ mm}^2$.
*   Moment of inertia, $I = (b h^3)/12 = (100 \times 200^3)/12 = 66.67 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4$.
*   Prestressing force, $P = 200$ kN.
*   Eccentricity, $e = 50$ mm.
*   Distance from centroid to top/bottom fiber, $y = h/2 = 200/2 = 100$ mm.

*   **Stress due to axial force ($P/A$):**
    *   $\sigma_{axial} = -P/A = -200 \text{ kN} / 20000 \text{ mm}^2 = -10 \text{ N/mm}^2$ (compression).

*   **Stress due to bending moment ($Pe \cdot y / I$):**
    *   Bending moment, $M = P \times e = 200 \text{ kN} \times 50 \text{ mm} = 10000 \text{ kNm} = 10 \times 10^6 \text{ Nmm}$.
    *   **Stress at top fiber:** $\sigma_{bend, top} = +(M \cdot y_{top}) / I = +(10 \times 10^6 \text{ Nmm} \times 100 \text{ mm}) / (66.67 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4) \approx +15 \text{ N/mm}^2$ (tension).
    *   **Stress at bottom fiber:** $\sigma_{bend, bottom} = -(M \cdot y_{bottom}) / I = -(10 \times 10^6 \text{ Nmm} \times 100 \text{ mm}) / (66.67 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4) \approx -15 \text{ N/mm}^2$ (compression).

*   **Total Stress:**
    *   **At top fiber:** $\sigma_{top} = \sigma_{axial} + \sigma_{bend, top} = -10 \text{ N/mm}^2 + 15 \text{ N/mm}^2 = +5 \text{ N/mm}^2$ (tension).
    *   **At bottom fiber:** $\sigma_{bottom} = \sigma_{axial} + \sigma_{bend, bottom} = -10 \text{ N/mm}^2 - 15 \text{ N/mm}^2 = -25 \text{ N/mm}^2$ (compression).

    *   **Therefore:** The stress at the top fiber is $5 \text{ N/mm}^2$ tension, and the stress at the bottom fiber is $25 \text{ N/mm}^2$ compression.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Prestressing introduces internal compressive stresses** to counteract tensile stresses from external loads.
*   **Concrete is weak in tension**, making prestressing essential for efficient structural behavior.
*   **Pre-tensioning** involves tensioning tendons *before* casting concrete and relies on *bond* for stress transfer.
*   **Post-tensioning** involves tensioning tendons *after* concrete hardening and relies on *end anchorages* for stress transfer; it allows for *curved tendon profiles*.
*   **Eccentric prestressing** induces both axial compression and bending stresses.
*   **Load balancing** is a key concept where prestressing is designed to directly counteract external load effects.
*   The **analysis of prestressed members** involves superimposing stresses from prestressing and external loads.
*   **Losses of prestress** are a critical aspect that will be covered in later modules.
