# MATHEMATICS FOR ELECTRICAL SCIENCE AND PHYSICAL SCIENCE - 1

## Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order

### Topic: Solutions of the Form $y = e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$

Welcome back, everyone! Today, we're going to dive into a very specific, yet incredibly important, type of solution for homogeneous linear second-order ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with constant coefficients. We've already learned how to find solutions when the characteristic equation gives us real, distinct roots, and when it gives us real, repeated roots. Now, we're tackling the case where the roots are complex.

Specifically, we're focusing on solutions of the form $y = e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$. This form is crucial because it represents **damped oscillatory behavior**, which you'll see everywhere in electrical circuits (like RLC circuits) and in physical systems exhibiting oscillations with damping (like a pendulum with air resistance). Understanding this form directly helps us achieve **Course Outcome CO2: Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equation with constant coefficients.**

#### 1. The Foundation: Complex Roots of the Characteristic Equation

Let's start with our familiar general form of a homogeneous linear second-order ODE with constant coefficients:

$ay'' + by' + cy = 0$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants.

The heart of solving these ODEs lies in the **characteristic equation** (also called the auxiliary equation), which we get by assuming a solution of the form $y = e^{rx}$:

$ar^2 + br + c = 0$

We've seen that the nature of the roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ of this quadratic equation dictates the form of our general solution.

*   **Case 1: Real, Distinct Roots ($r_1 \neq r_2$)**
    $y = c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x}$
*   **Case 2: Real, Repeated Roots ($r_1 = r_2 = r$)**
    $y = c_1 e^{rx} + c_2 x e^{rx}$

Now, let's look at **Case 3: Complex Conjugate Roots.**

When the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ is negative, the roots of the characteristic equation are complex. We can write these roots in the form:

$r = \alpha \pm i\omega$

where $\alpha = -\frac{b}{2a}$ and $\omega = \frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}$. Here, $\alpha$ is the real part and $\omega$ is the imaginary part (and we take $\omega$ to be positive).

#### 2. Deriving the Real-Valued Solution from Complex Roots

If we substitute these complex roots directly into $y = e^{rx}$, we'd get solutions involving $e^{(\alpha \pm i\omega)x}$. Using Euler's formula, $e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i \sin(\theta)$, we can express these as:

$y_1 = e^{(\alpha + i\omega)x} = e^{\alpha x} e^{i\omega x} = e^{\alpha x} (\cos(\omega x) + i \sin(\omega x))$
$y_2 = e^{(\alpha - i\omega)x} = e^{\alpha x} e^{-i\omega x} = e^{\alpha x} (\cos(\omega x) - i \sin(\omega x))$

Now, the general solution is a linear combination of these: $y = C_1 y_1 + C_2 y_2$. However, in most physical and engineering applications, we are interested in *real-valued* solutions. We can obtain real-valued solutions by taking linear combinations of $y_1$ and $y_2$.

A very convenient way to get two linearly independent *real* solutions is to consider:

*   $\frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} = \frac{e^{\alpha x} (\cos(\omega x) + i \sin(\omega x)) + e^{\alpha x} (\cos(\omega x) - i \sin(\omega x))}{2} = \frac{2 e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)}{2} = e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$
*   $\frac{y_1 - y_2}{2i} = \frac{e^{\alpha x} (\cos(\omega x) + i \sin(\omega x)) - e^{\alpha x} (\cos(\omega x) - i \sin(\omega x))}{2i} = \frac{e^{\alpha x} (2i \sin(\omega x))}{2i} = e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$

So, our two linearly independent real solutions are:

$y_A = e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$
$y_B = e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$

The **general solution** for the case of complex conjugate roots $r = \alpha \pm i\omega$ is then:

$y(x) = c_1 e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x) + c_2 e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$

or, often written in a more compact form using amplitude and phase:

$y(x) = A e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x - \phi)$

where $A$ and $\phi$ are constants determined by initial or boundary conditions. This form is directly related to how we describe oscillations in physics and electrical engineering. Remember this general form! It's your ticket to solving many oscillating systems.

**Think of it this way:** If you push a swing, it oscillates. If there's air resistance, the swing eventually stops – it's *damped*. The $e^{\alpha x}$ term represents this damping (if $\alpha < 0$, the amplitude decreases). The $\cos(\omega x)$ part is the pure oscillation, and the $\alpha$ in the exponent tells you *how fast* the oscillations die down. This ties in beautifully with **Course Outcome CO2**.

#### 3. Practical Examples and Problem Solving

Let's walk through some examples to solidify this.

**Example 1: A Simple Damped Oscillator**

Consider the ODE: $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$

*   **Step 1: Form the characteristic equation.**
    $r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0$

*   **Step 2: Solve for the roots.**
    We use the quadratic formula: $r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$
    Here, $a=1$, $b=2$, $c=5$.
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(5)}}{2(1)}$
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2}$
    $r = -1 \pm 2i$

*   **Step 3: Identify $\alpha$ and $\omega$.**
    Our roots are in the form $\alpha \pm i\omega$.
    So, $\alpha = -1$ and $\omega = 2$.

*   **Step 4: Write the general solution.**
    Using the form $y(x) = c_1 e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x) + c_2 e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$, we get:
    $y(x) = c_1 e^{-x} \cos(2x) + c_2 e^{-x} \sin(2x)$
    or
    $y(x) = A e^{-x} \cos(2x - \phi)$

**What does this solution tell us?**
The term $e^{-x}$ indicates that the amplitude of the oscillation decays exponentially as $x$ increases. The $\cos(2x)$ part means it oscillates with a frequency related to 2. If this ODE represented the voltage across a component in an RLC circuit, this solution would describe how the voltage settles down after a disturbance.

**Example 2: Finding a Specific Solution with Initial Conditions**

Let's solve the ODE $y'' - 6y' + 13y = 0$ with initial conditions $y(0) = 2$ and $y'(0) = 4$.

*   **Step 1: Characteristic Equation.**
    $r^2 - 6r + 13 = 0$

*   **Step 2: Solve for Roots.**
    $r = \frac{-(-6) \pm \sqrt{(-6)^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2(1)}$
    $r = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 52}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{6 \pm 4i}{2}$
    $r = 3 \pm 2i$

*   **Step 3: Identify $\alpha$ and $\omega$.**
    $\alpha = 3$, $\omega = 2$.

*   **Step 4: General Solution.**
    $y(x) = c_1 e^{3x} \cos(2x) + c_2 e^{3x} \sin(2x)$
    $y(x) = e^{3x} (c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x))$

*   **Step 5: Apply Initial Conditions.**
    First condition: $y(0) = 2$.
    $2 = e^{3(0)} (c_1 \cos(2(0)) + c_2 \sin(2(0)))$
    $2 = 1 (c_1 \cdot 1 + c_2 \cdot 0)$
    So, $c_1 = 2$.

    Now our solution is $y(x) = e^{3x} (2 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x))$.

    We need $y'(x)$ to apply the second condition. Let's differentiate $y(x)$ using the product rule and chain rule.
    $y'(x) = \frac{d}{dx} [e^{3x} (2 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x))]$
    $y'(x) = (3e^{3x}) (2 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)) + e^{3x} (-4 \sin(2x) + 2c_2 \cos(2x))$

    Second condition: $y'(0) = 4$.
    $4 = (3e^0) (2 \cos(0) + c_2 \sin(0)) + e^0 (-4 \sin(0) + 2c_2 \cos(0))$
    $4 = (3)(2 \cdot 1 + c_2 \cdot 0) + 1 (-4 \cdot 0 + 2c_2 \cdot 1)$
    $4 = 3(2) + 1(2c_2)$
    $4 = 6 + 2c_2$
    $2c_2 = 4 - 6$
    $2c_2 = -2$
    $c_2 = -1$.

*   **Step 6: Write the specific solution.**
    Substitute $c_1=2$ and $c_2=-1$ back into the general solution:
    $y(x) = e^{3x} (2 \cos(2x) - \sin(2x))$

**Interpretation:** This specific solution starts at $y=2$ when $x=0$ and has a slope of $y'=4$. The $e^{3x}$ term indicates that this oscillation is *growing* in amplitude. Think of a microphone picking up sound and feeding it back into speakers – if not managed, it can lead to a screeching feedback loop, where the oscillations get louder and louder. This is often called *unstable* behavior, corresponding to $\alpha > 0$.

**Connection to Physics/Engineering:**
These solutions are fundamental to understanding:
*   **RLC Circuits:** Voltage or current in these circuits often exhibit oscillatory behavior described by such ODEs. The term $e^{\alpha x}$ corresponds to the decay (or growth) due to resistance and inductance/capacitance.
*   **Mechanical Vibrations:** Damped harmonic oscillators, like a mass on a spring with friction, are modeled by ODEs with these types of solutions.
*   **Wave Phenomena:** While not directly solving wave equations, the underlying oscillatory behavior has connections.

**Kreyszig's Approach (Section 2.5 in 10th Edition):**
Kreyszig also emphasizes the derivation from complex roots $\alpha \pm i\omega$ and presents the solutions as $e^{\alpha x}(\cos \omega x, \sin \omega x)$. He carefully explains the linear independence of these functions, which is critical for constructing the general solution. The process is very consistent with what we've discussed.

#### 4. Common Pitfalls and Exam Tips

*   **Sign Errors:** Be extremely careful with signs when calculating $\alpha$ and $\omega$, especially from the characteristic equation. Double-check your quadratic formula application.
*   **Forgetting the $e^{\alpha x}$ Term:** The damping factor is crucial! It's not just $\cos(\omega x)$ or $\sin(\omega x)$.
*   **Differentiating Carefully:** When applying initial conditions for $y'$, the product rule can be tricky. Write it out step-by-step.
*   **Confusing $\omega$ with Frequency:** While $\omega$ is related to frequency (angular frequency), remember it comes directly from the imaginary part of the root.
*   **Real vs. Complex Solutions:** Always aim for real-valued solutions unless specifically asked otherwise. The method of taking linear combinations of $y_1$ and $y_2$ is key here.

**Exam Focus:** Expect to be asked to find the general solution given an ODE with complex roots. Also, problems involving initial conditions are very common, testing your ability to find specific solutions. Understanding the physical interpretation of $\alpha$ (damping/growth) and $\omega$ (oscillation rate) is also important for conceptual questions. This directly supports **Course Outcome CO2**.

#### 5. Relating to Other Course Outcomes

*   **CO1 (Linear Systems and Matrices):** While this topic is about differential equations, the characteristic equation itself is a quadratic equation, a fundamental concept in algebra. The roots of polynomials are related to eigenvalues of matrices, a topic covered in CO1. Understanding complex numbers and their properties, crucial for complex roots, is also a foundation from linear algebra.
*   **CO3 (Laplace Transform):** Laplace transforms provide an alternative powerful method to solve ODEs, including those with complex roots. The behavior we've described ($e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x), e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$) has well-defined Laplace transforms, which are used to solve circuits and systems problems.
*   **CO4 (Taylor and Fourier Series):** Oscillatory functions like $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ are the building blocks of Fourier series. Understanding their behavior and representation is foundational for Fourier analysis. Taylor series can be used to approximate these functions, though direct solution of ODEs is generally preferred here.

### Sample Questions and Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
For the ODE $y'' - 4y' + 13y = 0$, what is the nature of the solution? Describe the physical interpretation of the components of the solution.

**Answer 1:**
The characteristic equation is $r^2 - 4r + 13 = 0$.
Using the quadratic formula:
$r = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2} = 2 \pm 3i$.
Since the roots are complex conjugates ($2 \pm 3i$), the general solution will be of the form $y(x) = e^{2x}(c_1 \cos(3x) + c_2 \sin(3x))$.
Here, $\alpha = 2$ and $\omega = 3$.
The term $e^{2x}$ indicates that the amplitude of the oscillation is *growing* exponentially. The $\cos(3x)$ and $\sin(3x)$ terms represent the oscillatory behavior with an angular frequency of 3. This describes an unstable oscillatory system, where the amplitude increases with time, such as a resonating system without damping or with positive feedback.

**Question 2 (Problem Solving):**
Find the particular solution of $y'' + 4y = 0$ given $y(\pi/2) = 0$ and $y'(\pi/2) = 2$.

**Answer 2:**
The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 4 = 0$, which gives $r^2 = -4$, so $r = \pm 2i$.
This is a case of purely imaginary roots, where $\alpha = 0$ and $\omega = 2$.
The general solution is $y(x) = c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)$.

Now we apply the initial conditions:
1.  $y(\pi/2) = 0$:
    $0 = c_1 \cos(2 \cdot \pi/2) + c_2 \sin(2 \cdot \pi/2)$
    $0 = c_1 \cos(\pi) + c_2 \sin(\pi)$
    $0 = c_1 (-1) + c_2 (0)$
    $0 = -c_1 \implies c_1 = 0$.

    So, the solution becomes $y(x) = c_2 \sin(2x)$.

2.  We need $y'(x)$ to apply the second condition.
    $y'(x) = \frac{d}{dx} (c_2 \sin(2x)) = c_2 \cdot 2 \cos(2x) = 2c_2 \cos(2x)$.

3.  $y'(\pi/2) = 2$:
    $2 = 2c_2 \cos(2 \cdot \pi/2)$
    $2 = 2c_2 \cos(\pi)$
    $2 = 2c_2 (-1)$
    $2 = -2c_2 \implies c_2 = -1$.

Therefore, the particular solution is $y(x) = - \sin(2x)$.

**Question 3 (Exam Style):**
Which of the following ODEs will have solutions of the form $y(x) = e^{-x} (c_1 \cos(3x) + c_2 \sin(3x))$?
(A) $y'' + 6y' + 10y = 0$
(B) $y'' + 2y' + 10y = 0$
(C) $y'' - 2y' + 10y = 0$
(D) $y'' + 2y' + 4y = 0$

**Answer 3:**
The form $y(x) = e^{\alpha x} (c_1 \cos(\omega x) + c_2 \sin(\omega x))$ arises when the characteristic equation has roots $\alpha \pm i\omega$.
In our case, the form suggests $\alpha = -1$ and $\omega = 3$. Therefore, the roots should be $-1 \pm 3i$.

The characteristic equation for such roots is $(r - (-1+3i))(r - (-1-3i)) = 0$.
This expands to $((r+1) - 3i)((r+1) + 3i) = 0$.
Using $(A-B)(A+B) = A^2 - B^2$, we get $(r+1)^2 - (3i)^2 = 0$.
$(r^2 + 2r + 1) - (9i^2) = 0$.
Since $i^2 = -1$, this becomes $(r^2 + 2r + 1) - (-9) = 0$.
$r^2 + 2r + 1 + 9 = 0$.
$r^2 + 2r + 10 = 0$.

This characteristic equation corresponds to the ODE $y'' + 2y' + 10y = 0$.
Thus, the correct option is **(B)**.

This covers our understanding of solutions of the form $e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$ and their relation to complex roots. Keep practicing these!
