# MATHEMATICS FOR ELECTRICAL SCIENCE AND PHYSICAL SCIENCE - 1

## Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order

### Topic: Solutions of the form $y = e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$

Welcome back, everyone! In our journey through the fascinating world of differential equations, we've already laid some solid groundwork. We understand what a second-order homogeneous linear ordinary differential equation (ODE) looks like: it's an equation of the form $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants, and $y$, $y'$, and $y''$ are involved in a linear fashion. This type of equation pops up *everywhere* in physical sciences and electrical engineering – think about circuits with resistors, inductors, and capacitors, or systems undergoing oscillations like springs and pendulums.

Our goal in this module is to become masters at solving these equations. We've already seen that the "magic bullet" for these equations lies in the **characteristic equation**, $ar^2 + br + c = 0$. The roots of this equation ($r_1$, $r_2$) dictate the form of our solutions.

Today, we’re going to dive into a specific, yet incredibly important, type of solution that arises when the roots of the characteristic equation are **complex conjugates**. This is where our topic of $y = e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$ and its related form $y = e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$ comes into play. These solutions are crucial for understanding oscillatory behavior, damping, and resonance – concepts absolutely fundamental to electrical science (AC circuits, signal processing) and physical science (vibrations, wave phenomena).

### 1. The Case of Complex Conjugate Roots

Let's recap what happens when the discriminant of our characteristic equation, $b^2 - 4ac$, is negative. This is the condition for complex roots. The roots are given by the quadratic formula:

$r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

If $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, we can write $b^2 - 4ac = -(4ac - b^2)$, and $\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac} = \sqrt{-(4ac - b^2)} = i\sqrt{4ac - b^2}$.
Let $\Delta = 4ac - b^2 > 0$. Then the roots are:

$r = \frac{-b \pm i\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a} = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm i\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$

We can express these roots in the standard complex form $r = \alpha \pm i\beta$, where:

*   $\alpha = -\frac{b}{2a}$
*   $\beta = \frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}$

Now, the theory of ODEs tells us that if $r_1 = \alpha + i\beta$ and $r_2 = \alpha - i\beta$ are the roots of the characteristic equation, then two linearly independent solutions are $y_1 = e^{r_1 x}$ and $y_2 = e^{r_2 x}$.

So, we have solutions like $y_1 = e^{(\alpha + i\beta)x}$ and $y_2 = e^{(\alpha - i\beta)x}$.
Using Euler's formula, $e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i\sin(\theta)$, we can expand these:

$y_1 = e^{\alpha x} e^{i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x))$
$y_2 = e^{\alpha x} e^{-i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x))$

While these are valid solutions, they are complex-valued, and often in physical systems, we're interested in real-valued solutions. The good news is that any linear combination of solutions is also a solution. So, we can form new, real-valued solutions by taking linear combinations of $y_1$ and $y_2$:

Consider:
$y_3 = \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} = \frac{e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x)) + e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x))}{2}$
$y_3 = \frac{2e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)}{2} = e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)$

And:
$y_4 = \frac{y_1 - y_2}{2i} = \frac{e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x)) - e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x))}{2i}$
$y_4 = \frac{2ie^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)}{2i} = e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$

And voilà! We have obtained two real-valued, linearly independent solutions: $e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)$ and $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$.

**This is the core idea:** When the characteristic equation yields complex conjugate roots of the form $\alpha \pm i\beta$, the general solution to the homogeneous linear ODE $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ is given by:

$y(x) = c_1 e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x) + c_2 e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$

or equivalently, written as:

$y(x) = e^{\alpha x} (c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x))$

Here, $c_1$ and $c_2$ are arbitrary constants determined by initial or boundary conditions. This form is extremely useful, as it directly describes **damped oscillations**.

*   **The $e^{\alpha x}$ term:** This is the **damping factor**.
    *   If $\alpha < 0$, the amplitude of the oscillation decays over time (damped oscillation). Think of a pendulum with friction.
    *   If $\alpha = 0$, the amplitude remains constant (undamped oscillation). Think of an ideal spring-mass system without friction.
    *   If $\alpha > 0$, the amplitude grows exponentially (over-damped or unstable oscillation). This is less common in passive physical systems but can occur in control systems or with external energy input.
*   **The $\cos(\beta x)$ and $\sin(\beta x)$ terms:** These represent the **oscillatory behavior**.
    *   $\beta$ is related to the **frequency** of the oscillation. A larger $\beta$ means faster oscillations.

This directly relates to **Course Outcome CO2**: "Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equation with constant coefficients." We are specifically addressing the homogeneous case here, and the complex root scenario is a major part of mastering CO2.

### 2. Connecting to Physical and Electrical Phenomena

Why is this form $e^{\alpha x}(c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x))$ so important?

Imagine an RLC circuit (Resistor-Inductor-Capacitor). The governing differential equation for the charge $q(t)$ on the capacitor or the current $i(t)$ in the circuit often turns out to be a second-order linear homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients.

For example, considering the voltage drops around a series RLC circuit: $L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri + \frac{1}{C}\int i\, dt = V(t)$. If $V(t)=0$ (no external voltage source) and $i(t) = q'(t)$, we get $Lq''(t) + Rq'(t) + \frac{1}{C}q(t) = 0$.

The characteristic equation is $Lr^2 + Rr + \frac{1}{C} = 0$.
The roots are $r = \frac{-R \pm \sqrt{R^2 - 4L/C}}{2L}$.

If the resistance $R$ is "low enough" relative to the inductance $L$ and capacitance $C$, specifically if $R^2 < 4L/C$, then the term inside the square root is negative, leading to complex roots. In this case, the charge (or current) will oscillate.

*   The term $e^{-(R/2L)t}$ corresponds to our $e^{\alpha t}$ with $\alpha = -R/2L$. Since $R, L > 0$, $\alpha$ is negative, meaning the oscillations **decay** over time due to the energy dissipated by the resistor. This is called **underdamped oscillation**.
*   The frequency of oscillation is related to $\beta = \frac{\sqrt{4L/C - R^2}}{2L}$.

This is a perfect example of where our $e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)$ and $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$ solutions are not just mathematical constructs but describe real physical behavior. They are key to understanding how systems respond to initial conditions when energy can be stored in multiple forms (like in an inductor and capacitor) and dissipated.

This also ties into **Course Outcome CO1** (solving systems of linear equations and diagonalizing matrices) in a subtle way. While we aren't explicitly diagonalizing here, the concept of finding basis solutions ($e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)$ and $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$) for the solution space is analogous to finding a basis for a vector space. The set of all solutions forms a vector space, and these two functions form a basis for it.

### 3. Finding the Solution: A Step-by-Step Approach

Let's formalize the process of finding the solution when we encounter complex roots.

**Step 1: Form the Characteristic Equation**
For an ODE $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, the characteristic equation is $ar^2 + br + c = 0$.

**Step 2: Solve the Characteristic Equation**
Use the quadratic formula to find the roots $r_1, r_2$.
$r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

**Step 3: Identify Complex Conjugate Roots**
If $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, the roots will be complex conjugates of the form $r = \alpha \pm i\beta$.
Identify $\alpha$ and $\beta$:
$\alpha = -\frac{b}{2a}$
$\beta = \frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}$

**Step 4: Write the General Solution**
The general solution is then:
$y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x))$

**Step 5: Apply Initial Conditions (if given)**
If initial conditions are provided (e.g., $y(x_0) = y_0$, $y'(x_0) = y'_0$), you can find the specific values of $c_1$ and $c_2$ by substituting these conditions into the general solution and its derivative.

Let's walk through an example.

**Example 1: An Undamped Oscillator**

Consider the ODE: $y'' + 4y = 0$.

*   **Step 1:** The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 4 = 0$.
*   **Step 2:** Solving for $r$: $r^2 = -4 \implies r = \pm\sqrt{-4} = \pm 2i$.
*   **Step 3:** These roots are complex conjugates. We can write them as $0 \pm 2i$.
    So, $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 2$.
*   **Step 4:** The general solution is $y(x) = e^{0x}(c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x))$, which simplifies to:
    $y(x) = c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)$.

This form ($y(x) = c_1 \cos(\omega x) + c_2 \sin(\omega x)$) is the classic solution for undamped simple harmonic motion, like a frictionless pendulum or an LC circuit. Here, $\alpha=0$, so there's no damping.

Now, let's add some initial conditions to find a specific solution.

Suppose for $y'' + 4y = 0$, we have $y(0) = 1$ and $y'(0) = 2$.

*   **Step 5 (Applying Initial Conditions):**
    Our general solution is $y(x) = c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)$.
    Using $y(0) = 1$:
    $1 = c_1 \cos(0) + c_2 \sin(0)$
    $1 = c_1 (1) + c_2 (0)$
    So, $c_1 = 1$.

    Now we need $y'(x)$. Differentiating our general solution:
    $y'(x) = -2c_1 \sin(2x) + 2c_2 \cos(2x)$.
    Using $y'(0) = 2$:
    $2 = -2c_1 \sin(0) + 2c_2 \cos(0)$
    $2 = -2c_1 (0) + 2c_2 (1)$
    $2 = 2c_2$
    So, $c_2 = 1$.

    The specific solution is $y(x) = \cos(2x) + \sin(2x)$.

**Example 2: A Damped Oscillator**

Consider the ODE: $y'' + 6y' + 13y = 0$.

*   **Step 1:** Characteristic equation: $r^2 + 6r + 13 = 0$.
*   **Step 2:** Use the quadratic formula:
    $r = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{6^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2(1)}$
    $r = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 52}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{-6 \pm 4i}{2}$
    $r = -3 \pm 2i$.
*   **Step 3:** The roots are complex conjugates: $r_1 = -3 + 2i$ and $r_2 = -3 - 2i$.
    Here, $\alpha = -3$ and $\beta = 2$.
*   **Step 4:** The general solution is:
    $y(x) = e^{-3x}(c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x))$.

This represents a system that oscillates with a frequency related to $\beta=2$, but the amplitude of the oscillation decays exponentially because $\alpha=-3$ is negative. This is a classic **underdamped** scenario.

**Relatable Analogy: A Swing Set**
Think about pushing a child on a swing.
*   If there's no wind or friction, the swing goes back and forth at a roughly constant amplitude. This is like the undamped case ($y'' + \omega^2 y = 0$), where $\alpha=0$.
*   If there's a gentle breeze pushing against the swing, or friction in the chains, the amplitude of the swing will gradually decrease. This is like the underdamped case ($e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)$ with $\alpha < 0$). The swing is still oscillating ($\cos(\beta x)$ term), but the maximum height it reaches gets smaller and smaller ($e^{\alpha x}$ term).
*   If the resistance is very high (like trying to swing in thick mud!), the swing might not oscillate at all, or only make a small movement and then stop. This is a different scenario (over-damped or critically damped) that we'll cover later, but it's useful to see how different types of roots lead to different physical behaviors.

### 4. Incorporating $\boldsymbol{e}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha x}}$ into Solutions

Our topic explicitly mentions $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$. This is indeed one of the fundamental real solutions when roots are $\alpha \pm i\omega$. The other fundamental solution is $e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega x)$. The general solution is a linear combination of these two.

The form $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x))$ is directly derived from the complex roots and is the standard way to express solutions for oscillatory phenomena with damping.

**Important Note for Exams:** When you get complex roots $\alpha \pm i\beta$, immediately write down $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x))$. Don't get stuck trying to use the $e^{rx}$ forms directly if real-valued solutions are expected. Kreyszig, in Chapter 2.4 of his "Advanced Engineering Mathematics," covers this extensively, showing how the complex exponential solutions can be combined to yield real sine and cosine solutions.

### 5. Alternative Formulations of the General Solution

Sometimes, the general solution can be expressed in a slightly different, yet equivalent, form. Instead of $c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x)$, we can write it as:

$y(x) = A e^{\alpha x} \cos(\beta x - \phi)$

or

$y(x) = A e^{\alpha x} \sin(\beta x + \phi')$

where $A$, $\phi$, and $\phi'$ are constants. These forms are useful for analyzing the amplitude and phase of oscillations.

*   $A$ represents the **amplitude** of the oscillation, which is itself decaying or growing due to the $e^{\alpha x}$ term.
*   $\phi$ (or $\phi'$) is the **phase shift**, which tells us about the initial displacement or velocity of the system.

To get from the $c_1, c_2$ form to the $A, \phi$ form:
We know that $A\cos(\beta x - \phi) = A(\cos(\beta x)\cos(\phi) + \sin(\beta x)\sin(\phi))$.
Comparing $c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x)$ with $A\cos(\phi) \cos(\beta x) + A\sin(\phi) \sin(\beta x)$, we can see that:
$c_1 = A\cos(\phi)$
$c_2 = A\sin(\phi)$

From these, we can find $A$ and $\phi$:
$A^2 = c_1^2 + c_2^2 \implies A = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}$
$\tan(\phi) = \frac{c_2}{c_1} \implies \phi = \arctan\left(\frac{c_2}{c_1}\right)$ (being careful about the quadrant of $\phi$ based on the signs of $c_1$ and $c_2$).

This transformation is relevant to understanding signal analysis, as explored in books like Haykin and Van Veen's "Signals and Systems." They often work with these amplitude-phase forms to analyze the spectral content and time-domain behavior of signals. This connects to **Course Outcome CO3** where Laplace transforms are used to solve ODEs, as the nature of the roots (real, repeated, complex) directly dictates the form of the transformed function and its inverse transform, which often results in these oscillatory or exponential terms.

### 6. Summary and Key Takeaways

Remember this: When solving a homogeneous linear second-order ODE with constant coefficients, the nature of the roots of the characteristic equation dictates the form of your solution.

*   **Distinct Real Roots ($r_1, r_2$):** $y(x) = c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x}$
*   **Repeated Real Roots ($r$):** $y(x) = c_1 e^{r x} + c_2 x e^{r x}$
*   **Complex Conjugate Roots ($\alpha \pm i\beta$):** $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x))$

Our focus today has been the third case. The presence of $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ (and $e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega x)$) in solutions signifies oscillatory behavior, potentially with a decaying or growing amplitude depending on the sign of $\alpha$. This is crucial for understanding phenomena like damped vibrations in mechanical systems and the transient response of electrical circuits.

Always double-check your arithmetic when calculating roots and identifying $\alpha$ and $\beta$. A small mistake there can lead to a completely wrong solution form.

### Sample Questions and Answers

**Q1. Conceptual Question:**
What type of physical behavior is typically represented by a solution of the form $y(x) = e^{-2x}(c_1 \cos(3x) + c_2 \sin(3x))$ for a second-order ODE?

**Answer:**
This solution represents **underdamped oscillations**.
*   The $\cos(3x)$ and $\sin(3x)$ terms indicate oscillatory behavior with a frequency related to $\beta=3$.
*   The $e^{-2x}$ term is a **damping factor**. Since the exponent is negative ($\alpha = -2$), the amplitude of the oscillations decreases exponentially over time. This is characteristic of systems where energy is dissipated, like a damped pendulum or an RLC circuit with resistance.

**Q2. Exam-Oriented Question:**
Find the general solution of the differential equation $y'' - 4y' + 13y = 0$.

**Solution:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:** The characteristic equation is $r^2 - 4r + 13 = 0$.
2.  **Solve for Roots:** Using the quadratic formula $r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$:
    $r = \frac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2(1)}$
    $r = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2}$
    $r = 2 \pm 3i$.
3.  **Identify $\alpha$ and $\beta$:** The roots are complex conjugates of the form $\alpha \pm i\beta$.
    Here, $\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = 3$.
4.  **Write General Solution:** The general solution for complex conjugate roots is $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1 \cos(\beta x) + c_2 \sin(\beta x))$.
    Substituting $\alpha=2$ and $\beta=3$:
    $y(x) = e^{2x}(c_1 \cos(3x) + c_2 \sin(3x))$.

**Common Pitfall:** Forgetting the $e^{\alpha x}$ term or incorrectly identifying $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Make sure $\beta$ is the coefficient of $i$ and $\alpha$ is the real part.

**Q3. Application-Based Question:**
A system's behavior is described by the ODE $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$. If the initial conditions are $y(0)=0$ and $y'(0)=4$, find the specific solution.

**Solution:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:** $r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0$.
2.  **Solve for Roots:**
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(5)}}{2(1)}$
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2}$
    $r = -1 \pm 2i$.
3.  **Identify $\alpha$ and $\beta$:** $\alpha = -1$, $\beta = 2$.
4.  **General Solution:** $y(x) = e^{-x}(c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x))$.
5.  **Apply Initial Conditions:**
    *   Using $y(0)=0$:
        $0 = e^{-0}(c_1 \cos(0) + c_2 \sin(0))$
        $0 = 1(c_1(1) + c_2(0))$
        $c_1 = 0$.
    *   Now, find $y'(x)$. First, the derivative of the general solution:
        $y'(x) = -e^{-x}(c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)) + e^{-x}(-2c_1 \sin(2x) + 2c_2 \cos(2x))$.
    *   Substitute $c_1=0$:
        $y'(x) = -e^{-x}(0 \cdot \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)) + e^{-x}(-2 \cdot 0 \cdot \sin(2x) + 2c_2 \cos(2x))$
        $y'(x) = -c_2 e^{-x}\sin(2x) + 2c_2 e^{-x}\cos(2x)$.
    *   Using $y'(0)=4$:
        $4 = -c_2 e^{0}\sin(0) + 2c_2 e^{0}\cos(0)$
        $4 = -c_2 (1)(0) + 2c_2 (1)(1)$
        $4 = 2c_2$
        $c_2 = 2$.
6.  **Specific Solution:** Substitute $c_1=0$ and $c_2=2$ into the general solution:
    $y(x) = e^{-x}(0 \cdot \cos(2x) + 2 \sin(2x))$
    $y(x) = 2e^{-x}\sin(2x)$.

This specific solution represents a **decaying oscillation** that starts from zero displacement but has an initial velocity.
