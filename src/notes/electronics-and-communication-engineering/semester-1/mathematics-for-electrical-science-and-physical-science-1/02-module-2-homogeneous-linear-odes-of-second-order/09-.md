# Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order

## Topic: The $k \sin(\omega x)$ Forcing Function and Resonance

Welcome back, everyone! In our journey through the fascinating world of differential equations, we've already tackled homogeneous linear ODEs. We've learned how to find the general solution based on the roots of the characteristic equation – real, distinct, repeated, and complex. This is the bedrock of our understanding. Today, we're going to extend this knowledge to a very common and important scenario: dealing with **non-homogeneous** second-order linear ODEs when the forcing function is of the form $k \sin(\omega x)$. This type of forcing function is incredibly relevant in electrical circuits (like AC voltage sources) and mechanical vibrations.

This topic directly ties into **Course Outcome 2 (CO2): Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equations with constant coefficients.** Specifically, we're focusing on the non-homogeneous part. It also lays crucial groundwork for **Course Outcome 3 (CO3): Compute Laplace transform and apply it to solve ODEs arising in engineering**, as you'll often see these types of ODEs solved using Laplace transforms later on.

Let's remind ourselves of the general form of a second-order linear non-homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients:

$$ay'' + by' + cy = g(x)$$

where $a, b, c$ are constants and $g(x)$ is the forcing function. Today, we're specifically interested in the case where $g(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$, where $k$ and $\omega$ are constants. Think of $k$ as the amplitude of the forcing and $\omega$ as its frequency.

### The General Strategy: Complementary and Particular Solutions

Remember, the general solution to a non-homogeneous linear ODE is always the sum of the **complementary solution** ($y_c$) and a **particular solution** ($y_p$):

$$y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x)$$

The complementary solution, $y_c(x)$, is the general solution to the associated homogeneous equation ($ay'' + by' + cy = 0$). We've mastered this! You find it by solving the characteristic equation $ar^2 + br + c = 0$.

The real challenge, and the focus of our discussion today, is finding a suitable **particular solution**, $y_p(x)$, that satisfies the non-homogeneous equation with $g(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$.

### Method of Undetermined Coefficients for $k \sin(\omega x)$

The most straightforward method for finding $y_p$ when $g(x)$ is a sine or cosine function (or a combination thereof, or even a polynomial) is the **Method of Undetermined Coefficients**. This method is beautifully explained in Kreyszig's *Advanced Engineering Mathematics*. The core idea is to "guess" a form for $y_p$ that matches the structure of $g(x)$ and then determine the unknown coefficients in that guess by plugging it back into the ODE.

**The Basic Guess for $k \sin(\omega x)$**

If our forcing function is $g(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$, what's our intelligent guess for $y_p$? Since differentiating sine functions typically results in cosine functions, and vice-versa, our guess for $y_p$ should include both sine and cosine terms of the same frequency $\omega$.

So, our initial guess is:

$$y_p(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$$

where $A$ and $B$ are the coefficients we need to determine.

**Why both $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$?**

Imagine if $g(x) = \sin(2x)$. If we only guessed $y_p = A \sin(2x)$, and we plugged it into the ODE, the derivatives might produce cosine terms. If our ODE had a $y'$ term (meaning $b \neq 0$), those cosine terms would need to be "cancelled out" by some other cosine term in our guess. Including both sine and cosine ensures we have the necessary components to match whatever appears on the right-hand side after differentiation. It's like bringing a whole toolkit to a job, not just one screwdriver!

**The Process**

1.  **Find $y_p'$ and $y_p''$:** Differentiate our guess $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$.
    *   $y_p'(x) = -A\omega \sin(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x)$
    *   $y_p''(x) = -A\omega^2 \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 \sin(\omega x)$

2.  **Substitute into the ODE:** Plug $y_p$, $y_p'$, and $y_p''$ into the original equation $ay'' + by' + cy = k \sin(\omega x)$.

    $a(-A\omega^2 \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 \sin(\omega x)) + b(-A\omega \sin(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x)) + c(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)) = k \sin(\omega x)$

3.  **Equate Coefficients:** Rearrange the left side to group terms with $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$:

    $(a(-A\omega^2) + b(B\omega) + cA) \cos(\omega x) + (a(-B\omega^2) + b(-A\omega) + cB) \sin(\omega x) = k \sin(\omega x)$

    For this equation to hold true for all $x$, the coefficients of $\cos(\omega x)$ on both sides must be equal, and the coefficients of $\sin(\omega x)$ on both sides must be equal.

    *   **Coefficient of $\cos(\omega x)$:** $-aA\omega^2 + bB\omega + cA = 0$
    *   **Coefficient of $\sin(\omega x)$:** $-aB\omega^2 - bA\omega + cB = k$

4.  **Solve the System of Linear Equations:** We now have a system of two linear equations with two unknowns ($A$ and $B$).

    Let's rewrite them:
    $(c - a\omega^2)A + (b\omega)B = 0$  (Equation 1)
    $(-b\omega)A + (c - a\omega^2)B = k$ (Equation 2)

    This system can be solved using methods from linear algebra (which we'll cover more in CO1). We can use substitution or Cramer's rule.

    From Equation 1, if $b\omega \neq 0$, we can express $B$ in terms of $A$:
    $B = -\frac{c - a\omega^2}{b\omega} A$

    Substitute this into Equation 2:
    $(-b\omega)A + (c - a\omega^2) \left(-\frac{c - a\omega^2}{b\omega} A\right) = k$
    $-b\omega A - \frac{(c - a\omega^2)^2}{b\omega} A = k$
    $A \left(-b\omega - \frac{(c - a\omega^2)^2}{b\omega}\right) = k$
    $A \left(\frac{-(b\omega)^2 - (c - a\omega^2)^2}{b\omega}\right) = k$
    $A = \frac{-k \cdot b\omega}{(b\omega)^2 + (c - a\omega^2)^2}$

    Now, substitute $A$ back into the expression for $B$:
    $B = -\frac{c - a\omega^2}{b\omega} \left(\frac{-k \cdot b\omega}{(b\omega)^2 + (c - a\omega^2)^2}\right)$
    $B = \frac{k (c - a\omega^2)}{(b\omega)^2 + (c - a\omega^2)^2}$

    So, our particular solution is:
    $y_p(x) = \left(\frac{-k b\omega}{(b\omega)^2 + (c - a\omega^2)^2}\right) \cos(\omega x) + \left(\frac{k (c - a\omega^2)}{(b\omega)^2 + (c - a\omega^2)^2}\right) \sin(\omega x)$

    This looks a bit unwieldy, but the structure is important!

**What if the basic guess overlaps with $y_c$? The Critical Case: Resonance!**

This is where things get really interesting, and a common pitfall in exams. The method of undetermined coefficients has a "rule modification" just like when dealing with repeated roots for homogeneous solutions.

**The Rule:** If any term in our assumed $y_p$ (or its derivatives) is a solution to the associated homogeneous equation, then our guess is not sufficient. We must multiply our initial guess by $x$. If that still overlaps, we multiply by $x^2$.

For $g(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$, overlap occurs when the roots of the characteristic equation $ar^2 + br + c = 0$ are purely imaginary and equal to $\pm i\omega$.

Let's see why. The roots are $r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$.
For purely imaginary roots, we need:
1.  $b^2 - 4ac < 0$ (complex roots)
2.  The real part of the roots to be zero, which means $b=0$.
3.  The imaginary part to be $\pm \omega$, so $\frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} = \omega$. With $b=0$, this becomes $\frac{\sqrt{4ac}}{2a} = \frac{2\sqrt{ac}}{2a} = \sqrt{\frac{c}{a}} = \omega$.
    So, if $b=0$, the roots are $\pm i\sqrt{c/a}$. If $\omega = \sqrt{c/a}$, then the roots are $r = \pm i\omega$.

When the roots of the characteristic equation are $r = \pm i\omega$, the complementary solution is of the form:
$y_c(x) = C_1 \cos(\omega x) + C_2 \sin(\omega x)$.

Our initial guess for $y_p$ was $A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$. See the problem? This guess is precisely of the same form as $y_c$. If we tried to plug it in, we'd end up with $0=k$ after equating coefficients, which is impossible if $k \neq 0$.

**The Modified Guess in Case of Resonance**

When $r = \pm i\omega$ are the roots of the characteristic equation (meaning $b=0$ and $\omega^2 = c/a$), our initial guess $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$ will not work. We must multiply it by $x$:

$$y_p(x) = x(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x))$$

Now, we need to find $A$ and $B$ using this new guess. This involves more differentiation and algebraic manipulation. Let's denote $y_{p, \text{basic}}(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$.
So, $y_p(x) = x y_{p, \text{basic}}(x)$.

Using the product rule:
$y_p'(x) = y_{p, \text{basic}}(x) + x y_{p, \text{basic}}'(x)$
$y_p''(x) = y_{p, \text{basic}}'(x) + y_{p, \text{basic}}'(x) + x y_{p, \text{basic}}''(x) = 2y_{p, \text{basic}}'(x) + x y_{p, \text{basic}}''(x)$

Now substitute $y_p$, $y_p'$, and $y_p''$ into $ay'' + by' + cy = k \sin(\omega x)$.
Since we are in the case where $b=0$, the ODE is $ay'' + cy = k \sin(\omega x)$.
And we know that $\omega^2 = c/a$, so $c = a\omega^2$.
The ODE becomes $ay'' + a\omega^2 y = k \sin(\omega x)$.
Or, dividing by $a$: $y'' + \omega^2 y = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$.

Let's substitute our modified guess into this simplified ODE:
$y_p = Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x)$

$y_p' = A \cos(\omega x) - Ax\omega \sin(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x) + Bx\omega \cos(\omega x)$
$y_p' = (A + B\omega x) \cos(\omega x) + (B - A\omega x) \sin(\omega x)$

$y_p'' = (B\omega) \cos(\omega x) - (A + B\omega x)\omega \sin(\omega x) + (-A\omega) \sin(\omega x) + (B - A\omega x)\omega \cos(\omega x)$
$y_p'' = B\omega \cos(\omega x) - A\omega \sin(\omega x) - B\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x) - A\omega \sin(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x)$
$y_p'' = (2B\omega - A\omega^2 x) \cos(\omega x) + (-2A\omega - B\omega^2 x) \sin(\omega x)$

Now, substitute into $y'' + \omega^2 y = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$:

$[(2B\omega - A\omega^2 x) \cos(\omega x) + (-2A\omega - B\omega^2 x) \sin(\omega x)] + \omega^2 [Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x)] = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$

Let's expand and group terms by $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$:
$\cos(\omega x)$ terms: $(2B\omega - A\omega^2 x) + \omega^2 (Ax) = 2B\omega - A\omega^2 x + A\omega^2 x = 2B\omega$
$\sin(\omega x)$ terms: $(-2A\omega - B\omega^2 x) + \omega^2 (Bx) = -2A\omega - B\omega^2 x + B\omega^2 x = -2A\omega$

So, the equation becomes:
$2B\omega \cos(\omega x) - 2A\omega \sin(\omega x) = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$

Now, equate coefficients:
*   **Coefficient of $\cos(\omega x)$:** $2B\omega = 0 \implies B = 0$ (since $\omega \neq 0$)
*   **Coefficient of $\sin(\omega x)$:** $-2A\omega = \frac{k}{a} \implies A = -\frac{k}{2a\omega}$

Therefore, the particular solution in the case of resonance is:

$$y_p(x) = -\frac{k}{2a\omega} x \cos(\omega x)$$

This is a classic example of **resonance**. When the forcing frequency $\omega$ matches the natural frequency of the system ($\sqrt{c/a}$ when $b=0$), the amplitude of the response grows linearly with time (or $x$ in this context). Think of pushing a swing at its natural frequency – each push adds energy, and the swing goes higher and higher.

### Analogy: Pushing a Swing

Imagine you're pushing a child on a swing. The swing has a natural frequency at which it likes to oscillate.

*   **No Damping ($b=0$), Forcing at Natural Frequency ($\omega = \sqrt{c/a}$): Resonance!** If you push the swing exactly at its natural frequency, each push you give (the forcing function $k \sin(\omega x)$) adds energy to the system. You don't have to push very hard (small $k$), but if your timing is perfect (frequency matches), the amplitude of the swing (the solution $y(x)$) will grow larger and larger with each push. This is like our $y_p(x) = -\frac{k}{2a\omega} x \cos(\omega x)$, where the $x$ factor indicates the amplitude growing linearly. This can be exciting, but in many physical systems (like bridges or tall buildings), resonance can lead to catastrophic failure if the forces are strong enough.

*   **No Damping ($b=0$), Forcing at a Different Frequency:** If you push the swing at a frequency different from its natural frequency, your pushes will often work against the swing's motion. You might get a small, erratic oscillation, but the amplitude won't grow uncontrollably. This is like the non-resonance case where $y_p$ is a simple sine/cosine combination.

*   **With Damping ($b>0$):** If there's friction or air resistance (represented by the $y'$ term), it's like you're trying to push the swing while someone is gently holding it back. Even if you push at the natural frequency, the amplitude won't grow indefinitely. The damping limits the amplitude to a steady-state value. This is represented by the $A$ and $B$ coefficients in the non-resonance case, which are finite values.

This analogy is key to understanding *why* resonance is special and why the method of undetermined coefficients needs modification.

### Example 1: No Resonance

Let's solve the ODE: $y'' + 4y = 8 \sin(3x)$.

Here, $a=1$, $b=0$, $c=4$, and $g(x) = 8 \sin(3x)$, so $k=8, \omega=3$.

1.  **Complementary Solution ($y_c$):**
    The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 4 = 0$.
    $r^2 = -4 \implies r = \pm 2i$.
    The roots are complex: $r = 0 \pm 2i$.
    So, $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 2$.
    The complementary solution is $y_c(x) = e^{0x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x)) = C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x)$.

2.  **Particular Solution ($y_p$):**
    Our forcing function is $8 \sin(3x)$.
    The frequency of the forcing is $\omega = 3$.
    The natural frequency of the homogeneous part is $\sqrt{c/a} = \sqrt{4/1} = 2$.
    Since $\omega = 3 \neq 2$, we are NOT in resonance.
    Our initial guess for $y_p$ is of the form $y_p(x) = A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x)$.

    Let's find the derivatives:
    $y_p'(x) = -3A \sin(3x) + 3B \cos(3x)$
    $y_p''(x) = -9A \cos(3x) - 9B \sin(3x)$

    Substitute into $y'' + 4y = 8 \sin(3x)$:
    $(-9A \cos(3x) - 9B \sin(3x)) + 4(A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x)) = 8 \sin(3x)$

    Group terms:
    $(-9A + 4A) \cos(3x) + (-9B + 4B) \sin(3x) = 8 \sin(3x)$
    $-5A \cos(3x) - 5B \sin(3x) = 8 \sin(3x)$

    Equate coefficients:
    *   $\cos(3x)$: $-5A = 0 \implies A = 0$
    *   $\sin(3x)$: $-5B = 8 \implies B = -\frac{8}{5}$

    So, the particular solution is $y_p(x) = -\frac{8}{5} \sin(3x)$.

3.  **General Solution:**
    $y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x) = C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x) - \frac{8}{5} \sin(3x)$.

This result makes sense. The system oscillates at its natural frequency (2 radians/sec) due to the homogeneous part, and it's also forced to oscillate at the driving frequency (3 radians/sec) due to the particular solution.

### Example 2: Resonance

Let's solve the ODE: $y'' + 9y = 6 \sin(3x)$.

Here, $a=1$, $b=0$, $c=9$, and $g(x) = 6 \sin(3x)$, so $k=6, \omega=3$.

1.  **Complementary Solution ($y_c$):**
    The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 9 = 0$.
    $r^2 = -9 \implies r = \pm 3i$.
    The roots are complex: $r = 0 \pm 3i$.
    So, $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 3$.
    The complementary solution is $y_c(x) = e^{0x}(C_1 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x)) = C_1 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x)$.

2.  **Particular Solution ($y_p$):**
    Our forcing function is $6 \sin(3x)$, with $\omega = 3$.
    The natural frequency of the homogeneous part is $\sqrt{c/a} = \sqrt{9/1} = 3$.
    Since $\omega = 3$, which matches the imaginary part of the roots of the characteristic equation, we are in the **resonance case**.

    Our initial guess $A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x)$ would overlap with $y_c$.
    Therefore, we must use the modified guess:
    $y_p(x) = x(A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x)) = Ax \cos(3x) + Bx \sin(3x)$.

    We need to substitute this into the ODE: $y'' + 9y = 6 \sin(3x)$.
    From our earlier derivation for the resonance case ($y'' + \omega^2 y = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$), we had:
    $2B\omega \cos(\omega x) - 2A\omega \sin(\omega x) = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$.

    In this problem: $a=1, k=6, \omega=3$.
    So, the equation to match is $2B(3) \cos(3x) - 2A(3) \sin(3x) = \frac{6}{1} \sin(3x)$.
    $6B \cos(3x) - 6A \sin(3x) = 6 \sin(3x)$.

    Equate coefficients:
    *   $\cos(3x)$: $6B = 0 \implies B = 0$.
    *   $\sin(3x)$: $-6A = 6 \implies A = -1$.

    So, the particular solution is $y_p(x) = x(-1 \cos(3x) + 0 \sin(3x)) = -x \cos(3x)$.

3.  **General Solution:**
    $y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x) = C_1 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x) - x \cos(3x)$.

    Notice how the term $-x \cos(3x)$ grows with $x$. This is the characteristic of resonance.

### Important Considerations and Exam Tips

*   **Always Check for Resonance First:** Before diving into calculations for $y_p$, always compare the forcing frequency $\omega$ with the natural frequency $\sqrt{c/a}$ (or the imaginary part of the complex roots if $b \neq 0$). If they match (and $b=0$), you're in resonance.
*   **The Characteristic Equation is Key:** The roots of $ar^2+br+c=0$ dictate the form of $y_c$ and whether resonance occurs. Be precise with your calculation of these roots.
*   **The Method of Undetermined Coefficients:** Forcing functions of the form $k \sin(\omega x)$ or $k \cos(\omega x)$ require a $y_p$ of the form $A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$. If the forcing function is a sum, like $\sin(\omega_1 x) + \cos(\omega_2 x)$, you'd use a guess of the form $A \cos(\omega_1 x) + B \sin(\omega_1 x) + C \cos(\omega_2 x) + D \sin(\omega_2 x)$ (assuming no resonance issues for either frequency). Kreyszig's Chapter 4.3 provides a comprehensive table for various forms of $g(x)$.
*   **What if $b \neq 0$ and the roots are complex, $r = \alpha \pm i\beta$?** If the forcing function is $k \sin(\beta x)$ or $k \cos(\beta x)$, then resonance occurs because $\beta$ is the imaginary part of the homogeneous solution's roots. The $y_p$ guess would need to be multiplied by $x$.
*   **Physical Interpretation:** Always try to connect the math back to the physical system. Resonance typically implies an unbounded or significantly amplified response, while non-resonance leads to a bounded response oscillating at both natural and forcing frequencies.
*   **Careful with Signs and Derivatives:** These problems can be algebraically intensive. Double-check your derivatives and the signs when equating coefficients. A small mistake here can lead to a completely wrong $y_p$.
*   **Connecting to CO1 and CO2:** Solving the system of linear equations for $A$ and $B$ uses concepts from linear algebra (CO1). The entire process of finding $y_c$ and $y_p$ directly addresses CO2.

### Relating to Textbooks

Chapter 4.3, "Non-homogeneous Linear Equations with Constant Coefficients," is where you'll find detailed explanations of the method of undetermined coefficients, including tables for various forcing functions and the rules for modification due to overlap with the complementary solution. The concept of resonance is implicitly handled by these modification rules.

Understanding the concepts here will deepen your appreciation for *why* we solve these types of equations.

### Sample Questions with Answers

**Q1. For the ODE $y'' + 5y = 10 \cos(2x)$, what is the form of the particular solution $y_p(x)$ according to the Method of Undetermined Coefficients?**

**Answer:**
First, we find the characteristic equation for the homogeneous part: $r^2 + 5 = 0$.
The roots are $r = \pm i\sqrt{5}$.
The homogeneous solution $y_c$ will be of the form $C_1 \cos(\sqrt{5}x) + C_2 \sin(\sqrt{5}x)$.
The forcing function is $g(x) = 10 \cos(2x)$, with frequency $\omega = 2$.
The frequency of the forcing function ($\omega=2$) does not match the imaginary part of the roots of the characteristic equation ($\beta=\sqrt{5}$). Therefore, we are **not** in a resonance case.
The standard form for the particular solution for a cosine forcing function is $y_p(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$.
Thus, the form of the particular solution is $y_p(x) = A \cos(2x) + B \sin(2x)$.

**Q2. Consider the ODE $y'' + 2y' + y = 3 \sin(x)$. Identify if resonance occurs and state the appropriate form for $y_p(x)$.**

**Answer:**
The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 2r + 1 = 0$, which factors as $(r+1)^2 = 0$.
The roots are $r = -1$ (a repeated real root).
The homogeneous solution $y_c$ is of the form $C_1 e^{-x} + C_2 x e^{-x}$.
The forcing function is $g(x) = 3 \sin(x)$, with frequency $\omega = 1$.
The roots of the characteristic equation are real ($r=-1$), not purely imaginary. Therefore, **resonance does not occur** in the sense of the forcing frequency matching the natural oscillatory frequency.
The form of the particular solution $y_p(x)$ for $k \sin(\omega x)$ or $k \cos(\omega x)$ is generally $A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$.
Since $\cos(x)$ and $\sin(x)$ are not part of $y_c$, our initial guess is sufficient.
The form of the particular solution is $y_p(x) = A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)$.

**Q3. Solve the ODE $y'' + 16y = 5 \sin(4x)$, given $y(0)=0$ and $y'(0)=0$.**

**Answer:**
This problem requires solving a non-homogeneous ODE and applying initial conditions.

**Step 1: Find the Complementary Solution ($y_c$).**
The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 16 = 0$.
$r^2 = -16 \implies r = \pm 4i$.
The roots are complex: $r = 0 \pm 4i$.
So, $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 4$.
The complementary solution is $y_c(x) = e^{0x}(C_1 \cos(4x) + C_2 \sin(4x)) = C_1 \cos(4x) + C_2 \sin(4x)$.

**Step 2: Find the Particular Solution ($y_p$).**
The forcing function is $g(x) = 5 \sin(4x)$, with $\omega = 4$.
The natural frequency of the homogeneous part is $\sqrt{c/a} = \sqrt{16/1} = 4$.
Since $\omega = 4$ matches the imaginary part of the roots of the characteristic equation, **resonance occurs**.
Our initial guess $A \cos(4x) + B \sin(4x)$ overlaps with $y_c$.
We must use the modified guess: $y_p(x) = x(A \cos(4x) + B \sin(4x)) = Ax \cos(4x) + Bx \sin(4x)$.

Substitute this into the ODE $y'' + 16y = 5 \sin(4x)$.
We derived earlier that for $y'' + \omega^2 y = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$, the $y_p$ is $Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x)$ where $A = -\frac{k}{2a\omega}$ and $B=0$.

In our case, $a=1$, $k=5$, $\omega=4$.
$A = -\frac{5}{2(1)(4)} = -\frac{5}{8}$.
$B = 0$.

So, the particular solution is $y_p(x) = -\frac{5}{8} x \cos(4x)$.

**Step 3: Find the General Solution.**
$y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x) = C_1 \cos(4x) + C_2 \sin(4x) - \frac{5}{8} x \cos(4x)$.

**Step 4: Apply Initial Conditions.**
We are given $y(0)=0$ and $y'(0)=0$.

First, apply $y(0)=0$:
$y(0) = C_1 \cos(0) + C_2 \sin(0) - \frac{5}{8} (0) \cos(0)$
$0 = C_1(1) + C_2(0) - 0$
$C_1 = 0$.

So, $y(x) = C_2 \sin(4x) - \frac{5}{8} x \cos(4x)$.

Now, we need $y'(x)$. Using the product rule for the last term:
$y'(x) = 4C_2 \cos(4x) - \frac{5}{8} [1 \cdot \cos(4x) + x \cdot (-4 \sin(4x))]$
$y'(x) = 4C_2 \cos(4x) - \frac{5}{8} \cos(4x) + \frac{20}{8} x \sin(4x)$
$y'(x) = 4C_2 \cos(4x) - \frac{5}{8} \cos(4x) + \frac{5}{2} x \sin(4x)$.

Apply $y'(0)=0$:
$y'(0) = 4C_2 \cos(0) - \frac{5}{8} \cos(0) + \frac{5}{2} (0) \sin(0)$
$0 = 4C_2(1) - \frac{5}{8}(1) + 0$
$0 = 4C_2 - \frac{5}{8}$
$4C_2 = \frac{5}{8} \implies C_2 = \frac{5}{32}$.

**Step 5: The Final Solution.**
Substituting $C_1=0$ and $C_2=\frac{5}{32}$ into the general solution:
$y(x) = \frac{5}{32} \sin(4x) - \frac{5}{8} x \cos(4x)$.

This solution shows the oscillatory behavior at the natural frequency $\sin(4x)$ with a smaller amplitude, and the growing resonance term $- \frac{5}{8} x \cos(4x)$ which dominates as $x$ increases.

This concludes our discussion on forcing functions of the form $k \sin(\omega x)$ for homogeneous linear ODEs of second order. Remember to always check for resonance! It's a concept that pops up frequently in electrical science and physical systems. Keep practicing, and these ODEs will become second nature.
