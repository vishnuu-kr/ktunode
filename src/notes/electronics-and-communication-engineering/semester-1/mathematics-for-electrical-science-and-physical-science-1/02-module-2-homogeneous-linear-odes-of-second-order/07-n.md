# Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order

## Topic: $kx^n$ Type Equations

Welcome back, everyone! Today, we're diving into a rather interesting class of second-order homogeneous linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that appear quite frequently in various physical and engineering problems. Specifically, we'll be looking at equations where the coefficients involve powers of the independent variable, $x$. This type of equation is often referred to as a **Cauchy-Euler equation** or sometimes an **equidimensional equation**. The general form we're interested in today is:

$x^2 \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + ax \frac{dy}{dx} + by = 0$

where $a$ and $b$ are constants. Now, you might be thinking, "Why this particular form?" Well, equations like this often arise when dealing with physical phenomena that exhibit scaling behavior with respect to a certain variable, say distance or radius. Think about how the force of gravity between two objects changes with distance, or how the heat distribution in a spherical object might behave. These situations can sometimes lead to ODEs of this structure.

Our goal today is to understand how to solve these equations systematically. This ties directly into **Course Outcome 2 (CO2)**, which is about solving homogeneous linear differential equations. While CO2 specifically mentions "constant coefficients," the techniques we'll learn here are a natural extension and often require some clever manipulation to transform them into something we can handle more easily.

### The Power of Substitution: Transforming the Equation

The key to solving these equations lies in a special substitution. Notice the structure: $x^2$ multiplying the second derivative, and $x$ multiplying the first derivative. This suggests that a power of $x$ might be a good candidate for our dependent variable.

Let's try a substitution of the form $y = x^m$. If we make this substitution, we'll need to find the first and second derivatives of $y$ with respect to $x$:

$\frac{dy}{dx} = m x^{m-1}$

$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = m(m-1) x^{m-2}$

Now, let's plug these into our general Cauchy-Euler equation:

$x^2 [m(m-1) x^{m-2}] + ax [m x^{m-1}] + b [x^m] = 0$

Let's simplify this expression. Remember that when you multiply powers of $x$, you add the exponents:

$m(m-1) x^{m-2+2} + am x^{m-1+1} + b x^m = 0$

$m(m-1) x^m + am x^m + b x^m = 0$

We can factor out $x^m$ from every term, assuming $x \neq 0$:

$x^m [m(m-1) + am + b] = 0$

Since $x^m$ cannot be zero for our solutions (unless we're looking for trivial solutions), the expression inside the brackets must be zero:

$m(m-1) + am + b = 0$

This is a quadratic equation in $m$. Let's expand it and rearrange it into a standard quadratic form:

$m^2 - m + am + b = 0$

$m^2 + (a-1)m + b = 0$

This is often called the **auxiliary equation** or **characteristic equation** for the Cauchy-Euler equation. The roots of this quadratic equation, $m_1$ and $m_2$, will tell us about the form of our solutions.

### Understanding the Roots of the Auxiliary Equation

Just like with constant coefficient ODEs, the nature of the roots of the auxiliary equation dictates the form of the general solution. There are three cases:

**Case 1: Distinct Real Roots ($m_1 \neq m_2$)**

If the auxiliary equation yields two distinct real roots, $m_1$ and $m_2$, then our initial assumption $y = x^m$ leads to two independent solutions: $y_1 = x^{m_1}$ and $y_2 = x^{m_2}$. The general solution is then a linear combination of these two solutions:

$y(x) = c_1 x^{m_1} + c_2 x^{m_2}$

where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are arbitrary constants.

**Example 1:** Let's solve the equation $x^2 y'' - 2xy' + 2y = 0$.

Here, $a = -2$ and $b = 2$. The auxiliary equation is:

$m^2 + (-2-1)m + 2 = 0$
$m^2 - 3m + 2 = 0$

Factoring this quadratic, we get:

$(m-1)(m-2) = 0$

So, the roots are $m_1 = 1$ and $m_2 = 2$. These are distinct real roots. Therefore, our solutions are $y_1 = x^1 = x$ and $y_2 = x^2$.

The general solution is:

$y(x) = c_1 x + c_2 x^2$

This is a straightforward application of our substitution. Remember this process: find the auxiliary equation, solve for $m$, and then write down the solution based on the nature of the roots. This process directly addresses **CO2** by providing a method to solve a specific type of homogeneous linear ODE.

**Case 2: Repeated Real Roots ($m_1 = m_2 = m$)**

What happens if the auxiliary equation has only one real root? This occurs when the discriminant $(a-1)^2 - 4b$ is zero. Let the repeated root be $m$. Our first solution is $y_1 = x^m$. However, we need a second, linearly independent solution.

This is where things get a bit more interesting and are analogous to the constant coefficient case. When we have repeated roots, we often use a technique called **reduction of order**. If $y_1(x)$ is a solution, we look for a second solution of the form $y_2(x) = v(x) y_1(x)$.

However, for Cauchy-Euler equations, the pattern is simpler. If $m_1 = m_2 = m$, the second solution is $y_2 = x^m \ln|x|$. The general solution then becomes:

$y(x) = c_1 x^m + c_2 x^m \ln|x|$

**Example 2:** Let's solve $x^2 y'' + 5xy' + 4y = 0$.

Here, $a = 5$ and $b = 4$. The auxiliary equation is:

$m^2 + (5-1)m + 4 = 0$
$m^2 + 4m + 4 = 0$

Factoring this, we get:

$(m+2)^2 = 0$

So, we have a repeated real root $m = -2$. Our first solution is $y_1 = x^{-2}$. The second solution is $y_2 = x^{-2} \ln|x|$.

The general solution is:

$y(x) = c_1 x^{-2} + c_2 x^{-2} \ln|x|$

or equivalently, $y(x) = x^{-2} (c_1 + c_2 \ln|x|)$.

This case highlights the subtle differences between constant coefficient and variable coefficient ODEs. While the underlying principle of linear independence remains, the form of the second solution changes.

**Case 3: Complex Conjugate Roots ($m = \lambda \pm i\mu$)**

If the discriminant $(a-1)^2 - 4b$ is negative, we'll have complex conjugate roots for $m$. Let these roots be $m = \lambda \pm i\mu$, where $\lambda$ is the real part and $\mu$ is the imaginary part.

Our solutions would initially appear as $y_1 = x^{\lambda + i\mu}$ and $y_2 = x^{\lambda - i\mu}$. However, we usually prefer real-valued solutions in physical applications. We can use Euler's formula ($e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$) and the properties of exponents to convert these complex solutions into real ones.

Recall that $x^z = e^{z \ln x}$. So, for our complex roots:

$y_1 = x^{\lambda + i\mu} = x^\lambda x^{i\mu} = x^\lambda e^{i\mu \ln x}$
$y_2 = x^{\lambda - i\mu} = x^\lambda x^{-i\mu} = x^\lambda e^{-i\mu \ln x}$

Using Euler's formula, $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, we have:

$e^{i\mu \ln x} = \cos(\mu \ln x) + i\sin(\mu \ln x)$
$e^{-i\mu \ln x} = \cos(\mu \ln x) - i\sin(\mu \ln x)$

Now, we can form linear combinations of $y_1$ and $y_2$ to get real solutions. A common choice for the real solutions are:

$y_{real,1} = \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} = x^\lambda \cos(\mu \ln x)$
$y_{real,2} = \frac{y_1 - y_2}{2i} = x^\lambda \sin(\mu \ln x)$

The general solution for complex roots is then:

$y(x) = x^\lambda [c_1 \cos(\mu \ln x) + c_2 \sin(\mu \ln x)]$

**Example 3:** Solve $x^2 y'' - x y' + 2y = 0$.

Here, $a = -1$ and $b = 2$. The auxiliary equation is:

$m^2 + (-1-1)m + 2 = 0$
$m^2 - 2m + 2 = 0$

Using the quadratic formula, $m = \frac{-(-2) \pm \sqrt{(-2)^2 - 4(1)(2)}}{2(1)}$:

$m = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 8}}{2}$
$m = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{-4}}{2}$
$m = \frac{2 \pm 2i}{2}$
$m = 1 \pm i$

So, we have complex roots with $\lambda = 1$ and $\mu = 1$.

The general solution is:

$y(x) = x^1 [c_1 \cos(1 \ln x) + c_2 \sin(1 \ln x)]$
$y(x) = x [c_1 \cos(\ln x) + c_2 \sin(\ln x)]$

This covers the three fundamental cases for solving Cauchy-Euler equations. These types of problems are excellent for testing your understanding of how different types of roots in the auxiliary equation lead to different forms of solutions, a core concept in **CO2**.

### A Note on the Domain of Solution

It's important to be mindful of the domain where these solutions are valid. Our substitution $y = x^m$ implicitly assumes $x > 0$. If we are interested in solutions for $x < 0$, we can often make the substitution $t = -x$. Then $t > 0$, and we can solve the ODE in terms of $t$. The solutions for $x < 0$ can then be obtained by replacing $t$ with $-x$.

For example, if $y(x) = x^m$, for $x < 0$, we can write $y(x) = (-1)^m x^m$ or $|x|^m$. The logarithm term also needs careful consideration for negative $x$. Typically, we use $\ln|x|$ in the solution to cover both positive and negative $x$.

### Connecting to Other Course Outcomes

While our primary focus today has been **CO2**, the techniques we use to derive the solutions touch upon other areas:

*   **CO1: Linear Independence and Systems of Equations:** The requirement for two linearly independent solutions when dealing with second-order ODEs is a direct link to the concept of linear independence of functions, which is fundamental in linear algebra and solving systems of equations. The solutions we found ($x^{m_1}$, $x^{m_2}$, $x^m \ln|x|$, $x^\lambda \cos(\mu \ln x)$, $x^\lambda \sin(\mu \ln x)$) are all linearly independent sets of functions.
*   **CO3: Laplace Transforms:** While not the primary method for *this specific topic*, the ability to solve ODEs is what makes Laplace transforms so powerful in engineering. If a Cauchy-Euler equation were part of a larger problem (perhaps with non-homogeneous terms or initial conditions), Laplace transforms would be an alternative approach, especially if the coefficients weren't powers of $x$. However, the direct substitution method is generally more efficient for pure Cauchy-Euler equations.
*   **CO4: Series Expansions:** The appearance of $\ln|x|$ in the solution for repeated roots and $\cos(\ln x)$, $\sin(\ln x)$ in the complex root case might remind you of series expansions. For instance, the Taylor series for $\cos u$ and $\sin u$ are $(1 - u^2/2! + u^4/4! - \dots)$ and $(u - u^3/3! + u^5/5! - \dots)$ respectively. If we were to expand $\cos(\ln x)$ around a suitable point, we'd see similar terms. This topic provides a bridge to understanding how functions that appear in ODE solutions are related to series representations.

**Kreyszig's Advanced Engineering Mathematics**, particularly in Chapter 2 on Second-Order Linear ODEs, provides a thorough treatment of Cauchy-Euler equations. Anton's *Calculus* and *Elementary Linear Algebra* provide the foundational understanding of algebra, calculus, and linear independence that underpins these ODE techniques. Bird's and Ramana's Higher Engineering Mathematics books also cover these topics comprehensively.

### Summary: The Cauchy-Euler Method

To recap, solving a Cauchy-Euler equation of the form $x^2 y'' + ax y' + by = 0$:

1.  **Assume a solution:** Try $y = x^m$.
2.  **Find derivatives:** Calculate $y'$ and $y''$.
3.  **Substitute:** Plug $y, y', y''$ into the ODE.
4.  **Form the auxiliary equation:** Simplify to obtain $m^2 + (a-1)m + b = 0$.
5.  **Solve for $m$:** Find the roots $m_1, m_2$.
6.  **Write the general solution:**
    *   **Distinct real roots ($m_1, m_2$):** $y(x) = c_1 x^{m_1} + c_2 x^{m_2}$
    *   **Repeated real root ($m$):** $y(x) = c_1 x^m + c_2 x^m \ln|x|$
    *   **Complex conjugate roots ($\lambda \pm i\mu$):** $y(x) = x^\lambda [c_1 \cos(\mu \ln x) + c_2 \sin(\mu \ln x)]$

Remember this strategy; it's the bread and butter for solving these kinds of equations!

## Sample Questions and Answers

Here are a few questions to test your understanding, covering both conceptual aspects and common exam formats:

**Question 1 (Conceptual):** What is the auxiliary equation for the Cauchy-Euler equation $x^2 y'' - 3xy' + 4y = 0$, and what is the nature of its roots?

**Answer 1:**
The given equation is $x^2 y'' - 3xy' + 4y = 0$.
Comparing with the general form $x^2 y'' + ax y' + by = 0$, we have $a = -3$ and $b = 4$.
The auxiliary equation is $m^2 + (a-1)m + b = 0$.
Substituting the values of $a$ and $b$:
$m^2 + (-3-1)m + 4 = 0$
$m^2 - 4m + 4 = 0$
This is a quadratic equation. To determine the nature of the roots, we look at the discriminant, $\Delta = (a-1)^2 - 4b = (-4)^2 - 4(4) = 16 - 16 = 0$.
Since the discriminant is zero, the auxiliary equation has **repeated real roots**.
Factoring the equation: $(m-2)^2 = 0$, so $m=2$ (repeated root).

**Question 2 (Application):** Find the general solution of the Cauchy-Euler equation $x^2 y'' + 4xy' + 2y = 0$.

**Answer 2:**
The given equation is $x^2 y'' + 4xy' + 2y = 0$.
Here, $a = 4$ and $b = 2$.
The auxiliary equation is $m^2 + (a-1)m + b = 0$.
Substituting the values:
$m^2 + (4-1)m + 2 = 0$
$m^2 + 3m + 2 = 0$
Factor the quadratic equation:
$(m+1)(m+2) = 0$
The roots are $m_1 = -1$ and $m_2 = -2$.
Since the roots are distinct and real, the general solution is of the form $y(x) = c_1 x^{m_1} + c_2 x^{m_2}$.
Therefore, the general solution is:
$y(x) = c_1 x^{-1} + c_2 x^{-2}$
or $y(x) = \frac{c_1}{x} + \frac{c_2}{x^2}$.

**Question 3 (Conceptual/Application):** For the Cauchy-Euler equation $x^2 y'' - x y' + 5y = 0$, what form does the general solution take, and why? (Hint: What are the roots of the auxiliary equation?)

**Answer 3:**
The given equation is $x^2 y'' - x y' + 5y = 0$.
Here, $a = -1$ and $b = 5$.
The auxiliary equation is $m^2 + (a-1)m + b = 0$.
Substituting the values:
$m^2 + (-1-1)m + 5 = 0$
$m^2 - 2m + 5 = 0$
To find the nature of the roots, we calculate the discriminant: $\Delta = (-2)^2 - 4(1)(5) = 4 - 20 = -16$.
Since the discriminant is negative, the auxiliary equation has **complex conjugate roots**.
We find the roots using the quadratic formula:
$m = \frac{-(-2) \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2(1)} = \frac{2 \pm 4i}{2} = 1 \pm 2i$.
The roots are of the form $\lambda \pm i\mu$, where $\lambda = 1$ and $\mu = 2$.
For complex conjugate roots, the general solution is of the form $y(x) = x^\lambda [c_1 \cos(\mu \ln x) + c_2 \sin(\mu \ln x)]$.
Therefore, the general solution is:
$y(x) = x^1 [c_1 \cos(2 \ln x) + c_2 \sin(2 \ln x)]$
or $y(x) = x [c_1 \cos(2 \ln x) + c_2 \sin(2 \ln x)]$.

**Question 4 (Exam Pitfall):** A student is solving $x^2 y'' + 3xy' + y = 0$. They correctly find the auxiliary equation to be $m^2 + 2m + 1 = 0$, which gives a repeated root $m=-1$. The student writes the general solution as $y(x) = c_1 x^{-1} + c_2 x^{-1} \ln x$. What is correct and what might be a potential pitfall in their reasoning or final answer?

**Answer 4:**
The student's process is largely correct, and the resulting general solution $y(x) = c_1 x^{-1} + c_2 x^{-1} \ln x$ is indeed the correct form for a Cauchy-Euler equation with a repeated root.

**Potential Pitfall/Nuance:** The common pitfall here is not about the form of the solution itself, but rather the domain of $x$. The use of $\ln x$ implicitly assumes $x>0$. If the problem context or initial conditions involve $x<0$, the student should be using $\ln|x|$. While many introductory problems implicitly assume $x>0$, in more advanced contexts or when dealing with physics problems where $x$ might represent a coordinate that can be negative, using $\ln|x|$ is crucial for generality. So, a more complete answer would be $y(x) = x^{-1} (c_1 + c_2 \ln|x|)$. The student's answer is correct if the domain is restricted to $x>0$. It's important to be aware of this detail, as it can be a point of subtle scoring in exams.

These examples cover the three main cases and highlight the process of setting up and solving these equations, which directly relates to achieving **CO2**.
