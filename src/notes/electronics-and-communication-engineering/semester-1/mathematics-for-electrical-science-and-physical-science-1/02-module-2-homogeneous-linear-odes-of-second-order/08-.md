### Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order

**Topic: The Case of $k \cos(\omega x)$ as a particular solution**

Welcome, everyone, to our journey into the fascinating world of differential equations! Today, we're going to build upon our understanding of homogeneous linear ODEs of the second order and explore a specific, very common scenario: how to find a particular solution when the forcing function on the right-hand side of our non-homogeneous equation involves a cosine term. This is a cornerstone for understanding many physical phenomena, especially in electrical circuits and mechanical vibrations.

We've already established that a second-order linear non-homogeneous ODE looks like this:

$ay'' + by' + cy = g(x)$

where $a, b, c$ are constants, and $g(x)$ is our "forcing function" or "input."

The general solution, as you know, is the sum of the complementary solution ($y_c$) and a particular solution ($y_p$):

$y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x)$

We've learned how to find $y_c$ by solving the characteristic equation associated with the homogeneous part ($ay'' + by' + cy = 0$). Now, we're focusing on finding that crucial $y_p$.

#### Understanding the Forcing Function: $g(x) = k \cos(\omega x)$

When our forcing function is a cosine, like $k \cos(\omega x)$, it represents a sinusoidal input. Think of an AC voltage source in an electrical circuit or a driven oscillation in a mechanical system. The constant $k$ is the amplitude of this input, and $\omega$ is its frequency.

**How do we guess a particular solution $y_p$?**

This is where the method of Undetermined Coefficients shines. The general rule of thumb is to guess a form for $y_p$ that mirrors the form of $g(x)$, but with undetermined coefficients.

If $g(x)$ is a constant, we guess a constant. If $g(x)$ is a polynomial, we guess a polynomial of the same degree. And if $g(x)$ is a sine or cosine, we guess a combination of sine and cosine of the same frequency.

So, if $g(x) = k \cos(\omega x)$, what should our guess for $y_p$ be?

A common initial thought might be to guess $y_p = A \cos(\omega x)$, where $A$ is a constant we need to determine. However, this isn't quite right. Why? Because when we substitute $y_p$ and its derivatives into the ODE, we might get terms involving $\sin(\omega x)$ that can't be cancelled out if our guess only includes cosine.

The more robust guess, and the one that covers all bases, is to include **both** cosine and sine terms of the same frequency:

**Our first guess for $y_p$ is: $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$**

Here, $A$ and $B$ are the coefficients we need to determine by plugging this $y_p$ back into the original non-homogeneous ODE. This approach, as you'll find in Kreyszig's *Advanced Engineering Mathematics*, is a systematic way to handle these types of forcing functions.

Let's walk through the process.

**Step-by-Step Procedure to Find $y_p$ for $g(x) = k \cos(\omega x)$**

1.  **Write down the ODE:** $ay'' + by' + cy = k \cos(\omega x)$
2.  **Make the initial guess for $y_p$:** $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$.
3.  **Calculate the first and second derivatives of $y_p$:**
    *   $y_p' = -A\omega \sin(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x)$
    *   $y_p'' = -A\omega^2 \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 \sin(\omega x)$
4.  **Substitute $y_p$, $y_p'$, and $y_p''$ into the ODE:**
    $a(-A\omega^2 \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 \sin(\omega x)) + b(-A\omega \sin(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x)) + c(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)) = k \cos(\omega x)$
5.  **Group terms by $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$:**
    *   Collect all terms with $\cos(\omega x)$:
        $(-aA\omega^2 + bB\omega + cA) \cos(\omega x)$
    *   Collect all terms with $\sin(\omega x)$:
        $(-aB\omega^2 - bA\omega + cB) \sin(\omega x)$
    The equation now looks like:
    $(-aA\omega^2 + bB\omega + cA) \cos(\omega x) + (-aB\omega^2 - bA\omega + cB) \sin(\omega x) = k \cos(\omega x)$
6.  **Equate coefficients:** Since $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ are linearly independent, we can equate the coefficients of $\cos(\omega x)$ on both sides and the coefficients of $\sin(\omega x)$ on both sides.
    *   For $\cos(\omega x)$:  $A(c - a\omega^2) + B(b\omega) = k$
    *   For $\sin(\omega x)$:  $A(-b\omega) + B(c - a\omega^2) = 0$

    This gives us a system of two linear equations for the two unknowns, $A$ and $B$.

7.  **Solve the system of linear equations for $A$ and $B$.** This is where our knowledge of linear algebra (CO1) comes into play! We can use substitution or matrix methods to solve for $A$ and $B$.

8.  **Substitute the found values of $A$ and $B$ back into the guess for $y_p$.**

**A Crucial Modification: The "Special Case"**

Now, what happens if our guess $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$ is *part* of the complementary solution $y_c$? This is the "special case" you need to watch out for, just like when $g(x)$ is a polynomial and matches a term in $y_c$.

If either $\cos(\omega x)$ or $\sin(\omega x)$ (or both) are solutions to the homogeneous equation $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, our initial guess will not work, and we'll end up with $0=k$ or $0=0$ after equating coefficients, which doesn't help us find $A$ and $B$.

When does this happen? This occurs when $\omega$ is related to the roots of the characteristic equation.
Specifically, if the characteristic equation $ar^2 + br + c = 0$ has roots $r_1$ and $r_2$, and either $r_1 = i\omega$ or $r_2 = i\omega$ (or $r_1 = -i\omega$ or $r_2 = -i\omega$), then our guess is a part of $y_c$.

*   If $\omega$ is not equal to the imaginary part of the roots of the characteristic equation (i.e., $r = \pm i\omega$ are NOT roots), our original guess $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$ works.
*   **If $\omega$ *is* equal to the imaginary part of the roots, and the roots are purely imaginary (like $r = \pm i\omega$), meaning $b=0$ and $c=a\omega^2$, then $y_c$ contains $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$.** In this scenario, we must multiply our guess by $x$.
    **The modified guess becomes: $y_p = x(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x))$**
*   **What if the roots are real or complex, but not purely imaginary such that they match $\omega$?** The situation gets more complex. However, for this topic focused on $k \cos(\omega x)$, the primary concern is when $\omega$ corresponds to the homogeneous solution. Typically, in introductory courses, this special case refers to when the homogeneous solution itself involves $\cos(\omega x)$ or $\sin(\omega x)$.

Let's clarify this "special case" more precisely:
The characteristic equation is $ar^2 + br + c = 0$. The roots are $r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$.
Our forcing function is $k \cos(\omega x)$.
If the roots of the characteristic equation are purely imaginary, say $r = \pm i\omega_0$, then the complementary solution is $y_c = c_1 \cos(\omega_0 x) + c_2 \sin(\omega_0 x)$.
If our forcing function has the same frequency, i.e., $\omega = \omega_0$, then our initial guess $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$ is already present in $y_c$.
In this case, we must increase the "order" of our guess by multiplying by $x$.

**Modified Guess for $y_p$ when $y_c$ contains $\cos(\omega x)$ or $\sin(\omega x)$:**
$y_p = x(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x))$

You'll need to compute $y_p'$, $y_p''$ for this modified guess and substitute them back into the ODE. This will result in a system of linear equations for $A$ and $B$ again.

**Let's look at an example to solidify this.**

**Example 1: No Overlap (Standard Case)**

Find a particular solution for $y'' + 4y = \cos(x)$.

Here, $a=1$, $b=0$, $c=4$, and $g(x) = \cos(x)$, so $k=1, \omega=1$.

1.  **Characteristic equation:** $r^2 + 4 = 0$. Roots are $r = \pm 2i$.
    So, $y_c = c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)$.
2.  **Our forcing frequency is $\omega = 1$.** The homogeneous solution has frequency $\omega_0 = 2$. Since $\omega \neq \omega_0$, we are NOT in the special case.
3.  **Initial guess:** $y_p = A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)$.
4.  **Derivatives:**
    *   $y_p' = -A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)$
    *   $y_p'' = -A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)$
5.  **Substitute into ODE:**
    $(-A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)) + 4(A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)) = \cos(x)$
6.  **Group terms:**
    $(-A + 4A) \cos(x) + (-B + 4B) \sin(x) = \cos(x)$
    $(3A) \cos(x) + (3B) \sin(x) = 1 \cos(x) + 0 \sin(x)$
7.  **Equate coefficients:**
    *   For $\cos(x)$: $3A = 1 \implies A = 1/3$
    *   For $\sin(x)$: $3B = 0 \implies B = 0$
8.  **Particular solution:** $y_p = \frac{1}{3} \cos(x)$.

This is a straightforward application. The forcing function's frequency didn't interfere with the natural oscillation of the system.

**Example 2: The Special Case**

Find a particular solution for $y'' + 4y = \cos(2x)$.

Here, $a=1$, $b=0$, $c=4$, and $g(x) = \cos(2x)$, so $k=1, \omega=2$.

1.  **Characteristic equation:** $r^2 + 4 = 0$. Roots are $r = \pm 2i$.
    So, $y_c = c_1 \cos(2x) + c_2 \sin(2x)$.
2.  **Our forcing frequency is $\omega = 2$.** The homogeneous solution has frequencies $\omega_0 = 2$. Since $\omega = \omega_0$, we **ARE** in the special case. Our initial guess $A \cos(2x) + B \sin(2x)$ is part of $y_c$.
3.  **Modified guess:** $y_p = x(A \cos(2x) + B \sin(2x)) = Ax \cos(2x) + Bx \sin(2x)$.
4.  **Calculate derivatives (this is where it gets a bit tedious!):**
    *   $y_p' = A(\cos(2x) - 2x \sin(2x)) + B(\sin(2x) + 2x \cos(2x))$
        $y_p' = (A + 2Bx) \cos(2x) + (B - 2Ax) \sin(2x)$
    *   $y_p'' =$ [Derivative of $(A + 2Bx) \cos(2x)$] + [Derivative of $(B - 2Ax) \sin(2x)$]
        $y_p'' = [2B \cos(2x) - 2(A + 2Bx) \sin(2x)] + [-2A \sin(2x) + 2(B - 2Ax) \cos(2x)]$
        $y_p'' = (2B + 2B - 4Ax) \cos(2x) + (-2A - 2A - 4Bx) \sin(2x)$
        $y_p'' = (4B - 4Ax) \cos(2x) + (-4A - 4Bx) \sin(2x)$
5.  **Substitute into ODE ($y'' + 4y = \cos(2x)$):**
    $[(4B - 4Ax) \cos(2x) + (-4A - 4Bx) \sin(2x)] + 4[Ax \cos(2x) + Bx \sin(2x)] = \cos(2x)$
6.  **Group terms:**
    *   $\cos(2x)$ terms: $(4B - 4Ax) + 4Ax = 4B$
    *   $\sin(2x)$ terms: $(-4A - 4Bx) + 4Bx = -4A$
    The equation simplifies to:
    $(4B) \cos(2x) + (-4A) \sin(2x) = 1 \cos(2x) + 0 \sin(2x)$
7.  **Equate coefficients:**
    *   For $\cos(2x)$: $4B = 1 \implies B = 1/4$
    *   For $\sin(2x)$: $-4A = 0 \implies A = 0$
8.  **Particular solution:**
    $y_p = x(0 \cos(2x) + \frac{1}{4} \sin(2x)) = \frac{1}{4} x \sin(2x)$.

Notice how the $x$ in the guess "un-cancels" the problem. The coefficients of $x\cos(2x)$ and $x\sin(2x)$ vanished, leaving us with simple constants to equate. This is the hallmark of correctly applying the modification.

**Connecting to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO1: Solve systems of linear equations and diagonalize matrices.** This is directly used in Step 7 where we equate coefficients to form a system of linear equations for $A$ and $B$. For instance, in the first example, we had a simple $3A=1$ and $3B=0$. In the second example, it was $4B=1$ and $-4A=0$. While simple here, more complex forcing functions lead to larger systems.
*   **CO2: Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equations with constant coefficients.** This entire topic is about solving the non-homogeneous part. We find $y_c$ (homogeneous solution) and then $y_p$ (particular solution), and their sum $y = y_c + y_p$ is the general solution to the non-homogeneous ODE. This is the core of this learning outcome.
*   **Connecting with other course outcomes (indirectly but importantly):** Understanding how to find $y_p$ for sinusoidal inputs is fundamental for analyzing systems that exhibit oscillatory behavior. This knowledge directly feeds into topics like resonance, damping, and forced oscillations, which are vital in electrical circuits (RLC circuits) and mechanical systems (mass-spring systems). For example, if you're studying an RLC circuit driven by a sinusoidal voltage $V(t) = E_0 \cos(\omega t)$, the resulting differential equation will have a forcing function of this form. The particular solution $y_p$ will represent the steady-state response of the circuit. This links to our textbook references like Haykin's *Signals and Systems*.

**Intuitive Analogy:**

Imagine pushing a swing.

*   **Homogeneous part ($y_c$)**: If you just give the swing a push and let it go, it will swing back and forth with its own natural frequency (like $\cos(\omega_0 x)$ or $\sin(\omega_0 x)$). This is its free oscillation.
*   **Forcing function ($g(x)$):** Now, imagine you are pushing the swing periodically.
    *   If you push it at a frequency *different* from its natural frequency ($ \omega \neq \omega_0$), your pushes will add to the swing's motion. A particular solution of the form $A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$ describes how the swing settles into responding to your pushes. It will swing with the frequency of your pushes.
    *   If you push the swing at *exactly* its natural frequency ($ \omega = \omega_0$), you are "in sync" with the swing. Each push adds more energy, and the amplitude of the swing grows significantly. This is resonance! Our ODE reflects this by requiring the $x$ multiplier in $y_p$. The amplitude of the swing increases linearly with time ($Ax \sin(\omega x)$), meaning it can go higher and higher. This is why the $x$ factor is crucial in the "special case."

**Key Takeaways and Exam Focus:**

*   **Always check if your forcing frequency $\omega$ matches the frequency of the complementary solution's trigonometric terms.** This is the most common pitfall. If $y_c$ has $\cos(\omega_0 x)$ or $\sin(\omega_0 x)$, and your $g(x)$ is $k \cos(\omega x)$ with $\omega = \omega_0$, you MUST use the $y_p = x(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x))$ guess.
*   **The derivative calculations in the special case can be tricky.** Take your time, use the product rule carefully, and double-check your work. Grouping terms by $\cos(\omega x)$, $\sin(\omega x)$, $x\cos(\omega x)$, and $x\sin(\omega x)$ is essential.
*   **The method of Undetermined Coefficients is an educated guess.** The form of the guess is guided by the forcing function $g(x)$. For $k\cos(\omega x)$, the guess is always a combination of $A\cos(\omega x)$ and $B\sin(\omega x)$, possibly multiplied by $x$.
*   **The system of equations for $A$ and $B$ is paramount.** Solving it correctly gives you the specific coefficients that make the particular solution satisfy the ODE.
*   **Remember to add the particular solution to the complementary solution** to get the general solution $y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x)$. This is what the full problem often asks for.

Let's look at a slightly more complex example involving the $b$ term.

**Example 3: With a Damping Term ($b \neq 0$)**

Find a particular solution for $y'' + 3y' + 2y = \cos(x)$.

Here, $a=1$, $b=3$, $c=2$, and $g(x) = \cos(x)$, so $k=1, \omega=1$.

1.  **Characteristic equation:** $r^2 + 3r + 2 = 0$. This factors as $(r+1)(r+2) = 0$, so roots are $r_1 = -1, r_2 = -2$.
    The complementary solution is $y_c = c_1 e^{-x} + c_2 e^{-2x}$.
2.  **Our forcing frequency is $\omega = 1$.** The complementary solution does not involve any trigonometric terms. Therefore, we are NOT in the special case.
3.  **Initial guess:** $y_p = A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)$.
4.  **Derivatives:**
    *   $y_p' = -A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)$
    *   $y_p'' = -A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)$
5.  **Substitute into ODE ($y'' + 3y' + 2y = \cos(x)$):**
    $(-A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)) + 3(-A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)) + 2(A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)) = \cos(x)$
6.  **Group terms:**
    *   $\cos(x)$ terms: $-A + 3B + 2A = A + 3B$
    *   $\sin(x)$ terms: $-B - 3A + 2B = -3A + B$
    The equation becomes:
    $(A + 3B) \cos(x) + (-3A + B) \sin(x) = 1 \cos(x) + 0 \sin(x)$
7.  **Equate coefficients:**
    *   For $\cos(x)$: $A + 3B = 1$
    *   For $\sin(x)$: $-3A + B = 0$
8.  **Solve the system:** From the second equation, $B = 3A$. Substitute this into the first equation:
    $A + 3(3A) = 1$
    $A + 9A = 1$
    $10A = 1 \implies A = 1/10$
    Then, $B = 3A = 3(1/10) = 3/10$.
9.  **Particular solution:** $y_p = \frac{1}{10} \cos(x) + \frac{3}{10} \sin(x)$.

This example shows how the $b$ term (damping) influences the system's response to the forcing function, leading to both cosine and sine terms in the particular solution, even if the forcing function is purely cosine. The coefficients $A$ and $B$ capture this interplay.

Remember this principle: the form of $y_p$ is dictated by $g(x)$, and the coefficients are determined by ensuring $y_p$ satisfies the original ODE. Always be vigilant about the special case where $y_p$'s form overlaps with $y_c$. This topic is fundamental for understanding the forced and damped oscillations so prevalent in your electrical science and physical science studies.

### Sample Questions and Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
What is the standard form of the particular solution ($y_p$) when the forcing function of a second-order linear non-homogeneous ODE is $g(x) = 5 \sin(3x)$?

**Answer 1:**
The standard guess for a particular solution when the forcing function is a sine term like $5 \sin(3x)$ is a combination of sine and cosine terms of the same frequency. Therefore, the standard form of $y_p$ is:
$y_p = A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x)$
where $A$ and $B$ are undetermined coefficients. We include the cosine term because its derivative might produce a sine term, and vice-versa, and we need to ensure all parts of the forcing function can be matched.

**Question 2 (Procedural / Special Case Identification):**
Consider the ODE $y'' + 9y = 4 \cos(3x)$.
(a) What is the complementary solution ($y_c$)?
(b) Is there a "special case" overlap between the forcing function and the complementary solution? Justify your answer.
(c) What is the appropriate form for the particular solution ($y_p$)?

**Answer 2:**
(a) The homogeneous part of the ODE is $y'' + 9y = 0$. The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 9 = 0$, which gives $r^2 = -9$, so $r = \pm 3i$. Since the roots are purely imaginary, the complementary solution is:
$y_c = c_1 \cos(3x) + c_2 \sin(3x)$.

(b) The forcing function is $g(x) = 4 \cos(3x)$, which has a frequency $\omega = 3$. The complementary solution $y_c$ contains terms of the form $\cos(3x)$ and $\sin(3x)$. Since the frequency of the forcing function ($\omega=3$) matches the frequency present in the complementary solution, there *is* a special case overlap.

(c) Because of the special case overlap identified in part (b), our initial guess of $A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x)$ would be part of $y_c$. To correct for this, we must multiply our guess by $x$. Therefore, the appropriate form for the particular solution is:
$y_p = x(A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x)) = Ax \cos(3x) + Bx \sin(3x)$.

**Question 3 (Application / Calculation):**
Find the general solution to the ODE $y'' - 4y = \sin(2x)$.

**Answer 3:**
1.  **Find the complementary solution ($y_c$):**
    The homogeneous ODE is $y'' - 4y = 0$.
    Characteristic equation: $r^2 - 4 = 0 \implies r^2 = 4 \implies r = \pm 2$.
    Since the roots are real and distinct, $y_c = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{-2x}$.

2.  **Determine the form of the particular solution ($y_p$):**
    The forcing function is $g(x) = \sin(2x)$. Here, $\omega = 2$.
    The complementary solution $y_c$ has terms $e^{2x}$ and $e^{-2x}$. It does *not* contain trigonometric terms. Therefore, there is no special case overlap based on the frequency matching.
    The initial guess for $y_p$ is: $y_p = A \cos(2x) + B \sin(2x)$.

3.  **Calculate derivatives of $y_p$:**
    $y_p' = -2A \sin(2x) + 2B \cos(2x)$
    $y_p'' = -4A \cos(2x) - 4B \sin(2x)$

4.  **Substitute into the ODE ($y'' - 4y = \sin(2x)$):**
    $(-4A \cos(2x) - 4B \sin(2x)) - 4(A \cos(2x) + B \sin(2x)) = \sin(2x)$

5.  **Group terms and equate coefficients:**
    $(-4A - 4A) \cos(2x) + (-4B - 4B) \sin(2x) = \sin(2x)$
    $(-8A) \cos(2x) + (-8B) \sin(2x) = 0 \cos(2x) + 1 \sin(2x)$

    Equating coefficients:
    For $\cos(2x)$: $-8A = 0 \implies A = 0$.
    For $\sin(2x)$: $-8B = 1 \implies B = -1/8$.

6.  **Form the particular solution:**
    $y_p = 0 \cos(2x) - \frac{1}{8} \sin(2x) = -\frac{1}{8} \sin(2x)$.

7.  **Write the general solution:**
    $y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x) = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{-2x} - \frac{1}{8} \sin(2x)$.

This question tests your ability to correctly identify the complementary solution, choose the appropriate guess for $y_p$ (checking for the special case), perform the substitutions and calculations, and finally combine $y_c$ and $y_p$.
