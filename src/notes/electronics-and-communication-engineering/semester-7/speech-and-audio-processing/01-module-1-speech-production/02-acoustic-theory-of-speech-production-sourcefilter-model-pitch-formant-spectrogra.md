## 1. Acoustic Theory of Speech Production

The acoustic theory of speech production describes how the complex sounds of human speech are generated by manipulating the airflow from the lungs through a series of resonators and constrictions.

### 1.1. The Speech Production Mechanism

The primary components involved in speech production are:

*   **The Power Source:** The lungs, diaphragm, and rib cage provide the airflow (usually exhaled air). This airflow is the energy source for speech.
    *   **Example:** The act of breathing in and out.
*   **The Sound Source (Larynx/Vocal Cords):** Located in the throat, the larynx contains the vocal cords.
    *   **Voiced Sounds:** When air passes through the vocal cords and causes them to vibrate, a periodic buzzing sound is produced. This is the basis for voiced sounds (e.g., /a/, /i/, /u/, /b/, /d/, /g/, /z/, /v/).
        *   **Reference:** O'Shaughnessy (2nd Ed.), Chapter 3, discusses the vocal cords and phonation.
    *   **Unvoiced Sounds:** When the vocal cords do not vibrate, the sound produced is aperiodic or noise-like. This is the basis for unvoiced sounds (e.g., /s/, /f/, /sh/, /t/, /p/, /k/).
        *   **Example:** The sound of "s" in "sun" is created by turbulent airflow through a narrow constriction.
*   **The Vocal Tract (Articulators):** This is the resonating cavity formed by the throat, mouth, and nasal cavity. The shape of the vocal tract is modified by articulators (tongue, lips, teeth, jaw, velum) to change the resonant frequencies, thereby shaping the sound source into distinct speech sounds (phonemes).
    *   **Articulators:**
        *   **Tongue:** The primary articulator, capable of moving in many ways to change the shape of the oral cavity.
        *   **Lips:** Can be rounded, spread, or protruded to alter resonance.
        *   **Jaw:** Controls the opening of the mouth.
        *   **Velum (Soft Palate):** Can be raised to block airflow to the nasal cavity (oral sounds) or lowered to allow air to pass through the nose (nasal sounds).
    *   **Example:** Producing the vowel /i/ (as in "see") involves raising the tongue high and forward in the mouth, constricting the airflow near the hard palate. Producing the vowel /u/ (as in "too") involves lowering and retracting the tongue and rounding the lips.

## 2. Source/Filter Model of Speech Production

The Source/Filter model is a fundamental concept in speech processing that simplifies the complex process of speech production into two main components: a sound source and a vocal tract filter.

### 2.1. Model Description

*   **Sound Source (Excitation):** This component generates the raw acoustic energy. It can be:
    *   **Periodic (Vocal Fold Vibration):** For voiced sounds, this source is approximately a periodic train of glottal pulses.
    *   **Aperiodic (Turbulence/Noise):** For unvoiced sounds, this source is broadband noise generated by constrictions or obstructions in the vocal tract.
*   **Vocal Tract (Filter):** The vocal tract acts as a linear time-varying filter. It modifies the spectrum of the sound source by amplifying or attenuating certain frequencies based on its resonant frequencies (formants). The shape of the vocal tract, and thus its filtering characteristics, changes rapidly during speech.

### 2.2. Mathematical Representation

The output speech signal, $s(t)$, can be modeled as the convolution of the source signal, $u(t)$, and the impulse response of the vocal tract filter, $h(t)$:

$s(t) = u(t) * h(t)$

where:
*   $s(t)$ is the resulting speech signal.
*   $u(t)$ is the source signal (e.g., glottal pulse train or noise).
*   $h(t)$ is the impulse response of the vocal tract.
*   $*$ denotes convolution.

In the frequency domain, convolution becomes multiplication:

$S(j\omega) = U(j\omega) * H(j\omega)$

where:
*   $S(j\omega)$, $U(j\omega)$, and $H(j\omega)$ are the Fourier Transforms of $s(t)$, $u(t)$, and $h(t)$ respectively.

**Important Note:** This is a simplified model. In reality, the source and filter are not perfectly independent. For example, the glottis itself has acoustic properties that influence the sound source. However, the Source/Filter model provides a powerful framework for understanding and analyzing speech.

**Reference:** Quatieri (2001), Chapter 2, introduces the source-filter model as a key concept for speech analysis.

## 3. Pitch and Formant

These are two critical acoustic characteristics of speech signals that are directly related to the Source/Filter model.

### 3.1. Pitch

*   **Definition:** Pitch is the perceptual correlate of the fundamental frequency ($F_0$) of the vocal fold vibration. It is what makes a sound perceived as "high" or "low" in terms of its musicality.
*   **Source Property:** Pitch is primarily determined by the **sound source** (vocal fold vibration).
    *   **High Pitch:** Corresponds to a high fundamental frequency ($F_0$), meaning the vocal cords vibrate rapidly.
    *   **Low Pitch:** Corresponds to a low fundamental frequency ($F_0$), meaning the vocal cords vibrate slowly.
*   **Factors Affecting Pitch:**
    *   Tension of vocal cords.
    *   Airflow rate.
    *   Vocal cord thickness and length.
*   **Example:** A male voice typically has a lower pitch than a female voice due to longer, thicker vocal cords. The intonation in a sentence (e.g., rising pitch for a question) is a change in $F_0$.

### 3.2. Formant

*   **Definition:** Formants are the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract. They are the frequencies at which the vocal tract amplifies the sound source most efficiently.
*   **Filter Property:** Formants are primarily determined by the **shape of the vocal tract** (the filter).
    *   When the frequency of the sound source (e.g., harmonics of $F_0$) matches a resonant frequency of the vocal tract, that frequency is amplified, creating a peak in the speech spectrum.
*   **Formant Frequencies:** The first three or four formants (F1, F2, F3, F4) are most important for distinguishing speech sounds, especially vowels.
    *   **F1:** Related to the openness of the mouth (jaw opening and tongue height). Lower F1 for high vowels (like /i/, /u/), higher F1 for low vowels (like /a/).
    *   **F2:** Related to the position of the tongue front/back. Higher F2 for front vowels (like /i/, /e/), lower F2 for back vowels (like /u/, /o/).
    *   **F3:** Also contributes to vowel quality and is important for certain consonants.
*   **Vowel Identification:** Vowels are largely characterized by their formant frequencies. Different combinations of F1 and F2 frequencies map to different vowels.
    *   **Example:** The vowel /i/ (as in "see") has a low F1 and a high F2. The vowel /a/ (as in "father") has a high F1 and a mid-range F2.
*   **Vocal Tract Modification:** Changing the position of the tongue, lips, and jaw alters the vocal tract's shape and thus its formants.
    *   **Example:** Rounding the lips tends to lower the formants (especially F2 and F3).

**Important Point to Remember:** Pitch is related to the *rate* of vibration of the vocal cords (source), while formants are related to the *resonant properties* of the vocal tract (filter).

**Reference:** O'Shaughnessy (2nd Ed.), Chapters 3 and 4, delve into the acoustic correlates of speech, including pitch and formants.

## 4. Spectrogram

A spectrogram is a visual representation of the spectrum of frequencies of a signal as it varies with time. It is an indispensable tool for analyzing speech signals.

### 4.1. What it Shows

A spectrogram displays:

*   **Time:** Along the horizontal axis.
*   **Frequency:** Along the vertical axis.
*   **Intensity/Amplitude:** Represented by the darkness or color of the plot at each time-frequency point. Brighter or darker areas indicate higher amplitude at that specific frequency and time.

### 4.2. How it's Generated

1.  **Windowing:** The speech signal is divided into short, overlapping segments (frames) of typically 10-30 milliseconds.
2.  **Fourier Transform:** A Fourier Transform (or similar frequency analysis like the Fast Fourier Transform - FFT) is applied to each frame to determine the frequency content within that short time interval.
3.  **Display:** The results are plotted, with time on the x-axis, frequency on the y-axis, and the magnitude of the frequency components represented by intensity.

### 4.3. Interpretation of Speech Spectrograms

*   **Voiced Segments:** Show horizontal striations (bands of energy) corresponding to the harmonics of the fundamental frequency ($F_0$). The spacing between these striations is the $F_0$.
*   **Formants:** Appear as dark bands or concentrations of energy at specific frequency regions. These are the peaks in the vocal tract's frequency response.
*   **Unvoiced Segments:** Show a diffuse or noisy pattern spread across a wider range of frequencies, without clear harmonic structure.
*   **Transitions:** The changes in formants and the presence/absence of harmonic structure clearly show the transitions between speech sounds.

**Example:** Listening to the word "see" and looking at its spectrogram:
*   The "s" sound will appear as a wide band of noise (high frequency energy).
*   The vowel /i/ will show clear formant bands, with a low F1 and high F2.
*   The transitions between the "s" and /i/ will be visible as changes in the spectral content.

**Reference:** O'Shaughnessy (2nd Ed.), Chapter 4, provides detailed examples and explanations of spectrogram analysis. Quatieri (2001) also discusses spectral analysis techniques.

## 5. Discrete Model for Speech Production

For digital processing, speech signals are sampled and quantized, making them discrete-time signals. The Source/Filter model needs to be adapted for this discrete-time domain.

### 5.1. Discrete-Time Source/Filter Model

In the discrete-time domain, the vocal tract is modeled as a **Linear Predictive Coding (LPC)** filter.

*   **Discrete-Time Filter:** The vocal tract's impulse response, $h(n)$, in discrete time can be represented by a Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filter or an Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) filter. FIR filters are often preferred for their stability and linear phase properties.
*   **LPC Model:** The most common discrete-time model uses an Autoregressive (AR) model, often referred to as the LPC model. This model assumes that the current speech sample can be predicted as a linear combination of past speech samples plus a driving signal.
    *   $s(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i s(n-i) + u(n)$
    where:
        *   $s(n)$ is the current speech sample.
        *   $a_i$ are the predictor coefficients (representing the vocal tract filter).
        *   $p$ is the order of the filter (number of coefficients).
        *   $u(n)$ is the excitation signal (discrete-time source).
*   **Filter Coefficients ($a_i$):** These coefficients are derived from the spectral characteristics of the vocal tract. They capture the formant frequencies and their bandwidths.
*   **Excitation Signal ($u(n)$):**
    *   For voiced sounds, $u(n)$ is a pulse train with a period corresponding to the pitch period.
    *   For unvoiced sounds, $u(n)$ is a random noise signal.

### 5.2. Advantages of the Discrete Model

*   **Efficient Representation:** The LPC coefficients provide a compact way to represent the spectral envelope of the speech signal.
*   **Parameterization:** Allows for the extraction of key speech parameters like formants (derived from $a_i$), pitch, and voicing.
*   **Synthesis:** Speech can be synthesized by generating an appropriate excitation signal and passing it through an LPC filter with estimated coefficients.
*   **Analysis/Recognition:** Used in speech recognition systems to extract features from the speech signal.

**Reference:** Quatieri (2001), Chapter 5, provides a comprehensive treatment of LPC models for speech production. Rabiner (2003) also covers various discrete-time models.

### **Key Points to Remember:**

*   Speech production involves airflow from the lungs, modulated by the vocal cords (source) and shaped by the vocal tract (filter).
*   The Source/Filter model decomposes speech into an excitation signal and a vocal tract transfer function.
*   **Pitch** is perceived loudness and relates to the fundamental frequency ($F_0$) of vocal cord vibration (source).
*   **Formants** are resonant frequencies of the vocal tract, determined by its shape, and are crucial for distinguishing speech sounds like vowels.
*   A **spectrogram** visualizes speech in the time-frequency domain, showing how spectral content changes over time.
*   The **discrete model** uses parameters like LPC coefficients to represent the vocal tract filter in digital signal processing, enabling analysis and synthesis.

### **Answers to Practice Questions:**

**Answer 1:**
The three main components are:
1.  **Power Source:** The lungs, diaphragm, and rib cage, which provide airflow.
2.  **Sound Source:** The larynx and vocal cords, which vibrate to create voiced sounds or produce turbulence for unvoiced sounds.
3.  **Vocal Tract:** The resonating cavities (throat, mouth, nasal cavity) that shape the sound produced by the source.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Voiced sounds:** Produced by the vibration of the vocal cords, resulting in a quasi-periodic sound source with a fundamental frequency ($F_0$) and its harmonics.
*   **Unvoiced sounds:** Produced without vocal cord vibration, typically by turbulence created by constricting airflow, resulting in an aperiodic, noise-like sound source spread across a broad frequency range.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Pitch** is primarily determined by the **sound source** (vocal fold vibration, specifically its fundamental frequency, $F_0$).
*   **Formants** are primarily determined by the **vocal tract** (its shape and resonant frequencies).

**Answer 4:**
*   **Voiced segments** on a spectrogram will show clear horizontal striations (bands of energy) corresponding to the harmonics of the fundamental frequency ($F_0$). The spacing between these striations represents the $F_0$.
*   **Unvoiced segments** on a spectrogram will appear as diffuse, noisy patterns spread across a wide range of frequencies, without distinct harmonic structure.

**Answer 5:**
Vowel sounds are primarily defined by their **formant frequencies**, particularly the frequencies of the first two formants (F1 and F2). The specific combination of F1 and F2 values determines which vowel is perceived.

**Answer 6:**
The LPC model represents the vocal tract's resonant characteristics in discrete time using a set of predictor coefficients ($a_i$). It allows for efficient parameterization of the speech signal's spectral envelope, enabling analysis (like formant estimation) and synthesis by modeling the vocal tract as a digital filter.
