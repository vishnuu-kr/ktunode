# Module 2: Satellite Link Design - Intermodulation Interference

## 1. Introduction to Intermodulation Interference (CO1, CO2, K2, K3)

Intermodulation interference is a significant factor in satellite communication system design, particularly when multiple carriers are transmitted through a single nonlinear amplifier, such as a Traveling Wave Tube (TWT) or a Solid-State Power Amplifier (SSPA) onboard the satellite.

### 1.1 What is Intermodulation Interference?

*   **Definition:** Intermodulation (IM) occurs when two or more signals pass through a nonlinear device. The nonlinear device generates unwanted signals at frequencies that are sums and differences of the original signal frequencies and their harmonics.
*   **Cause:** Amplifiers, especially those operating near saturation, exhibit nonlinearity. This nonlinearity causes the output signal to be a distorted version of the input signal, containing not only the amplified original signals but also new, spurious frequencies.
*   **Impact:** These spurious frequencies can fall into the same frequency bands as other desired signals, causing interference. This interference degrades the quality of the received signals, leading to increased bit error rates (BER) in digital systems or noise and distortion in analog systems.

### 1.2 Why is it Crucial in Satellite Links?

*   **Frequency Reuse:** Satellites often carry multiple independent carriers (transponders) simultaneously. To maximize the utilization of the limited frequency spectrum, these carriers are frequently amplified by a single high-power amplifier.
*   **Nonlinearity of Amplifiers:** Satellite amplifiers, particularly TWTs, are known for their high power efficiency but inherent nonlinearity, especially when operated close to their saturation point to achieve maximum output power. SSPAs are generally more linear but still exhibit some nonlinearity.
*   **Bandwidth Constraints:** The output of the satellite amplifier is typically filtered to limit the signal to the intended transponder bandwidth. However, IM products can fall within these adjacent transponder bandwidths, causing interference.

## 2. Sources and Types of Intermodulation Products (CO1, CO2, K2, K3)

Intermodulation products are categorized based on the order of the nonlinear equation that generates them.

### 2.1 Nonlinearity and Taylor Series Expansion

The nonlinear behavior of an amplifier can be approximated by a Taylor series expansion of the output voltage ($v_{out}$) as a function of the input voltage ($v_{in}$):

$v_{out}(t) = a_1 v_{in}(t) + a_2 v_{in}^2(t) + a_3 v_{in}^3(t) + ...$

Where:
*   $a_1$ is the linear gain term.
*   $a_2$ is the coefficient of the second-order nonlinearity.
*   $a_3$ is the coefficient of the third-order nonlinearity.
*   Higher-order terms ($a_4, a_5$, etc.) represent increasingly complex nonlinearities.

### 2.2 Third-Order Intermodulation (IMD3) Products

*   **Generation:** Third-order nonlinearities ($a_3$) are the most significant contributors to intermodulation interference in practice.
*   **Mechanism:** When two signals, $f_1$ and $f_2$, are present at the input, the $a_3 v_{in}^3(t)$ term generates new frequencies.
    *   If $v_{in}(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_1 t) + B \cos(2\pi f_2 t)$, then $v_{in}^3(t)$ contains terms like $\cos(3 \times 2\pi f_1 t)$, $\cos(3 \times 2\pi f_2 t)$, and importantly, terms that result in frequencies at $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$.
*   **IMD3 Frequencies:** The critical IMD3 products are located at:
    *   $2f_1 - f_2$
    *   $2f_2 - f_1$
*   **Significance:** These third-order products are particularly problematic because they fall symmetrically around the original signals. If $f_1$ and $f_2$ are closely spaced, the IMD3 products can fall within the bandwidth of adjacent transponders or even within the same transponder, causing severe interference.

**Example:**
Consider two carriers at $f_1 = 10 \text{ GHz}$ and $f_2 = 10.01 \text{ GHz}$.
The third-order intermodulation products would be at:
*   $2f_1 - f_2 = 2(10) - 10.01 = 19.99 \text{ GHz}$
*   $2f_2 - f_1 = 2(10.01) - 10 = 20.02 - 10 = 10.02 \text{ GHz}$

If the transponder bandwidth is 40 MHz (e.g., from 10 GHz to 10.04 GHz), then both $f_1$, $f_2$, and the IMD3 products can fall within or very close to the operational bandwidths of adjacent transponders.

### 2.3 Other Order Intermodulation Products

*   **Second-Order Intermodulation (IMD2):** Generated by the $a_2 v_{in}^2(t)$ term. For $v_{in}(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_1 t) + B \cos(2\pi f_2 t)$, this term produces frequencies at $f_1 + f_2$ and $f_1 - f_2$.
    *   **Problem:** Second-order products are less of a problem in satellite systems for two main reasons:
        1.  **Satellite Transponder Planning:** Satellite transponders are typically planned so that $f_1 + f_2$ does not fall into any other operating channel.
        2.  **Satellite Architectures:** Up-link and down-link frequencies are separated. If a second-order product of two up-link signals falls onto the down-link frequency, it would cause interference. However, this is often managed by careful frequency allocation.
*   **Higher-Order Intermodulation (IMD5, IMD7, etc.):** Generated by $a_5 v_{in}^5(t)$, $a_7 v_{in}^7(t)$, etc.
    *   **Problem:** These products are generally weaker than IMD3 products and are located at frequencies further away from the original carriers. They are usually filtered out by the output filters. However, in highly nonlinear devices, they can become significant.

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Ed., 2021), Chapter 7:** Discusses amplifier nonlinearities and their effect on multi-carrier signals, with a focus on IMD3 products. They emphasize the importance of the $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$ products for adjacent channel interference.

## 3. Quantifying Intermodulation Interference (CO2, K3)

The impact of intermodulation interference is quantified by the power of these unwanted products relative to the desired signal power.

### 3.1 Third-Order Intermodulation Products (IMD3)

*   **Definition:** The power of the third-order intermodulation products relative to the power of the original carriers.
*   **IMD3 Components:** For two carriers at $f_1$ and $f_2$, there are typically six third-order intermodulation products:
    *   $2f_1 - f_2$
    *   $2f_2 - f_1$
    *   $f_1 + f_1 - f_2$ (same as $2f_1-f_2$)
    *   $f_2 + f_2 - f_1$ (same as $2f_2-f_1$)
    *   $f_1 + f_2 - f_1$ (same as $f_2$) - not an IMD product
    *   $f_1 + f_2 - f_2$ (same as $f_1$) - not an IMD product
    *   The important ones are $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$.
*   **Power Level:** If the input powers of the two carriers are $P_1$ and $P_2$, and the amplifier has a third-order intercept point ($IP_3$), the power of the IMD3 products can be calculated.
    *   The power of the IMD3 products is typically $30 \text{ dB}$ below the power of the original carriers if the carriers are equally powered and the amplifier is operating at a specific point related to its 1 dB compression point ($P_{1dB}$).
    *   A common metric is the **IMD3 ratio**, which is the ratio of the desired carrier power to the IMD3 product power, usually expressed in dB.

### 3.2 Third-Order Intercept Point ($IP_3$)

*   **Definition:** The $IP_3$ is a hypothetical point on the extrapolated power transfer curve of a nonlinear device. At this point, the power of the third-order intermodulation products would theoretically be equal to the power of the fundamental (desired) signals.
*   **Significance:** $IP_3$ is a measure of a device's linearity. A higher $IP_3$ indicates a more linear device.
*   **Relationship with $P_{1dB}$:** For many devices, $IP_3$ is approximately $3.5 \text{ dB}$ to $4.5 \text{ dB}$ above the $P_{1dB}$ point. A common approximation is $IP_3 \approx P_{1dB} + 4 \text{ dB}$.
*   **Calculating IMD3 Power:** The power of an IMD3 product ($P_{IMD3}$) can be related to the carrier power ($P_c$) and the $IP_3$ by:

$P_{IMD3} (\text{dBm}) = 3 \times P_c (\text{dBm}) - 2 \times IP_3 (\text{dBm})$

This formula assumes equal input power for both carriers. If the input powers are unequal, the calculation becomes more complex.

**Skolnik (2nd Ed., 2017), Chapter 6 (Radar Receiver):** While primarily focused on radar, Skolnik discusses nonlinearities and intermodulation distortion, providing general principles applicable to communication systems. He explains the concept of intercept points ($IP_2, IP_3$) as measures of linearity.

## 4. Impact on Link Performance (CO1, CO2, K3)

Intermodulation products directly affect the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and, consequently, the bit error rate (BER) of a satellite communication link.

### 4.1 Carrier-to-Intermodulation Ratio (C/I_IM)

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the power of a desired carrier to the power of the strongest intermodulation product that interferes with it.
*   **Calculation:**
    *   Consider two carriers $f_1$ and $f_2$ with powers $P_1$ and $P_2$.
    *   The IMD3 product at $2f_1 - f_2$ interferes with carrier $f_2$.
    *   The IMD3 product at $2f_2 - f_1$ interferes with carrier $f_1$.
    *   The C/I_IM is calculated for each carrier and its interfering IMD products. For example, for carrier $f_1$:
        $C/I_{IM1} = P_1 - P_{IMD3}$ (where $P_{IMD3}$ is the power of the $2f_2 - f_1$ product).
*   **Impact on SNR:** The total noise in the receiver is the sum of thermal noise and intermodulation interference.
    *   $SNR_{total} = \frac{P_{signal}}{P_{noise} + P_{IMD}}$
    *   This degraded SNR leads to a higher BER.

### 4.2 Impact on BER

*   **Digital Systems:** In digital modulation schemes (like QPSK, 8PSK), a lower SNR directly translates to a higher BER. The link designer must ensure that the C/I_IM is sufficient to meet the required BER performance.
*   **Analog Systems:** In analog systems (like FM), intermodulation interference manifests as increased noise and distortion in the audio or video signal.

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Ed., 2021), Chapter 7:** Details how intermodulation products affect the overall noise power and consequently the SNR and BER of the link. They often express the interference in terms of an equivalent noise temperature or noise power.

## 5. Mitigation Techniques (CO2, K3)

Several techniques are employed to minimize the impact of intermodulation interference.

### 5.1 Operating Amplifiers Back-off

*   **Concept:** Amplifiers are operated below their saturation point, meaning their output power is reduced. This reduces the nonlinearity.
*   **Back-off:** The amount of power reduction from the saturation point is called "back-off."
    *   A larger back-off means lower output power but higher linearity and thus less IMD.
    *   A smaller back-off means higher output power but lower linearity and more IMD.
*   **Trade-off:** Backing off an amplifier reduces its output power, which in turn reduces the received signal power at the ground station. This directly affects the SNR and can necessitate larger ground antennas or higher power on the uplink. It is a crucial trade-off in satellite link design.

### 5.2 Carrier Spacing and Arrangement

*   **Concept:** Careful planning of the spacing between adjacent carriers and their placement within the transponder bandwidth.
*   **Guard Bands:** Introducing guard bands (unused frequency gaps) between adjacent carriers.
*   **IMD Product Placement:** Arranging carriers such that the IMD3 products ($2f_1 - f_2$, $2f_2 - f_1$) fall outside the operating bandwidth of the transponder or into unused portions of the spectrum.
*   **Example:** If carriers are $f_1, f_2, f_3, f_4$, and the amplifier amplifies all of them, the IMD products generated by $f_1$ and $f_2$ could interfere with $f_3$ or $f_4$. Spacing them out, or alternating their positions, can help.

### 5.3 Use of Linearizers

*   **Concept:** Devices that pre-distort the signal to counteract the nonlinearities of the amplifier.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Pre-distortion Linearizers:** Applied at the input of the amplifier.
    *   **Feedforward Linearizers:** Use a parallel amplifier path to generate an "anti-signal" that cancels the distortion products.
*   **Effectiveness:** Linearizers can significantly improve the linearity of amplifiers, allowing them to operate closer to saturation with reduced IMD.

### 5.4 Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM)

*   **Concept:** A modulation technique that divides a wide band into many narrow, closely spaced orthogonal sub-carriers.
*   **Advantage:** OFDM is inherently more robust to frequency-selective fading. While it doesn't eliminate IMD, the nature of IMD products from OFDM sub-carriers can be managed differently, and the overall system can be designed to be more resilient. However, IMD in OFDM can still cause inter-sub-carrier interference.

### 5.5 Single Carrier Per Transponder (SCPC)

*   **Concept:** Instead of multiple carriers in one transponder, each transponder carries only a single carrier.
*   **Advantage:** This completely eliminates intermodulation interference between carriers within the same transponder, as there is only one signal passing through the nonlinear amplifier.
*   **Disadvantage:** Less efficient use of transponder bandwidth and power compared to Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM) or Time Division Multiplexing (TDM) of multiple carriers.

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Ed., 2021), Chapter 7:** Provides a detailed discussion on amplifier back-off strategies, linearizers, and their impact on link performance. They present the trade-offs between power efficiency and linearity.
**Ha (2nd Ed., 2017), Chapter 5:** Also discusses amplifier linearity, back-off, and the use of linearization techniques.

## 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1 (CO1, CO2, K2, K3):**
Define intermodulation interference in the context of satellite communication. What are the most problematic types of intermodulation products and why?

**Answer 1:**
Intermodulation interference occurs when multiple signals pass through a nonlinear device, such as a satellite amplifier. This nonlinearity generates unwanted spurious signals at frequencies that are sums and differences of the original signal frequencies and their harmonics. The most problematic are third-order intermodulation (IMD3) products, specifically at frequencies $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$. These products are problematic because they fall close to the original signals and can easily fall into adjacent transponder bandwidths, causing significant interference and degrading signal quality.

---

**Question 2 (CO2, K3):**
Two carriers, $f_1 = 10.5 \text{ GHz}$ and $f_2 = 10.52 \text{ GHz}$, are amplified by a satellite transponder. If the amplifier has a third-order intercept point ($IP_3$) of $+40 \text{ dBm}$, and each carrier has an input power of $+10 \text{ dBm}$, calculate the power of the third-order intermodulation products that fall at $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$. Assume equal input power for simplicity.

**Answer 2:**
The formula for the power of a third-order intermodulation product for equal input powers ($P_c$) is:
$P_{IMD3} (\text{dBm}) = 3 \times P_c (\text{dBm}) - 2 \times IP_3 (\text{dBm})$

Given:
*   $P_c = +10 \text{ dBm}$ (power of each carrier)
*   $IP_3 = +40 \text{ dBm}$

$P_{IMD3} (\text{dBm}) = 3 \times (10 \text{ dBm}) - 2 \times (40 \text{ dBm})$
$P_{IMD3} (\text{dBm}) = 30 \text{ dBm} - 80 \text{ dBm}$
$P_{IMD3} (\text{dBm}) = -50 \text{ dBm}$

The power of the third-order intermodulation products at $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$ is $-50 \text{ dBm}$.

---

**Question 3 (CO2, K3):**
If the desired carrier power for $f_1$ is $+10 \text{ dBm}$, and the IMD3 product at $2f_2 - f_1$ interfering with $f_1$ has a power of $-50 \text{ dBm}$, what is the Carrier-to-Intermodulation Ratio (C/I_IM) for carrier $f_1$?

**Answer 3:**
The Carrier-to-Intermodulation Ratio (C/I_IM) is the ratio of the desired carrier power to the interfering IMD product power.
$C/I_{IM} = P_{carrier} - P_{IMD3}$
$C/I_{IM} = (+10 \text{ dBm}) - (-50 \text{ dBm})$
$C/I_{IM} = 10 + 50 \text{ dB}$
$C/I_{IM} = 60 \text{ dB}$

The C/I_IM for carrier $f_1$ is 60 dB.

**Question 4 (CO2, K3):**
Describe two common techniques used to mitigate intermodulation interference in satellite communication systems.

**Answer 4:**
Two common techniques are:

1.  **Amplifier Back-off:** The satellite amplifier (e.g., TWT) is operated at a lower power output than its maximum capability. This "backing off" reduces the amplifier's nonlinearity, thereby reducing the generation of intermodulation products. The trade-off is reduced effective radiated power (ERP).
2.  **Careful Carrier Spacing and Arrangement:** By strategically spacing carriers within a transponder and leaving guard bands, engineers can ensure that the critical IMD3 products fall into unused frequency bands or outside the transponder's operational spectrum. This prevents them from interfering with desired signals.

---

**Question 5 (CO1, CO2, K2, K3):**
Explain why second-order intermodulation products are generally less of a concern in typical geostationary satellite communication systems compared to third-order products.

**Answer 5:**
Second-order intermodulation products are generated at frequencies $f_1 + f_2$ and $f_1 - f_2$. While they can be problematic, they are typically managed in satellite systems due to:

*   **Frequency Planning:** Satellite transponders are designed with specific uplink and downlink frequency bands. Frequency planning is done such that the sum frequency ($f_1 + f_2$) of two uplink carriers does not fall into any downlink channel. The difference frequency ($f_1 - f_2$) is also considered.
*   **No Odd-Order Nonlinearities:** The Taylor series expansion of nonlinearities shows that odd-order terms (3rd, 5th, 7th) produce products that are closer to the original signals. Even-order terms (2nd, 4th, 6th) produce products symmetrically spaced, and often further away or in regions that can be managed through allocation. The third-order terms produce products ($2f_1-f_2$, $2f_2-f_1$) that are very close to the original carriers, making them much harder to filter out.

## 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Nonlinearity is Key:** Intermodulation interference arises from the nonlinear amplification of multiple signals.
*   **IMD3 is the Villain:** Third-order intermodulation products ($2f_1 - f_2$, $2f_2 - f_1$) are the most problematic due to their proximity to the original carriers.
*   **$IP_3$ is a Metric:** A higher $IP_3$ indicates a more linear device.
*   **Trade-offs:** Managing IMD often involves trade-offs, such as amplifier back-off (reduced power) versus linearity.
*   **Link Budget Impact:** IMD directly degrades the SNR and increases the BER in digital links.
*   **System Design:** Careful frequency planning, carrier arrangement, and amplifier choice are crucial for mitigating IMD.
