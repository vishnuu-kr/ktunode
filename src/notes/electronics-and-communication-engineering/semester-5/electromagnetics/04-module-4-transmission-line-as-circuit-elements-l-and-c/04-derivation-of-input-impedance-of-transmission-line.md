# ELECTROMAGNETICS: Module 4 - Transmission Lines as Circuit Elements (L and C)

## Topic: Derivation of Input Impedance of Transmission Line

### Introduction

This module explores the behavior of transmission lines from a circuit perspective, treating them as distributed L and C elements. We will derive the crucial concept of input impedance, which is fundamental to understanding how transmission lines behave when connected to a source and a load. This topic is directly related to **CO4: Analyze the propagation of EM waves in transmission lines and wave guides** (Knowledge Level: K3), as the input impedance dictates the power transfer and wave behavior at the source end.

### 1. Understanding Transmission Lines as Distributed Elements

**Key Concept:** Unlike lumped circuit elements (resistors, capacitors, inductors) where the electrical properties are concentrated at a single point, transmission lines have distributed inductance (L) and capacitance (C) along their length. This means that the electrical behavior is spread out over the entire length of the line.

*   **Inductance (L):** Arises from the magnetic field generated by the current flowing in the conductors.
*   **Capacitance (C):** Arises from the electric field between the conductors.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Sadiku (7th ed.):** Chapter 10 on Transmission Lines discusses the distributed nature of inductance and capacitance per unit length. He introduces the primary and secondary constants of a transmission line.
*   **Hayt & Buck (9th ed.):** Chapter 6, "The Line as a Circuit Element," provides a thorough treatment of the voltage and current relationships along a uniform transmission line, leading to the concept of wave propagation and impedance.

### 2. The Telegrapher's Equations

**Key Concept:** The behavior of voltage and current along a uniform transmission line can be described by a set of partial differential equations known as the Telegrapher's Equations. These equations are derived by considering a differential section of the transmission line and applying Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws.

Consider a differential section of the transmission line of length $dz$, with series resistance $R$ (ohms/meter), shunt conductance $G$ (mhos/meter), series inductance $L$ (henries/meter), and shunt capacitance $C$ (farads/meter).

*   **Voltage Drop across Series Inductance and Resistance:**
    $dV = -(RI + L \frac{\partial I}{\partial t}) dz$
    $\frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = -(RI + L \frac{\partial I}{\partial t})$  (Equation 1)

*   **Current Change due to Shunt Conductance and Capacitance:**
    $dI = -(GV + C \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}) dz$
    $\frac{\partial I}{\partial z} = -(GV + C \frac{\partial V}{\partial t})$  (Equation 2)

**Important Point:** For lossless transmission lines, $R=0$ and $G=0$. In this case, the Telegrapher's Equations simplify to:
$\frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = -L \frac{\partial I}{\partial t}$
$\frac{\partial I}{\partial z} = -C \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}$

### 3. Wave Propagation and Characteristic Impedance

**Key Concept:** When sinusoidal time-varying voltages and currents are applied, we can use phasor analysis. Assuming $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = j\omega$, the Telegrapher's Equations become:
$\frac{dV(z)}{dz} = -(R + j\omega L) I(z)$
$\frac{dI(z)}{dz} = -(G + j\omega C) V(z)$

These equations lead to second-order differential equations for voltage and current, whose solutions are of the form of traveling waves:
$V(z) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + V_0^- e^{\gamma z}$
$I(z) = I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + I_0^- e^{\gamma z}$

where:
*   $V_0^+$ and $I_0^+$ are the amplitudes of the forward-traveling waves.
*   $V_0^-$ and $I_0^-$ are the amplitudes of the backward-traveling waves.
*   $\gamma = \sqrt{(R+j\omega L)(G+j\omega C)}$ is the propagation constant.

**Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$):**

The ratio of the voltage wave to the current wave for a wave traveling in one direction is a constant, known as the characteristic impedance. For the forward-traveling wave:
$Z_0 = \frac{V_0^+}{I_0^+} = \sqrt{\frac{R+j\omega L}{G+j\omega C}}$

For a lossless line ($R=0, G=0$):
$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{j\omega L}{j\omega C}} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$

**Important Point:** The characteristic impedance ($Z_0$) is a property of the transmission line itself and is independent of the length of the line or the load connected to it. It represents the impedance the line "looks like" to a wave traveling along it.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Reddy (1st ed.):** Chapter 3, "Transmission Line Parameters and Equations," provides a detailed derivation of the characteristic impedance for both lossy and lossless lines.
*   **Nahvi & Edminister (Schaum's Outline, 5th ed.):** Chapter 11, "Transmission Lines," offers numerous solved problems demonstrating the calculation of characteristic impedance for various line configurations.

### 4. Deriving the Input Impedance ($Z_{in}$)

**Key Concept:** The input impedance of a transmission line is the ratio of the voltage to the current at the input terminals (source end) of the line. It depends on the characteristic impedance ($Z_0$), the length of the line ($l$), and the impedance of the load ($Z_L$) connected at the other end.

Let the transmission line have a length $l$. We are interested in the impedance looking into the line at $z=0$, with the load $Z_L$ connected at $z=l$.

The general solutions for voltage and current are:
$V(z) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + V_0^- e^{\gamma z}$
$I(z) = I_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + I_0^- e^{\gamma z}$

We know that $I(z) = \frac{V(z)}{Z_0}$ for the forward wave and $I(z) = -\frac{V(z)}{Z_0}$ for the backward wave. This implies:
$I(z) = \frac{1}{Z_0} (V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} - V_0^- e^{\gamma z})$

At the load end ($z=l$):
$V(l) = V_L = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} + V_0^- e^{\gamma l}$
$I(l) = I_L = \frac{1}{Z_0} (V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} - V_0^- e^{\gamma l})$

The load impedance is defined as $Z_L = \frac{V(l)}{I(l)}$.
$Z_L = \frac{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} + V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{\frac{1}{Z_0} (V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} - V_0^- e^{\gamma l})}$
$Z_L = Z_0 \frac{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} + V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} - V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}$

We can express the ratio of backward to forward voltage amplitudes at the input as the reflection coefficient at the load, $\Gamma_L$:
$\Gamma_L = \frac{V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l}} = \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+} e^{2\gamma l}$

This is not the standard reflection coefficient, but rather related to it. Let's define the reflection coefficient at the load plane ($z=l$) as $\Gamma_{z=l} = \frac{V_0^- e^{-\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{\gamma l}}$.

From the $Z_L$ expression:
$Z_L = Z_0 \frac{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} (1 + \frac{V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l}})}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} (1 - \frac{V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l}})}$
$Z_L = Z_0 \frac{1 + \Gamma_L}{1 - \Gamma_L}$, where $\Gamma_L = \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+} e^{2\gamma l}$.

Rearranging to find the ratio of amplitudes:
$Z_L(1 - \Gamma_L) = Z_0(1 + \Gamma_L)$
$Z_L - Z_L \Gamma_L = Z_0 + Z_0 \Gamma_L$
$Z_L - Z_0 = \Gamma_L (Z_0 + Z_L)$
$\Gamma_L = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$

Now, we want to find the input impedance $Z_{in} = \frac{V(0)}{I(0)}$.
$V(0) = V_0^+ + V_0^-$
$I(0) = \frac{1}{Z_0} (V_0^+ - V_0^-)$

$Z_{in} = \frac{V_0^+ + V_0^-}{\frac{1}{Z_0} (V_0^+ - V_0^-)} = Z_0 \frac{V_0^+ + V_0^-}{V_0^+ - V_0^-}$

We can rewrite this in terms of $\Gamma = \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+}$ (the reflection coefficient at the input plane, $z=0$):
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{1 + \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+}}{1 - \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+}} = Z_0 \frac{1 + \Gamma}{1 - \Gamma}$

To relate $\Gamma$ to $\Gamma_L$, we need to consider the propagation through the line.
At $z=0$: $V(0) = V_0^+ + V_0^-$ and $I(0) = \frac{1}{Z_0}(V_0^+ - V_0^-)$.
At $z=l$: $V(l) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} + V_0^- e^{\gamma l}$ and $I(l) = \frac{1}{Z_0}(V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} - V_0^- e^{\gamma l})$.

We know that $Z_L = V(l)/I(l)$. Let $\Gamma_L = \frac{V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l}}$. This is the ratio of the backward wave voltage at $z=l$ to the forward wave voltage at $z=l$.
$Z_L = Z_0 \frac{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} + V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l} - V_0^- e^{\gamma l}} = Z_0 \frac{1 + \frac{V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l}}}{1 - \frac{V_0^- e^{\gamma l}}{V_0^+ e^{-\gamma l}}} = Z_0 \frac{1 + \Gamma_L'}{1 - \Gamma_L'}$ where $\Gamma_L' = \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+} e^{2\gamma l}$.
So, $\Gamma_L' = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$.

Now, we want $Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{V_0^+ + V_0^-}{V_0^+ - V_0^-}$.
We can express $V_0^-$ in terms of $V_0^+$ and $\Gamma_L'$: $V_0^- = \frac{\Gamma_L'}{e^{2\gamma l}} V_0^+$.
Substitute this into the $Z_{in}$ expression:
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{V_0^+ + \frac{\Gamma_L'}{e^{2\gamma l}} V_0^+}{V_0^+ - \frac{\Gamma_L'}{e^{2\gamma l}} V_0^+} = Z_0 \frac{1 + \Gamma_L' e^{-2\gamma l}}{1 - \Gamma_L' e^{-2\gamma l}}$

Substitute $\Gamma_L' = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$:
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{1 + \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} e^{-2\gamma l}}{1 - \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} e^{-2\gamma l}}$
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{(Z_L + Z_0) + (Z_L - Z_0) e^{-2\gamma l}}{(Z_L + Z_0) - (Z_L - Z_0) e^{-2\gamma l}}$

This is the most general form of the input impedance.

**For Lossless Transmission Lines ($R=0, G=0$):**
The propagation constant becomes purely imaginary: $\gamma = j\beta$, where $\beta = \omega \sqrt{LC}$ is the phase constant.
The characteristic impedance is $Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$.
The input impedance formula simplifies to:
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{(Z_L + Z_0) + (Z_L - Z_0) e^{-j2\beta l}}{(Z_L + Z_0) - (Z_L - Z_0) e^{-j2\beta l}}$

Using Euler's formula, $e^{-j2\beta l} = \cos(2\beta l) - j\sin(2\beta l)$.
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + Z_0 + (Z_L - Z_0)(\cos(2\beta l) - j\sin(2\beta l))}{Z_L + Z_0 - (Z_L - Z_0)(\cos(2\beta l) - j\sin(2\beta l))}$

This expression can be further simplified by separating the real and imaginary parts, or by using hyperbolic functions.

**Using Hyperbolic Functions (for lossless lines):**
We know that $e^{-\gamma l} = e^{-j\beta l} = \cos(\beta l) - j\sin(\beta l)$ and $e^{\gamma l} = e^{j\beta l} = \cos(\beta l) + j\sin(\beta l)$.
The input impedance can also be written as:
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + Z_0 \tanh(\gamma l)}{Z_0 + Z_L \tanh(\gamma l)}$

For lossless lines, $\tanh(\gamma l) = \tanh(j\beta l) = j \tan(\beta l)$.
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta l)}$

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The input impedance is frequency-dependent due to the $\beta l$ term (which depends on frequency).
*   The input impedance is periodic with respect to the electrical length $\beta l$.
*   **Matching:** If $Z_L = Z_0$, then $Z_{in} = Z_0$. This is the condition for perfect matching, where there is no reflection.
*   **Open Circuit:** If $Z_L = \infty$, then $Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{1}{j \tan(\beta l)}$.
*   **Short Circuit:** If $Z_L = 0$, then $Z_{in} = j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)$.

**From Textbooks:**

*   **Hayt & Buck (9th ed.):** Chapter 6, "The Line as a Circuit Element," provides a thorough derivation of the input impedance for both lossy and lossless lines, including the simplified forms using tangent.
*   **Sadiku (7th ed.):** Chapter 10 on Transmission Lines details the derivation of input impedance for lossless lines, emphasizing the role of the characteristic impedance and load.
*   **Reddy (1st ed.):** Chapter 4, "Transmission Line Matching," discusses the input impedance and its application in impedance matching techniques, particularly for lossless lines.
*   **Basu (1st ed.):** Chapter 5, "Transmission Lines," offers clear explanations and derivations of the input impedance, often using a step-by-step approach.

### 5. Special Cases and Examples

**Case 1: Lossless Line, Matched Load ($Z_L = Z_0$)**

If $Z_L = Z_0$, then:
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_0 + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)} = Z_0$

**Example:** A lossless 50 $\Omega$ transmission line is terminated in a 50 $\Omega$ load. What is the input impedance?
**Answer:** $Z_{in} = 50 \, \Omega$.

**Case 2: Lossless Line, Open Circuit ($Z_L = \infty$)**

$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{\infty + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j \infty \tan(\beta l)} = Z_0 \frac{1}{j \tan(\beta l)} = -j Z_0 \cot(\beta l)$

This represents a purely reactive impedance, behaving like a series combination of an inductor (if $\cot(\beta l) > 0$) or a capacitor (if $\cot(\beta l) < 0$).

**Example:** A lossless 75 $\Omega$ transmission line of length $l$ is open-circuited at the end. If $\beta l = \pi/4$, what is the input impedance?
$Z_{in} = -j 75 \cot(\pi/4) = -j 75 (1) = -j 75 \, \Omega$. This behaves like a capacitor.

**Case 3: Lossless Line, Short Circuit ($Z_L = 0$)**

$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{0 + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j 0 \tan(\beta l)} = Z_0 \frac{j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0} = j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)$

This represents a purely reactive impedance, behaving like a series combination of an inductor (if $\tan(\beta l) > 0$) or a capacitor (if $\tan(\beta l) < 0$).

**Example:** A lossless 50 $\Omega$ transmission line of length $l$ is short-circuited at the end. If $\beta l = \pi/3$, what is the input impedance?
$Z_{in} = j 50 \tan(\pi/3) = j 50 (\sqrt{3}) = j 86.6 \, \Omega$. This behaves like an inductor.

**Case 4: Quarter-Wave Transformer (Lossless Line)**

A quarter-wave transformer has a length $l = \lambda/4$, where $\lambda$ is the wavelength. For a lossless line, $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda$.
So, $\beta l = (2\pi/\lambda)(\lambda/4) = \pi/2$.

If $Z_L = 0$ (short circuit), then $Z_{in} = j Z_0 \tan(\pi/2) = j Z_0 (\infty) = \infty$ (open circuit).
If $Z_L = \infty$ (open circuit), then $Z_{in} = -j Z_0 \cot(\pi/2) = -j Z_0 (0) = 0$ (short circuit).

This behavior can be used for impedance transformation. Consider a lossless transmission line of length $l=\lambda/4$ with characteristic impedance $Z_0$ connecting a load $Z_L$ to a source.
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta l)}$

For $l=\lambda/4$, $\beta l = \pi/2$, and $\tan(\beta l) \to \infty$.
$Z_{in} \approx Z_0 \frac{j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{j Z_L \tan(\beta l)} = \frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L}$

This means a quarter-wave line can transform an impedance $Z_L$ to $Z_0^2/Z_L$. If we choose $Z_0 = \sqrt{Z_s Z_L}$ (where $Z_s$ is the source impedance), then the line transforms $Z_L$ to $Z_s$, achieving matching.

**Example:** You want to match a load $Z_L = 25 \, \Omega$ to a source with impedance $Z_s = 100 \, \Omega$ using a quarter-wave transformer. What should be the characteristic impedance ($Z_0$) of the transformer?
**Answer:** $Z_0 = \sqrt{Z_s Z_L} = \sqrt{100 \times 25} = \sqrt{2500} = 50 \, \Omega$.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A lossless transmission line with characteristic impedance $Z_0 = 50 \, \Omega$ has a length of $l = 0.25 \lambda$ (where $\lambda$ is the wavelength). If the line is terminated with a load $Z_L = 100 + j50 \, \Omega$, calculate the input impedance $Z_{in}$ at the source end.
*(Hint: Use $Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta l)}$ and $\beta l = \pi/2$ for $l = \lambda/4$.)*

**Answer 1:**
For $l = \lambda/4$, $\beta l = \pi/2$, so $\tan(\beta l) \to \infty$.
The formula $Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta l)}$ can be simplified by dividing the numerator and denominator by $\tan(\beta l)$:
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L/\tan(\beta l) + j Z_0}{Z_0/\tan(\beta l) + j Z_L}$
As $\tan(\beta l) \to \infty$, $1/\tan(\beta l) \to 0$.
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{0 + j Z_0}{0 + j Z_L} = Z_0 \frac{j Z_0}{j Z_L} = \frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L}$
Given $Z_0 = 50 \, \Omega$ and $Z_L = 100 + j50 \, \Omega$:
$Z_{in} = \frac{(50)^2}{100 + j50} = \frac{2500}{100 + j50} = \frac{2500}{50(2 + j1)} = \frac{50}{2 + j1}$
To simplify, multiply by the conjugate of the denominator:
$Z_{in} = \frac{50}{2 + j1} \times \frac{2 - j1}{2 - j1} = \frac{50(2 - j1)}{4 - (j)^2} = \frac{50(2 - j1)}{4 + 1} = \frac{50(2 - j1)}{5} = 10(2 - j1) = 20 - j10 \, \Omega$.

**Question 2:**
A lossless transmission line with $Z_0 = 100 \, \Omega$ and length $l$ is terminated in a short circuit ($Z_L = 0$). If the input impedance is $Z_{in} = j 100 \, \Omega$, what is the electrical length $\beta l$ of the line?

**Answer 2:**
For a short-circuited lossless line, $Z_{in} = j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)$.
Given $Z_{in} = j 100 \, \Omega$ and $Z_0 = 100 \, \Omega$:
$j 100 = j 100 \tan(\beta l)$
$1 = \tan(\beta l)$
The principal value for $\beta l$ is $\pi/4$ radians. Other possible values are $\pi/4 + n\pi$, where $n$ is an integer.

**Question 3:**
Derive the input impedance of a lossless transmission line terminated in an arbitrary load $Z_L$. Express your answer in terms of $Z_0$, $Z_L$, and $\beta l$.

**Answer 3:**
The derivation is as presented in Section 4 of these notes, leading to:
$Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta l)}$

### 8. Summary and Key Takeaways

*   Transmission lines possess distributed inductance (L) and capacitance (C) per unit length.
*   The behavior of voltage and current along a transmission line is described by the Telegrapher's Equations.
*   The **characteristic impedance** ($Z_0$) is a fundamental property of the line, representing the impedance to traveling waves and given by $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}$ for lossless lines.
*   The **input impedance** ($Z_{in}$) of a transmission line is the impedance seen at the source end and depends on $Z_0$, the line length ($l$), and the load impedance ($Z_L$).
*   The general formula for the input impedance of a lossless line is $Z_{in} = Z_0 \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta l)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta l)}$.
*   Special cases like matched load ($Z_L = Z_0$), open circuit ($Z_L = \infty$), and short circuit ($Z_L = 0$) simplify the input impedance expression.
*   The quarter-wave transformer utilizes the properties of transmission lines with $l = \lambda/4$ to transform impedance according to the relation $Z_{in} = Z_0^2 / Z_L$.
*   Understanding input impedance is crucial for impedance matching, power transfer, and analyzing wave reflection phenomena on transmission lines.

This comprehensive study of input impedance lays the groundwork for more advanced topics in transmission line analysis and application.
