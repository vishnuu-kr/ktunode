# ANALOG AND DIGITAL COMMUNICATION - Module 1: Block Diagram of a Communication System

## Topic: Noise: External, Internal, White Noise

### 1. Introduction to Noise in Communication Systems

Noise is an unwanted disturbance that interferes with the desired signal in a communication system. It degrades the quality of the received signal, leading to errors or loss of information. Understanding the sources and characteristics of noise is crucial for designing effective communication systems.

**Key Concept:** Noise is any signal that is not part of the intended transmitted message.

**Importance:** Noise is a fundamental limitation in communication systems. Minimizing its impact is a primary goal in system design.

**Learning Outcome Covered:** This section introduces the fundamental concept of noise, which is essential for understanding the overall operation of a communication system, contributing to CO1 and CO2.

### 2. Sources of Noise

Noise can originate from various sources, broadly categorized into external and internal noise.

#### 2.1. External Noise

External noise is generated outside the communication system itself.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Atmospheric Noise:** Caused by natural electrical disturbances in the Earth's atmosphere, such as lightning discharges.
    *   **Characteristics:** Varies with time of day, season, and geographic location. More prominent at lower frequencies.
    *   **Example:** Static or crackling sounds heard on AM radio during thunderstorms.
    *   **(Reference: Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 2)**
*   **Extraterrestrial Noise:** Originates from sources outside the Earth's atmosphere.
    *   **Solar Noise:** Radiation from the Sun. Intensity varies with solar activity (e.g., sunspots).
        *   **Example:** Interference on satellite communication systems during periods of high solar activity.
    *   **Cosmic Noise:** Radiation from stars and other celestial bodies. Generally low-level but can be significant for deep-space communication.
        *   **(Reference: Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2)**
*   **Man-Made Noise (Industrial Noise):** Generated by man-made electrical and electronic devices.
    *   **Sources:** Ignition systems of internal combustion engines, electric motors, switching power supplies, fluorescent lights, digital devices, etc.
    *   **Characteristics:** Often impulsive and broadband. Its intensity depends on the proximity and operation of the noise source.
    *   **Example:** Static from a car's ignition system interfering with radio reception, or buzzing from electrical equipment.
    *   **(Reference: Kennedy, 6th Ed., Chapter 3)**

#### 2.2. Internal Noise

Internal noise is generated within the communication system itself.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Thermal Noise (Johnson-Nyquist Noise):** Caused by the random thermal agitation of charge carriers (electrons) within conductors and semiconductors.
    *   **Characteristics:** Present in all electronic components with resistance, regardless of whether a voltage is applied. It is uniformly distributed over a wide range of frequencies, hence the term "white noise."
    *   **Power Spectral Density:** $N_0/2$ Watts/Hz.
    *   **Total Noise Power:** $P_n = kTB$, where:
        *   $k$ is Boltzmann's constant ($1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K).
        *   $T$ is the absolute temperature of the conductor in Kelvin.
        *   $B$ is the bandwidth of the system in Hertz.
    *   **Example:** The faint hiss heard when no signal is present on an audio amplifier or radio receiver.
    *   **(Reference: Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2)**
*   **Shot Noise:** Caused by the discrete nature of electric charge. It arises from the random arrival of charge carriers (electrons or holes) at a junction (e.g., in a transistor or diode).
    *   **Characteristics:** Occurs in active devices like transistors and diodes. Its intensity is proportional to the DC current flowing through the device.
    *   **Example:** Random fluctuations in the output current of a photodiode when illuminated.
    *   **(Reference: Taub & Schilling, 4th Ed., Chapter 11)**
*   **Flicker Noise (1/f Noise):** Occurs primarily in semiconductor devices, particularly at low frequencies. Its power spectral density is inversely proportional to frequency.
    *   **Characteristics:** More dominant at lower frequencies. Its physical origin is not as well understood as thermal noise or shot noise.
    *   **Example:** Can cause drift in DC amplifiers or affect low-frequency signals.
    *   **(Reference: Proakis & Salehi, 6th Ed., Chapter 1)**
*   **Transit-Time Noise:** Occurs in active devices when the time taken by charge carriers to traverse the device becomes comparable to the period of the signal.
    *   **Characteristics:** Significant in high-frequency devices like vacuum tubes and transistors operating at microwave frequencies.
    *   **(Reference: Kennedy, 6th Ed., Chapter 3)**

### 3. White Noise

White noise is a fundamental concept in signal processing and communications, characterized by its uniform power spectral density.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Definition:** A random process whose power spectral density is constant for all frequencies. It has zero autocorrelation for non-zero time lags.
*   **Mathematical Representation:** The power spectral density (PSD) of white noise, denoted by $S_n(f)$, is constant:
    $S_n(f) = N_0/2$ for all frequencies $f$, where $N_0/2$ is the two-sided power spectral density in Watts/Hz.
*   **Properties:**
    *   **Flat Power Spectrum:** The power is equally distributed across all frequencies.
    *   **Zero Autocorrelation (for $\tau \neq 0$):** $R_n(\tau) = E[n(t)n(t+\tau)] = \delta(\tau) \times (\text{Power})$, where $\delta(\tau)$ is the Dirac delta function. This means that the value of the noise at one instant is uncorrelated with its value at any other instant.
    *   **Gaussian Distribution:** In many practical scenarios, the amplitude distribution of white noise is Gaussian (normal). This leads to the term **Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN)**, which is a widely used model for noise in communication systems.
*   **Origin:** While ideally infinite in bandwidth, in practice, white noise refers to noise that is uniform over the bandwidth of interest of the system. Thermal noise is the most common physical manifestation of white noise.
*   **Impact on Signals:** When white noise is added to a signal, it affects all frequency components of the signal equally. This can lead to distortion and an increase in the bit error rate (BER) in digital communication systems.

**Mathematical Derivation (Optional, but good for understanding):**
The power spectral density $S_n(f)$ is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function $R_n(\tau)$:
$S_n(f) = \mathcal{F}\{R_n(\tau)\}$

For white noise with autocorrelation $R_n(\tau) = \frac{N_0}{2} \delta(\tau)$, its Fourier transform is:
$S_n(f) = \mathcal{F}\{\frac{N_0}{2} \delta(\tau)\} = \frac{N_0}{2}$

The total average power of the noise over a bandwidth $B$ (from $-B/2$ to $B/2$) is given by:
$P_n = \int_{-B/2}^{B/2} S_n(f) df = \int_{-B/2}^{B/2} \frac{N_0}{2} df = \frac{N_0}{2} [f]_{-B/2}^{B/2} = \frac{N_0}{2} (B/2 - (-B/2)) = \frac{N_0 B}{2}$.
*Note: Often, $N_0$ is defined as the total power in a $1 \text{ Hz}$ bandwidth (e.g., $-1/2$ to $1/2$). If $N_0$ represents the two-sided power spectral density, the integral is from $-B/2$ to $B/2$. If $N_0$ is one-sided (for $f \ge 0$), the integral is from 0 to $B$, and $S_n(f) = N_0$. Let's stick to the common two-sided definition of $N_0/2$.*

**Example:** The output of a resistor at a non-zero temperature is a source of thermal noise, which is often modeled as white noise.

**Importance in Digital Communications:**
*   **AWGN Channel Model:** The Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) channel is a fundamental model for analyzing the performance of digital communication systems.
*   **Performance Metrics:** The presence of AWGN directly impacts the Bit Error Rate (BER) of a digital receiver. Higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) leads to lower BER.
*   **(Reference: Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Proakis & Salehi, 6th Ed., Chapter 1)**

### 4. Noise in the Communication System Block Diagram

Noise can enter a communication system at various points:

*   **At the Transmitter:** Noise picked up by the antenna or generated within the transmitter circuitry can affect the transmitted signal.
*   **During Transmission:** External noise sources (atmospheric, man-made) can corrupt the signal as it propagates through the channel (e.g., free space, coaxial cable).
*   **At the Receiver:** Noise generated within the receiver circuitry (thermal noise in resistors, shot noise in active components) is often the most significant contributor to signal degradation. The first few stages of the receiver, especially the RF amplifier and mixer, are critical as they operate on low-level signals, making them more susceptible to noise.

**Impact on different blocks:**
*   **Modulator:** If noise is present before or during modulation, it can distort the modulated carrier.
*   **Channel:** The channel itself is a primary source of external noise.
*   **Demodulator:** Noise corrupts the received signal, making it difficult for the demodulator to correctly extract the original message.
*   **Detector/Decoder:** Noise at the output of the demodulator can lead to misinterpretation of the received data.

**Key Concept:** Noise added at different stages has varying impacts. Minimizing noise in the early stages of the receiver is crucial.

**Example:** Consider a radio receiver. Noise entering the RF amplifier stage will be amplified along with the desired signal, significantly reducing the SNR at the output of the amplifier.

**(Reference: Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2)**

### 5. Noise Figure and Noise Temperature

These are parameters used to quantify the noise performance of a system or component, particularly receivers.

#### 5.1. Noise Figure (NF)

**Definition:** The noise figure of a two-port network (like an amplifier) is the ratio of the total output noise power to the noise power that would be present if the network were noiseless, referred to the input. It essentially quantifies how much the noise is increased by the component.

**Formula:**
$NF = \frac{\text{Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) at Input}}{\text{Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) at Output}} = \frac{SNR_{in}}{SNR_{out}}$

Alternatively, in terms of noise power:
$NF = \frac{P_{n,out}}{G \cdot P_{n,in\_ideal}}$, where:
*   $P_{n,out}$ is the total noise power at the output.
*   $G$ is the power gain of the component.
*   $P_{n,in\_ideal}$ is the noise power at the input assuming a noiseless component, which is $kTB$ where $B$ is the bandwidth.

**Characteristics:**
*   Always greater than or equal to 1 (or 0 dB).
*   A lower noise figure indicates better performance.
*   For a passive component (like a resistor), NF = Loss (where Loss is the reciprocal of gain, e.g., for a $3$ dB attenuator, Loss = 2).
*   For an active component like an amplifier, NF > 1.

**Cascaded Noise Figure:** For a system with multiple stages, the overall noise figure is determined by the noise figures and gains of individual stages. The Friis formula for noise figure is used:
$NF_{total} = NF_1 + \frac{NF_2 - 1}{G_1} + \frac{NF_3 - 1}{G_1 G_2} + \dots$
where $NF_i$ and $G_i$ are the noise figure and gain of the $i$-th stage, respectively. This formula highlights the importance of having a low-noise first stage with high gain.

**(Reference: Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Kennedy, 6th Ed., Chapter 3)**

#### 5.2. Noise Temperature ($T_e$)

**Definition:** The noise temperature of a component or system is the temperature of an equivalent noiseless resistor that would produce the same amount of noise power as the component. It's an alternative way to express noise, particularly useful in radio astronomy and satellite communications.

**Formula:**
$NF = 1 + \frac{T_e}{T_0}$, where:
*   $T_e$ is the effective noise temperature of the component (in Kelvin).
*   $T_0$ is the standard reference temperature, usually $290$ K.

**Relationship:** From the formula, $T_e = T_0(NF - 1)$.

**Advantages:**
*   Noise temperature can be added linearly for cascaded systems: $T_{e,total} = T_{e1} + T_{e2}/G_1 + T_{e3}/(G_1G_2) + \dots$ This is a simpler form of the Friis formula when expressed in terms of temperature.

**(Reference: Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Kennedy, 6th Ed., Chapter 3)**

### 6. Impact of Noise on Communication Systems

Noise fundamentally limits the performance of any communication system.

*   **Analog Communication:**
    *   **Degradation of Quality:** Noise manifests as static, hiss, or distortion in audio and video signals.
    *   **Reduced SNR:** The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at the output of the receiver is a measure of signal quality. Noise reduces the SNR.
    *   **Threshold Effect:** For certain modulation schemes (like FM and PM), there's a threshold SNR below which the noise suddenly becomes dominant and the output quality degrades drastically.
    *   **(Reference: Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 2)**

*   **Digital Communication:**
    *   **Bit Errors:** Noise can cause the receiver to misinterpret a transmitted bit (e.g., a '0' is received as a '1' or vice versa).
    *   **Bit Error Rate (BER):** The probability of a bit error occurring is a key performance metric. Higher noise levels lead to higher BER.
    *   **Impact on Modulation Schemes:** Different digital modulation schemes have varying sensitivities to noise. For example, M-ary Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM) and Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) are more susceptible to noise than Frequency Shift Keying (FSK) at the same bandwidth efficiency.
    *   **Decision Thresholds:** In digital receivers, noise can cause the received signal to cross the decision thresholds incorrectly, leading to bit errors.
    *   **(Reference: Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Proakis & Salehi, 6th Ed., Chapter 1)**

### 7. Noise Reduction Techniques

While noise cannot be eliminated entirely, several techniques are employed to mitigate its effects.

*   **Increase Transmitted Power:** A higher transmitted power increases the SNR at the receiver, making the signal more robust against noise. However, this can lead to increased power consumption and regulatory issues.
*   **Improve Receiver Sensitivity:** Using low-noise amplifiers (LNAs) in the first stages of the receiver is crucial.
*   **Bandwidth Limiting:** Filtering the received signal to the minimum necessary bandwidth can reduce the amount of noise power entering the system. This is a trade-off, as excessive bandwidth reduction can distort the signal.
    *   **Example:** Using a matched filter in a digital receiver to maximize the SNR at the sampling instant.
*   **Error Control Coding:** In digital communication, error control codes (like block codes and convolutional codes) add redundancy to the transmitted data, allowing the receiver to detect and correct errors caused by noise.
    *   **Example:** Parity checks, Hamming codes, Viterbi decoding for convolutional codes.
*   **Modulation Techniques:** Choosing modulation schemes that are inherently more robust to noise (e.g., FM for analog, FSK for digital) can improve performance.
*   **Diversity Techniques:** Transmitting the same information over multiple independent channels or paths (e.g., space diversity, frequency diversity) can significantly reduce the probability of errors caused by fading or interference, which often act like noise.

**(Reference: Haykin & Moher, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Lathi & Ding, 5th Ed., Chapter 2; Proakis & Salehi, 6th Ed., Chapter 1)**

### 8. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
What is the primary cause of thermal noise in a communication system?
a) Random arrival of charge carriers in semiconductors
b) Electrical disturbances in the atmosphere
c) Random thermal agitation of charge carriers in conductors
d) High-frequency oscillations in active devices

**Answer:** c) Random thermal agitation of charge carriers in conductors

**Question 2:**
A communication channel has a bandwidth of 5 MHz and a noise spectral density of $N_0/2 = 10^{-12}$ W/Hz. Calculate the total noise power in Watts.
a) $2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ W
b) $5 \times 10^{-6}$ W
c) $10 \times 10^{-6}$ W
d) $2.5 \times 10^{-5}$ W

**Solution:**
Noise power $P_n = kTB$. However, the question gives noise spectral density $N_0/2$.
The total noise power in a bandwidth $B$ is given by $P_n = N_0 \times B$ (assuming $N_0$ is the total noise power in a $1 \text{ Hz}$ bandwidth).
In the context of PSD $S_n(f) = N_0/2$, the power over bandwidth $B$ (from $-B/2$ to $B/2$) is $\int_{-B/2}^{B/2} (N_0/2) df = N_0 B$.
Here, $B = 5 \text{ MHz} = 5 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz}$.
And $N_0/2 = 10^{-12}$ W/Hz.
Therefore, $N_0 = 2 \times 10^{-12}$ W/Hz.
$P_n = N_0 \times B = (2 \times 10^{-12} \text{ W/Hz}) \times (5 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz}) = 10 \times 10^{-6}$ W.

**Answer:** c) $10 \times 10^{-6}$ W

**Question 3:**
Define White Noise. What are its key characteristics?

**Answer:**
White noise is a random process whose power spectral density is constant across all frequencies. Its key characteristics are:
1.  **Flat Power Spectrum:** The power is uniformly distributed over all frequencies.
2.  **Zero Autocorrelation (for $\tau \neq 0$):** Its value at any instant is uncorrelated with its value at any other instant.
3.  **Often Gaussian:** In many practical systems, the amplitude distribution of white noise is Gaussian, leading to the concept of Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN).

**Question 4:**
Explain the difference between external and internal noise sources in a communication system. Provide an example for each.

**Answer:**
*   **External Noise:** Noise originating from sources outside the communication system.
    *   **Example:** Atmospheric noise caused by lightning discharges.
*   **Internal Noise:** Noise generated within the electronic components of the communication system.
    *   **Example:** Thermal noise generated by resistors within the receiver.

**Question 5:**
What is Noise Figure? Why is it important to minimize the noise figure of the initial stages of a receiver?

**Answer:**
Noise Figure (NF) quantifies how much a component or system degrades the signal-to-noise ratio. It's the ratio of input SNR to output SNR. It is crucial to minimize the noise figure of the initial stages of a receiver because any noise introduced in these early stages (which operate on weak signals) gets amplified by subsequent amplifier stages, leading to a significantly reduced overall SNR at the output. According to the Friis formula, the noise figure of the first stage has the most dominant impact on the total noise figure of a cascaded system.

### 9. Important Points to Remember

*   **Noise is unavoidable:** It's a fundamental limitation in all communication systems.
*   **Categorization:** Noise sources are broadly classified into external and internal.
*   **Thermal Noise:** A major source of internal noise, often modeled as white noise. Its power is proportional to bandwidth and temperature.
*   **White Noise:** Characterized by a flat power spectral density and zero autocorrelation for non-zero time lags.
*   **AWGN:** The Additive White Gaussian Noise channel model is a fundamental tool for analyzing digital communication performance.
*   **SNR:** Signal-to-Noise Ratio is a critical measure of signal quality. Noise degrades SNR.
*   **Receiver Design:** Minimizing noise in the early stages of the receiver (using LNAs) is paramount.
*   **Noise Figure:** A standard metric for quantifying noise in components.
*   **Mitigation:** Techniques like increasing power, filtering, coding, and robust modulation schemes are used to combat noise.
