## Introduction

While this module focuses on the cardiovascular system, understanding the nervous system is crucial because it exerts significant control over heart rate, blood pressure, and vascular tone. Moreover, the principles of bioelectric signal generation and recording are fundamental to both cardiovascular and neurological diagnostics. This topic will delve into the basic units of the nervous system, how electrical activity is generated and measured, and their applications in understanding brain function and detecting abnormalities.

## 1. Neurons: The Basic Units of the Nervous System

Neurons are specialized cells that transmit information through electrical and chemical signals.

### 1.1 Structure of a Neuron

*   **Cell Body (Soma):** Contains the nucleus and organelles.
*   **Dendrites:** Branched extensions that receive signals from other neurons.
*   **Axon:** A long projection that transmits signals away from the cell body to other neurons, muscles, or glands.
*   **Axon Hillock:** The region where the axon originates from the cell body; a key site for action potential initiation.
*   **Myelin Sheath:** An insulating layer around the axon (formed by glial cells like Schwann cells and oligodendrocytes) that speeds up signal transmission.
*   **Nodes of Ranvier:** Gaps in the myelin sheath where the action potential is regenerated.
*   **Synapse:** The junction between two neurons where information is transmitted, typically via neurotransmitters.

### 1.2 Resting Membrane Potential

*   **Definition:** The electrical potential difference across the plasma membrane of a neuron when it is not actively transmitting a signal.
*   **Typical Value:** Around -70 millivolts (mV), with the inside of the cell being negative relative to the outside.
*   **Mechanisms:**
    *   **Sodium-Potassium Pump:** Actively transports 3 sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell for every 2 potassium ions (K⁺) pumped in, maintaining concentration gradients.
    *   **Selective Permeability of the Membrane:** The membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ due to the presence of leak channels, allowing K⁺ to diffuse out of the cell down its concentration gradient.
*   **Implication (CO1):** The resting membrane potential is the baseline electrical state of excitable cells, including neurons and cardiac muscle cells. Deviations from this state are indicative of cellular activity and potential pathologies.

## 2. Action Potential of the Brain (Neural Excitation)

The action potential is a rapid, transient change in the membrane potential of a neuron that allows it to transmit signals. While the "action potential of the brain" is a simplification, it refers to the fundamental process of neuronal electrical signaling.

### 2.1 Phases of an Action Potential

1.  **Resting State:** Membrane potential is at resting potential (-70 mV).
2.  **Depolarization:** Stimulus causes influx of Na⁺ ions, making the inside of the cell less negative, approaching zero and becoming positive (e.g., to +30 mV). This is due to the opening of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
3.  **Repolarization:** Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels inactivate, and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, allowing K⁺ ions to exit the cell, making the inside negative again.
4.  **Hyperpolarization:** The membrane potential briefly becomes more negative than the resting potential due to the slow closing of voltage-gated K⁺ channels.
5.  **Return to Resting State:** Ion gradients are restored by the sodium-potassium pump.

### 2.2 All-or-None Principle

*   An action potential is generated only when the stimulus reaches a certain threshold (typically -55 mV).
*   If the threshold is reached, the action potential is generated with a consistent amplitude and duration, regardless of the strength of the stimulus.
*   Stronger stimuli cause an increase in the *frequency* of action potentials, not their amplitude.

### 2.3 Propagation of Action Potentials

*   **Unmyelinated Axons:** The action potential propagates in a continuous wave along the axon membrane.
*   **Myelinated Axons (Saltatory Conduction):** The action potential jumps from one Node of Ranvier to the next, significantly increasing the speed of transmission. This is a crucial efficiency mechanism in the nervous system.

### 2.4 Relevance to Cardiovascular System

*   **CO1:** The principles of action potential generation (depolarization, repolarization, ion channels) are directly applicable to the electrophysiology of the heart muscle cells, forming the basis of electrocardiography (ECG).
*   **CO2:** Understanding how electrical signals are generated and propagate in excitable tissues is fundamental to diagnosing cardiac abnormalities.

## 3. Brain Waves

Brain waves are rhythmic patterns of electrical activity in the brain, measured by Electroencephalography (EEG). They reflect the synchronized activity of large populations of neurons.

### 3.1 Types of Brain Waves

*   **Delta (δ) Waves:**
    *   Frequency: 0.5-4 Hz
    *   Amplitude: High
    *   Associated with: Deep sleep, coma, brain damage.
*   **Theta (θ) Waves:**
    *   Frequency: 4-8 Hz
    *   Amplitude: Moderate
    *   Associated with: Drowsiness, light sleep, meditation, early stages of learning.
*   **Alpha (α) Waves:**
    *   Frequency: 8-13 Hz
    *   Amplitude: Moderate
    *   Associated with: Relaxed wakefulness, particularly when eyes are closed, prominent over the occipital lobe.
*   **Beta (β) Waves:**
    *   Frequency: 13-30 Hz
    *   Amplitude: Low
    *   Associated with: Active thinking, concentration, alertness, motor activity.
*   **Gamma (γ) Waves:**
    *   Frequency: > 30 Hz
    *   Amplitude: Very low
    *   Associated with: Higher cognitive functions, learning, memory, sensory processing.

### 3.2 Factors Affecting Brain Waves

*   **State of Consciousness:** Sleep, wakefulness, arousal.
*   **Cognitive Activity:** Thinking, problem-solving, attention.
*   **Sensory Input:** Visual, auditory, somatosensory stimulation.
*   **Brain Pathology:** Tumors, epilepsy, stroke, degenerative diseases.

### 3.3 Significance in Diagnostics (CO1, CO3)

*   **Epilepsy:** Abnormal, synchronized bursts of electrical activity (epileptiform discharges) are detected.
*   **Sleep Disorders:** Characteristic patterns of brain waves are used to diagnose different sleep stages and disorders.
*   **Brain Death:** Absence of electrical activity.
*   **Cognitive Impairment:** Altered brain wave patterns can indicate various neurological conditions.
*   **Monitoring Anesthesia:** Brain wave patterns change with depth of anesthesia.

## 4. Placement of Electrodes for EEG Recording

The accurate placement of electrodes on the scalp is crucial for obtaining meaningful EEG data. The International 10-20 System is the most widely used standard.

### 4.1 The International 10-20 System

*   **Principle:** Based on the relative positions of the skull landmarks and the percentage of the distance between them. This ensures reproducible placement across individuals and studies.
*   **Key Landmarks:**
    *   **Cz (Central):** Vertex, top of the head.
    *   **Fpz (Frontopolar):** Midpoint between the nasion (indentation at the root of the nose) and the bregma (junction of the sagittal and coronal sutures).
    *   **Nz (Nasion):** Indentation at the root of the nose.
    *   **Oz (Occipital):** Inion (prominence at the base of the skull).
    *   **T3, T4, T5, T6 (Temporal):** Positions along the temporal line.
    *   **Pz (Parietal):** Midpoint between Cz and the imaginary line connecting T5 and T6.
    *   **Fp1, Fp2 (Frontopolar):** Positions to the left and right of Fpz.
    *   **F3, F4, F7, F8 (Frontal):** Positions in the frontal lobe.
    *   **C3, C4 (Central):** Positions to the left and right of Cz.
    *   **P3, P4, Pz (Parietal):** Positions in the parietal lobe.
    *   **O1, O2 (Occipital):** Positions to the left and right of Oz.
*   **Electrode Labeling:**
    *   Letters indicate the brain region (F=Frontal, C=Central, P=Parietal, O=Occipital, T=Temporal).
    *   Numbers indicate the hemisphere (odd numbers for left, even numbers for right).
    *   "z" indicates midline (e.g., Cz, Pz).
*   **Number of Electrodes:** Typically 19-21 standard electrodes are used, but more can be added for specific diagnostic purposes.

### 4.2 Electrode Mounting

*   **Method:** Electrodes are typically attached to the scalp using a conductive paste or gel to ensure good electrical contact. They can be mounted on caps or individually placed.
*   **Importance of Good Contact:** Poor contact increases impedance, leading to noisy recordings and inaccurate data.

## 5. EEG Recording (Electroencephalography)

EEG is a non-invasive neurophysiological technique used to record the electrical activity of the brain.

### 5.1 Principle of EEG Recording

*   **Bioelectric Potential Generation:** Large populations of neurons firing synchronously produce small electrical potentials on the scalp.
*   **Measurement:** Electrodes placed on the scalp detect these potential differences.
*   **Amplification:** The detected potentials are very small (tens of microvolts, µV) and need to be amplified significantly by a differential amplifier.
*   **Filtering:** The amplified signal is filtered to remove unwanted artifacts (e.g., muscle activity, eye movements, electrical interference).
*   **Recording and Display:** The processed signal is recorded and displayed as a waveform (brain waves) on a monitor or paper.

### 5.2 Instrumentation for EEG

*   **Electrodes:** Conductive metal discs (e.g., Ag/AgCl) or cups.
*   **Amplifier:** High-gain, low-noise differential amplifiers are essential.
    *   **Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR):** High CMRR is critical to reject common noise sources affecting both inputs.
    *   **Input Impedance:** High input impedance is needed to prevent loading of the scalp potentials.
*   **Filters:**
    *   **High-Pass Filter:** Removes slow drift artifacts (e.g., from electrode movement).
    *   **Low-Pass Filter:** Removes high-frequency noise (e.g., from muscle activity).
    *   **Notch Filter:** Removes mains hum (50 or 60 Hz).
*   **Recording System:** Digital acquisition systems with analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) for computer processing and storage.

### 5.3 Artifacts in EEG

*   **Physiological Artifacts:**
    *   **Eye Blinks and Eye Movements:** Large deflections, especially in frontal leads.
    *   **Muscle Activity (EMG):** High-frequency, low-amplitude activity, particularly from scalp muscles.
    *   **Heartbeat (ECG):** Can be seen as periodic artifacts if electrodes are close to the head.
*   **Technical Artifacts:**
    *   **Electrode Movement:** Causes sudden amplitude changes.
    *   **Electrical Interference:** Hum from power lines or equipment.

### 5.4 Applications of EEG

*   **Diagnosis of Epilepsy:** Detecting abnormal spike and wave discharges.
*   **Sleep Studies:** Identifying sleep stages and diagnosing sleep disorders (e.g., narcolepsy, sleep apnea).
*   **Encephalopathy:** Assessing diffuse brain dysfunction due to metabolic, toxic, or anoxic causes.
*   **Brain Death Determination:** Absence of EEG activity.
*   **Monitoring during Surgery/Anesthesia:** Assessing brain function.
*   **Brain Computer Interfaces (BCIs):** Using brain waves to control external devices.

### 5.5 Textbooks & Relevance

*   **Khandpur (Handbook of Biomedical Instrumentation):** Likely covers principles of amplification, filtering, and instrumentation relevant to bio-signal acquisition like EEG.
*   **Cromwell, Weibell, Pfeiffer (Biomedical Instrumentation and Measurement):** Provides foundational knowledge on measurement techniques and instrumentation for biological signals, including bioelectric potentials.
*   **CO4:** Understanding EEG recording directly illustrates the principle and working of a key biomedical equipment/device used for neurological assessment.

## 6. Evoked Potential (EP) / Event-Related Potentials (ERPs)

Evoked potentials are small electrical potentials that are time-locked to specific sensory stimuli or cognitive events. They are typically measured by averaging EEG signals over many trials.

### 6.1 Principle of Evoked Potential Measurement

*   **Stimulus Presentation:** A specific sensory stimulus (visual, auditory, somatosensory) is presented to the subject.
*   **EEG Recording:** EEG is recorded continuously during stimulus presentation.
*   **Signal Averaging:** The EEG signals are time-locked to the stimulus onset. By averaging the EEG segments over many stimulus presentations, the background EEG (which is random and not time-locked) is reduced, and the small, consistent evoked response becomes visible.
    *   **Formula:** Average = (Signal1 + Signal2 + ... + SignalN) / N
*   **Amplification:** High-gain amplifiers are used, similar to EEG.
*   **Filtering:** Appropriate filters are applied.

### 6.2 Types of Evoked Potentials

1.  **Visual Evoked Potentials (VEPs):**
    *   **Stimulus:** Flashing lights, patterned visual stimuli.
    *   **Recording Site:** Occipital scalp electrodes (O1, O2, Oz).
    *   **Applications:** Diagnosing visual pathway disorders (e.g., optic neuritis, multiple sclerosis, amblyopia), assessing visual acuity.
2.  **Auditory Evoked Potentials (AEPs) / Brainstem Auditory Evoked Responses (BAERs):**
    *   **Stimulus:** Clicks or tone bursts delivered to the ears.
    *   **Recording Site:** Temporal and posterior scalp electrodes (e.g., Fz, Cz, Pz, T3, T4).
    *   **Applications:** Diagnosing hearing impairments, assessing auditory nerve and brainstem function, detecting acoustic neuromas, used in newborn hearing screening.
3.  **Somatosensory Evoked Potentials (SSEPs):**
    *   **Stimulus:** Electrical stimulation of peripheral nerves (e.g., median nerve at the wrist, tibial nerve at the ankle).
    *   **Recording Site:** Scalp electrodes over the somatosensory cortex (e.g., C3, C4), spinal cord electrodes.
    *   **Applications:** Diagnosing sensory pathway lesions, spinal cord injuries, evaluating brain function in coma, monitoring during surgery (e.g., spinal surgery, carotid endarterectomy).

### 6.3 Components of Evoked Potentials

*   **Exogenous Components (Far-field Potentials):** Reflect the sensory pathway's integrity and are not significantly influenced by cognitive factors. They occur earlier after the stimulus (e.g., BAERs, early VEPs).
*   **Endogenous Components (Event-Related Potentials - ERPs):** Reflect cognitive processing and are influenced by attention, expectation, and task demands. They occur later after the stimulus (e.g., P300 component).

### 6.4 Clinical Significance (CO1, CO3)

*   **Localization of Lesions:** By testing different sensory pathways, EPs can help pinpoint the location of neurological damage.
*   **Quantification of Functional Deficits:** The latency (time delay) and amplitude of EP components provide quantitative measures of pathway function.
*   **Monitoring Neurological Status:** EPs are invaluable for monitoring brain function during critical care or surgery.

*   **Webster (Medical Instrumentation application and design):** Likely provides detailed coverage of signal acquisition, processing, and applications of various biomedical instrumentation, including EP techniques.
*   **Carr (Introduction to Biomedical Equipment Technology):** Offers insights into the technical aspects of recording and processing bio-signals, which is central to EP measurement.
*   **Aston (Principle of Biomedical Instrumentation and Measurement):** Provides fundamental principles applicable to EP recording and analysis.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Neurons** are the fundamental units of the nervous system, communicating via electrical and chemical signals.
*   The **resting membrane potential** is the polarized state of a neuron, crucial for signal transmission.
*   **Action potentials** are rapid, all-or-none electrical impulses that propagate along axons.
*   **Brain waves** reflect synchronized neural activity and are categorized by frequency (Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, Gamma).
*   The **International 10-20 System** provides a standardized method for electrode placement in EEG.
*   **EEG** is a non-invasive technique to record brain's electrical activity, used in diagnosing epilepsy, sleep disorders, and other neurological conditions.
*   **Evoked Potentials (EPs)** are time-locked brain responses to stimuli, measured using signal averaging, and are crucial for assessing sensory pathway integrity (VEPs, AEPs, SSEPs).
*   Understanding the principles of **bioelectric potential generation, amplification, and filtering** is vital for both cardiovascular and neurological diagnostic instrumentation.
