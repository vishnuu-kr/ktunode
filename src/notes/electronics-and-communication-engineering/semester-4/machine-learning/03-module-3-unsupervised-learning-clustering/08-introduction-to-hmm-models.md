## Module 3: Unsupervised Learning - Clustering

### Topic: Introduction to Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)

This topic introduces Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), a powerful class of probabilistic graphical models that are particularly useful for modeling sequential data. While not strictly a clustering algorithm in the traditional sense (like K-Means), HMMs can be used to discover underlying patterns and states within sequential data, which can be interpreted as a form of temporal or sequential clustering.

This section aims to provide a foundational understanding of HMMs, laying the groundwork for their application in various unsupervised learning scenarios.

### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

#### 2.1 What is a Hidden Markov Model?

A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is a statistical model that describes a system that transitions between a finite number of states over time, where the states themselves are not directly observable (hence "hidden"). Instead, we observe a sequence of outputs that are probabilistically dependent on the hidden states.

**Key Components of an HMM:**

*   **States (Hidden States):** A finite set of unobservable states that the system can be in. Let's denote these states as $S = \{s_1, s_2, ..., s_N\}$, where $N$ is the number of states.
*   **Observations (Visible Symbols):** A finite set of observable symbols or data points that are generated by the system. Let's denote these as $O = \{o_1, o_2, ..., o_M\}$, where $M$ is the number of distinct observations.
*   **Transition Probabilities:** The probability of transitioning from one hidden state to another. This is represented by a transition probability matrix $\mathbf{A}$, where $a_{ij} = P(q_{t+1} = s_j | q_t = s_i)$ is the probability of moving from state $s_i$ at time $t$ to state $s_j$ at time $t+1$. The rows of $\mathbf{A}$ must sum to 1.
*   **Emission Probabilities (Output Probabilities):** The probability of observing a particular symbol given that the system is in a specific hidden state. This is represented by an emission probability matrix $\mathbf{B}$, where $b_{jk} = P(v_t = o_k | q_t = s_j)$ is the probability of emitting observation $o_k$ when the system is in state $s_j$ at time $t$. The rows of $\mathbf{B}$ must sum to 1.
*   **Initial State Probabilities:** The probability of the system starting in each hidden state. This is represented by a vector $\mathbf{\pi}$, where $\pi_i = P(q_1 = s_i)$ is the probability of the system being in state $s_i$ at the first time step. The elements of $\mathbf{\pi}$ must sum to 1.

**Mathematical Representation:**

An HMM can be formally defined by the set of parameters: $\lambda = (A, B, \pi)$.

#### 2.2 The Markov Property

The "Markov" in Hidden Markov Model refers to the **Markov property**. This property states that the probability of being in a particular state at time $t+1$ depends *only* on the state at time $t$, and not on any previous states or observations. Mathematically:

$P(q_{t+1} | q_t, q_{t-1}, ..., q_1) = P(q_{t+1} | q_t)$

Similarly, the probability of an observation at time $t$ depends *only* on the hidden state at time $t$:

$P(v_t | q_t, q_{t-1}, ..., q_1, v_{t-1}, ..., v_1) = P(v_t | q_t)$

**Reference:** Bishop (2006) provides a thorough mathematical treatment of these probabilistic dependencies in Chapter 13, "Graphical Models."

#### 2.3 Analogy: The Weather and Ice Cream Example

A common and intuitive example to understand HMMs is predicting whether someone will eat ice cream based on the weather, where the weather is the "hidden state" and ice cream consumption is the "observable."

*   **Hidden States (Weather):** Sunny, Rainy
*   **Observations (Ice Cream Consumption):** Eat Ice Cream, Don't Eat Ice Cream

**Parameters:**

*   **Transition Probabilities (A):**
    *   $P(\text{Sunny today} | \text{Sunny yesterday}) = 0.8$
    *   $P(\text{Rainy today} | \text{Sunny yesterday}) = 0.2$
    *   $P(\text{Sunny today} | \text{Rainy yesterday}) = 0.4$
    *   $P(\text{Rainy today} | \text{Rainy yesterday}) = 0.6$

*   **Emission Probabilities (B):**
    *   $P(\text{Eat Ice Cream} | \text{Sunny}) = 0.7$
    *   $P(\text{Don't Eat Ice Cream} | \text{Sunny}) = 0.3$
    *   $P(\text{Eat Ice Cream} | \text{Rainy}) = 0.3$
    *   $P(\text{Don't Eat Ice Cream} | \text{Rainy}) = 0.7$

*   **Initial State Probabilities ($\mathbf{\pi}$):**
    *   $P(\text{Sunny on Day 1}) = 0.6$
    *   $P(\text{Rainy on Day 1}) = 0.4$

**Scenario:** Suppose we observe the sequence of ice cream consumption: "Eat Ice Cream", "Don't Eat Ice Cream". We want to infer the most likely sequence of weather (hidden states) that produced this observation sequence.

#### 2.4 Relation to Clustering (Sequential Clustering)

While HMMs are not direct clustering algorithms like K-Means, they can be viewed as a form of **sequential clustering** or **pattern discovery in sequences**. The hidden states can be thought of as representing distinct "regimes" or "patterns" within the sequential data. For example:

*   In speech recognition, hidden states might represent phonemes or sub-phoneme units.
*   In biological sequences, hidden states might represent different functional regions of DNA.
*   In financial time series, hidden states could represent bull or bear markets.

By learning the parameters of an HMM from data, we are essentially learning to segment the sequence into these underlying hidden states, which can be seen as a clustering of segments of the sequence.

**Connection to Course Outcome CO4:** "Apply unsupervised learning techniques and understand reinforcement learning for complex problem-solving." HMMs are a key unsupervised learning technique for sequential data and can be used to model complex dynamic systems.

### 3. Fundamental Problems with HMMs

There are three fundamental problems that are typically addressed when working with HMMs:

#### 3.1 The Evaluation Problem (Forward Algorithm)

**Problem:** Given an HMM $\lambda$ and an observation sequence $O = (o_1, o_2, ..., o_T)$, what is the probability of this observation sequence occurring, i.e., $P(O | \lambda)$?

**Solution:** The **Forward Algorithm** is used to efficiently compute this probability. It works by iteratively calculating the probability of observing the first $t$ observations and ending in each possible hidden state.

**Intuition:** The forward algorithm uses dynamic programming. It calculates the probability of being in state $j$ at time $t$ having observed the sequence up to time $t$. This probability depends on the probabilities of being in any state $i$ at time $t-1$ and transitioning to state $j$, while emitting the observation $o_t$.

Let $\alpha_t(j) = P(o_1, o_2, ..., o_t, q_t = s_j | \lambda)$.

**Recurrence Relation:**
$\alpha_t(j) = \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_{t-1}(i) a_{ij} \right] b_{j}(o_t)$

**Base Case:**
$\alpha_1(j) = \pi_j b_j(o_1)$ for all $j = 1, ..., N$.

**Final Result:**
$P(O | \lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_T(j)$

**Reference:** "Machine learning for absolute beginners" by Oliver Theobald might touch upon probabilistic models and sequence generation, though detailed algorithms like Forward may be in more advanced texts. Bishop (2006) covers the Forward algorithm in detail in Chapter 13.

#### 3.2 The Decoding Problem (Viterbi Algorithm)

**Problem:** Given an HMM $\lambda$ and an observation sequence $O = (o_1, o_2, ..., o_T)$, what is the most likely sequence of hidden states $Q = (q_1, q_2, ..., q_T)$ that produced this observation sequence?

**Solution:** The **Viterbi Algorithm** is a dynamic programming algorithm used to find the single most likely sequence of hidden states.

**Intuition:** Similar to the Forward algorithm, Viterbi builds up the solution by finding the most likely path to each state at each time step. It keeps track of the maximum probability path and the corresponding state.

Let $\delta_t(j) = \max_{q_1, ..., q_{t-1}} P(q_1, ..., q_{t-1}, q_t = s_j, o_1, ..., o_t | \lambda)$. This represents the probability of the most likely state sequence ending in state $s_j$ at time $t$, given the observations.
Let $\psi_t(j)$ be the state that preceded state $s_j$ at time $t$ in the most likely path.

**Recurrence Relation:**
$\delta_t(j) = \max_{i=1}^{N} [\delta_{t-1}(i) a_{ij}] \cdot b_j(o_t)$
$\psi_t(j) = \arg\max_{i=1}^{N} [\delta_{t-1}(i) a_{ij}]$

**Base Case:**
$\delta_1(j) = \pi_j b_j(o_1)$ for all $j = 1, ..., N$.
$\psi_1(j) = 0$ (or some indicator of the start).

**Backtracking:** After computing $\delta_T(j)$ for all $j$, find the state $q_T^* = \arg\max_j \delta_T(j)$. Then, traceback using $\psi$ to find the entire sequence: $q_t^* = \psi_{t+1}(q_{t+1}^*)$.

**Example:** If the observation sequence is "Eat Ice Cream", "Don't Eat Ice Cream", the Viterbi algorithm would help us find the most likely weather sequence (e.g., "Sunny", "Rainy").

**Connection to Course Outcome CO3:** "Design and execute clustering techniques, and assess their effectiveness using various proximity measures." The Viterbi algorithm can be seen as finding the "best cluster assignment" over time for sequential data.

#### 3.3 The Learning Problem (Baum-Welch Algorithm / EM Algorithm)

**Problem:** Given an observation sequence $O$ (or multiple sequences), how do we estimate the HMM parameters $\lambda = (A, B, \pi)$?

**Solution:** The **Baum-Welch Algorithm**, which is a special case of the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, is used to estimate the HMM parameters when the hidden states are unknown.

**Intuition:** The EM algorithm iteratively refines the parameter estimates. In each iteration:
1.  **Expectation (E-step):** Estimate the expected number of times each state transition and emission occurs given the current model parameters and the observed data. This involves using the Forward-Backward algorithm (an extension of the Forward algorithm to compute backward probabilities $\beta_t(j) = P(o_{t+1}, ..., o_T | q_t = s_j, \lambda)$).
2.  **Maximization (M-step):** Re-estimate the HMM parameters ($\pi, A, B$) to maximize the likelihood of the observed data, given the expected counts computed in the E-step.

**Reference:** "Hands-on Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn, Keras, and TensorFlow" by Aurélien Géron might introduce the EM algorithm in a general context of learning latent variable models, which HMMs are an example of. Bishop (2006) and Theodoridis & Koutroumbas (2003) provide detailed derivations of the Baum-Welch algorithm.

### 4. Applications of HMMs

HMMs are widely used in various fields due to their ability to model sequential data:

*   **Speech Recognition:** Identifying spoken words based on acoustic signals. The hidden states can represent phonemes or speech units.
*   **Natural Language Processing (NLP):**
    *   **Part-of-Speech Tagging:** Assigning grammatical tags (noun, verb, adjective) to words in a sentence. Hidden states are POS tags.
    *   **Named Entity Recognition (NER):** Identifying and classifying named entities (people, organizations, locations) in text.
*   **Bioinformatics:**
    *   **Gene Finding:** Identifying coding and non-coding regions in DNA sequences.
    *   **Protein Sequence Analysis:** Modeling protein structures and functions.
*   **Financial Modeling:** Predicting stock prices or market regimes.
*   **Robotics and Control:** Tracking the position and orientation of robots.
*   **Gesture Recognition:** Recognizing human gestures from sensor data.

**Connection to Course Outcomes CO1 and CO4:** HMMs are versatile unsupervised learning techniques applicable to diverse data-driven problems and complex sequential decision-making scenarios.

### 5. Examples Where Relevant

*   **Part-of-Speech Tagging Example:**
    *   **Observation Sequence:** "The", "cat", "sat", "on", "the", "mat"
    *   **Hidden States:** Noun (NN), Verb (VB), Preposition (IN), Determiner (DT)
    *   An HMM can learn the probability of transitioning between POS tags (e.g., DT followed by NN is common) and the probability of a word belonging to a particular POS tag (e.g., "cat" is likely an NN). The Viterbi algorithm would then find the most likely sequence of POS tags for the given sentence.

*   **Speech Recognition Example:**
    *   **Observation Sequence:** A sequence of acoustic features extracted from speech.
    *   **Hidden States:** Phonemes (e.g., /k/, /æ/, /t/ for "cat").
    *   The HMM models the acoustic characteristics of each phoneme and the transitions between them.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Consider an HMM with two states, S1 and S2, and two possible observations, O1 and O2. The parameters are:
*   $\pi = [0.6, 0.4]$ (S1, S2)
*   $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}$ (S1->S1, S1->S2; S2->S1, S2->S2)
*   $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0.9 \\ 0.8 & 0.2 \end{pmatrix}$ (S1 emits O1/O2; S2 emits O1/O2)

Calculate the probability of the observation sequence O1, O2 using the Forward Algorithm.

**Answer 1:**

We need to calculate $P(O1, O2 | \lambda)$.
Let $\alpha_t(j)$ be the probability of observing the first $t$ observations and ending in state $j$.

**Time t=1, Observation O1:**
$\alpha_1(S1) = \pi_{S1} \cdot b_{S1}(O1) = 0.6 \cdot 0.1 = 0.06$
$\alpha_1(S2) = \pi_{S2} \cdot b_{S2}(O1) = 0.4 \cdot 0.8 = 0.32$

**Time t=2, Observation O2:**
$\alpha_2(S1) = [\alpha_1(S1) \cdot a_{S1 \to S1} + \alpha_1(S2) \cdot a_{S2 \to S1}] \cdot b_{S1}(O2)$
$\alpha_2(S1) = [0.06 \cdot 0.7 + 0.32 \cdot 0.4] \cdot 0.9$
$\alpha_2(S1) = [0.042 + 0.128] \cdot 0.9$
$\alpha_2(S1) = 0.17 \cdot 0.9 = 0.153$

$\alpha_2(S2) = [\alpha_1(S1) \cdot a_{S1 \to S2} + \alpha_1(S2) \cdot a_{S2 \to S2}] \cdot b_{S2}(O2)$
$\alpha_2(S2) = [0.06 \cdot 0.3 + 0.32 \cdot 0.6] \cdot 0.2$
$\alpha_2(S2) = [0.018 + 0.192] \cdot 0.2$
$\alpha_2(S2) = 0.21 \cdot 0.2 = 0.042$

**Final Probability:**
$P(O1, O2 | \lambda) = \alpha_2(S1) + \alpha_2(S2) = 0.153 + 0.042 = 0.195$

**Question 2:**
Suppose you have an observation sequence of "Hot", "Hot", "Cold" from a simplified weather model where states are "Hot Day" and "Cold Day". You are given the following transition and emission probabilities:

*   $P(\text{Hot today} | \text{Hot yesterday}) = 0.8$
*   $P(\text{Cold today} | \text{Hot yesterday}) = 0.2$
*   $P(\text{Hot today} | \text{Cold yesterday}) = 0.3$
*   $P(\text{Cold today} | \text{Cold yesterday}) = 0.7$
*   $P(\text{Hot observation} | \text{Hot Day}) = 0.9$
*   $P(\text{Cold observation} | \text{Hot Day}) = 0.1$
*   $P(\text{Hot observation} | \text{Cold Day}) = 0.4$
*   $P(\text{Cold observation} | \text{Cold Day}) = 0.6$
*   Assume initial probabilities favor a "Hot Day" ($P(\text{Hot Day on day 1}) = 0.7$).

Using the Viterbi algorithm, what is the most likely sequence of weather (hidden states)?

**Answer 2:**
This requires computing $\delta_t(j)$ and $\psi_t(j)$. Let's denote states as H (Hot Day) and C (Cold Day).

**Initial State Probabilities:**
$\pi_H = 0.7, \pi_C = 0.3$

**Observations:** O1="Hot", O2="Hot", O3="Cold"

**Time t=1 (Observation: "Hot"):**
$\delta_1(H) = \pi_H \cdot P(\text{Hot} | H) = 0.7 \cdot 0.9 = 0.63$
$\psi_1(H) = \text{START}$

$\delta_1(C) = \pi_C \cdot P(\text{Hot} | C) = 0.3 \cdot 0.4 = 0.12$
$\psi_1(C) = \text{START}$

**Time t=2 (Observation: "Hot"):**
To calculate $\delta_2(H)$:
From H: $\delta_1(H) \cdot P(H|H) = 0.63 \cdot 0.8 = 0.504$
From C: $\delta_1(C) \cdot P(H|C) = 0.12 \cdot 0.3 = 0.036$
$\max(0.504, 0.036) = 0.504$. So, $\delta_2(H) = 0.504 \cdot P(\text{Hot} | H) = 0.504 \cdot 0.9 = 0.4536$.
$\psi_2(H) = H$ (since the max came from state H at t=1).

To calculate $\delta_2(C)$:
From H: $\delta_1(H) \cdot P(C|H) = 0.63 \cdot 0.2 = 0.126$
From C: $\delta_1(C) \cdot P(C|C) = 0.12 \cdot 0.7 = 0.084$
$\max(0.126, 0.084) = 0.126$. So, $\delta_2(C) = 0.126 \cdot P(\text{Hot} | C) = 0.126 \cdot 0.4 = 0.0504$.
$\psi_2(C) = H$ (since the max came from state H at t=1).

**Time t=3 (Observation: "Cold"):**
To calculate $\delta_3(H)$:
From H: $\delta_2(H) \cdot P(H|H) = 0.4536 \cdot 0.8 = 0.36288$
From C: $\delta_2(C) \cdot P(H|C) = 0.0504 \cdot 0.3 = 0.01512$
$\max(0.36288, 0.01512) = 0.36288$. So, $\delta_3(H) = 0.36288 \cdot P(\text{Cold} | H) = 0.36288 \cdot 0.1 = 0.036288$.
$\psi_3(H) = H$ (since the max came from state H at t=2).

To calculate $\delta_3(C)$:
From H: $\delta_2(H) \cdot P(C|H) = 0.4536 \cdot 0.2 = 0.09072$
From C: $\delta_2(C) \cdot P(C|C) = 0.0504 \cdot 0.7 = 0.03528$
$\max(0.09072, 0.03528) = 0.09072$. So, $\delta_3(C) = 0.09072 \cdot P(\text{Cold} | C) = 0.09072 \cdot 0.6 = 0.054432$.
$\psi_3(C) = H$ (since the max came from state H at t=2).

**Backtracking:**
At t=3, $\delta_3(H) = 0.036288$ and $\delta_3(C) = 0.054432$.
The maximum is $\delta_3(C)$, so the last state is **Cold Day (C)**.

Now use $\psi$ to backtrack:
$q_3^* = C$
$q_2^* = \psi_3(C) = H$
$q_1^* = \psi_2(H) = H$

Therefore, the most likely sequence of hidden states is **Hot Day, Hot Day, Cold Day**.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   HMMs are probabilistic models for **sequential data**.
*   They consist of unobservable **hidden states** and observable **emissions**.
*   The **Markov property** is crucial: current state depends only on the previous state, and current observation depends only on the current state.
*   The three fundamental problems are **evaluation** (Forward), **decoding** (Viterbi), and **learning** (Baum-Welch/EM).
*   HMMs can be seen as a form of **sequential clustering**, where hidden states represent underlying patterns or regimes in the data.
*   HMMs have broad applications in areas like speech recognition, NLP, and bioinformatics.
