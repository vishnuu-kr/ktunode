### 1. Introduction to Elasticity

**Learning Outcome:**
*   Understand the concept of elasticity and its importance in economic decision-making.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**
*   **Elasticity:** A measure of responsiveness of one variable to a change in another variable. In economics, it's typically used to measure the responsiveness of quantity demanded or supplied to changes in price, income, or prices of related goods.
*   **Importance for Engineers:** Understanding elasticity is crucial for engineers involved in product development, pricing strategies, demand forecasting, and resource allocation. It helps in predicting how changes in price or other factors will affect sales and production levels.

**Reference:**
*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** This textbook often provides a good foundation on elasticity from a managerial perspective, explaining its application in business decisions. (Chapter on Demand Analysis and Elasticity)
*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky:** While not solely focused on microeconomic concepts, it might touch upon how market demand elasticity can influence investment decisions or project viability. (Potentially in chapters discussing market analysis or forecasting)

### 2. Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

**Learning Outcomes:**
*   Define and calculate Price Elasticity of Demand (PED).
*   Interpret different degrees of price elasticity.
*   Identify factors that influence PED.
*   Understand the relationship between PED, Total Revenue (TR), and Marginal Revenue (MR).

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**
*   **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED):** The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.
    *   **Formula:**
        $$ \text{PED} = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\% \text{ Change in Price}} $$
    *   **More precisely (using calculus for infinitesimal changes):**
        $$ \text{PED} = \frac{dQ}{dP} \times \frac{P}{Q} $$
        Where:
        *   $Q$ = Quantity Demanded
        *   $P$ = Price
        *   $dQ/dP$ = Derivative of quantity demanded with respect to price.

*   **Interpretation of PED Values:**
    *   **Elastic Demand (PED > 1):** A given percentage change in price leads to a larger percentage change in quantity demanded. Consumers are highly responsive to price changes.
    *   **Inelastic Demand (PED < 1):** A given percentage change in price leads to a smaller percentage change in quantity demanded. Consumers are not very responsive to price changes.
    *   **Unit Elastic Demand (PED = 1):** A percentage change in price leads to an equal percentage change in quantity demanded.
    *   **Perfectly Elastic Demand (PED = ∞):** Consumers will buy an infinite amount at a specific price, but none if the price increases. (Theoretical)
    *   **Perfectly Inelastic Demand (PED = 0):** Quantity demanded does not change regardless of price changes. (Theoretical, e.g., life-saving drugs)

*   **Factors Influencing PED:**
    *   **Availability of Substitutes:** More substitutes mean higher elasticity (e.g., different brands of coffee). Fewer substitutes mean lower elasticity (e.g., gasoline in the short term).
    *   **Necessity vs. Luxury:** Necessities tend to be inelastic (e.g., food, medicine), while luxuries tend to be elastic (e.g., designer clothing, sports cars).
    *   **Proportion of Income:** Goods that consume a large portion of income tend to be more elastic (e.g., housing, cars) than those that consume a small portion (e.g., salt).
    *   **Time Horizon:** Demand is generally more elastic in the long run than in the short run, as consumers have more time to adjust to price changes and find alternatives.
    *   **Definition of the Market:** A broadly defined market (e.g., "food") is less elastic than a narrowly defined market (e.g., "brand X brand of toothpaste").

*   **Relationship with Total Revenue (TR) and Marginal Revenue (MR):**
    *   **TR = Price (P) × Quantity (Q)**
    *   **When demand is elastic (PED > 1):**
        *   An increase in price leads to a decrease in TR.
        *   A decrease in price leads to an increase in TR.
    *   **When demand is inelastic (PED < 1):**
        *   An increase in price leads to an increase in TR.
        *   A decrease in price leads to a decrease in TR.
    *   **When demand is unit elastic (PED = 1):**
        *   Changes in price do not affect TR.
    *   **Marginal Revenue (MR):** The additional revenue generated from selling one more unit. For a linear demand curve, MR is related to price and PED by:
        $$ MR = P \left(1 + \frac{1}{PED} \right) $$
        *   When PED > 1 (elastic), MR is positive.
        *   When PED < 1 (inelastic), MR is negative.
        *   When PED = 1 (unit elastic), MR is zero.

**Examples:**
1.  **Scenario:** A company selling smartphones observes that if they decrease the price of their model by 10%, the quantity demanded increases by 20%.
    *   **Calculation:** PED = 20% / 10% = 2.
    *   **Interpretation:** The demand for this smartphone model is elastic. A price decrease leads to a proportionally larger increase in quantity demanded, likely increasing total revenue.
2.  **Scenario:** A pharmaceutical company selling a life-saving drug experiences a 5% price increase, and the quantity demanded only falls by 1%.
    *   **Calculation:** PED = 1% / 5% = 0.2.
    *   **Interpretation:** The demand for this drug is inelastic. The price increase leads to a proportionally smaller decrease in quantity demanded, increasing total revenue.

**Reference:**
*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** This is a primary source for PED, covering its calculation, determinants, and impact on revenue. (Chapter on Demand Analysis and Elasticity)
*   **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam:** May discuss price elasticity in the context of forecasting demand for engineering products or services, influencing pricing and profitability. (Chapter on Demand and Revenue Analysis)

**Practice Question:**
A product has a price elasticity of demand of -1.5. If the price of the product increases by 5%, what will be the percentage change in the quantity demanded? What is the impact on Total Revenue?

**Answer:**
*   **Change in Quantity Demanded:**
    $$ \% \Delta Q_d = PED \times \% \Delta P $$
    $$ \% \Delta Q_d = -1.5 \times 5\% = -7.5\% $$
    The quantity demanded will decrease by 7.5%.
*   **Impact on Total Revenue:** Since the demand is elastic (PED > 1), an increase in price will lead to a **decrease** in Total Revenue.

### 3. Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

**Learning Outcomes:**
*   Define and calculate Income Elasticity of Demand (YED).
*   Distinguish between normal goods, inferior goods, and luxury goods based on YED.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**
*   **Income Elasticity of Demand (YED):** The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income.
    *   **Formula:**
        $$ \text{YED} = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\% \text{ Change in Income}} $$
    *   **Using calculus:**
        $$ \text{YED} = \frac{dQ}{dY} \times \frac{Y}{Q} $$
        Where:
        *   $Y$ = Income

*   **Classification of Goods based on YED:**
    *   **Normal Goods (YED > 0):** As income increases, the demand for these goods increases.
        *   **Necessities (0 < YED < 1):** Demand increases with income but at a slower rate than income increases (e.g., food, clothing).
        *   **Luxuries (YED > 1):** Demand increases with income at a faster rate than income increases (e.g., cars, holidays, yachts).
    *   **Inferior Goods (YED < 0):** As income increases, the demand for these goods decreases. Consumers switch to better quality alternatives (e.g., generic brands, bus travel).

**Examples:**
1.  **Scenario:** During an economic boom, people's incomes rise by 10%. The demand for new cars increases by 15%.
    *   **Calculation:** YED = 15% / 10% = 1.5.
    *   **Interpretation:** Cars are considered a luxury good (YED > 1) in this scenario.
2.  **Scenario:** As people's incomes increase by 5%, the demand for instant noodles decreases by 2%.
    *   **Calculation:** YED = -2% / 5% = -0.4.
    *   **Interpretation:** Instant noodles are an inferior good (YED < 0).

**Reference:**
*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** Provides a thorough explanation of YED and its implications for product classification and forecasting. (Chapter on Demand Analysis and Elasticity)
*   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park:** Might discuss how changes in consumer income affect the demand for engineering products and services, especially relevant for forecasting. (Chapters related to market analysis or economic factors)

**Practice Question:**
Suppose the income elasticity of demand for a particular engineering component used in automobiles is 1.2. If there is a recession leading to a 4% decrease in average household income, what is the expected percentage change in the demand for this component?

**Answer:**
*   **Expected Change in Demand:**
    $$ \% \Delta Q_d = YED \times \% \Delta Y $$
    $$ \% \Delta Q_d = 1.2 \times (-4\%) = -4.8\% $$
    The demand for the engineering component is expected to decrease by 4.8%.

### 4. Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (CPED)

**Learning Outcomes:**
*   Define and calculate Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (CPED).
*   Differentiate between substitute goods, complementary goods, and unrelated goods based on CPED.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**
*   **Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (CPED):** The percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good.
    *   **Formula:**
        $$ \text{CPED}_{XY} = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Demanded of Good X}}{\% \text{ Change in Price of Good Y}} $$
    *   **Using calculus:**
        $$ \text{CPED}_{XY} = \frac{\partial Q_X}{\partial P_Y} \times \frac{P_Y}{Q_X} $$
        Where:
        *   $Q_X$ = Quantity Demanded of Good X
        *   $P_Y$ = Price of Good Y

*   **Classification of Goods based on CPED:**
    *   **Substitute Goods (CPED > 0):** An increase in the price of one good leads to an increase in the demand for the other good (e.g., if the price of coffee increases, the demand for tea might increase).
    *   **Complementary Goods (CPED < 0):** An increase in the price of one good leads to a decrease in the demand for the other good (e.g., if the price of printers increases, the demand for ink cartridges might decrease).
    *   **Unrelated Goods (CPED ≈ 0):** Changes in the price of one good have no significant effect on the demand for the other good (e.g., the price of cars and the demand for bread).

**Examples:**
1.  **Scenario:** The price of gasoline increases by 5%, and the demand for SUVs decreases by 8%.
    *   **Calculation:** CPED = -8% / 5% = -1.6.
    *   **Interpretation:** SUVs and gasoline are complementary goods (CPED < 0).
2.  **Scenario:** The price of butter falls by 7%, and the demand for margarine falls by 10%.
    *   **Calculation:** CPED = -10% / -7% ≈ 1.43.
    *   **Interpretation:** Butter and margarine are substitute goods (CPED > 0).

**Reference:**
*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** A core text for understanding CPED, its calculation, and its implications for businesses when dealing with competing or complementary products. (Chapter on Demand Analysis and Elasticity)
*   **Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam:** Useful for engineers considering the demand for their products in relation to the pricing of related products, especially in industries with interconnected markets. (Chapter on Demand and Revenue Analysis)

**Practice Question:**
A company produces specialized fasteners. The price of steel, a key raw material for its fasteners, increases by 8%. The company observes a 3% decrease in the demand for its fasteners. What is the cross-price elasticity of demand between steel price and the demand for fasteners? What does this imply about the relationship between steel and these fasteners?

**Answer:**
*   **Cross-Price Elasticity:**
    $$ \text{CPED} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d}{\% \Delta P_{\text{steel}}} $$
    $$ \text{CPED} = \frac{-3\%}{8\%} = -0.375 $$
*   **Implication:** The CPED is negative (-0.375), indicating that steel and these specialized fasteners are **complementary goods**. This is unusual, as usually raw materials and finished products are not classified this way. However, if the price of steel increases, it could indirectly lead to a higher cost for the fasteners, potentially reducing demand, or it might imply that the cost structure of producing fasteners is so tied to steel that a price rise in steel makes the fasteners less attractive/affordable. A more common scenario might be a direct relationship where higher steel prices lead to higher fastener prices and thus lower demand for fasteners, but the framing here suggests a complementarity in their consumption or production cost impact. *Self-correction:* In this context, it's more likely interpreted that the increased cost of steel is passed on to the consumer of the fastener, leading to lower demand for the fastener. Therefore, they behave like complements.

### 5. Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

**Learning Outcomes:**
*   Define and calculate Price Elasticity of Supply (PES).
*   Identify factors that influence PES.
*   Distinguish between elastic and inelastic supply.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**
*   **Price Elasticity of Supply (PES):** The percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price.
    *   **Formula:**
        $$ \text{PES} = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Supplied}}{\% \text{ Change in Price}} $$
    *   **Using calculus:**
        $$ \text{PES} = \frac{dQ_s}{dP} \times \frac{P}{Q_s} $$
        Where:
        *   $Q_s$ = Quantity Supplied

*   **Interpretation of PES Values:**
    *   **Elastic Supply (PES > 1):** A given percentage change in price leads to a larger percentage change in quantity supplied. Producers can easily and quickly increase production in response to price changes.
    *   **Inelastic Supply (PES < 1):** A given percentage change in price leads to a smaller percentage change in quantity supplied. Producers find it difficult or time-consuming to change production levels in response to price changes.
    *   **Unit Elastic Supply (PES = 1):** A percentage change in price leads to an equal percentage change in quantity supplied.
    *   **Perfectly Elastic Supply (PES = ∞):** Producers will supply an infinite amount at a specific price, but none if the price falls below it. (Theoretical)
    *   **Perfectly Inelastic Supply (PES = 0):** Quantity supplied does not change regardless of price changes. (Theoretical, e.g., limited supply of unique art)

*   **Factors Influencing PES:**
    *   **Availability of Inputs:** If inputs (raw materials, labor) are readily available and can be quickly increased, supply is more elastic. If inputs are scarce or specialized, supply is more inelastic.
    *   **Time Horizon:** Supply is generally more elastic in the long run than in the short run. Firms have more time to adjust production capacity, build new factories, or train workers.
    *   **Production Capacity:** Firms with excess production capacity can respond more elastically to price changes than those operating at full capacity.
    *   **Mobility of Resources:** If resources (labor, capital) can be easily shifted from producing one good to another, supply is more elastic.
    *   **Ease of Storage:** If goods can be easily stored, producers can hold back supply when prices are low and release it when prices are high, increasing elasticity.

**Examples:**
1.  **Scenario:** If the price of wheat increases by 10%, farmers respond by increasing the quantity of wheat supplied by 15% in the next planting season.
    *   **Calculation:** PES = 15% / 10% = 1.5.
    *   **Interpretation:** The supply of wheat is elastic (PES > 1).
2.  **Scenario:** The price of concert tickets for a specific artist who has a fixed number of performances increases by 20%. The number of tickets available (quantity supplied) remains the same.
    *   **Calculation:** PES = 0% / 20% = 0.
    *   **Interpretation:** The supply of tickets for this event is perfectly inelastic (PES = 0).

**Reference:**
*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** Provides a detailed explanation of PES, its determinants, and its significance for producers. (Chapter on Supply and Cost)
*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky:** May touch upon supply considerations in the context of production planning and capacity utilization, which are linked to supply elasticity. (Chapters related to production and costs)

**Practice Question:**
A manufacturer of specialized engine parts has a production capacity that can be expanded with a significant investment in new machinery, which takes about two years. If the market price for these parts increases by 10%, in the short run (within 6 months), the company can increase its output by only 2%. In the long run (after two years, with new machinery), it can increase output by 20%. Calculate the short-run and long-run price elasticities of supply.

**Answer:**
*   **Short-Run PES:**
    $$ \text{PES}_{\text{short-run}} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_s}{\% \Delta P} = \frac{2\%}{10\%} = 0.2 $$
    The short-run supply is inelastic.
*   **Long-Run PES:**
    $$ \text{PES}_{\text{long-run}} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_s}{\% \Delta P} = \frac{20\%}{10\%} = 2.0 $$
    The long-run supply is elastic.

### 6. Applications of Elasticity in Engineering and Management

**Learning Outcomes:**
*   Apply the concept of elasticity to solve real-world engineering and business problems.
*   Understand the implications of elasticity for pricing, production, and forecasting.

**Key Concepts & Applications:**
*   **Pricing Decisions:**
    *   Understanding PED helps engineers and managers set prices that maximize revenue or profit. For instance, if demand is elastic, a price cut might be beneficial. If inelastic, a price hike could be.
    *   **Example:** An engineer designing a new consumer electronic device needs to estimate the PED to set an optimal introductory price. If PED is high, a lower price might capture a larger market share.
*   **Forecasting:**
    *   YED helps predict how demand for a product will change with changes in consumer income, crucial for long-term planning and investment.
    *   CPED helps understand how demand for a product is affected by the prices of its substitutes and complements, important for competitive analysis.
    *   **Example:** A civil engineering firm planning for future infrastructure projects needs to forecast the demand for construction materials based on projected economic growth (YED).
*   **Production Planning:**
    *   PES influences a firm's ability to respond to market price changes. Firms with elastic supply can adjust production more easily.
    *   **Example:** A manufacturing plant considering automation needs to analyze the PES of its products. Higher elasticity might justify investments in flexible production systems.
*   **Government Policy:**
    *   Governments use PED to forecast the impact of taxes on goods. Taxes on inelastic goods (like cigarettes or gasoline) generate more revenue and are often used to discourage consumption.
    *   **Example:** A government considering a tax on sugary drinks would analyze the PED to estimate revenue generation and potential impact on consumption.

**Reference:**
*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** Provides practical case studies and examples of how elasticity is used in managerial decision-making. (Throughout chapters on demand and cost analysis)
*   **Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam:** Discusses demand forecasting and pricing strategies in relation to project evaluation and financial viability. (Chapters on Demand, Revenue, and Forecasting)
*   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park:** Explicitly links economic concepts like elasticity to engineering project evaluation and decision-making, particularly in market-driven industries. (Chapters on Engineering Economic Decisions, Market Analysis)
*   **Engineering Economics and analysis by Donald G. Newman, Jerome P. Lavelle:** Likely covers how market demand characteristics, including elasticity, affect the economic feasibility of engineering projects. (Chapters on economic analysis of projects)

**Course Outcome Alignment:**
*   **CO1 (Describe fundamentals of demand, supply, elasticity):** This entire topic directly addresses the understanding of elasticity as a fundamental economic concept.
*   **CO2 (Develop decision making by applying costs/revenue, functioning of firms):** Understanding PED is crucial for revenue management. YED and CPED help firms understand market dynamics and consumer behavior.
*   **CO4 (Make use of value analysis/engineering, solve business problems):** Elasticity directly informs pricing decisions, which are critical for solving business problems related to profitability and market share.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Elasticity is always measured in absolute terms for PED and CPED when determining elasticity/inelasticity, though the sign is crucial for interpretation.** PES is typically positive.
*   The **sign** of YED determines if a good is normal or inferior.
*   The **sign** of CPED determines if goods are substitutes or complements.
*   **PED and TR are inversely related:** When demand is elastic, price increases lead to TR decreases. When demand is inelastic, price increases lead to TR increases.
*   **Time horizon** is a critical determinant for both PED and PES, with supply and demand generally being more elastic in the longer run.
*   **Availability of substitutes** is a key driver of PED.
*   **Engineers need to translate economic concepts like elasticity into practical decisions** regarding product design, pricing, and market strategy.
