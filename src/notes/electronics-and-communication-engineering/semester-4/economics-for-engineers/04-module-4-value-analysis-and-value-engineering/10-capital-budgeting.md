### 1. Introduction to Capital Budgeting

**Definition:** Capital budgeting is the process a business uses to evaluate potential major projects or investments. It involves a firm making decisions on which assets or projects to undertake in order to maximize shareholder wealth. These decisions involve significant upfront investments and are expected to generate returns over a long period.

**Importance for Engineers:**

*   **Long-Term Investments:** Engineering projects often involve substantial capital expenditure with long-lasting impacts on the firm's profitability and operational efficiency.
*   **Resource Allocation:** It guides the allocation of scarce financial resources to the most promising projects.
*   **Strategic Decisions:** Capital budgeting decisions are crucial for a company's long-term strategy, competitiveness, and growth.
*   **Risk Assessment:** It helps in evaluating the risks associated with different investment opportunities.
*   **Value Maximization:** The ultimate goal is to select projects that add the most value to the firm.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Capital Expenditure:** Funds spent on acquiring or upgrading physical assets such as property, buildings, and equipment.
*   **Investment Decision:** Deciding whether to invest in a particular project or asset.
*   **Time Value of Money:** The concept that money available at the present time is worth more than the same amount in the future due to its potential earning capacity.

**References:**

*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** Likely discusses capital budgeting as a key tool for managerial decision-making and investment appraisal.
*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky:** A foundational text that will cover the principles of capital budgeting from an engineering perspective, emphasizing cash flows and economic evaluation.
*   **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam:** Will provide a comprehensive overview of investment appraisal techniques and their application in engineering projects.

### 2. The Capital Budgeting Process

The capital budgeting process typically involves the following steps:

1.  **Identification of Investment Proposals:** Generating ideas for potential projects. These can arise from various sources:
    *   **Replacement of Old Equipment:** To maintain production levels or improve efficiency.
    *   **Expansion of Existing Facilities:** To increase capacity or introduce new product lines.
    *   **New Products or Services:** Ventures into new markets or offerings.
    *   **Regulatory Requirements:** Projects mandated by government regulations (e.g., environmental compliance).
    *   **Research and Development (R&D):** Investing in future innovations.

2.  **Evaluation of Proposals:** Analyzing the financial and economic viability of each proposal. This is the core of capital budgeting and involves various analytical techniques.

3.  **Selection of Projects:** Choosing the projects that offer the best return on investment, considering the firm's objectives and constraints.

4.  **Implementation:** Executing the chosen projects, which involves acquiring assets, hiring personnel, and commencing operations.

5.  **Monitoring and Review:** Tracking the performance of implemented projects against their expected outcomes and making necessary adjustments.

### 3. Capital Budgeting Techniques

These techniques are used to evaluate the profitability and financial feasibility of long-term investment proposals. They primarily focus on the cash flows generated by the project and the time value of money.

#### 3.1. Non-Discounted Cash Flow Techniques (Simpler, Ignores Time Value of Money)

These methods are easier to calculate but less accurate as they do not consider the time value of money.

**a) Payback Period (PP)**

*   **Definition:** The time required for the cumulative net cash inflows from a project to equal the initial investment.
*   **Formula:**
    *   For uniform cash flows: `Payback Period = Initial Investment / Annual Cash Inflow`
    *   For non-uniform cash flows: Calculate cumulative cash flows year by year until the initial investment is recovered.
*   **Decision Rule:** Projects with a shorter payback period are generally preferred, especially in industries with rapid technological change or high uncertainty.
*   **Advantages:** Simple to understand and calculate; provides a measure of liquidity and risk (shorter payback implies quicker recovery of investment).
*   **Disadvantages:** Ignores cash flows beyond the payback period; ignores the time value of money; does not consider the profitability of the project beyond the recovery period.
*   **Example:**
    *   Project A: Initial Investment = $50,000; Annual Cash Inflow = $15,000.
    *   Payback Period = $50,000 / $15,000 = 3.33 years.
    *   Project B: Initial Investment = $50,000; Cash Flows: Year 1 = $10,000, Year 2 = $15,000, Year 3 = $20,000, Year 4 = $25,000.
        *   Year 1 Cum: $10,000
        *   Year 2 Cum: $10,000 + $15,000 = $25,000
        *   Year 3 Cum: $25,000 + $20,000 = $45,000
        *   Year 4 Cum: $45,000 + $25,000 = $70,000
        *   Remaining to recover at end of Year 3 = $50,000 - $45,000 = $5,000
        *   Payback Period = 3 years + ($5,000 / $25,000) = 3.2 years.

**b) Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)**

*   **Definition:** The average annual profit from a project expressed as a percentage of the initial investment. Also known as the "average rate of return" or "return on investment."
*   **Formula:** `ARR = Average Annual Profit / Initial Investment`
    *   `Average Annual Profit = (Total Net Profit) / (Useful Life of the Project)`
    *   `Total Net Profit = Total Revenue - Total Costs (excluding depreciation) - Taxes`
    *   **Note:** Some variations use average investment `(Initial Investment + Salvage Value) / 2` in the denominator.
*   **Decision Rule:** Projects with an ARR greater than the company's target rate of return are accepted.
*   **Advantages:** Easy to calculate and understand; uses accounting profit, which is readily available.
*   **Disadvantages:** Ignores the time value of money; uses accounting profits rather than cash flows (which can be affected by depreciation and non-cash items); the definition of "average investment" can vary.
*   **Example:**
    *   Project C: Initial Investment = $100,000; Expected life = 5 years; Total profits (after tax, before depreciation) = $150,000. Depreciation (straight-line) = $100,000 / 5 = $20,000 per year.
    *   Average Annual Profit = ($150,000 - ($20,000 * 5)) / 5 = ($150,000 - $100,000) / 5 = $50,000 / 5 = $10,000.
    *   ARR = $10,000 / $100,000 = 10%.

#### 3.2. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Techniques (Consider Time Value of Money)

These are generally considered superior as they account for the time value of money.

**a) Net Present Value (NPV)**

*   **Definition:** The difference between the present value of future cash inflows and the present value of the initial investment. It measures the absolute increase in value to the firm from undertaking a project.
*   **Formula:**
    $$NPV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} - Initial Investment$$
    Where:
    *   $CF_t$ = Net cash flow in year $t$
    *   $r$ = Discount rate (required rate of return or cost of capital)
    *   $n$ = Useful life of the project
    *   $t$ = Time period (year)
*   **Decision Rule:**
    *   If NPV > 0: Accept the project (it is expected to add value to the firm).
    *   If NPV < 0: Reject the project (it is expected to decrease value).
    *   If NPV = 0: The project is expected to earn exactly the required rate of return.
*   **Advantages:** Considers the time value of money; considers all cash flows over the project's life; provides a direct measure of the increase in firm value.
*   **Disadvantages:** Requires estimation of the discount rate, which can be subjective; can be complex to calculate manually for long projects.
*   **References:**
    *   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** Will extensively cover NPV as a primary capital budgeting tool.
    *   **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam:** Will detail the calculation and interpretation of NPV for engineering investments.
    *   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park:** Will emphasize NPV's role in strategic investment decisions.
*   **Example:**
    *   Project D: Initial Investment = $10,000; Discount Rate (r) = 10%; Project Life = 3 years.
    *   Cash Flows: Year 1 = $4,000, Year 2 = $5,000, Year 3 = $6,000.
    *   PV of Cash Flows:
        *   Year 1: $4,000 / (1.10)^1 = $3,636.36
        *   Year 2: $5,000 / (1.10)^2 = $4,132.23
        *   Year 3: $6,000 / (1.10)^3 = $4,507.88
    *   Total PV of Inflows = $3,636.36 + $4,132.23 + $4,507.88 = $12,276.47
    *   NPV = $12,276.47 - $10,000 = $2,276.47
    *   Since NPV > 0, Project D should be accepted.

**b) Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**

*   **Definition:** The discount rate at which the Net Present Value (NPV) of a project's cash flows equals zero. It represents the effective rate of return a project is expected to generate.
*   **Formula:** Find the rate `r` such that:
    $$ \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t} = Initial Investment $$
*   **Decision Rule:**
    *   If IRR > Cost of Capital (or required rate of return): Accept the project.
    *   If IRR < Cost of Capital: Reject the project.
*   **Advantages:** Considers the time value of money; considers all cash flows; intuitive to understand as a "rate of return."
*   **Disadvantages:** Can be difficult to calculate manually (often requires trial and error or financial calculators/software); can yield multiple IRRs for projects with non-conventional cash flows (e.g., multiple sign changes in cash flows); can lead to incorrect decisions when comparing mutually exclusive projects of different scales or lifespans due to the implicit assumption of reinvesting cash flows at the IRR itself.
*   **References:**
    *   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky:** Will provide detailed explanations and methods for calculating IRR.
    *   **Engineering Economics and analysis by Donald G. Newman, Jerome P. Lavelle:** Likely offers practical methods and examples for IRR calculations.
*   **Example (using Project D from NPV):**
    *   Initial Investment = $10,000; Cash Flows: Year 1 = $4,000, Year 2 = $5,000, Year 3 = $6,000.
    *   We need to find the rate `r` where:
        $$ \frac{4000}{(1+r)^1} + \frac{5000}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{6000}{(1+r)^3} = 10000 $$
    *   *Trial and Error (or using a financial calculator/spreadsheet):*
        *   Let's try r = 15%:
            *   PV = $4000/1.15 + $5000/(1.15)^2 + $6000/(1.15)^3 = $3478.26 + $3787.65 + $3971.38 = $11237.29 (NPV is positive)
        *   Let's try r = 20%:
            *   PV = $4000/1.20 + $5000/(1.20)^2 + $6000/(1.20)^3 = $3333.33 + $3472.22 + $3472.22 = $10277.77 (NPV is positive but closer to zero)
        *   Let's try r = 22%:
            *   PV = $4000/1.22 + $5000/(1.22)^2 + $6000/(1.22)^3 = $3278.69 + $3374.43 + $3421.56 = $10074.68 (NPV is positive but very close to zero)
        *   Let's try r = 22.5%:
            *   PV = $4000/1.225 + $5000/(1.225)^2 + $6000/(1.225)^3 = $3265.31 + $3355.10 + $3402.04 = $10022.45 (NPV is positive and extremely close to zero)
        *   The IRR is approximately 22.5%. If the cost of capital is less than 22.5%, the project should be accepted.

**c) Profitability Index (PI)**

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the present value of future cash inflows to the initial investment. It measures the "bang for buck" or the value generated per unit of investment.
*   **Formula:**
    $$ PI = \frac{\text{Present Value of Future Cash Inflows}}{\text{Initial Investment}} $$
    or
    $$ PI = \frac{NPV + Initial Investment}{Initial Investment} = 1 + \frac{NPV}{Initial Investment} $$
*   **Decision Rule:**
    *   If PI > 1: Accept the project.
    *   If PI < 1: Reject the project.
    *   If PI = 1: The project is expected to earn exactly the required rate of return.
*   **Advantages:** Considers the time value of money; useful for ranking projects when capital is rationed (limited). A higher PI indicates a more efficient use of capital.
*   **Disadvantages:** Can be sensitive to the timing of cash flows.
*   **References:**
    *   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** Will likely present PI as a useful metric for capital rationing.
*   **Example (using Project D from NPV):**
    *   Total PV of Inflows = $12,276.47
    *   Initial Investment = $10,000
    *   PI = $12,276.47 / $10,000 = 1.2276
    *   Since PI > 1, Project D should be accepted.

**d) Discounted Payback Period**

*   **Definition:** The time required for the cumulative discounted cash inflows to equal the initial investment. It addresses the deficiency of the regular payback period by considering the time value of money.
*   **Calculation:** Calculate the present value of each year's cash flow and then find the period when the cumulative discounted cash flows recover the initial investment.
*   **Decision Rule:** Shorter discounted payback periods are preferred.
*   **Advantages:** Considers the time value of money and provides a measure of liquidity/risk.
*   **Disadvantages:** Ignores cash flows beyond the discounted payback period.

### 4. Factors to Consider in Capital Budgeting

*   **Cash Flows:** Focus on incremental cash flows, considering both inflows and outflows directly attributable to the project. This includes initial investment, operating cash flows, and salvage value.
*   **Timing of Cash Flows:** The timing is critical due to the time value of money.
*   **Discount Rate:** Represents the firm's cost of capital or required rate of return. It reflects the riskiness of the project and the opportunity cost of investing elsewhere.
*   **Risk and Uncertainty:** Projects with higher risk should demand a higher rate of return. Techniques like sensitivity analysis and scenario analysis can be used to assess risk.
*   **Inflation:** Expected inflation should be incorporated into cash flow forecasts and the discount rate.
*   **Taxes:** Cash flows should be considered on an after-tax basis.
*   **Salvage Value:** The estimated resale value of an asset at the end of its useful life.
*   **Depreciation:** While not a cash outflow, depreciation affects taxable income and thus the tax shield on profits.

**References:**

*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky:** Will provide in-depth discussion on cash flow estimation, discount rates, and risk.
*   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park:** Emphasizes the practical aspects of cash flow analysis and risk assessment in engineering investments.

### 5. Capital Rationing

**Definition:** A situation where a firm has more acceptable investment proposals than it has funds available to invest.

**Objective:** To select the combination of projects that maximizes the overall value of the firm given the limited capital.

**Technique:** When capital is rationed and projects are divisible, the **Profitability Index (PI)** is the best criterion for selecting projects. Projects should be ranked in descending order of their PIs and accepted until the capital budget is exhausted.

**Example:**
A company has a capital budget of $100,000 and is considering three projects:

| Project | Initial Investment | NPV     | PI    |
| :------ | :----------------- | :------ | :---- |
| A       | $50,000            | $15,000 | 1.30  |
| B       | $40,000            | $12,000 | 1.30  |
| C       | $30,000            | $10,000 | 1.33  |

**Analysis:**

1.  **Rank by PI:** C (1.33), A (1.30), B (1.30)
2.  **Select Projects:**
    *   Accept Project C: Investment = $30,000. Remaining Budget = $100,000 - $30,000 = $70,000.
    *   Accept Project A: Investment = $50,000. Remaining Budget = $70,000 - $50,000 = $20,000.
    *   Cannot accept Project B (requires $40,000, only $20,000 remaining).

**Optimal Combination:** Projects C and A, totaling an investment of $80,000 and generating an NPV of $10,000 + $15,000 = $25,000.

**Important Note:** If projects are indivisible, the PI method may not yield the truly optimal solution. In such cases, integer programming or enumeration of feasible combinations might be necessary.

### 6. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
Project Alpha requires an initial investment of $100,000 and is expected to generate cash flows of $30,000 per year for 5 years. The company's required rate of return is 12%. Calculate the Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR) of Project Alpha. Would you recommend accepting this project? (Assume cash flows occur at the end of each year).

**Question 2:**
A firm is considering two mutually exclusive projects, X and Y.
*   Project X: Initial Investment = $50,000; Cash flows = $15,000 annually for 5 years.
*   Project Y: Initial Investment = $80,000; Cash flows = $25,000 annually for 5 years.
The company's cost of capital is 10%.
a) Calculate the Payback Period for both projects.
b) Calculate the NPV for both projects.
c) Based on NPV, which project would you recommend?

**Question 3:**
A project has an initial investment of $20,000 and is expected to generate cash flows as follows: Year 1: $6,000, Year 2: $7,000, Year 3: $8,000, Year 4: $9,000. The discount rate is 10%. Calculate the Profitability Index (PI).

---

### 7. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**

*   **NPV Calculation:**
    *   PV Factor for 12% for 5 years (annuity) = $[1 - (1.12)^{-5}] / 0.12$ = 3.6048
    *   PV of Cash Inflows = $30,000 * 3.6048 = $108,144
    *   NPV = $108,144 - $100,000 = $8,144

*   **IRR Calculation:**
    *   We need to find `r` where: $30,000 * [1 - (1+r)^{-5}] / r = 100,000$
    *   $[1 - (1+r)^{-5}] / r = 100,000 / 30,000 = 3.3333$
    *   Using a financial calculator or trial and error:
        *   At 12%, the factor is 3.6048 (NPV > 0)
        *   At 15%, the factor is 3.3522 (NPV still positive, but closer to zero)
        *   At 16%, the factor is 3.2743 (NPV negative)
    *   The IRR is between 15% and 16%. Interpolating or using a calculator, IRR ≈ 15.4%.

*   **Recommendation:** Since the NPV ($8,144) is positive and the IRR (≈15.4%) is greater than the required rate of return (12%), Project Alpha is financially viable and should be accepted.

**Answer 2:**

*   **a) Payback Period:**
    *   Project X: $50,000 / $15,000 = **3.33 years**
    *   Project Y: $80,000 / $25,000 = **3.2 years**

*   **b) NPV Calculation (r = 10%):**
    *   PV Factor for 10% for 5 years (annuity) = $[1 - (1.10)^{-5}] / 0.10$ = 3.7908
    *   **Project X:**
        *   PV of Cash Inflows = $15,000 * 3.7908 = $56,862
        *   NPV_X = $56,862 - $50,000 = **$6,862**
    *   **Project Y:**
        *   PV of Cash Inflows = $25,000 * 3.7908 = $94,770
        *   NPV_Y = $94,770 - $80,000 = **$14,770**

*   **c) Recommendation:** Based on NPV, Project Y ($14,770) is preferred over Project X ($6,862) because it is expected to add more value to the firm. Even though Project X has a slightly shorter payback period, NPV is a more robust measure.

**Answer 3:**

*   **Calculate Present Value of Cash Inflows:**
    *   Year 1: $6,000 / (1.10)^1 = $5,454.55
    *   Year 2: $7,000 / (1.10)^2 = $5,785.12
    *   Year 3: $8,000 / (1.10)^3 = $6,010.79
    *   Year 4: $9,000 / (1.10)^4 = $6,150.59
    *   Total PV of Inflows = $5,454.55 + $5,785.12 + $6,010.79 + $6,150.59 = $23,401.05

*   **Calculate PI:**
    *   Initial Investment = $20,000
    *   PI = $23,401.05 / $20,000 = **1.17**

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Focus on Cash Flows:** Always use incremental cash flows, not accounting profits, for capital budgeting decisions.
*   **Time Value of Money:** Discounted cash flow techniques (NPV, IRR, PI) are generally preferred over non-discounted methods.
*   **Cost of Capital:** The discount rate is crucial for DCF analysis; it represents the opportunity cost of investing in the project.
*   **Mutually Exclusive vs. Independent Projects:** Understand the difference and how to choose between them. NPV is usually the best for mutually exclusive projects.
*   **Capital Rationing:** Use PI to rank projects when capital is limited.
*   **Risk Adjustment:** Higher-risk projects should be evaluated using a higher discount rate or through sensitivity analysis.
