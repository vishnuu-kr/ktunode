# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 4: Value Analysis and Value Engineering

## Topic: Cost Value

---

### **Introduction to Cost Value in the Context of Value Analysis and Value Engineering**

In Value Analysis (VA) and Value Engineering (VE), understanding the relationship between **cost** and **value** is paramount. It's not simply about reducing costs; it's about maximizing the value delivered for a given cost or achieving the required value at a lower cost. This topic will explore the fundamental concepts of cost and value, their interplay, and how engineers can leverage this understanding to improve products, processes, and services.

---

### **1. Understanding Cost**

**Definition:** Cost refers to the resources (money, time, labor, materials, etc.) expended in the production, acquisition, or delivery of a good or service.

**Key Concepts Related to Cost:**

*   **Types of Costs:**
    *   **Direct Costs:** Costs directly attributable to a specific product or project.
        *   *Example:* Raw materials used in manufacturing a car, labor directly involved in assembling the car.
    *   **Indirect Costs (Overheads):** Costs not directly tied to a specific product but are necessary for the overall operation.
        *   *Example:* Factory rent, administrative salaries, utility bills for the factory.
    *   **Fixed Costs:** Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run.
        *   *Example:* Rent, salaries of permanent staff, depreciation of machinery.
    *   **Variable Costs:** Costs that change directly with the level of output.
        *   *Example:* Raw materials per unit, direct labor per unit, energy consumed per unit of production.
    *   **Total Cost:** Fixed Costs + Variable Costs.
    *   **Average Cost:** Total Cost / Quantity Produced.
    *   **Marginal Cost:** The additional cost incurred by producing one more unit.
    *   **Opportunity Cost:** The value of the next best alternative foregone when a particular choice is made.
        *   *Example:* If an engineer spends time designing a new component, the opportunity cost is the value of the other projects they could have been working on.
    *   **Life Cycle Cost:** The total cost of ownership over the entire life of a product, including design, manufacturing, operation, maintenance, and disposal. This is particularly relevant in VE.

*   **Cost Behavior:** How costs change in response to changes in the volume of activity (e.g., production volume, sales volume).

*   **Cost Estimation:** The process of determining the expected costs for a particular activity or project. This is crucial for both financial planning and decision-making.

**Referencing Textbooks:**

*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury:** This textbook extensively covers various cost concepts, cost-volume-profit relationships, and cost behavior analysis, which are foundational for understanding cost in a business context.
*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky:** This book emphasizes the economic evaluation of engineering alternatives, where accurate cost estimation and understanding of cost behavior are critical for making sound investment decisions.
*   **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam:** Provides a practical approach to cost analysis, including methods for estimating costs and understanding their impact on profitability and decision-making.

---

### **2. Understanding Value**

**Definition:** Value, in the context of VA/VE, is defined as the ratio of **function** to **cost**.

**Value = Function / Cost**

**Key Concepts Related to Value:**

*   **Function:** What a product or service does; its purpose or utility. Functions can be categorized as:
    *   **Primary Functions:** The main purpose for which the product/service is designed.
        *   *Example:* For a chair, the primary function is to support a person.
    *   **Secondary Functions:** Ancillary or supporting purposes.
        *   *Example:* For a chair, secondary functions might include aesthetic appeal or portability.
    *   **Use Functions:** Related to the basic performance or physical function.
    *   **Esteem Functions:** Related to psychological, aesthetic, or status benefits.

*   **Quantifying Value:**
    *   **Performance:** How well a function is performed.
    *   **Durability:** How long a function is expected to last.
    *   **Reliability:** The probability that a function will perform as expected.
    *   **Aesthetics:** The visual appeal or subjective desirability.
    *   **Convenience:** Ease of use or accessibility.
    *   **Safety:** Protection against harm.

*   **Perceived Value:** The value a customer assigns to a product or service based on their needs, preferences, and their assessment of its benefits versus its cost.

**Referencing Textbooks:**

*   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park:** This book offers a strong emphasis on understanding customer needs and translating them into functional requirements, which is central to defining value in VE. It also discusses techniques for measuring and improving value.
*   **Engineering Economics and analysis by Donald G. Newman, Jerome P. Lavelle:** This reference delves into how to quantify economic benefits and costs, providing a framework for evaluating alternatives based on their overall value proposition.

### **3. The Cost-Value Relationship in VA/VE**

The core principle of VA/VE is to **improve value by either:**

*   **Increasing Function while keeping Cost the same.**
*   **Decreasing Cost while keeping Function the same.**
*   **Increasing Function while decreasing Cost proportionally more.**
*   **Decreasing Function slightly while decreasing Cost significantly.**

**Analysis of Cost-Value Matrix:**

| Situation          | Function | Cost   | Value (Function/Cost) | Action for Improvement                                                                                                |
| :----------------- | :------- | :----- | :-------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **High Value**     | High     | Low    | High                  | Maintain and protect; seek ways to increase function further without significant cost increase.                         |
| **Low Value**      | Low      | High   | Low                   | Requires significant re-evaluation; investigate new materials, designs, or processes to improve function or reduce cost. |
| **Good Value**     | High     | High   | Moderate              | Explore minor improvements in function or cost reduction.                                                             |
| **Poor Value**     | Low      | Low    | Moderate              | Investigate why function is low; potential for significant improvement by increasing function.                            |

**Example:**

Consider a simple plastic component in a consumer electronic device.

*   **Scenario 1 (Poor Value):** The component is made of a heavy, brittle plastic that cracks easily (low function) but is expensive to produce (high cost). **Value = Low/High = Low.** VE might involve finding a lighter, more durable plastic that costs the same or slightly more but significantly improves reliability and longevity.
*   **Scenario 2 (Good Value):** The component is made of a strong, durable plastic and performs its function perfectly. The cost is also reasonable. **Value = High/High = Moderate.** VE might explore alternative manufacturing processes that reduce production time or energy consumption, thus lowering cost without sacrificing performance.
*   **Scenario 3 (High Value):** The component is made of a lightweight, incredibly durable material and performs its function flawlessly. The material and manufacturing process are optimized for low cost. **Value = High/Low = High.** This is the ideal scenario.

**Linking to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO4 (Value Analysis and Engineering):** This entire topic directly addresses CO4 by explaining the fundamental principles of VA/VE, which are about optimizing the cost-value relationship.
*   **CO2 (Decision Making, Costs and Revenue):** Understanding cost-value helps engineers make informed decisions by considering not just the cost of a component or process but its contribution to the overall value delivered to the customer, thus influencing profitability.

### **4. Techniques for Cost Analysis in VA/VE**

Engineers use various techniques to analyze costs to identify opportunities for value improvement.

*   **Break-Even Analysis:** While not solely a VA/VE tool, understanding the break-even point is crucial. It helps determine the sales volume needed to cover all costs. In VE, a change in cost structure might shift the break-even point, influencing profitability at different production levels.
    *   *Formula:* Break-Even Point (Units) = Fixed Costs / (Sales Price per Unit - Variable Cost per Unit)
    *   **Referencing:** This concept is well-covered in **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury** and **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam**.

*   **Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA):** This technique compares the total expected costs against the total expected benefits of a project or decision. In VA/VE, CBA is used to justify proposed changes by demonstrating that the benefits (e.g., cost savings, improved performance) outweigh the costs of implementing the change.
    *   **Referencing:** **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam** and **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky** provide frameworks for conducting CBA in engineering projects.

*   **Life Cycle Costing (LCC):** As mentioned earlier, LCC considers all costs associated with a product from cradle to grave. VA/VE often uses LCC to identify cost reduction opportunities not just in manufacturing but also in operation, maintenance, and disposal.
    *   *Example:* A cheaper manufacturing process might lead to a product that requires more frequent maintenance, resulting in a higher overall LCC. VE would aim to optimize for the lowest LCC.
    *   **Referencing:** **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park** strongly advocates for LCC in making sustainable engineering decisions.

*   **Target Costing:** This is a cost management strategy where the company determines the acceptable market price for a product and then subtracts the desired profit margin to arrive at the allowable cost. VA/VE principles are then applied to achieve this target cost while meeting the required function.
    *   *Example:* If a competitor's similar product sells for $10 and the desired profit is $2, the target cost is $8. VE efforts would focus on reducing the product's cost to $8.
    *   **Referencing:** **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury** often discusses cost management strategies like target costing.

### **5. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**

A company is manufacturing a widget. The fixed costs are $50,000 per month. The variable cost per widget is $10, and the selling price per widget is $20.
a) Calculate the break-even point in units.
b) If the company wants to achieve a profit of $20,000 per month, how many widgets must they sell?
c) The company is considering a Value Engineering change that will increase fixed costs by $5,000 per month but reduce variable costs by $2 per widget. If the selling price remains $20, what is the new break-even point in units? Does this VE change appear beneficial from a break-even perspective?

**Answer 1:**

a) Break-Even Point (Units) = Fixed Costs / (Sales Price per Unit - Variable Cost per Unit)
   = $50,000 / ($20 - $10)
   = $50,000 / $10
   = **5,000 units**

b) Target Profit = Sales Revenue - Total Costs
   Target Profit = (Selling Price * Quantity) - (Fixed Costs + Variable Costs * Quantity)
   $20,000 = ($20 * Q) - ($50,000 + $10 * Q)
   $20,000 = $20Q - $50,000 - $10Q
   $20,000 = $10Q - $50,000
   $70,000 = $10Q
   Q = **7,000 units**

c) New Fixed Costs = $50,000 + $5,000 = $55,000
   New Variable Cost per Widget = $10 - $2 = $8
   New Break-Even Point (Units) = New Fixed Costs / (Sales Price per Unit - New Variable Cost per Unit)
   = $55,000 / ($20 - $8)
   = $55,000 / $12
   = **4,583.33 units** (approximately 4,584 units)

   *Benefit Analysis:* The new break-even point is lower (4,584 vs. 5,000 units). This means the company needs to sell fewer widgets to cover its costs, making it potentially more profitable, especially if sales volumes are around the original break-even point. The VE change appears beneficial from a break-even perspective.

**Question 2:**

A product's current design has a perceived value of $50. It costs $30 to manufacture, and it performs a primary function effectively. A Value Engineering team proposes a design change that reduces manufacturing cost to $20. However, they estimate that this change will slightly reduce the durability of the product, impacting the primary function by 10%.
a) Calculate the current value of the product.
b) Calculate the value of the product after the VE change, assuming the reduced durability corresponds to a 10% reduction in the "function" score.
c) Based on the value calculation, would you recommend the VE change?

**Answer 2:**

a) Current Value = Function / Cost
   Let's assume the current level of function is 100 units (representing "effective performance").
   Current Value = 100 / $30 = **3.33 (units of function per dollar)**

b) Value after VE change:
   New Cost = $20
   New Function = 100 * (1 - 0.10) = 90 units
   New Value = New Function / New Cost
   New Value = 90 / $20 = **4.50 (units of function per dollar)**

c) *Recommendation:* Based on the calculated value, the VE change is recommended. The value per dollar (or functional output per dollar spent) increases significantly from 3.33 to 4.50. Even though the absolute function score decreases, the cost reduction is more substantial, leading to a net improvement in overall value.

### **6. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Value is not just low cost:** It's about achieving the required function at the lowest possible cost.
*   **Function is key:** Always define and understand the functions of a product or process before attempting to reduce costs.
*   **Holistic approach:** Consider all costs, including life cycle costs, not just manufacturing costs.
*   **Customer perspective:** Value is ultimately determined by what the customer is willing to pay for the functions provided.
*   **Trade-offs are common:** VE often involves making intelligent trade-offs between cost, function, and performance.
*   **Quantify where possible:** Use metrics and analysis to support decisions about cost and value improvements.
*   **VE is a systematic process:** It involves a series of steps designed to analyze and improve value.
