# RF Engineering: Module 3 - Amplifier Design using S-Parameters

## Topic: Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) Design

### 1. Introduction to Low Noise Amplifiers (LNAs)

*   **Definition:** A Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) is an amplifier designed to add as little noise as possible to an input signal.
*   **Purpose:** LNAs are crucial in RF systems as they are typically the first active component in the receive chain. Their primary function is to amplify weak incoming signals from antennas while minimizing the amplification of unwanted noise.
*   **Importance:** The noise figure of the LNA sets the overall noise performance of the entire receive system. A poor LNA can render even subsequent high-gain, low-distortion stages useless.

### 2. Noise in RF Systems

Before diving into LNA design, understanding the sources and characterization of noise is essential.

*   **Sources of Noise:**
    *   **Thermal Noise (Johnson-Nyquist Noise):** Generated by the random motion of charge carriers in conductors due to temperature. It's inherent to all resistive components.
        *   *Formula:* $N = kTB$, where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant ($1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K), $T$ is the absolute temperature in Kelvin, and $B$ is the bandwidth in Hz.
    *   **Shot Noise:** Generated by the random arrival of charge carriers (electrons or holes) across potential barriers in semiconductor devices (e.g., diodes, transistors).
    *   **Flicker Noise (1/f Noise):** Occurs at lower frequencies and is related to surface imperfections and charge trapping in semiconductor devices. Typically less significant at typical RF operating frequencies for LNAs.

*   **Characterizing Noise:**
    *   **Noise Voltage and Current:** Noise can be represented as an equivalent noise voltage source in series with a component or a noise current source in parallel.
    *   **Noise Power:** Noise power is often expressed in Watts or dBm.
    *   **Noise Figure (NF):** A measure of the degradation of the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) caused by a component. It's the ratio of the input SNR to the output SNR.
        *   *Formula:* $NF = \frac{SNR_{in}}{SNR_{out}}$
        *   *In dB:* $NF_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(NF)$
        *   *Ideal LNA:* NF = 1 (0 dB)
        *   *Practical LNA:* NF > 1 (positive dB value)
        *   **(Pozar, 2/e, Chapter 3):** Discusses noise in terms of noise equivalent temperature and noise factor. The noise factor ($F$) is $SNR_{in}/SNR_{out}$.
    *   **Noise Temperature ($T_e$):** An alternative way to express the noise performance of a device. It's the temperature of a fictitious resistor that would generate the same noise power as the device.
        *   *Relationship with Noise Figure:* $T_e = T_0 (NF - 1)$, where $T_0$ is the standard reference temperature (usually 290 K or 17°C).
        *   *Advantage:* Noise temperature is additive for cascaded stages when the source impedance is matched.

### 3. S-Parameters for LNA Design

S-parameters are fundamental to designing RF circuits, including LNAs, as they describe the input/output relationships of a two-port network under matched conditions.

*   **Definition of S-Parameters:**
    *   Represent the ratio of outgoing waves to incoming waves at each port of a network, assuming the network is terminated in a characteristic impedance ($Z_0$, typically 50 $\Omega$).
    *   For a two-port network, the S-parameter matrix is:
        $$
        \begin{bmatrix} V_{out1}^{-} \\ V_{out2}^{-} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} \\ S_{21} & S_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} V_{in1}^{+} \\ V_{in2}^{+} \end{bmatrix}
        $$
        Where:
        *   $V_{in}^{+}$ and $V_{out}^{-}$ are incident and reflected voltage waves, respectively.
        *   Subscripts 1 and 2 refer to port 1 (input) and port 2 (output).

*   **Key S-Parameters for LNA Design:**
    *   $S_{11}$: Input reflection coefficient. Indicates how well the input is matched to the source impedance. For an LNA, a low $|S_{11}|$ is desired for good input matching and minimal signal reflection.
    *   $S_{21}$: Forward transmission coefficient. Represents the gain of the amplifier. A high $|S_{21}|$ is desired for amplification.
    *   $S_{22}$: Output reflection coefficient. Indicates how well the output is matched to the load impedance. A low $|S_{22}|$ is desired for good output matching.
    *   $S_{12}$: Reverse transmission coefficient. Represents backward coupling from output to input. A low $|S_{12}|$ is desired for stability and isolation.

*   **Gain and Stability:**
    *   **Transducer Power Gain ($G_T$):** The ratio of power delivered to the load to the power available from the source.
        *   $G_T = |S_{21}|^2 \frac{1 - |\Gamma_S|^2}{1 - |\Gamma_{in}|^2}$, where $\Gamma_S$ is the source reflection coefficient and $\Gamma_{in}$ is the input reflection coefficient of the amplifier.
        *   When the amplifier is matched to the source ($\Gamma_{in} = \Gamma_S$), $G_T = |S_{21}|^2$.
    *   **Available Power Gain ($G_A$):** The ratio of power delivered to the load to the power available from the source when the amplifier is terminated in its conjugate match.
    *   **Maximum Available Gain (MAG) / Unilateral Gain (U):** Used for unmatched situations to assess the maximum possible gain.
    *   **Stability:** An amplifier is stable if it does not oscillate when connected to any passive source and load impedances.
        *   **Stability Factors:**
            *   **Rollett's Stability Factor ($K$):** $K = \frac{1 - |S_{11}|^2 - |S_{22}|^2 + |\Delta|^2}{2 |S_{12} S_{21}|}$, where $\Delta = S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}S_{21}$.
            *   **Conditions for unconditional stability:** $K > 1$ and $|\Delta| < 1$.
            *   If these conditions are not met, the amplifier is potentially unstable, and stability circles (regions of operation that guarantee stability) need to be considered.

### 4. Noise Parameters of a Two-Port Network

To design an LNA, we need to characterize the noise contribution of the active device.

*   **Noise Parameters:** For a transistor, the noise performance can be characterized by four noise parameters:
    *   **Minimum Noise Figure ($NF_{min}$):** The lowest noise figure achievable for the device at a given frequency, achieved at a specific source admittance ($Y_{opt}$).
    *   **Optimal Source Admittance ($Y_{opt}$):** The complex conjugate of the source admittance that results in the minimum noise figure.
    *   **Noise Resistance ($R_n$):** A resistance associated with the device that contributes to noise. It can also be expressed as a noise correlation matrix.
    *   **Equivalent Noise Resistance ($R_N$) or Noise Resistance ($r_n$):** Related to the magnitude of the noise current or voltage source.
    *   **(Pozar, 2/e, Chapter 3):** Introduces the noise parameters ($NF_{min}$, $Y_{opt}$, $R_n$) and the formula for noise figure as a function of source admittance.

*   **Noise Figure as a Function of Source Admittance:**
    *   $NF = NF_{min} + \frac{R_n}{G_S} |Y_S - Y_{opt}|^2$
    *   Where:
        *   $NF$ is the noise figure for a given source admittance $Y_S$.
        *   $NF_{min}$ is the minimum noise figure.
        *   $R_n$ is the noise resistance.
        *   $G_S$ is the real part of the source admittance ($Y_S = G_S + jB_S$).
        *   $Y_{opt}$ is the optimal source admittance.

*   **Noise Figure Circles (Ni Circles):**
    *   Lines of constant noise figure on the Smith Chart, plotted for a given device.
    *   The center and radius of these circles are determined by $Y_{opt}$ and $NF_{min}$, $R_n$.
    *   The smallest circle (radius 0) is centered at $Y_{opt}$ and corresponds to $NF_{min}$.
    *   **(Carr, 2/e, Chapter 4):** Explains plotting and using noise figure circles for optimal source impedance matching for minimum noise.

### 5. LNA Design Process

The design process involves selecting a suitable active device and then matching the source and load impedances to achieve the desired performance (low NF and good gain).

*   **Step 1: Device Selection**
    *   Choose a transistor (e.g., MOSFET, MESFET, HEMT) that offers low noise parameters ($NF_{min}$, $Y_{opt}$) at the desired operating frequency.
    *   Consider other device parameters like gain, linearity, breakdown voltage, and power consumption.
    *   **(Pozar, 2/e, Chapter 7):** Discusses the selection of active devices for low-noise applications, highlighting the advantages of devices like HEMTs.

*   **Step 2: Determine Optimal Source Impedance for Noise**
    *   Use the device's noise parameters ($NF_{min}$, $Y_{opt}$) to identify the source impedance that minimizes the noise figure.
    *   This is often represented by the optimal source reflection coefficient $\Gamma_{opt}$.
        *   $\Gamma_{opt} = \frac{Z_{opt} - Z_0}{Z_{opt} + Z_0}$, where $Z_{opt}$ is the optimal source impedance.

*   **Step 3: Source Matching Network Design**
    *   Design a passive matching network (e.g., using stubs, transmission lines, or lumped elements) to transform the actual source impedance ($Z_S$, usually 50 $\Omega$) to the optimal source impedance ($Z_{opt}$) seen by the transistor.
    *   **Objective:** To achieve minimum noise figure. This might involve sacrificing some input match ($|S_{11}|$) or gain.
    *   **Smith Chart Usage:** The Smith Chart is indispensable for designing matching networks.
        *   Plot $Z_{opt}$ or $\Gamma_{opt}$.
        *   Add series or shunt elements (L, C, transmission lines) to move towards the desired impedance.
        *   **(Ludwig & Reinhold, 2/e, Chapter 5):** Provides detailed techniques for impedance matching using Smith Charts and various circuit configurations.

*   **Step 4: Determine Optimal Load Impedance for Gain**
    *   After matching for noise, the next step is often to maximize the power gain.
    *   The load impedance that maximizes the available power gain ($Z_{L,opt}$) is the conjugate of the output impedance of the amplifier ($Z_{out}$), when the input is matched to the source impedance.
    *   More practically, for maximum gain, the load impedance is chosen to be the conjugate of the transistor's output impedance ($Z_{out} = Z_{22}'$ after source matching).
    *   The reflection coefficient for maximum gain, $\Gamma_{optG}$, is related to $S_{22}^*$.

*   **Step 5: Load Matching Network Design**
    *   Design a matching network to transform the actual load impedance ($Z_L$, usually 50 $\Omega$) to the optimal load impedance ($Z_{L,opt}$) for maximum gain.
    *   **Trade-off:** There's often a trade-off between achieving minimum noise figure and maximum gain. The source match for minimum noise might not result in the highest gain, and vice-versa.
    *   **Stability Consideration:** Ensure that the chosen matching networks maintain amplifier stability.
    *   **(Pozar, 2/e, Chapter 7):** Discusses the interdependency of noise, gain, and VSWR matching, emphasizing the need to define design priorities.

*   **Step 6: Stability Analysis and Enhancement**
    *   After designing the matching networks, verify the stability of the amplifier using the stability factors ($K$ and $|\Delta|$).
    *   If the amplifier is potentially unstable, add stabilizing components or adjust matching networks to ensure operation well within the stable region.
    *   Common stabilization techniques include:
        *   Adding series resistors at the input or output.
        *   Using feedback networks (e.g., capacitive feedback from output to input).
        *   **(Radmanesh, 2/e, Chapter 5):** Explains stability analysis using gain circles, noise circles, and the implementation of stabilization techniques.

*   **Step 7: Iteration and Optimization**
    *   The design process is often iterative. Simulate the circuit using RF design software (e.g., ADS, Microwave Office) and adjust matching networks and potentially the active device to meet all performance specifications.
    *   **S-Parameter Simulations:** Predict gain, input/output match (VSWR, $S_{11}, S_{22}$), isolation ($S_{12}$), and noise figure.

### 6. Noise Figure vs. Gain Trade-off

*   **The Fundamental Trade-off:** It is generally impossible to simultaneously achieve the absolute minimum noise figure and the maximum gain by simply matching the source and load impedances to their respective optimal values.
    *   The source impedance that yields $NF_{min}$ is usually different from the source impedance that yields maximum gain.
    *   Similarly, the load impedance for $NF_{min}$ might differ from the load impedance for maximum gain.
*   **Design Priority:** The designer must prioritize.
    *   **For sensitive receivers:** Noise figure is the primary concern. The source is matched for $NF_{min}$. The output is then matched for maximum gain with the input already set for low noise.
    *   **For transmit amplifiers (where noise is less critical):** Gain and output power might be prioritized.
*   **(Rohde & Newkirk, 2nd Ed., Chapter 5):** Discusses this trade-off in detail, emphasizing how design objectives dictate the choice of matching impedances.

### 7. Examples of LNA Architectures

*   **Common Source/Drain (MOSFETs) or Common Emitter (BJTs) LNA:** A basic amplifier stage.
*   **Common Gate (MOSFETs) or Common Base (BJTs) LNA:** Offers good input matching (low $S_{11}$) inherently, which can simplify matching for noise.
*   **Cascode LNA:** Two transistors in series. The top transistor isolates the output from the input, reducingMiller capacitance and improving stability and gain. It also offers good linearity and isolation.
*   **Common Source/Emitter with Inductive Degeneration:** Adding an inductor in the source/emitter path can provide a real part to the input impedance, aiding in achieving a good input match for noise.
*   **(Bowick, Ajluni, & Blyler, 2nd Ed., Chapter 6):** Provides practical examples and schematics of various LNA configurations, including common source and cascode designs.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **NF defines the system's sensitivity.** A lower NF is crucial for weak signal detection.
*   **The first stage determines the overall NF.** Subsequent stages must have significantly lower NF or higher gain to not degrade the system NF.
*   **Stability is paramount.** An oscillating amplifier is useless. Always verify stability after matching.
*   **S-parameters and noise parameters are key.** They are obtained from datasheets or measurements.
*   **Smith Chart is your best friend** for impedance matching and visualizing the design process.
*   **There's a trade-off between noise and gain.** Prioritize based on application requirements.
*   **Passive components (inductors, capacitors, transmission lines) are used for matching.** Their parasitic effects become significant at RF.
*   **Device parasitics** (e.g., parasitic capacitance, resistance) influence the achievable performance.

### 9. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** A two-port network has the following S-parameters at 1 GHz:
$S_{11} = 0.5 \angle 30^\circ$, $S_{12} = 0.1 \angle 40^\circ$, $S_{21} = 2.0 \angle 60^\circ$, $S_{22} = 0.4 \angle 20^\circ$.
Calculate the transducer power gain ($G_T$) if the amplifier is connected to a source with $\Gamma_S = 0.2 \angle -50^\circ$ and a load of $50 \Omega$. Assume $Z_0 = 50 \Omega$.

**Answer:**
First, calculate the input reflection coefficient $\Gamma_{in}$:
$\Gamma_{in} = S_{11} + \frac{S_{21}S_{12}\Gamma_L}{1 - S_{22}\Gamma_L}$
We need $\Gamma_L$, which is for the load impedance $Z_L = 50 \Omega$. Since $Z_0 = 50 \Omega$, $Z_L = Z_0$, so $\Gamma_L = 0$.
Therefore, $\Gamma_{in} = S_{11} + 0 = S_{11} = 0.5 \angle 30^\circ$.

Now calculate the transducer power gain:
$G_T = |S_{21}|^2 \frac{1 - |\Gamma_S|^2}{1 - |\Gamma_{in}|^2}$
$|\Gamma_S| = 0.2$, $|\Gamma_{in}| = |S_{11}| = 0.5$
$|S_{21}| = 2.0$

$G_T = (2.0)^2 \frac{1 - (0.2)^2}{1 - (0.5)^2}$
$G_T = 4 \frac{1 - 0.04}{1 - 0.25}$
$G_T = 4 \frac{0.96}{0.75}$
$G_T = 4 \times 1.28 = 5.12$

In dB: $G_{T_{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(5.12) \approx 7.09 \text{ dB}$.

**Question 2:** What are the conditions for unconditional stability of a two-port network?

**Answer:**
For unconditional stability, the following two conditions must be met:
1.  $K > 1$ (Rollett's Stability Factor is greater than 1)
2.  $|\Delta| < 1$ (The magnitude of the determinant of the S-parameter matrix is less than 1)

Where $\Delta = S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}S_{21}$.

**Question 3:** If an LNA has $NF_{min} = 0.5$ dB and $T_0 = 290$ K, what is its minimum noise temperature ($T_{e,min}$)?

**Answer:**
First, convert $NF_{min}$ from dB to a linear factor:
$NF_{min, linear} = 10^{\frac{NF_{min, dB}}{10}} = 10^{\frac{0.5}{10}} = 10^{0.05} \approx 1.122$

Now, use the formula $T_{e,min} = T_0 (NF_{min, linear} - 1)$:
$T_{e,min} = 290 \text{ K} (1.122 - 1)$
$T_{e,min} = 290 \text{ K} (0.122) \approx 35.38 \text{ K}$

**Question 4:** Describe the primary goal of an LNA design and why the first stage is critical in a receiver chain.

**Answer:**
The primary goal of an LNA design is to amplify a weak input signal with the minimum possible addition of noise. This is critical because the LNA is typically the first active component in a receiver chain. Any noise introduced by the LNA will be amplified by subsequent stages, significantly degrading the overall signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the received signal. Therefore, the LNA's noise figure (NF) directly dictates the ultimate sensitivity of the receiver.
