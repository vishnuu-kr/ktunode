# RF Engineering: Module 1: RF Circuit Introduction

## Topic: RF Behaviour of Resistors, Inductors, and Capacitors

This module introduces the fundamental concepts of RF circuits by examining how passive components – resistors, inductors, and capacitors – behave differently at radio frequencies compared to their behavior at lower, DC frequencies. Understanding these parasitic effects is crucial for designing effective RF circuits.

---

### 1. Introduction to RF Circuit Behaviour (K2 - CO1, CO2)

At low frequencies, ideal circuit component models are often sufficient. However, as frequencies increase into the RF and microwave ranges, parasitic effects within these components become significant and can no longer be ignored. These parasitics can drastically alter the intended circuit performance.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Parasitic Elements:** Unwanted or unintentional R, L, and C elements that exist in real-world components due to their physical construction.
*   **Frequency Dependence:** The impedance and behavior of passive components become strongly dependent on frequency at RF.
*   **Ideal vs. Real Components:** Understanding the deviation of real components from their ideal models is essential.

**Reference:**

*   *RF Circuit Design: Theory & Applications* by Ludwig & Reinhold: Emphasizes the departure of real components from ideal models at high frequencies and the need to account for parasitic inductance and capacitance.
*   *Microwave and RF Design of Wireless Systems* by Pozar: Introduces the concept of distributed effects and how they become dominant at microwave frequencies, necessitating different modeling techniques.

---

### 2. RF Behaviour of Resistors (K2 - CO2)

While a resistor is intended to provide a constant resistance, real resistors exhibit parasitic inductance and capacitance due to their physical construction.

**2.1. Ideal Resistor:**

*   Impedance: $Z_R = R$ (constant, independent of frequency)
*   Behavior: Dissipates energy as heat.

**2.2. Real Resistor at RF:**

*   **Parasitic Inductance ($L_p$):**
    *   **Origin:** Inductance arises from the magnetic field generated by current flowing through the resistive element and its leads. This is especially true for wirewound resistors. Metal film and carbon composite resistors generally have lower parasitic inductance.
    *   **Effect:** Adds an inductive reactance ($X_L = \omega L_p$) in series with the ideal resistance.
    *   **Model:** A real resistor can be modeled as an ideal resistor ($R$) in series with a small parasitic inductance ($L_p$) and a parasitic capacitance ($C_p$).
    *   **Self-Resonant Frequency (SRF):** The frequency at which the parasitic inductance and capacitance form a resonant circuit. Above SRF, the component behaves inductively.
        $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_p C_p}}$
*   **Parasitic Capacitance ($C_p$):**
    *   **Origin:** Capacitance exists between different parts of the resistive element and its leads, and between the component and its surroundings (e.g., PCB traces, ground plane).
    *   **Effect:** Adds a capacitive reactance ($X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C_p}$) in parallel with the series combination of $R$ and $L_p$. More commonly, it's modeled in parallel with the effective resistance of the component at lower frequencies.
    *   **Model:** Often modeled as a series R-L, with a parallel capacitance to ground or across the terminals. For high-frequency applications, it's better to think of the parallel capacitance between the terminals of the resistor element.

**2.3. Modeling a Real Resistor:**

A common model for a real resistor at RF is an ideal resistor $R$ in series with a parasitic inductance $L_p$, and a parasitic capacitance $C_p$ in parallel with the series combination.

$$Z_{resistor} = R + j\omega L_p + \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$$

However, for many applications, a simplified model is used:

*   **Series Model:** $Z_{resistor} \approx R + j\omega L_p$ (when $C_p$ is insignificant or at frequencies below SRF where inductive behavior dominates).
*   **Parallel Model:** $Z_{resistor} \approx R || \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$ (when $L_p$ is insignificant or at frequencies above SRF where capacitive behavior dominates). A better parallel model accounts for the inductive nature: $Z_{resistor} \approx (R+j\omega L_p) || \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$.

**2.4. Practical Considerations:**

*   **SMD Resistors:** Surface-mount device (SMD) resistors generally have lower parasitic inductance and capacitance compared to axial-lead resistors due to their shorter lead lengths.
*   **Wirewound Resistors:** These are generally avoided in RF circuits above a few MHz due to their significant parasitic inductance.
*   **Power Dissipation:** At RF, current distribution within the resistive element can become non-uniform (skin effect), leading to increased effective resistance and power dissipation.

**Important Point to Remember:** The self-resonant frequency (SRF) is a critical parameter for resistors. Above the SRF, the resistor behaves like an inductor, which can disrupt circuit operation.

**Example:**

Consider a 1 k$\Omega$ resistor with $L_p = 10$ nH and $C_p = 0.5$ pF.
Calculate its SRF.

$SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_p C_p}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(10 \times 10^{-9} H)(0.5 \times 10^{-12} F)}}$
$SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{5 \times 10^{-21}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 7.07 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 2.25 \text{ GHz}$

At frequencies below 2.25 GHz, the resistor might exhibit capacitive behavior (due to $C_p$) or resistive behavior (if $j\omega L_p$ is small). Above 2.25 GHz, it will primarily behave as an inductor.

### 3. RF Behaviour of Inductors (K2 - CO2)

Inductors are essential for filtering, impedance matching, and creating resonant circuits. At RF, their ideal behavior is significantly affected by parasitic elements.

**3.1. Ideal Inductor:**

*   Impedance: $Z_L = j\omega L$ (purely inductive reactance)
*   Behavior: Stores energy in a magnetic field.

**3.2. Real Inductor at RF:**

*   **Parasitic Capacitance ($C_p$):**
    *   **Origin:** This is the dominant parasitic for inductors. It arises from the capacitance between adjacent turns of the coil, between layers of windings, and between the coil winding and any core material or shield. This inter-winding capacitance is often referred to as "inter-turn capacitance" or "winding capacitance."
    *   **Effect:** Acts as a capacitor in parallel with the inductor's series resistance and inductance.
    *   **Model:** A real inductor is modeled as an ideal inductor ($L$) in series with a parasitic resistance ($R_s$ - representing winding resistance and eddy current losses) and a parallel parasitic capacitance ($C_p$).
*   **Parasitic Resistance ($R_s$):**
    *   **Origin:** Primarily due to the DC resistance of the wire. At RF, this resistance increases due to the **skin effect** (current flowing on the surface of the conductor) and **proximity effect** (current distribution being affected by the magnetic fields of adjacent conductors).
    *   **Effect:** Reduces the quality factor (Q) of the inductor.
*   **Core Losses:** If a magnetic core (ferrite, iron powder) is used, core losses (hysteresis, eddy currents) can significantly affect the inductor's performance and introduce frequency-dependent resistance.
*   **Self-Resonant Frequency (SRF):** The frequency at which the inductor's inductance resonates with its parallel parasitic capacitance.
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L C_p}}$
    Above SRF, the inductor behaves like a capacitor.

**3.3. Modeling a Real Inductor:**

The most common model for a real inductor at RF is an ideal inductor $L$ in series with a parasitic resistance $R_s$, with a parasitic capacitance $C_p$ in parallel with this series combination.

$$Z_{inductor} = R_s + j\omega L + \frac{1}{j\omega C_p} || (R_s + j\omega L)$$

A simplified and often used model is:

$$Z_{inductor} = R_s + j\omega L + \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$$ (This assumes $C_p$ is in series, which is less accurate than parallel $C_p$. The parallel model is more representative)

A more accurate simplified model captures the parallel nature of $C_p$:

$$Z_{inductor} = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{R_s + j\omega L} + j\omega C_p}}$$
When $R_s \ll \omega L$, this simplifies to:
$$Z_{inductor} \approx R_s + j\omega L || \frac{1}{j\omega C_p} = R_s + j\omega L \left( \frac{1}{1 - \omega^2 LC_p} \right)$$
Near resonance ($\omega^2 LC_p \approx 1$), the impedance becomes very high.

**3.4. Quality Factor (Q):**

The Q-factor of an inductor is a measure of its efficiency and is defined as the ratio of its stored energy to dissipated energy per cycle.

$$Q = \frac{\omega L}{R_s}$$
The parasitic capacitance $C_p$ also affects the effective Q-factor of the inductor *as a resonant circuit*.

**3.5. Practical Considerations:**

*   **Air-core Inductors:** Generally have lower parasitic capacitance and are preferred for higher frequencies if high inductance is not required.
*   **Ferrite/Iron Powder Core Inductors:** Offer higher inductance values in smaller sizes but have higher losses and lower SRFs due to the core material.
*   **SMD Inductors:** Offer advantages in terms of parasitic reduction due to shorter physical dimensions.
*   **Shielding:** Shielded inductors help reduce radiation and coupling to other components, which can be important in sensitive RF circuits.

**Important Point to Remember:** The self-resonant frequency (SRF) is paramount for inductors. Above SRF, an inductor behaves capacitively, which is the opposite of its intended function and can cause circuit instability.

**Example:**

Consider an inductor with $L = 100$ nH, $R_s = 5 \Omega$, and $C_p = 1$ pF.
Calculate its SRF.
Calculate its Q-factor at 500 MHz.

$SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L C_p}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(100 \times 10^{-9} H)(1 \times 10^{-12} F)}}$
$SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{100 \times 10^{-21}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 10 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 1.59 \text{ GHz}$

At 500 MHz (0.5 GHz):
$Q = \frac{\omega L}{R_s} = \frac{2\pi f L}{R_s} = \frac{2\pi (500 \times 10^6 Hz)(100 \times 10^{-9} H)}{5 \Omega}$
$Q = \frac{2\pi \times 50}{5} = 20\pi \approx 62.8$

### 4. RF Behaviour of Capacitors (K2 - CO2)

Capacitors are used for filtering, decoupling, and coupling. At RF, they also exhibit parasitic effects.

**4.1. Ideal Capacitor:**

*   Impedance: $Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}$ (purely capacitive reactance)
*   Behavior: Stores energy in an electric field.

**4.2. Real Capacitor at RF:**

*   **Equivalent Series Inductance (ESL):**
    *   **Origin:** Inductance arises from the physical construction of the capacitor, particularly from the leads, plates, and internal connections. For multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs) and chip capacitors, the ESL is often very low due to their compact construction and short current paths.
    *   **Effect:** Acts as an inductor in series with the ideal capacitor.
*   **Equivalent Series Resistance (ESR):**
    *   **Origin:** Resistance comes from the dielectric material, the conductive plates, and the connections. It accounts for dielectric losses and conductor losses.
    *   **Effect:** Dissipates energy as heat, reducing the Q-factor.
*   **Self-Resonant Frequency (SRF):** The frequency at which the capacitor's capacitance resonates with its series parasitic inductance (ESL).
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{ESL \cdot C}}$
    Above SRF, the capacitor behaves inductively.

**4.3. Modeling a Real Capacitor:**

A real capacitor is modeled as an ideal capacitor $C$ in series with its Equivalent Series Inductance (ESL) and Equivalent Series Resistance (ESR).

$$Z_{capacitor} = ESR + j\omega ESL + \frac{1}{j\omega C}$$

**4.4. Quality Factor (Q):**

For a capacitor, the Q-factor is defined as:

$$Q = \frac{1}{\omega C \cdot ESR}$$
A higher Q-factor indicates lower losses.

**4.5. Practical Considerations:**

*   **Capacitor Type:**
    *   **Ceramic Capacitors (MLCCs):** Generally have very low ESL and ESR and are excellent for RF applications. However, Class 2 ceramics (e.g., X7R, Y5V) can have capacitance values that vary significantly with DC bias voltage and temperature. Class 1 ceramics (e.g., C0G/NP0) have stable capacitance but lower dielectric constants.
    *   **Tantalum Capacitors:** Have higher ESR and ESL compared to ceramics and are not ideal for very high frequencies.
    *   **Electrolytic Capacitors:** Have very high ESR and ESL and are unsuitable for RF circuits.
    *   **Film Capacitors:** Offer good stability but can have higher ESL than ceramics.
*   **Decoupling Capacitors:** Placed close to active components (ICs) to provide a low-impedance path to ground for high-frequency noise. A combination of capacitors with different values (e.g., 100 nF and 10 nF) is often used to cover a wide frequency range.
*   **Series Resonance:** At SRF, the capacitor exhibits minimum impedance (ideally zero, but limited by ESR). Above SRF, it acts as an inductor.

**Important Point to Remember:** The self-resonant frequency (SRF) is critical for capacitors. Above the SRF, a capacitor behaves inductively, making it ineffective for its intended capacitive function and potentially causing circuit instability. For decoupling, a capacitor's effectiveness is determined by its impedance at the frequency of the noise, which is heavily influenced by ESL and ESR.

**Example:**

Consider a capacitor with $C = 10$ nF, $ESR = 0.02 \Omega$, and $ESL = 2$ nH.
Calculate its SRF.
Calculate its Q-factor at 100 MHz.

$SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{ESL \cdot C}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(2 \times 10^{-9} H)(10 \times 10^{-9} F)}}$
$SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{20 \times 10^{-18}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 4.47 \times 10^{-9}} \approx 35.5 \text{ MHz}$

At 100 MHz (0.1 GHz):
$Q = \frac{1}{\omega C \cdot ESR} = \frac{1}{2\pi f C \cdot ESR} = \frac{1}{2\pi (100 \times 10^6 Hz)(10 \times 10^{-9} F)(0.02 \Omega)}$
$Q = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 10^{-1}} = \frac{10}{2\pi} \approx 1.59$

### 5. RF Behavior of Common Components and Applications (K2 - CO1, CO2)

Understanding the parasitic behavior of R, L, and C is crucial for their application in RF circuits.

**5.1. Impedance Matching:**

*   **Goal:** To transfer maximum power between circuits or to minimize reflections. This is achieved by making the impedance of the source equal to the complex conjugate of the load impedance.
*   **Role of Parasitics:** Parasitic inductance and capacitance in components can significantly alter the impedance of a network, making simple R-L-C matching networks more complex. Techniques like using series/shunt stubs or lumped element networks are employed, considering the parasitic behavior.

**5.2. Filters:**

*   **Low-Pass Filter (LPF):** Typically uses inductors in series and capacitors in shunt. At RF, the parasitic inductance of capacitors and parasitic capacitance of inductors can shift the cutoff frequency and degrade stopband rejection.
*   **High-Pass Filter (HPF):** Typically uses capacitors in series and inductors in shunt. Similar parasitic effects impact performance.
*   **Band-Pass Filter (BPF):** Uses combinations of L and C to create resonant circuits. The SRF of these components is critical in determining the actual center frequency and bandwidth of the filter.
*   **Quality Factor (Q) of Filter Components:** High-Q inductors and capacitors are essential for creating sharp, low-loss filters.

**5.3. Decoupling:**

*   **Purpose:** To supply a stable DC voltage to active devices by shunting high-frequency noise to ground.
*   **Capacitor Choice:** Low ESL capacitors are crucial. A combination of capacitors (e.g., a larger value for bulk decoupling and a smaller value with lower ESL for high-frequency decoupling) is often used. The smaller capacitor should have an SRF well above the noise frequencies it's meant to suppress.

**5.4. Resonance:**

*   **Parallel Resonance:** Occurs when an inductor and capacitor are placed in parallel. The impedance is very high at the resonant frequency ($f_r = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$). Real components have finite Q, so impedance is high but not infinite.
*   **Series Resonance:** Occurs when an inductor and capacitor are placed in series. The impedance is very low (ideally zero, limited by ESR) at the resonant frequency.
*   **Self-Resonance:** As discussed, SRF is the frequency where the parasitic inductance and capacitance within a single component resonate.

**Reference:**

*   *Secrets of RF Circuit Design* by Carr: Provides practical insights into the effects of parasitics on filter design and decoupling strategies.
*   *Radio-frequency and microwave communication circuits: analysis and design* by Misra: Discusses the impact of parasitics on impedance matching networks and filter synthesis at microwave frequencies.

**Important Point to Remember:** The performance of RF circuits such as filters, impedance matching networks, and decoupling circuits is heavily reliant on selecting components whose parasitic characteristics (SRF, ESL, ESR) are well-understood and managed.

### 7. Practice Questions with Answers

**Answer to Practice Question 1:**

*   **SRF:**
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_p C_p}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(5 \times 10^{-9} H)(0.2 \times 10^{-12} F)}}$
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1 \times 10^{-21}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 3.16 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 5.03 \text{ GHz}$

*   **Impedance at 1 GHz:**
    The resistor can be modeled as $R + j\omega L_p + \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$.
    At 1 GHz:
    $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi (1 \times 10^9) \text{ rad/s}$
    $j\omega L_p = j (2\pi \times 10^9) (5 \times 10^{-9}) = j 10\pi \Omega \approx j 31.4 \Omega$
    $\frac{1}{j\omega C_p} = \frac{1}{j (2\pi \times 10^9) (0.2 \times 10^{-12})} = \frac{1}{j 0.4\pi} \approx \frac{-j}{1.257} \Omega \approx -j 0.795 \Omega$

    The parallel capacitance $C_p$ is often considered in parallel with the series $R+j\omega L_p$. However, if we consider the series model $R + j\omega L_p + \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$:
    $Z \approx 100 \Omega + j 31.4 \Omega - j 0.795 \Omega$
    $Z \approx 100 + j 30.6 \Omega$

    If we use the more common series R-L with parallel C model:
    $Z \approx (100 + j 31.4) || \frac{1}{j 0.795}$
    This calculation is more complex. A simpler approximation: at 1 GHz, the inductive reactance ($31.4 \Omega$) is significant, and the capacitive reactance is much smaller. The overall impedance will be resistive and inductive. The parasitic capacitance's effect would be more prominent closer to resonance. Given that 1 GHz is much lower than the SRF (5.03 GHz), the capacitive path ($1/(j\omega C_p)$) would be very high impedance. Thus, the series R-L approximation is reasonable.
    $Z \approx 100 \Omega + j 31.4 \Omega$

**Answer to Practice Question 2:**

*   **SRF:**
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L C_p}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(50 \times 10^{-9} H)(0.5 \times 10^{-12} F)}}$
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{25 \times 10^{-21}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 5 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 3.18 \text{ GHz}$

*   **Q-factor at 1 GHz:**
    $Q = \frac{\omega L}{R_s} = \frac{2\pi f L}{R_s} = \frac{2\pi (1 \times 10^9 Hz)(50 \times 10^{-9} H)}{3 \Omega}$
    $Q = \frac{2\pi \times 50}{3} = \frac{100\pi}{3} \approx 104.7$

**Answer to Practice Question 3:**

*   **SRF:**
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{ESL \cdot C}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(1 \times 10^{-9} H)(1 \times 10^{-12} F)}}$
    $SRF = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1 \times 10^{-21}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 3.16 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 5.03 \text{ GHz}$

*   **Q-factor at 5 GHz:**
    $Q = \frac{1}{\omega C \cdot ESR} = \frac{1}{2\pi f C \cdot ESR} = \frac{1}{2\pi (5 \times 10^9 Hz)(1 \times 10^{-12} F)(0.05 \Omega)}$
    $Q = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 0.25} = \frac{1}{0.5\pi} = \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.637$

**Answer to Practice Question 4:**

*   **Why low-ESL capacitors for decoupling?**
    Decoupling capacitors are intended to provide a low impedance path for high-frequency noise to ground. The impedance of a capacitor is $Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} + ESR + j\omega ESL$. At high frequencies, the ESL term ($j\omega ESL$) becomes dominant. If the ESL is high, the capacitor's impedance will increase with frequency, and it will no longer provide an effective low-impedance path for high-frequency noise. Low-ESL capacitors maintain low impedance at much higher frequencies.

*   **Consequence of SRF below operating frequency:**
    If a capacitor's SRF is below the operating frequency, it means that at the operating frequency, the capacitor is operating above its self-resonant point. At this point, the parasitic inductance (ESL) dominates over the capacitance. Consequently, the component behaves like an inductor, not a capacitor. It will have a positive reactance ($+j\omega ESL$) rather than a negative capacitive reactance ($-j/\omega C$). This means it will not effectively shunt high-frequency noise to ground as intended, and it can even introduce unwanted inductive behavior into the circuit, potentially leading to instability or signal degradation.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Parasitics are ubiquitous:** Real-world passive components are never ideal at RF frequencies.
*   **SRF is critical:** The self-resonant frequency limits the useful operating range of a component for its intended function. Above SRF, R, L, and C components all exhibit undesirable behaviors (inductive for R and C, capacitive for L).
*   **ESL and ESR:** These are key parasitic parameters for capacitors, directly impacting their high-frequency performance and Q-factor.
*   **$R_s$ and $C_p$:** These are key parasitic parameters for inductors, affecting their Q-factor and SRF.
*   **Component Selection:** Choosing components with appropriate parasitic characteristics for the intended frequency of operation is vital for successful RF circuit design.
*   **Layout Matters:** The physical layout of components on a PCB can significantly influence parasitic capacitance and inductance. Shorter traces and proper grounding techniques are crucial.
