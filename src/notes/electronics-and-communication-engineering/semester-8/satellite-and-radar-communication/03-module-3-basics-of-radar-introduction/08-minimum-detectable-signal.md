# Module 3: Basics of Radar: Introduction - Minimum Detectable Signal

## 1. Introduction to Radar and its Fundamental Purpose

*   **Radar** is an acronym for **RA**dio **D**etection **A**nd **R**anging.
*   Its primary function is to detect the presence of objects (targets) and determine their location, speed, and other characteristics by transmitting radio waves and analyzing the reflected signals.
*   **Key Principle:** Radar transmits electromagnetic energy, and if this energy strikes an object, a portion of it is reflected back towards the radar antenna. The radar receiver detects this reflected signal (echo).
*   **Applications:** Radar is used in a vast array of applications, including air traffic control, weather forecasting, navigation, military surveillance, remote sensing, and even in some consumer devices. (CO3)

## 2. The Radar System Components

A basic radar system typically consists of the following key components:

*   **Transmitter:** Generates the radio frequency (RF) pulse or continuous wave (CW).
*   **Antenna:** Transmits the RF energy into space and receives the reflected echoes.
*   **Receiver:** Amplifies the weak echoes received by the antenna and processes them.
*   **Duplexer:** A switch that allows the same antenna to be used for both transmitting and receiving.
*   **Signal Processor:** Analyzes the received signals to extract information about the target (e.g., range, velocity).
*   **Display:** Presents the processed information to the operator (e.g., PPI - Plan Position Indicator).

## 3. The Radar Equation: A Foundation for Understanding Radar Performance

The **Radar Equation** is a fundamental equation that relates the received signal power to the various parameters of the radar system, the target, and the propagation environment. It allows us to estimate the maximum range at which a radar can detect a target.

### 3.1 Basic Pulsed Radar Equation (for detection of a point target)

The received power ($P_r$) is given by:

$$ P_r = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4 L} $$

Where:
*   $P_r$: Received power (Watts)
*   $P_t$: Transmitted peak power (Watts)
*   $G$: Antenna gain (dimensionless)
*   $\lambda$: Wavelength of the transmitted signal (meters)
*   $\sigma$: Radar cross-section (RCS) of the target (m²)
*   $R$: Range to the target (meters)
*   $L$: Total system losses (including propagation losses, transmission line losses, etc.) (dimensionless, $L \ge 1$)

**(Refer to Skolnik, Chapter 2 for detailed derivation and explanation)**

### 3.2 Key Takeaways from the Radar Equation:

*   **Range Dependence:** The received power decreases with the **fourth power of the range** ($R^4$). This is a critical factor limiting radar detection range. Doubling the range reduces the received power by a factor of 16.
*   **Power Dependence:** Received power is directly proportional to transmitted power ($P_t$).
*   **Antenna Gain:** Received power is proportional to the square of antenna gain ($G^2$). A higher gain antenna focuses energy more effectively.
*   **Wavelength:** Received power is proportional to the square of the wavelength ($\lambda^2$).
*   **Target RCS:** Received power is directly proportional to the target's Radar Cross-Section ($\sigma$). A larger or more reflective target returns a stronger signal.
*   **Losses:** Any system or propagation losses ($L$) degrade the received signal power.

## 4. Minimum Detectable Signal (MDS) - The Threshold of Detection

### 4.1 Definition

The **Minimum Detectable Signal (MDS)**, often denoted as $S_{min}$ or $P_{min}$, is the lowest level of received signal power that the radar receiver can reliably distinguish from noise and reliably declare the presence of a target.

*   It represents the **sensitivity** of the radar receiver.
*   A lower MDS means the radar can detect weaker signals, and thus potentially smaller or more distant targets.

### 4.2 Factors Influencing MDS

The MDS is primarily determined by the **noise** in the radar system and the **detection criteria** (false alarm rate and probability of detection).

#### 4.2.1 Noise in Radar Systems

Noise is any unwanted signal that interferes with the desired echo signal. The primary sources of noise are:

*   **Thermal Noise (Johnson-Nyquist Noise):** Generated by the random thermal motion of electrons in conductive materials within the receiver (e.g., resistors). This is often the dominant noise source.
*   **Atmospheric Noise:** Generated by natural phenomena like lightning.
*   **Man-made Noise:** Interference from other electronic devices.
*   **Internal Receiver Noise:** Noise generated by the active components of the receiver itself (e.g., transistors, vacuum tubes). This is often quantified by the **Noise Figure (NF)** of the receiver.

#### 4.2.2 The Noise Figure (NF)

*   The Noise Figure ($NF$) is a measure of how much the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is degraded by a component or system.
*   It is defined as the ratio of the SNR at the input of the component to the SNR at the output:
    $$ NF = \frac{SNR_{in}}{SNR_{out}} $$
*   Ideally, $NF = 1$ (or 0 dB), meaning no noise is added. In practice, $NF > 1$.
*   A lower Noise Figure indicates a better (quieter) receiver.

#### 4.2.3 Noise Temperature ($T_e$)

*   Another way to express the noise added by a receiver is through its **effective noise temperature** ($T_e$).
*   $T_e$ is the temperature of a hypothetical resistance that would generate the same amount of noise power as the receiver at its input.
*   The relationship between $NF$ and $T_e$ is:
    $$ T_e = T_0 (NF - 1) $$
    Where $T_0$ is the standard reference temperature, typically 290 Kelvin (17°C).

#### 4.2.4 Noise Power ($N$)

The noise power ($N$) in the receiver's bandwidth ($B$) is given by:

$$ N = k T_e B $$

Where:
*   $k$: Boltzmann's constant ($1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K)
*   $T_e$: Effective noise temperature of the receiver (Kelvin)
*   $B$: Receiver bandwidth (Hz)

**(Refer to Skolnik, Chapter 9 for noise analysis and NF)**

### 4.3 The Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

*   The **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)** is a crucial parameter in radar performance. It is the ratio of the desired signal power to the noise power:
    $$ SNR = \frac{S}{N} $$
    Where $S$ is the signal power and $N$ is the noise power.
*   To reliably detect a target, the received signal must be sufficiently above the noise level. This requires a certain minimum SNR.

### 4.4 Minimum Detectable Signal in Terms of Noise and SNR

The MDS is the minimum signal power ($S_{min}$) that provides the required SNR.

$$ S_{min} = (SNR)_{min} \times N $$

Substituting the noise power equation:

$$ S_{min} = (SNR)_{min} \times k T_e B $$

*   $(SNR)_{min}$: The minimum acceptable Signal-to-Noise Ratio to achieve the desired probability of detection and a given false alarm rate. This value depends on the specific detection criterion used.
*   $k$: Boltzmann's constant.
*   $T_e$: Effective noise temperature of the receiver.
*   $B$: Receiver bandwidth.

## 5. The Search Radar Equation and MDS (A More Complete Picture)

While the basic radar equation tells us the received signal power, the **search radar equation** incorporates the noise and detection criteria to determine the maximum range for a given performance.

The search radar equation, for a pulsed radar, can be expressed as:

$$ R_{max}^4 = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 k T_e B (SNR)_{min} L_{sys} L_{atm}} $$

Where:
*   $R_{max}$: Maximum unambiguous range (meters)
*   $P_t$: Transmitted peak power (Watts)
*   $G$: Antenna gain (dimensionless)
*   $\lambda$: Wavelength (meters)
*   $\sigma$: Radar cross-section of the target (m²)
*   $k$: Boltzmann's constant (J/K)
*   $T_e$: Effective noise temperature (Kelvin)
*   $B$: Receiver bandwidth (Hz)
*   $(SNR)_{min}$: Minimum required signal-to-noise ratio (dimensionless)
*   $L_{sys}$: System losses (dimensionless, $L_{sys} \ge 1$)
*   $L_{atm}$: Atmospheric losses (dimensionless, $L_{atm} \ge 1$)

**Connecting MDS to the Search Radar Equation:**

Notice that the term $k T_e B (SNR)_{min}$ represents the minimum detectable signal power, $S_{min}$. So the equation can be rewritten as:

$$ R_{max}^4 = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 S_{min} L_{sys} L_{atm}} $$

This explicitly shows that a lower $S_{min}$ (i.e., a more sensitive radar) leads to a greater maximum detection range ($R_{max}$).

**(Refer to Skolnik, Chapter 2 and 9; Edde, Chapter 4)**

## 6. Factors Affecting $S_{min}$ and $(SNR)_{min}$

### 6.1 Receiver Noise Figure (NF)

*   A lower NF directly leads to a lower $T_e$, which in turn reduces the noise power $N$.
*   For a given required $SNR$, a lower noise level means the minimum detectable signal power $S_{min}$ can be lower.
*   **Example:** A receiver with $NF = 3$ dB ($T_e \approx 580$ K) will have a higher MDS than a receiver with $NF = 1$ dB ($T_e \approx 290$ K), assuming all other factors are equal.

### 6.2 Receiver Bandwidth (B)

*   The bandwidth of the receiver is crucial. It must be wide enough to pass the radar pulse without significant distortion but narrow enough to minimize the noise power.
*   For a simple rectangular pulse of duration $\tau$, the bandwidth is often approximated as $B \approx 1/\tau$.
*   **Trade-off:** A shorter pulse ($B$ is wider) gives better range resolution but increases noise power. A longer pulse ($B$ is narrower) reduces noise power but degrades range resolution.

### 6.3 $(SNR)_{min}$ - Detection Criteria

The required $(SNR)_{min}$ is determined by the desired **Probability of Detection** ($P_d$) and the acceptable **Probability of False Alarm** ($P_{fa}$).

*   **Probability of Detection ($P_d$)**: The probability that the radar will correctly detect a target when it is present. Typically desired to be high (e.g., 90% or 99%).
*   **Probability of False Alarm ($P_{fa}$)**: The probability that the radar will incorrectly declare a target is present when there is no target (i.e., detecting noise as a signal). Typically desired to be very low (e.g., $10^{-6}$ or $10^{-9}$).

**(Refer to Skolnik, Chapter 9 for detection theory and CFAR)**

### 6.4 Detection Techniques (e.g., Constant False Alarm Rate - CFAR)

*   **Simple Threshold Detection:** A fixed threshold is set in the receiver. If the signal level exceeds this threshold, a target is declared. This is susceptible to variations in noise level.
*   **Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) Detectors:** These are more sophisticated processors that adjust the detection threshold dynamically based on the estimated noise and clutter levels in the surrounding range-Doppler cells. This ensures a constant $P_{fa}$ even when noise or clutter conditions change.
*   The specific type of CFAR processor used will influence the required $(SNR)_{min}$ for a given $P_d$ and $P_{fa}$.

## 7. Minimum Detectable Signal in Satellite Communication Context (Though this topic is primarily Radar)

While the core concepts of MDS are derived from radar, similar principles apply to the sensitivity of satellite communication receivers.

*   In satellite communication, the signal received from the satellite at the ground station antenna is often extremely weak due to the vast distance and path losses.
*   The receiver's sensitivity (its ability to detect weak signals) is paramount.
*   The **Minimum Detectable Signal** in satellite communication refers to the minimum received signal power level that the ground station receiver can detect above its internal noise and external interference.
*   This is often expressed in terms of the **$E_b/N_0$ ratio** (energy per bit to noise power spectral density ratio), which is the equivalent of SNR but normalized for the data rate. A higher $E_b/N_0$ indicates a stronger signal relative to noise, leading to a lower bit error rate (BER).
*   The sensitivity of the satellite communication receiver (low noise amplifier - LNA, system noise temperature) directly impacts its MDS.
*   **Link Budget Analysis:** Similar to the radar equation, satellite link budgets are used to calculate the received signal power and determine if it's sufficient for reliable communication, accounting for path loss, antenna gains, noise, and interference.

## 8. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
Which of the following factors will *decrease* the minimum detectable signal of a radar system?
(a) Increasing the receiver's noise figure.
(b) Decreasing the receiver's bandwidth.
(c) Decreasing the required probability of detection.
(d) Increasing the transmitted power.

**Answer 1:**
The correct answer is **(b) Decreasing the receiver's bandwidth.**

*   **Explanation:**
    *   (a) Increasing NF increases noise, thus increasing MDS.
    *   (b) Decreasing bandwidth reduces noise power ($N = k T_e B$), which can lower $S_{min}$ for a given SNR requirement.
    *   (c) Decreasing $P_d$ usually means a lower $(SNR)_{min}$ is needed, which *lowers* $S_{min}$. This is a valid way to decrease MDS, but the question asks which factor *will* decrease it among the options. Bandwidth reduction directly impacts noise power.
    *   (d) Increasing transmitted power improves the SNR, but it doesn't directly change the *minimum detectable signal level itself* which is defined by receiver sensitivity and noise. It improves the *ability to detect* signals at or above the MDS.

Let's re-evaluate option (c). If the goal is to lower $S_{min}$ as a characteristic of the receiver's *sensitivity*, then reducing the *requirement* for $(SNR)_{min}$ is indeed a way to achieve a lower $S_{min}$. However, the question is often interpreted as which factor *inherently* improves the receiver's sensitivity threshold. Bandwidth reduction is a direct way to lower the noise floor. If we consider MDS as the *required signal power*, then a lower $(SNR)_{min}$ requirement means a lower MDS.

Let's rephrase the question to be more precise: "Which of the following factors, when changed, results in a *lower noise floor* for the radar receiver, potentially leading to a lower minimum detectable signal?" In that case, (b) is the clear answer.

If the question implies "Which of the following actions would allow the radar to *detect* weaker signals (lower MDS)?" then both (b) and (c) are valid. However, typically, MDS is a measure of the receiver's inherent sensitivity.

Let's assume the question refers to inherent improvements in receiver sensitivity. In that context, reducing bandwidth is the better answer for impacting the noise floor.

**Question 2:**
The minimum detectable signal ($S_{min}$) is the minimum received signal power required to:
(a) Achieve maximum range.
(b) Detect a target with a specified probability of detection and probability of false alarm.
(c) Operate the radar system efficiently.
(d) Receive a clear signal free from interference.

**Answer 2:**
The correct answer is **(b) Detect a target with a specified probability of detection and probability of false alarm.**

*   **Explanation:** $S_{min}$ is defined by the detection criteria ($P_d$ and $P_{fa}$), which dictate the necessary SNR above the noise floor.

**Question 3:**
If the receiver noise temperature ($T_e$) of a radar is doubled, while all other parameters remain constant, how does the minimum detectable signal ($S_{min}$) change?

**Answer 3:**
The minimum detectable signal ($S_{min}$) will also double.

*   **Explanation:** $S_{min} = (SNR)_{min} \times k T_e B$. Since $(SNR)_{min}$, $k$, and $B$ are constant, $S_{min}$ is directly proportional to $T_e$. If $T_e$ is doubled, $S_{min}$ is doubled.

**Question 4:**
Explain the trade-off between range resolution and minimum detectable signal when choosing the pulse width ($\tau$) for a radar system.

**Answer 4:**
*   **Range Resolution:** Range resolution is the ability to distinguish between two closely spaced targets along the same line of sight. It is approximately half the pulse width ($\Delta R \approx c\tau/2$, where $c$ is the speed of light). A shorter pulse width provides better range resolution.
*   **Minimum Detectable Signal:** The receiver bandwidth ($B$) is typically inversely proportional to the pulse width ($B \approx 1/\tau$). Therefore, a shorter pulse width implies a wider bandwidth.
*   **Trade-off:** A wider bandwidth leads to a higher noise power ($N = k T_e B$). Since $S_{min} = (SNR)_{min} \times N$, a wider bandwidth (shorter pulse) will increase the minimum detectable signal, making the radar less sensitive to weak targets. Conversely, a longer pulse width provides better sensitivity (lower $S_{min}$) but poorer range resolution.

## 9. Important Points to Remember

*   **MDS is about receiver sensitivity.** It's the weakest signal the receiver can reliably detect above its noise.
*   **Noise is the enemy:** Thermal noise, internal receiver noise (quantified by NF and $T_e$), and external interference all contribute to the noise floor.
*   **SNR is key:** A minimum SNR is required for detection, which is driven by desired $P_d$ and $P_{fa}$.
*   **$S_{min}$ is directly proportional to $T_e$ and $B$.** Lowering these improves sensitivity.
*   **Radar Equation links parameters:** Understand how $P_t, G, \lambda, \sigma, R$, and $L$ affect received power and thus detection range.
*   **MDS is a threshold:** It's the signal power level that *just* meets the detection criteria.
