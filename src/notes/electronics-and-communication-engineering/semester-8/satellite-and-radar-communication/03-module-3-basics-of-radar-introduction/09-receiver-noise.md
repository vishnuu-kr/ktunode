# Module 3: Basics of Radar: Introduction - Receiver Noise

## Introduction to Receiver Noise in Radar Systems

This section delves into the fundamental concept of receiver noise in radar systems, a crucial factor that limits the detection of weak target echoes. Understanding receiver noise is essential for designing effective radar systems and analyzing their performance.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO3:** Illustrate Radar Fundamentals like Radar Equation and Applications. (Knowledge Level: K2) - Receiver noise directly impacts the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), a key parameter in the radar equation.
*   **CO1 & CO2:** While primarily focused on radar, the principles of noise in receivers are transferable and applicable to satellite communications, where similar noise sources exist. (Knowledge Level: K2 for illustrating principles).

**Learning Outcomes Covered:**

*   Understanding the concept of noise in a radar receiver.
*   Identifying different sources of receiver noise.
*   Quantifying noise using appropriate parameters.
*   Analyzing the impact of noise on radar performance.

### 1. What is Receiver Noise?

**Definition:** Receiver noise refers to any unwanted random fluctuations or disturbances within the radar receiver that are not part of the desired signal (target echo). These fluctuations can mask weak target signals, making them difficult or impossible to detect.

**Analogy:** Imagine trying to hear a faint whisper in a crowded, noisy room. The background chatter and sounds are analogous to receiver noise, making it harder to pick out the whisper (the target echo).

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Random Fluctuations:** Noise signals are typically random in nature, meaning their amplitude and phase vary unpredictably over time.
*   **Ubiquitous:** Noise is present in all electronic systems, including radar receivers, and cannot be completely eliminated. The goal is to minimize its impact and maximize the desired signal relative to the noise.
*   **Limiting Factor:** Noise is often the primary factor limiting the minimum detectable signal (MDS) in a radar system, and consequently, its maximum detection range.

### 2. Sources of Receiver Noise

Receiver noise can originate from various sources, both internal to the receiver components and external to the radar system.

#### 2.1. Internal Receiver Noise

These are noises generated by the electronic components within the radar receiver itself.

*   **Thermal Noise (Johnson-Nyquist Noise):**
    *   **Description:** This is the most fundamental and pervasive source of noise in electronic circuits. It arises from the random thermal motion of charge carriers (electrons) within conductive materials. As temperature increases, the kinetic energy of these carriers increases, leading to more random movement and thus, more noise voltage.
    *   **Key Characteristics:**
        *   **White Noise:** Thermal noise is generally considered "white noise" because its power is uniformly distributed across all frequencies within a given bandwidth.
        *   **Independent of Signal:** It is independent of the presence of the radar signal.
    *   **Formula (Power Spectral Density):** $P_n(f) = kT$ Watts/Hz (where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ is the absolute temperature in Kelvin, and $f$ is frequency).
    *   **Formula (Noise Power in a Bandwidth B):** $P_n = kTBF$ Watts (where $B$ is the bandwidth in Hz).
    *   **Example:** Resistors, conductors, and even semiconductor materials all exhibit thermal noise.

*   **Shot Noise:**
    *   **Description:** This noise occurs in devices where charge carriers cross a potential barrier, such as in diodes and transistors. The current flow is not perfectly smooth but rather a series of discrete charge carriers arriving at random intervals.
    *   **Key Characteristics:**
        *   **Dependent on DC Current:** The magnitude of shot noise is proportional to the DC current flowing through the device.
        *   **Random Fluctuations:** Similar to thermal noise, it causes random fluctuations in the output current.
    *   **Formula (Noise Current):** $I_n = \sqrt{2qI_{dc}B}$ Amperes (where $q$ is the elementary charge, $I_{dc}$ is the DC current, and $B$ is the bandwidth).
    *   **Example:** Diodes, vacuum tubes, and the base-emitter junction of bipolar transistors.

*   **Flicker Noise (1/f Noise):**
    *   **Description:** This noise is also known as pink noise. Its power spectral density is inversely proportional to frequency ($1/f$). It is often associated with imperfections in semiconductor materials and at interfaces between different materials.
    *   **Key Characteristics:**
        *   **Dominant at Low Frequencies:** Flicker noise is most significant at lower frequencies and becomes less important at the higher frequencies typically used in radar systems.
        *   **Originates in Semiconductor Devices:** Commonly observed in transistors, especially at low frequencies and in devices with surface effects.
    *   **Example:** Found in transistors, especially MOSFETs.

*   **Interference:**
    *   **Description:** While not strictly "noise" in the same sense as thermal or shot noise, man-made interference from other electronic devices (other radars, communication systems, electronic countermeasures) can act as a significant source of unwanted signals that degrade radar performance.
    *   **Key Characteristics:**
        *   **Often Non-Random:** Interference can be periodic or have specific spectral characteristics, unlike random noise.
        *   **External or Internal:** Can be picked up by the antenna or generated by internal circuitry.
    *   **Example:** Jamming signals, signals from nearby radio stations.

#### 2.2. External Noise

These are noises originating from sources outside the radar receiver itself.

*   **Atmospheric Noise:**
    *   **Description:** Generated by natural phenomena in the Earth's atmosphere, such as lightning discharges.
    *   **Key Characteristics:**
        *   **Frequency Dependent:** Varies significantly with frequency and weather conditions.
        *   **Can be Directional:** Stronger from directions of lightning activity.
    *   **Example:** Static crackles heard on AM radios during thunderstorms.

*   **Cosmic Noise:**
    *   **Description:** Radiation originating from outer space, primarily from celestial bodies like stars, galaxies, and quasars.
    *   **Key Characteristics:**
        *   **Generally Weak:** Usually less significant than other noise sources at typical radar frequencies, but can be a factor for very sensitive systems or at specific angles.
        *   **Directional:** Varies depending on the direction the antenna is pointing.
    *   **Example:** Background radiation from the Big Bang.

*   **Galactic Noise:**
    *   **Description:** A subset of cosmic noise specifically from our own Milky Way galaxy.

**Referencing Textbooks:**

*   **Skolnik (Introduction to Radar Systems):** Provides a comprehensive treatment of thermal noise and its impact on radar range. Discusses antenna noise temperature as a way to characterize external noise.
*   **Pratt & Allnutt (Satellite Communications):** While focused on satellites, the principles of noise, particularly thermal noise and its impact on signal reception, are directly applicable to understanding noise in any communication receiver, including radar. They often discuss noise figure, which is a key metric for receiver noise.

### 3. Quantifying Receiver Noise

To effectively analyze the impact of noise, we need ways to quantify its magnitude.

#### 3.1. Noise Power

*   **Description:** The total power contained within the noise signal over a specific bandwidth.
*   **Formula:** $P_n = kTBF$ Watts (as mentioned for thermal noise).
    *   $k$: Boltzmann's constant ($1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K)
    *   $T$: Absolute temperature (Kelvin)
    *   $B$: Bandwidth (Hz)
    *   $F$: Noise factor (dimensionless) - more on this later.

#### 3.2. Noise Voltage and Current

*   **Description:** Noise manifests as random fluctuations in voltage or current within the receiver circuitry.
*   **Relationship to Power:** For a matched load resistance $R$, $P_n = V_n^2 / R = I_n^2 R$.
*   **RMS Values:** Noise voltage and current are typically described by their RMS (Root Mean Square) values, which represent their effective magnitude.

#### 3.3. Noise Figure (F)

*   **Description:** A measure of how much the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) degrades as the signal passes through a receiver. It is defined as the ratio of the input SNR to the output SNR.
*   **Formula:** $F = \frac{\text{SNR}_{\text{in}}}{\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}}$
*   **Interpretation:**
    *   A Noise Figure of 1 (0 dB) indicates a "perfect" receiver with no added noise.
    *   A higher Noise Figure indicates a noisier receiver, meaning it degrades the SNR more.
*   **Relationship to Internal Noise:** The Noise Figure is directly related to the internal noise generated by the receiver.
*   **Calculation (for a single stage):** $F = \frac{P_{n, \text{out}}}{P_{n, \text{in}} \cdot G}$ where $G$ is the gain of the stage. Since $P_{n, \text{in}} = kT_0B$ (where $T_0$ is a reference temperature, typically 290 K) and $P_{n, \text{out}} = G k T_0 B + k T_{equiv} B$ (where $T_{equiv}$ is the equivalent noise temperature of the stage), we can also express:
    $F = 1 + \frac{T_{equiv}}{T_0}$
*   **Cascaded Stages:** For multiple cascaded stages, the overall noise figure $F_{\text{total}}$ is given by Friis' formula:
    $F_{\text{total}} = F_1 + \frac{F_2-1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3-1}{G_1G_2} + \dots$
    This highlights the importance of having a low noise figure in the first stage of the receiver.

#### 3.4. Noise Temperature ($T_e$ or $T_{equiv}$)

*   **Description:** An alternative and often more intuitive way to characterize the noise added by a component or system. It represents the temperature of a resistor that would produce the same amount of noise power as the component being characterized.
*   **Relationship to Noise Figure:** $T_e = T_0 (F-1)$, where $T_0$ is the reference temperature (290 K).
*   **Benefits:**
    *   Noise temperatures of different sources can be directly added to find the total noise temperature.
    *   Easier to combine noise contributions from different parts of the receiver and antenna.
*   **Antenna Noise Temperature ($T_a$):** The effective noise temperature contributed by the antenna system, including external noise sources and losses in the antenna.

#### 3.5. Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

*   **Description:** The ratio of the power of the desired signal to the power of the noise. It is a crucial metric for determining the detectability of a target.
*   **Formula:** $\text{SNR} = \frac{P_{\text{signal}}}{P_{\text{noise}}}$
*   **In Decibels (dB):** $\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{P_{\text{signal}}}{P_{\text{noise}}} \right)$
*   **Impact:** A higher SNR indicates a clearer signal and a greater likelihood of detection. Radar systems aim to maximize SNR.

**Referencing Textbooks:**

*   **Skolnik:** Heavily emphasizes the use of noise figure and noise temperature in radar system design and performance analysis.
*   **Pratt & Allnutt:** Discusses noise figure and noise temperature extensively in the context of satellite receiver design, including the impact of antenna noise temperature.

### 4. Impact of Receiver Noise on Radar Performance

Receiver noise directly affects the ability of a radar to detect targets, especially those that are far away or have small radar cross-sections.

#### 4.1. Minimum Detectable Signal (MDS)

*   **Description:** The weakest signal that the radar receiver can reliably detect. It is typically defined as the signal power that results in a specific minimum SNR (e.g., 10-15 dB) at the output of the receiver.
*   **Relationship to Noise:** MDS is directly proportional to the noise power in the receiver's bandwidth.
*   **Formula (simplified):** $MDS \propto P_{\text{noise}} = kTBF$ (assuming a fixed minimum SNR).

#### 4.2. Detection Range

*   **Description:** The maximum distance at which a radar can detect a target.
*   **Impact of Noise:** A higher noise level (larger $P_{\text{noise}}$) leads to a higher MDS. To achieve the same minimum SNR for a weak target echo, the transmitted power needs to be increased, or the receiver's sensitivity needs to be improved to reduce the effective noise. This ultimately limits the detection range.
*   **Radar Range Equation:** The radar range equation explicitly includes terms related to transmitted power, antenna gain, target cross-section, wavelength, and receiver characteristics, including noise. A higher noise level effectively reduces the maximum range.

#### 4.3. False Alarms

*   **Description:** A false alarm occurs when the radar incorrectly identifies noise or clutter as a target.
*   **Impact of Noise:** If the noise level is too high, or if the detection threshold is set too low, random noise fluctuations can cross the detection threshold, leading to false alarms. This reduces the overall efficiency and reliability of the radar system.

#### 4.4. Probability of Detection ($P_d$)

*   **Description:** The probability that a radar will correctly detect a target when it is present.
*   **Impact of Noise:** As the SNR decreases due to noise, the probability of detection also decreases. To achieve a high probability of detection for weak targets, a high SNR is required, which is directly challenged by receiver noise.

**Referencing Textbooks:**

*   **Skolnik:** Dedicates significant portions to how noise affects the radar range equation, probability of detection, and false alarm rate.
*   **Edde (Radar: Principles, Technology, Applications):** Discusses the practical implications of noise in radar system design, including trade-offs between sensitivity, noise, and range.

### 5. Minimizing the Impact of Receiver Noise

Several techniques are employed in radar system design to mitigate the detrimental effects of receiver noise.

*   **Low Noise Amplifiers (LNAs):**
    *   **Description:** The first stage of any sensitive receiver should be a Low Noise Amplifier (LNA). These amplifiers are specifically designed to have a very low noise figure.
    *   **Reasoning:** Due to Friis' formula for cascaded stages, the noise contribution of the first stage dominates the overall noise figure. Therefore, using an LNA at the input is critical.
    *   **Example:** Field-Effect Transistors (FETs) and Heterojunction Bipolar Transistors (HBTs) are commonly used in LNAs.

*   **Filtering:**
    *   **Description:** Band-limiting the receiver to the expected bandwidth of the target echo helps reduce the amount of noise that enters the receiver.
    *   **Reasoning:** Since thermal noise is white (uniformly distributed), reducing the bandwidth reduces the total noise power received ($P_n = kTBF$).
    *   **Trade-off:** The filter bandwidth must be wide enough to pass the Doppler-shifted target echo without excessive distortion.

*   **Cooling Components:**
    *   **Description:** Cooling the initial stages of the receiver, especially the LNA, can significantly reduce thermal noise.
    *   **Reasoning:** Thermal noise power is directly proportional to temperature ($P_n \propto T$). Lowering the temperature reduces the random motion of charge carriers.
    *   **Example:** Superconducting components operating at cryogenic temperatures can achieve extremely low noise figures.

*   **Signal Processing Techniques:**
    *   **Description:** Advanced signal processing methods can be used to distinguish weak target signals from noise.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Integration:** Integrating (summing) multiple pulses from the same target can increase the SNR by approximately $\sqrt{N}$ (where N is the number of pulses integrated).
        *   **Doppler Processing:** Exploits the Doppler shift caused by moving targets. Noise is generally not Doppler shifted in the same way as target echoes, allowing for separation.
        *   **Pulse Compression:** Techniques like chirp modulation and matched filtering allow for transmitting long pulses (for energy) while achieving high range resolution (equivalent to short pulses), which can improve SNR.

**Referencing Textbooks:**

*   **Skolnik:** Discusses the role of LNAs and filtering in radar receiver design.
*   **Edde:** Covers practical implementation aspects of low-noise amplification and other noise reduction techniques.

### 7. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
c) Thermal noise

**Answer 2:**
To calculate the equivalent noise temperature ($T_e$) from the Noise Figure (F) in dB, we first convert F to a linear ratio:
$F_{\text{linear}} = 10^{(F_{\text{dB}}/10)} = 10^{(3/10)} \approx 1.995$
Then, use the formula $T_e = T_0 (F_{\text{linear}} - 1)$:
$T_e = 290 \text{ K} \times (1.995 - 1) \approx 290 \text{ K} \times 0.995 \approx 288.55 \text{ K}$
However, the question likely implies a direct dB to temperature relationship for simplicity in some contexts. If we approximate F as 2 (for 3 dB), then $T_e \approx 290 \text{ K} \times (2-1) = 290 \text{ K}$. Let's re-examine the options and common interpretations.

A Noise Figure of 3 dB means the noise added by the receiver is equivalent to the thermal noise at 290K if the gain were such that the output noise equals the input noise multiplied by 2.

Let's use the formula $T_e = T_0(F-1)$. A Noise Figure of 3 dB corresponds to a linear Noise Figure of approximately 2.
$T_e = 290 \text{ K} \times (2 - 1) = 290 \text{ K}$.
This suggests there might be a slight misunderstanding of the options or a simplification in the question's intent. Often, 3dB is associated with a doubling of noise power.

Let's check the options again. If F = 3dB, then $F_{linear} \approx 1.995$.
$T_e = 290 * (1.995 - 1) = 290 * 0.995 \approx 288.55 K$.
None of the options match perfectly. This often happens with dB conversions.

Let's assume the question intends to test the understanding that a 3dB noise figure means the added noise is roughly equivalent to the thermal noise at the reference temperature.

A better formulation for the question might be: "If a receiver's noise figure is 3 dB, what is the *equivalent noise temperature *added by the receiver *if we consider the noise factor to be 2* (a common approximation for 3dB)?"
In that case, $T_e = T_0(F-1) = 290 \text{ K}(2-1) = 290 \text{ K}$.

Given the options, and the typical way these questions are posed, the closest interpretation is that 3dB noise figure implies the added noise temperature is similar to the base temperature. Let's consider how noise figure is presented in textbooks.

Skolnik (Introduction to Radar Systems) defines Noise Figure as $F = (S_i/N_i)/(S_o/N_o)$. And $N_o = G N_i + N_{added}$.
$N_{added} = G k T_e B$. And $N_i = k T_0 B$.
$F = (S_i / k T_0 B) / (G S_i / (G k T_0 B + G k T_e B)) = (S_i / k T_0 B) / (S_i / (k T_0 B + k T_e B)) = (k T_0 B + k T_e B) / k T_0 B = 1 + T_e/T_0$.
So $F-1 = T_e/T_0$. $T_e = T_0 (F-1)$.
If $F = 3$ dB, then $F_{linear} = 10^{3/10} \approx 1.995$.
$T_e = 290 \text{ K} \times (1.995 - 1) = 290 \text{ K} \times 0.995 \approx 288.55 \text{ K}$.

It's possible the question meant to imply that the total output noise power is twice the input noise power in terms of $kT_0B$ *after gain*.

Let's reconsider the options. If we assume F is exactly 2 (which is approximately 3dB), then $T_e = 290K * (2-1) = 290K$. Option (a) is 290K. This is the most plausible answer given the typical approximations.

**Answer 3:**
The first stage of a radar receiver is typically a Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) because the overall noise figure of a cascaded amplifier system is dominated by the noise figure of the first stage (according to Friis' formula: $F_{\text{total}} = F_1 + \frac{F_2-1}{G_1} + \dots$). By using an LNA with a very low noise figure ($F_1$), the total amount of noise added by the receiver is minimized, thereby maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at the input and improving the radar's ability to detect weak target echoes.

**Answer 4:**
Given:
Bandwidth ($B$) = 1 MHz = $1 \times 10^6$ Hz
Temperature ($T$) = 300 K
Boltzmann's constant ($k$) = $1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K

Noise Power ($P_n$) = $kTBF$
Here, we assume the noise factor F=1 for calculating fundamental thermal noise power.
$P_n = (1.38 \times 10^{-23} \text{ J/K}) \times (300 \text{ K}) \times (1 \times 10^6 \text{ Hz})$
$P_n = 4.14 \times 10^{-15} \times 10^6$ Watts
$P_n = 4.14 \times 10^{-9}$ Watts

To convert to dBm (decibels relative to 1 milliwatt):
1 Watt = 1000 milliwatts
$P_n (\text{dBm}) = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{P_n (\text{Watts})}{1 \text{ mW}} \right)$
$P_n (\text{dBm}) = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{4.14 \times 10^{-9} \text{ W}}{1 \times 10^{-3} \text{ W}} \right)$
$P_n (\text{dBm}) = 10 \log_{10} (4.14 \times 10^{-6})$
$P_n (\text{dBm}) = 10 \times (\log_{10}(4.14) + \log_{10}(10^{-6}))$
$P_n (\text{dBm}) = 10 \times (0.617 + (-6))$
$P_n (\text{dBm}) = 10 \times (-5.383)$
$P_n (\text{dBm}) \approx -53.83$ dBm

**Answer 5:**
*   **Probability of Detection ($P_d$):** An increase in receiver noise will decrease the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) for a given target echo. Since $P_d$ is a function of SNR (higher SNR generally means higher $P_d$), an increase in noise will lead to a **decrease** in the probability of detecting a target. The radar will be less likely to register a weak echo as a target.
*   **False Alarm Rate:** An increase in receiver noise means that random noise fluctuations are more likely to exceed the predetermined detection threshold. This will lead to an **increase** in the false alarm rate, as the radar will erroneously identify noise as a target more frequently.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Noise is inherent:** It's impossible to eliminate noise completely, but its impact can be minimized.
*   **Thermal noise is fundamental:** Caused by temperature, it's present in all resistive components.
*   **Noise Figure (F) and Noise Temperature ($T_e$):** Key metrics to quantify receiver noisiness. A lower value is better.
*   **LNA is crucial:** The first amplifier stage must have the lowest possible noise figure.
*   **Bandwidth matters:** Reducing receiver bandwidth reduces thermal noise power.
*   **SNR is key:** Radar performance (detection range, probability of detection) is directly related to the Signal-to-Noise Ratio.
*   **Trade-offs exist:** Reducing noise often involves trade-offs with other parameters like bandwidth, complexity, and cost.
