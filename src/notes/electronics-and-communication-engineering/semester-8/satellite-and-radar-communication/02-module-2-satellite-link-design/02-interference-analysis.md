# Satellite and Radar Communication: Module 2 - Satellite Link Design: Interference Analysis

## Introduction to Interference in Satellite Communication

Interference is a significant factor that degrades the performance of a satellite communication system. It refers to any unwanted signal that corrupts the desired signal, leading to a reduction in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and potentially causing errors in data transmission. Understanding and analyzing interference is crucial for designing robust and reliable satellite links that meet specified performance criteria.

**Key Concept:** Interference can originate from various sources, both within the satellite system itself and from external systems.

## 1. Sources of Interference

Interference in satellite communication can be broadly categorized into two main types:

### 1.1. Intersystem Interference

This occurs when signals from one satellite system interfere with signals in another satellite system. This is a significant concern due to the increasing number of satellites and the limited availability of the radio frequency spectrum.

*   **Coordination:** International and national regulatory bodies (like the ITU) play a vital role in coordinating the allocation and use of radio frequencies to minimize intersystem interference.
*   **Frequency Reuse:** Satellite systems often reuse frequencies in different beams or geographical areas to maximize spectral efficiency. This can lead to interference if not properly managed.
*   **Adjacent Satellite Interference:** Satellites operating in adjacent orbital slots might use similar frequencies, leading to interference due to antenna sidelobes.
    *   **Example:** Two geostationary satellites positioned closely in orbit, both transmitting in the Ku-band, could interfere with each other if their ground station antennas have broad sidelobes that point towards the adjacent satellite.
*   **Interference from Terrestrial Systems:** Ground-based communication systems operating in adjacent frequency bands can also interfere with satellite signals, especially during satellite link establishment or in areas where ground stations are located near terrestrial transmitters.

**Reference:** Pratt & Allnutt (2021) extensively discusses intersystem interference, particularly in the context of spectrum sharing and coordination.

### 1.2. Intrasystem Interference

This occurs within the same satellite system.

*   **Cross-Polarization Interference (XPI):** When signals transmitted on orthogonal polarizations (e.g., Horizontal and Vertical) leak into each other due to imperfect polarization discrimination of antennas or atmospheric effects.
    *   **Example:** A satellite transmitting a signal with vertical polarization might experience some of its signal energy being received with horizontal polarization at the ground station due to depolarization effects caused by rain or imperfect antenna performance. This leaked signal can interfere with a separate channel also transmitted with horizontal polarization.
*   **Adjacent Channel Interference (ACI):** When signals from adjacent frequency channels within the same satellite system spill over into the desired channel due to imperfect filtering.
    *   **Example:** If a satellite transponder is transmitting signals in channel A (e.g., 12.5 GHz to 12.6 GHz) and channel B (e.g., 12.6 GHz to 12.7 GHz), an improperly designed filter in the receiver could allow some of the signal from channel B to leak into the receiver for channel A.
*   **Intermodulation Interference:** Non-linearities in active components (like power amplifiers in the satellite or ground station) can generate spurious signals at frequencies that are sums and differences of the input frequencies. These intermodulation products can fall into other active channels and cause interference.
    *   **Example:** If a satellite amplifier is amplifying two carriers, carrier 1 and carrier 2, it might produce a third-order intermodulation product at $2f_2 - f_1$ or $2f_1 - f_2$. If one of these frequencies coincides with another carrier frequency, interference will occur.
*   **Interference from Satellite Subsystems:** Signals generated by onboard subsystems (like telemetry, tracking, and command - TT&C) can sometimes interfere with the communication transponders if not properly isolated.

**Reference:** Ha (2017) provides detailed explanations of intrasystem interference mechanisms, including XPI and intermodulation.

## 2. Types of Interference and their Impact

The impact of interference on a satellite link depends on its nature and the modulation and coding schemes used.

### 2.1. Continuous Interference

This type of interference is present consistently throughout the transmission.

*   **Characteristics:** Constant power, spectral distribution over a specific bandwidth.
*   **Impact:** Directly reduces the carrier-to-interference ratio (C/I) or signal-to-interference ratio (S/I). This leads to a degradation of the carrier-to-noise ratio (C/N) and consequently the bit error rate (BER).
*   **Analysis:** Can be analyzed by simply subtracting the interference power from the desired signal power (in dB) when calculating the overall C/N.

### 2.2. Non-Continuous/Intermittent Interference

This interference appears and disappears sporadically.

*   **Characteristics:** Can be due to switching operations, transient events, or sporadic sources.
*   **Impact:** Can cause temporary bursts of errors or complete loss of synchronization. The average impact might be less than continuous interference, but it can be more disruptive.
*   **Analysis:** Often more complex to analyze, requiring statistical methods or worst-case analysis.

### 2.3. Gaussian Noise

While not strictly "interference" in the sense of unwanted signals from other transmitters, thermal noise and shot noise are fundamental noise sources that limit the performance of any communication system. In interference analysis, it's often treated similarly.

*   **Sources:** Thermal agitation of electrons in electronic components, shot noise in semiconductor devices.
*   **Impact:** Directly degrades the C/N ratio, affecting BER.
*   **Analysis:** Characterized by its power spectral density and is typically assumed to be additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN).

**Important Point:** The combined effect of multiple interference sources and thermal noise is typically represented by an equivalent noise power that is the sum of the powers of all interfering sources and thermal noise.

## 3. Interference Analysis Techniques

To ensure reliable satellite communication, a thorough interference analysis is performed during the link design phase.

### 3.1. Carrier-to-Interference Ratio (C/I)

This is a fundamental metric used to quantify the strength of the desired signal relative to the interfering signals.

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the power of the desired carrier signal to the total power of all interfering signals, expressed in dB.
    *   $C/I = \frac{P_{carrier}}{P_{interference}}$
*   **Calculation:** In decibels, $C/I_{dB} = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{P_{carrier}}{P_{interference}} \right)$.
*   **Impact on C/N:** The total noise power in the receiver's bandwidth ($N_{total}$) is the sum of thermal noise power ($N_T$) and interference power ($I_{total}$).
    *   $N_{total} = N_T + I_{total}$
    *   The effective $C/N$ can be calculated as: $\frac{C}{N_{eff}} = \frac{C}{N_T + I_{total}}$
    *   In dB: $\frac{C}{N_{eff, dB}} = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{P_{carrier}}{P_{thermal} + P_{interference}} \right)$
    *   Alternatively, if $C/N_T$ and $C/I$ are known in dB:
        $\frac{C}{N_{eff, dB}} = \frac{C}{N_T} \left[ 1 + \frac{C}{N_T} \frac{1}{C/I} \right]^{-1}$ (This is an incorrect formula. The correct approach is to work with powers.)
    *   **Correct Power Addition (dBW):**
        Let $P_C$ be the carrier power in dBW, $N_T$ be the thermal noise power in dBW, and $P_I$ be the interference power in dBW.
        $C/N_T = P_C - N_T$
        $C/I = P_C - P_I$
        Total noise power $P_{total\_noise} = 10^{\frac{N_T}{10}} + 10^{\frac{P_I}{10}}$
        $C/N_{eff} = P_C - 10 \log_{10} (10^{\frac{N_T}{10}} + 10^{\frac{P_I}{10}})$

**Example:**
A satellite link has a $C/N_T$ of 25 dB-Hz. A continuous interfering signal is present with a $C/I$ of 30 dB-Hz. Calculate the effective $C/N$.

Let $P_C = 0$ dBW (for simplicity of calculation).
$N_T = P_C - (C/N_T)_{dB-Hz} = 0 - 25 = -25$ dBW
$P_I = P_C - (C/I)_{dB-Hz} = 0 - 30 = -30$ dBW

Total noise power $P_{noise\_total} = 10^{-2.5} + 10^{-3.0} \approx 0.00316 + 0.001 = 0.00416$ (in Watts, assuming $P_C=1W$)
$P_{noise\_total\_dBW} = 10 \log_{10} (0.00416) \approx -23.8$ dBW

$C/N_{eff} = P_C - P_{noise\_total\_dBW} = 0 - (-23.8) = 23.8$ dB-Hz.

### 3.2. Adjacent Channel Interference (ACI) Calculation

*   **Mechanism:** Spillover of energy from adjacent channels due to imperfect channel filtering.
*   **Modeling:** The interference power from an adjacent channel is often modeled as a fraction of the power in that adjacent channel, dependent on the channel spacing and filter characteristics.
    *   $I_{ACI} = K \cdot P_{adj\_channel}$
    *   Where $K$ is the interference factor, determined by the filter's shape factor and the channel spacing.
*   **Calculation:** In practice, the filter skirt attenuation at the adjacent channel's frequency is used. The interference power from a single adjacent channel can be calculated as:
    $P_{ACI} = P_{adjacent} \times \frac{\text{Filter Loss at Adjacent Channel Frequency}}{\text{Filter Loss at Desired Channel Frequency}}$
    (This needs to be expressed in terms of power levels).

**Example:** A satellite transponder uses filters with a loss of 1 dB at the desired channel center frequency and 40 dB at the center frequency of an adjacent channel. If the adjacent channel has a power of 10 dBW, the interference power into the desired channel is approximately $P_{ACI} = 10 \text{ dBW} - 40 \text{ dB} = -30 \text{ dBW}$.

### 3.3. Cross-Polarization Interference (XPI) Calculation

*   **Mechanism:** Leakage between orthogonally polarized signals.
*   **Quantification:** Measured by the Cross-Polarization Discrimination (XPD). XPD is the ratio of the power in the desired polarization to the power in the cross-polarization.
    *   $XPD = \frac{P_{co-polar}}{P_{cross-polar}}$
*   **Impact:** The interference power in the desired channel is the cross-polarized component of the signal transmitted on the orthogonal polarization.
    *   $P_{XPI} = P_{orthogonal\_polarized\_signal} \times \frac{1}{XPD}$
    *   In dB: $P_{XPI, dB} = P_{orthogonal\_polarized\_signal, dB} - XPD_{dB}$

**Example:** A satellite transmits two signals, one on Vertical Polarization (VP) with power 15 dBW and another on Horizontal Polarization (HP) with power 15 dBW. A ground station antenna has an XPD of 30 dB for the VP channel. Interference from the HP signal into the VP channel can be estimated. If the HP signal's power is what is relevant for interference into the VP channel's orthogonal component, then the XPI into the VP channel from the HP signal would be:
$P_{XPI} = P_{HP\_signal} - XPD_{dB} = 15 \text{ dBW} - 30 \text{ dB} = -15 \text{ dBW}$.

**Important Point:** XPD can be significantly degraded by rain, leading to increased XPI during heavy rainfall events.

### 3.4. Intermodulation Interference Calculation

*   **Mechanism:** Non-linearities in amplifiers generate spurious signals at sum and difference frequencies.
*   **Third-Order Intermodulation Products (3rd-OIP):** The most problematic as they often fall close to the desired signal frequencies. For two carriers $f_1$ and $f_2$, the products are $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$.
*   **Intercept Point:** The third-order intercept point (IP3) of an amplifier is a figure of merit indicating its linearity. A higher IP3 means better linearity.
*   **Calculation:** The power of the 3rd-OIP can be related to the carrier power and the amplifier's IP3.
    *   $P_{3rd-OIP} = P_{carrier} + \frac{IP3 - P_{carrier}}{2}$ (This is a simplified power relationship in dB).
    *   A more formal calculation involves the amplifier's gain and distortion coefficients. A common approximation for carrier-to-third-order-intermodulation product ratio is:
        $C/IM_3 = 2(IP3 - P_{carrier})$ (in dB)
        where $P_{carrier}$ is the power of one of the carriers.

**Example:** An amplifier has an IP3 of +30 dBm. If two carriers of equal power, each at +10 dBm, are input to this amplifier, the $C/IM_3$ is:
$C/IM_3 = 2(30 \text{ dBm} - 10 \text{ dBm}) = 2(20 \text{ dB}) = 40 \text{ dB}$.
This means the power of the third-order intermodulation products is 40 dB below the power of the individual carriers.

### 3.5. Interference from Terrestrial Sources

*   **Types:** Microwave links, radar systems, other radio services.
*   **Mitigation:** Careful frequency planning, shielding of ground station antennas, and the use of directional antennas.
*   **Analysis:** Involves calculating the power flux density from terrestrial transmitters and comparing it to the allowable limits defined by regulatory bodies.

**Reference:** Skolnik (2017) provides a foundation for understanding radio frequency interference, which is applicable to both radar and satellite systems.

## 4. Performance Degradation due to Interference

Interference, like noise, reduces the effective $C/N$ of the link. This degradation directly impacts the system's ability to achieve its target Bit Error Rate (BER) or signal-to-interference-and-noise ratio (SINR).

*   **Impact on BER:** For digital modulation schemes (like BPSK, QPSK), the BER is a function of $C/N$. When interference is present, the effective $C/N$ decreases, leading to a higher BER.
    *   For a given BER requirement, the link must be designed with a sufficient $C/N$ margin to account for the expected interference.
*   **Carrier-to-Interference plus Noise Ratio (SINR):** In systems with significant interference, SINR is a more appropriate metric than C/N.
    *   $SINR = \frac{P_{carrier}}{P_{interference} + P_{noise}}$
*   **Link Margin:** The difference between the received $C/N$ (or SINR) and the minimum required $C/N$ (or SINR) for a given performance level. Interference reduces this margin.

**Important Point to Remember:** When analyzing interference, it's crucial to consider all potential sources and their combined effect. The worst-case scenario or a statistical average of interference levels should be used depending on the criticality of the link.

## 5. Mitigation Techniques for Interference

*   **Frequency Planning and Coordination:** Strict adherence to allocated frequency bands and orbital slot coordination.
*   **Improved Antenna Design:** High sidelobe rejection antennas, beam shaping to avoid pointing towards interfering sources.
*   **Polarization Diversity:** Using different polarizations for adjacent channels or adjacent satellites to reduce XPI.
*   **Advanced Modulation and Coding Schemes:** Robust schemes that are more tolerant to interference.
*   **Filtering:** Sophisticated filters to minimize adjacent channel spillover.
*   **Power Control:** Adjusting transmit power to avoid causing excessive interference to other systems.
*   **Site Selection for Ground Stations:** Choosing locations that are shielded from terrestrial interference sources.

**Reference:** Pritchard (2006) discusses various techniques for mitigating interference in satellite communication systems.

## Practice Questions and Answers

**Q1.** Define the terms "intersystem interference" and "intrasystem interference" in the context of satellite communication. Provide one example for each.

**Answer:**
*   **Intersystem Interference:** Interference that occurs between signals from different satellite systems or between satellite systems and terrestrial systems. **Example:** Interference between two geostationary satellites in adjacent orbital slots using similar frequencies.
*   **Intrasystem Interference:** Interference that occurs within the same satellite system. **Example:** Cross-polarization interference between two carriers transmitted on orthogonal polarizations by the same satellite.

**Q2.** A satellite link has a nominal $C/N_T$ of 22 dB-Hz. An interfering signal is present, resulting in a $C/I$ of 28 dB-Hz. Calculate the effective $C/N$ for this link.

**Answer:**
Let $P_C = 0$ dBW.
$N_T = P_C - (C/N_T)_{dB-Hz} = 0 - 22 = -22$ dBW
$P_I = P_C - (C/I)_{dB-Hz} = 0 - 28 = -28$ dBW

Total noise power $P_{noise\_total} = 10^{\frac{N_T}{10}} + 10^{\frac{P_I}{10}}$
$P_{noise\_total} = 10^{-2.2} + 10^{-2.8}$ (in Watts, assuming $P_C=1W$)
$P_{noise\_total} \approx 0.00631 + 0.00158 = 0.00789$ Watts

$P_{noise\_total\_dBW} = 10 \log_{10} (0.00789) \approx -21.03$ dBW

Effective $C/N = P_C - P_{noise\_total\_dBW} = 0 - (-21.03) = 21.03$ dB-Hz.

**Q3.** What is the primary cause of Cross-Polarization Interference (XPI)? How is it quantified?

**Answer:**
The primary cause of XPI is the imperfect polarization discrimination of antennas, which allows some of the signal energy transmitted on one polarization to leak into the receiver tuned to the orthogonal polarization. Additionally, atmospheric effects like rain and scintillation can also depolarize the signal, leading to increased XPI.
XPI is quantified by the **Cross-Polarization Discrimination (XPD)**, which is the ratio of the power in the co-polarized signal to the power in the cross-polarized signal.

**Q4.** Explain the concept of third-order intermodulation products and why they are a concern in satellite communication.

**Answer:**
Third-order intermodulation products (3rd-OIP) are spurious signals generated when multiple carriers pass through a non-linear amplifier. For two input carriers at frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$, the most problematic 3rd-OIPs occur at frequencies $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$. These products are a concern because they can fall very close to the original carrier frequencies or into other adjacent channels within the satellite's transponder bandwidth, causing significant interference and degrading the link performance. The linearity of amplifiers, quantified by the third-order intercept point (IP3), is critical in minimizing these products.

**Q5.** A satellite payload amplifier has an IP3 of +35 dBm. If two carriers, each with a power of +15 dBm, are amplified, what is the $C/IM_3$ ratio?

**Answer:**
Using the formula $C/IM_3 = 2(IP3 - P_{carrier})$ (in dB):
$C/IM_3 = 2(+35 \text{ dBm} - +15 \text{ dBm})$
$C/IM_3 = 2(20 \text{ dB})$
$C/IM_3 = 40 \text{ dB}$

This means the interference power from the third-order intermodulation products is 40 dB below the power of each individual carrier.

## Important Points to Remember

*   **Interference is a major limiter of satellite link performance.**
*   **Understanding the sources and types of interference is crucial for effective analysis.**
*   **C/I and SINR are key metrics for quantifying the impact of interference.**
*   **Non-linearities in amplifiers (causing intermodulation) and imperfect filtering (causing ACI) are significant intrasystem interference sources.**
*   **Cross-polarization discrimination (XPD) is vital for managing XPI, especially in dual-polarization systems.**
*   **Rain and other atmospheric effects can significantly degrade XPD.**
*   **Careful frequency planning, antenna design, and modulation/coding schemes are essential for interference mitigation.**
*   **Regulatory coordination (e.g., ITU) is vital for minimizing intersystem interference.**
