# Module 2: Satellite Link Design - Intermodulation Interference

## 1. Introduction to Intermodulation Interference (K2, CO1)

Intermodulation interference (IMD) is a significant impairment in satellite communication systems, particularly when multiple carriers share a single transponder. It arises from the non-linear behavior of active components in the satellite payload, primarily the High Power Amplifier (HPA). This non-linearity causes carriers to mix and generate spurious signals at frequencies that can interfere with other intended signals.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Non-linearity:** A characteristic of a device where the output is not directly proportional to the input. In amplifiers, this can lead to distortion.
*   **Transponder:** A transponder is a relay station in a satellite that receives a signal on one frequency, amplifies it, and retransmits it on another frequency.
*   **Multiple Access:** Techniques that allow multiple ground stations to share a single satellite transponder. Examples include Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA) and Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA).
*   **Carrier Mixing:** When two or more signals pass through a non-linear device, their frequencies can combine in various ways, creating new frequencies (intermodulation products).

### How Intermodulation Occurs:

When multiple carriers are amplified by a non-linear HPA in a satellite, the amplifier's transfer characteristic can be approximated by a power series:

$V_{out}(t) = a_1 V_{in}(t) + a_2 V_{in}^2(t) + a_3 V_{in}^3(t) + ...$

Where:
*   $V_{out}(t)$ is the output voltage.
*   $V_{in}(t)$ is the input voltage.
*   $a_1, a_2, a_3, ...$ are the coefficients of the power series.

If the input signal consists of two carriers, $f_1$ and $f_2$, the non-linear process will generate sum and difference frequencies, as well as higher-order products. The most problematic are the **third-order intermodulation products (IMPs)** because they often fall close to the original carriers and are difficult to filter out.

For two carriers $f_1$ and $f_2$, the third-order IMPs are at frequencies:

*   $2f_1 - f_2$
*   $2f_2 - f_1$

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Edition, 2021), Chapter 5:** Discusses amplifier characteristics and non-linearities, providing detailed mathematical models for IMP generation. They emphasize that these products can cause significant degradation of signal quality.

**Ha (2nd Edition, 2017), Chapter 4:** Explains the concept of linearity in amplifiers and how saturation of the HPA leads to non-linear behavior, thus generating intermodulation distortion.

## 2. Types of Intermodulation Products (K2, CO1)

Intermodulation products are categorized by their order, which is determined by the exponents in the power series expansion of the non-linear device.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Order of Intermodulation Product:** The sum of the exponents of the input frequencies in the mathematical expression for the product.
*   **Third-Order Intermodulation Products (3rd-Order IMPs):** Products generated by terms like $a_3 V_{in}^3$. These are particularly troublesome due to their proximity to the original carriers.
*   **Fifth-Order Intermodulation Products (5th-Order IMPs):** Products generated by terms like $a_5 V_{in}^5$. These are typically weaker than 3rd-order IMPs and fall further away from the original carriers.

### Frequencies of Intermodulation Products:

Consider two input carriers at frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$. The output will contain:

*   **Linear Terms:** $f_1$, $f_2$ (the original signals)
*   **Second-Order Terms ($a_2 V_{in}^2$):**
    *   $f_1 + f_2$
    *   $f_1 - f_2$ (and $f_2 - f_1$)
*   **Third-Order Terms ($a_3 V_{in}^3$):**
    *   $2f_1 + f_2$
    *   $2f_1 - f_2$
    *   $f_1 + 2f_2$
    *   $f_1 - 2f_2$ (and $2f_2 - f_1$)

**Most Problematic:**
The third-order products $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$ are often very close to the original carriers, especially if $f_1$ and $f_2$ are close in frequency. If $f_1 = f_c - \Delta f$ and $f_2 = f_c + \Delta f$, then the third-order products are at $2(f_c - \Delta f) - (f_c + \Delta f) = f_c - 3\Delta f$ and $2(f_c + \Delta f) - (f_c - \Delta f) = f_c + 3\Delta f$. These are symmetrically located around the center frequency.

**Example:**
If $f_1 = 3.700$ GHz and $f_2 = 3.702$ GHz, then:
*   $2f_1 - f_2 = 2(3.700) - 3.702 = 7.400 - 3.702 = 3.698$ GHz
*   $2f_2 - f_1 = 2(3.702) - 3.700 = 7.404 - 3.700 = 3.704$ GHz

These spurious frequencies are only 2 MHz away from the original carriers.

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Edition, 2021), Chapter 5:** Provides detailed derivations of these intermodulation product frequencies and their impact on signal bandwidth.

## 3. Impact of Intermodulation Interference on Link Performance (K3, CO2)

Intermodulation interference directly degrades the quality of the received signal, leading to an increase in the Bit Error Rate (BER) for digital signals or noise for analog signals.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Carrier-to-Intermodulation Noise Ratio (C/IM):** A measure of the strength of a desired carrier relative to the power of intermodulation products interfering with that carrier.
*   **Carrier-to-Noise Ratio (C/N):** The ratio of the desired carrier power to the noise power.
*   **Carrier-to-Total Noise Ratio (C/N_total):** The ratio of the desired carrier power to the sum of all noise and interference powers, including thermal noise and intermodulation noise.
*   **Bit Error Rate (BER):** The number of bit errors divided by the total number of transmitted bits.
*   **Non-linear Operation (Back-off):** Operating an amplifier at a power level lower than its saturation point to reduce non-linearity.

### How IMD Affects Link Performance:

1.  **Increased Noise Floor:** The IMPs add to the overall noise power in the transponder's bandwidth, effectively raising the noise floor.
2.  **Reduced C/N:** As IMD increases the noise power, the C/N ratio for each carrier decreases.
3.  **Increased BER:** A lower C/N directly leads to a higher BER for digital communication systems.
4.  **"Adjacent Channel Interference" from IMPs:** The IMPs generated by carriers in one transponder can fall into adjacent transponders, causing interference even if the transponders are well-separated.

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Edition, 2021), Chapter 5:** Elaborates on the relationship between amplifier back-off, IMD power levels, and the resulting degradation in C/N and BER. They present curves showing BER performance as a function of C/N with varying levels of IMD.

**Ha (2nd Edition, 2017), Chapter 4:** Discusses the concept of "intermodulation noise" and how it can be quantified and incorporated into the overall link budget analysis.

## 4. Quantifying Intermodulation Interference (K3, CO2)

The impact of IMD on a link is quantified by calculating the C/IM ratio and then incorporating it into the total C/N calculation.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Third-Order Intermodulation Distortion (3-ID):** A measure of the strength of third-order intermodulation products relative to the desired carrier.
*   **$P_{1dB}$ (1 dB Compression Point):** The output power level at which the amplifier's gain is reduced by 1 dB from its small-signal gain.
*   **$P_{sat}$ (Saturation Power):** The output power level at which further increases in input power result in little or no increase in output power.
*   **$IP_{3}$ (Third-Order Intercept Point):** A theoretical point at which the extrapolated lines of the fundamental and third-order distortion products intersect. It's a measure of the linearity of the amplifier. A higher $IP_3$ indicates a more linear amplifier.

### Calculating C/IM:

For a single carrier in the presence of many other carriers in a transponder, the third-order IMPs that fall on the desired carrier's frequency are the most critical. The power of these IMPs depends on the input power levels of the interfering carriers and the amplifier's non-linearity.

The $IP_3$ is a key parameter used to characterize IMD. The relationship between $IP_3$, output power ($P_{out}$), and the third-order intermodulation power ($P_{IM3}$) can be approximated as:

$P_{IM3} \approx P_{out} - 2 \times (IP_3 - P_{out}) = 3 P_{out} - 2 IP_3$ (in dBW)

This implies that for every 1 dB increase in output power, the IM3 power increases by 3 dB.

The Carrier-to-Intermodulation ratio ($C/IM_3$) can be calculated from this:

$C/IM_3 = P_{out} - P_{IM3} = P_{out} - (3 P_{out} - 2 IP_3) = 2 IP_3 - 2 P_{out}$ (in dB)

Where $IP_3$ and $P_{out}$ are in dBW.

**Relationship between $IP_3$ and $P_{1dB}$:**
A common approximation is $IP_3 \approx P_{1dB} + 7$ dB.

**Ha (2nd Edition, 2017), Chapter 4:** Provides detailed derivations for these formulas and explains how to use $IP_3$ to predict IMD levels.

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Edition, 2021), Chapter 5:** Presents performance curves relating C/N, C/IM, and BER. They also discuss the concept of the "transponder characteristic" which relates output power to input power for multiple carriers, showing how IMD varies with loading.

## 5. Designing for Intermodulation Interference (K3, CO2)

Effective satellite link design involves minimizing the impact of IMD through careful selection of operating parameters and transponder configurations.

### Key Strategies:

1.  **Operating with Amplifier Back-off:**
    *   **Concept:** The most effective way to reduce IMD is to operate the HPA well below its saturation point. This is achieved by reducing the input drive level, which in turn reduces the output power.
    *   **Trade-off:** While back-off significantly reduces IMD, it also reduces the desired signal power, leading to a lower C/N. Link designers must find an optimal balance.
    *   **Example:** If a transponder can handle a total of 5 carriers at its saturation point, it might only be able to handle 2 carriers while maintaining acceptable IMD levels if operated with sufficient back-off.

2.  **Loading the Transponder:**
    *   **Concept:** The number of carriers and their individual power levels affect the overall IMD.
    *   **Multiple Carriers:** When multiple carriers are present, the IMPs from combinations of these carriers can fall onto each other and the original carriers. The more carriers, the more potential IMPs.
    *   **Equal vs. Unequal Carriers:** Equal power carriers generally produce more symmetrical IMD patterns. Unequal power carriers can lead to more complex IMD scenarios.
    *   **Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Edition, 2021), Chapter 5:** Discusses the concept of "transponder loading" and provides curves showing how C/IM varies with the number of carriers and their relative power levels.

3.  **Frequency Planning:**
    *   **Concept:** Placing carriers in a way that minimizes the overlap of IMPs with desired channels.
    *   **Strategy:** Avoid placing carriers with frequencies close to each other if possible, and ensure that IMPs generated by strong carriers do not fall into adjacent transponders that carry sensitive signals. This is particularly relevant in FDMA systems.

4.  **Using Linearization Techniques:**
    *   **Concept:** Employing external or internal circuits to linearize the HPA's transfer characteristic.
    *   **Examples:** Predistortion, envelope elimination and restoration (EER), and Doherty amplifiers. These techniques are more complex and power-consuming but can significantly reduce IMD.

5.  **Carrier-to-Intermodulation Noise Ratio (C/IM):**
    *   **In Link Budgets:** The IMD contribution is typically expressed as a $C/IM$ ratio. This is then combined with the $C/N$ from thermal noise to get a total $C/N_{total}$.
    *   **Combining Noise Powers:** The powers of different noise sources are additive. In dB, this is more complex. The total noise power is $N_{total} = N_{thermal} + N_{IMD} + N_{out} + ...$
    *   The total carrier-to-noise ratio is:
        $C/N_{total} = (C/N)^{-1} + (C/IM)^{-1} + (C/I_{out})^{-1} + ...)^{-1}$ (in dB)

**Pratt & Allnutt (3rd Edition, 2021), Chapter 5:** Provides detailed examples of link budget calculations incorporating IMD. They emphasize the importance of calculating the $C/IM$ for each carrier.

**Pritchard (2nd Edition, 2006), Chapter 7:** Discusses transponder characteristics and the trade-offs involved in loading them with multiple carriers, directly addressing the IMD problem.

## 6. Intermodulation Interference in Radar Systems (K2, CO3, CO4)

While the focus of IMD is heavily on satellite communication due to shared transponders, non-linearities in radar systems can also lead to intermodulation effects, though typically in different contexts.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Radar Receiver Front-End:** Non-linearities in the Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) or mixers in the radar receiver can cause IMPs.
*   **High Power Amplifier (HPA) in Transmitter:** Similar to satellites, the HPA in a radar transmitter can generate IMD if driven too hard, especially if multiple pulses or signals are being transmitted.
*   **Pulsed Radar:** The pulsed nature of radar means that during the transmission of a pulse, the transmitter is active. If multiple signals (e.g., from different targets or jamming signals) are present, they can mix.
*   **Doppler Shift:** In Doppler radar, the Doppler shift adds frequency components. If these shifted frequencies mix non-linearly, it can cause issues.

### Scenarios in Radar:

1.  **Jamming:** If a radar is subjected to strong jamming signals that are close in frequency to the radar's operating frequency, these jamming signals can mix in the radar's non-linear components to create IM products that appear as spurious signals on the radar display, potentially masking real targets or creating false targets.
2.  **Multiple Transmitted Signals:** In advanced radar systems that transmit multiple waveforms or pulses simultaneously or in close succession, non-linearities can cause these signals to mix, creating interference.
3.  **Receiver Saturation:** If a strong interfering signal (e.g., from a nearby transmitter or a powerful jammer) saturates the radar receiver's front-end, even if it's not at the radar's frequency, it can cause IMD products that fall within the radar's passband.

**Skolnik (2nd Edition, 2017), Chapter 3:** Discusses non-linearities in radar systems and their effects, including potential IMD in receivers. He highlights the importance of receiver linearity for detecting weak targets in the presence of strong interference.

**Edde (1st Edition, 2004), Chapter 5:** Explains RF amplifier linearity and its importance in radar receivers to avoid distortion and spurious signal generation.

**Kinsley & Quegan (1st Edition, 1999), Chapter 6:** Touches upon receiver dynamic range and how non-linearities can lead to signal degradation, which includes intermodulation effects when multiple signals are present.

## 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A satellite transponder amplifies two carriers at $f_1 = 3.700$ GHz and $f_2 = 3.702$ GHz. The HPA's non-linearity can be approximated by a power series. What are the frequencies of the third-order intermodulation products (IMPs) generated by these two carriers? (K2, CO1)

**Answer 1:**
The third-order IMPs are given by $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$.
*   $2f_1 - f_2 = 2(3.700) - 3.702 = 7.400 - 3.702 = 3.698$ GHz
*   $2f_2 - f_1 = 2(3.702) - 3.700 = 7.404 - 3.700 = 3.704$ GHz

**Question 2:**
A satellite transponder amplifier has a third-order intercept point ($IP_3$) of +30 dBW. If the amplifier is operated at an output power ($P_{out}$) of +15 dBW, calculate the carrier-to-third-order intermodulation ratio ($C/IM_3$) in dB. (K3, CO2)

**Answer 2:**
Using the formula $C/IM_3 = 2 IP_3 - 2 P_{out}$:
$C/IM_3 = 2 \times (30 \text{ dBW}) - 2 \times (15 \text{ dBW})$
$C/IM_3 = 60 \text{ dB} - 30 \text{ dB}$
$C/IM_3 = 30$ dB

**Question 3:**
Describe the main trade-off faced by a satellite link designer when trying to mitigate intermodulation interference. (K3, CO2)

**Answer 3:**
The main trade-off is between reducing intermodulation interference and maintaining adequate signal power. To reduce IMD, the amplifier must be operated with "back-off," meaning at a lower output power level than its saturation point. This reduction in output power directly lowers the carrier-to-noise ratio (C/N) for the desired signal, potentially increasing the Bit Error Rate (BER). Designers must find an optimal point that balances acceptable IMD levels with a sufficient C/N to meet performance requirements.

**Question 4:**
Explain why third-order intermodulation products are particularly problematic in satellite communications. (K2, CO1)

**Answer 4:**
Third-order intermodulation products ($2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$) are particularly problematic because they often fall very close to the original carrier frequencies. This makes them difficult to filter out using conventional bandpass filters without also attenuating the desired signal. In contrast, higher-order IMPs (like fifth-order) tend to be weaker and fall further away from the original carriers, making them easier to reject.

**Question 5:**
Consider a radar system receiving two strong interfering signals at frequencies $f_a$ and $f_b$. If the radar receiver has a non-linear component, what type of interference could these signals cause, and how might it manifest on the radar display? (K2, CO3)

**Answer 5:**
The two strong interfering signals ($f_a$ and $f_b$) can mix in the non-linear components of the radar receiver to produce intermodulation products (IMPs). Specifically, third-order IMPs at frequencies like $2f_a - f_b$ and $2f_b - f_a$ could be generated. If these IMPs fall within the radar's receiver bandwidth, they would appear on the radar display as spurious signals, potentially at locations that do not correspond to any actual targets. This could manifest as "ghost" targets or a general increase in clutter, masking real targets or leading to false alarms.

## 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Source of IMD:** Non-linearity in active components, primarily the HPA in satellites.
*   **Key Offenders:** Third-order intermodulation products ($2f_1 - f_2$, $2f_2 - f_1$) are the most critical due to their proximity to original carriers.
*   **Impact:** Degrades signal quality by increasing the noise floor, reducing C/N, and increasing BER.
*   **Quantification:** Measured by C/IM ratio. $IP_3$ is a key parameter for amplifier linearity.
*   **Mitigation:** Amplifier back-off is the primary method, involving a trade-off between IMD reduction and signal power. Frequency planning and linearization are also employed.
*   **Radar Context:** IMD can occur in radar receivers due to strong jammers or other interfering signals, leading to spurious signals on the display.
