# LOW POWER VLSI: Module 1 - Physics of Power Dissipation in MOSFET Devices

## Topic: Impact Ionization

### 1. Introduction to Impact Ionization

Impact ionization is a physical phenomenon that occurs in semiconductor devices when high-energy carriers (electrons or holes) collide with the crystal lattice and impart enough energy to generate new electron-hole pairs. This process is particularly significant in MOSFETs operating under high electric fields, which are often encountered in short-channel devices.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Carriers:** Mobile charge carriers in a semiconductor, namely electrons and holes.
*   **Crystal Lattice:** The regular, repeating arrangement of atoms in a semiconductor material.
*   **High Electric Field:** A strong electric field that accelerates carriers to high kinetic energies.
*   **Collision:** The interaction between a high-energy carrier and the crystal lattice.
*   **Generation of Electron-Hole Pairs:** The process where a sufficiently energetic carrier breaks a covalent bond, creating a new electron and a hole.

**Relevance to Power Dissipation:**

While not a primary *static* power dissipation mechanism, impact ionization contributes to **dynamic** power dissipation and can lead to reliability issues, especially in advanced technologies. The generated carriers can contribute to parasitic currents and affect device behavior.

### 2. Mechanism of Impact Ionization in MOSFETs

Impact ionization in MOSFETs is primarily driven by the high lateral electric field present in the channel region, especially near the drain end of a short-channel MOSFET.

**Process Steps:**

1.  **Carrier Acceleration:** Under a sufficiently high drain-to-source voltage ($V_{DS}$) and in a short channel, the electric field in the channel becomes very strong. This field accelerates the majority carriers (electrons in nMOS, holes in pMOS) towards the drain.
2.  **Carrier Collisions:** As these accelerated carriers gain significant kinetic energy, they collide with the silicon atoms in the crystal lattice.
3.  **Energy Transfer:** If the kinetic energy of an incident carrier exceeds a certain threshold energy (the impact ionization threshold energy, $E_{th}$), it can transfer enough energy to a valence electron to break a covalent bond.
4.  **Electron-Hole Pair Generation:** This bond breaking creates a new mobile electron-hole pair.
5.  **Secondary Carrier Multiplication:** The newly generated electron and hole are also accelerated by the electric field. If they gain enough energy, they can also cause further impact ionization events, leading to a multiplication of carriers.

**Example:**

Consider an nMOS transistor with a short channel. When a high $V_{DS}$ is applied, electrons flow from source to drain. Near the drain, the electric field is very high. An energetic electron can collide with the lattice, creating a new electron-hole pair. Both the original electron and the newly generated electron can move towards the drain, while the new hole can move towards the source or the substrate.

### 3. Key Parameters and Thresholds

*   **Impact Ionization Threshold Energy ($E_{th}$):** The minimum energy a carrier must possess to cause impact ionization. This energy is typically around 1.5 times the bandgap energy ($E_g$) of silicon (e.g., ~1.5 eV for silicon).
*   **Ionization Coefficient ($\alpha$ and $\beta$):** These coefficients describe the probability of ionization per unit distance traveled by an electron or hole, respectively, in the presence of an electric field. They are strongly dependent on the electric field strength.
    *   **Electron ionization coefficient ($\alpha$):** Probability of an electron causing ionization.
    *   **Hole ionization coefficient ($\beta$):** Probability of a hole causing ionization.
    *   Generally, $\alpha > \beta$ for silicon.
*   **Critical Electric Field ($E_{crit}$):** The electric field strength above which significant impact ionization occurs. This is related to the threshold energy.

**Relationship to Electric Field:**

The ionization coefficients are typically modeled by empirical relations, such as:

$\alpha = A \cdot e^{-B/E}$
$\beta = C \cdot e^{-D/E}$

Where $A, B, C, D$ are material-dependent constants, and $E$ is the electric field strength.

### 4. Impact Ionization Related Phenomena and Power Dissipation Aspects

Impact ionization leads to several phenomena that contribute to power dissipation and device degradation.

**4.1. Substrate Current Generation:**

*   **Mechanism:** The holes generated by impact ionization near the drain tend to flow towards the substrate terminal. This creates a substrate current ($I_{sub}$).
*   **Power Dissipation:** The substrate current flows through the substrate resistance, leading to a voltage drop and power dissipation ($I_{sub}^2 \cdot R_{sub}$). More importantly, it contributes to the **total current drawn from the power supply**.
*   **CO Alignment:** Directly relates to **CO1** (short channel effects) and **CO2** (sources of power dissipation).

**4.2. Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL) and Channel Length Modulation:**

*   **Mechanism:** The high electric field and the generated carriers can influence the potential barrier at the source, leading to DIBL. This effect, along with channel length modulation, can increase drain current at higher $V_{DS}$.
*   **Power Dissipation:** Increased drain current, especially at higher voltages, directly translates to increased dynamic and static power dissipation.
*   **CO Alignment:** Relates to **CO1**.

**4.3. Avalanche Breakdown:**

*   **Mechanism:** If the electric field is sufficiently high, carrier multiplication can become so severe that it leads to a runaway process, resulting in avalanche breakdown.
*   **Power Dissipation:** During breakdown, a very large current flows, causing significant power dissipation and potentially damaging the device.
*   **CO Alignment:** Relates to **CO1**.

**4.4. Hot Carrier Effects:**

*   **Mechanism:** Carriers accelerated to high energies by the strong electric field are termed "hot carriers." These hot carriers can have enough energy to overcome the potential barriers at the gate oxide-semiconductor interface.
*   **Consequences:**
    *   **Interface Trap Generation:** Hot carriers can break Si-H bonds at the Si-SiO2 interface, creating interface traps.
    *   **Charge Trapping:** Carriers can also be injected into and trapped within the gate oxide.
*   **Impact on Device Performance:**
    *   **Threshold Voltage Shift ($\Delta V_{th}$):** Trapped charge in the oxide and interface states alter the device's threshold voltage, leading to degraded performance.
    *   **Transconductance Degradation ($g_m$):** Reduced mobility due to scattering and interface states degrades the transconductance.
    *   **Increased Subthreshold Leakage:** Interface traps can increase subthreshold leakage current.
*   **Power Dissipation:** Degradation in $V_{th}$ and $g_m$ can lead to increased leakage power and reduced switching speed, indirectly affecting dynamic power. The primary concern here is reliability, but it impacts the power budget over the device's lifetime.
*   **CO Alignment:** Crucially relates to **CO1** (short channel effects and their impact) and indirectly to **CO2** (through leakage).

**4.5. Light Emission (Photon Generation):**

*   **Mechanism:** When hot carriers recombine or relax their energy after impact ionization, they can emit photons. This is known as **electroluminescence**.
*   **Power Dissipation:** This is a form of energy loss, though typically a very small component of overall power dissipation. It's more of an indicator of high field operation.
*   **CO Alignment:** Relates to **CO1** as a consequence of high field operation.

### 5. Mitigating Impact Ionization and Hot Carrier Effects

Since impact ionization and related hot carrier effects are detrimental to device performance and reliability, various design strategies aim to mitigate them.

**Design Strategies:**

*   **Device Engineering:**
    *   **Shallow Trench Isolation (STI):** Reduces bird's beak and helps in controlling the lateral electric field.
    *   **Halo/Lightly Doped Drain (LDD) Structures:** Introducing shallow boron implants (halos) around the source/drain junctions or using LDD structures can reduce the peak electric field at the drain junction. This spreads out the high field region, lowering the carrier energy.
    *   **Gate-Induced Drain Leakage (GIDL) Control:** Careful threshold voltage and gate overlap engineering.
*   **Circuit Design Techniques:**
    *   **Lowering Operating Voltages ($V_{DD}$):** Reduced supply voltage leads to lower electric fields within the MOSFET. This is a fundamental strategy for low-power design and directly mitigates impact ionization.
    *   **Stacking of Transistors:** In circuits where a high voltage drop is necessary, using multiple transistors in series can distribute the voltage across several devices, thus reducing the electric field in any single device. This is particularly relevant in high-voltage applications but can be conceptualized for moderating fields.
    *   **Body Biasing:** Forward or reverse biasing the body terminal can influence the electric fields and junction profiles, sometimes helping to reduce hot carrier injection.
    *   **Careful Layout:** Optimizing layout to avoid sharp corners and abrupt field changes.

**CO Alignment:**

*   **CO1:** Device engineering and voltage scaling are direct responses to short channel effects.
*   **CO3:** Lowering operating voltages is a key power management approach.
*   **CO4:** Design styles (e.g., pipelining or using different logic families) can indirectly influence voltage stress on transistors.

### 6. Examples and Calculations (Conceptual)

Let's consider a conceptual example to understand the relationship between electric field and ionization.

**Scenario:** A short-channel nMOS transistor operates with a high $V_{DS}$. Near the drain, the lateral electric field can be approximated as $E \approx V_{DS} / L_{eff}$, where $L_{eff}$ is the effective channel length.

If $L_{eff}$ is small (e.g., 50 nm) and $V_{DS}$ is high (e.g., 1.5 V), then $E \approx 1.5V / 50 \times 10^{-9}m = 30 MV/m$. This is a significant electric field.

Using an empirical model like $\alpha = A \cdot e^{-B/E}$:

*   If $E$ exceeds $E_{crit}$, the rate of ionization increases exponentially.
*   A higher $E$ means a higher $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
*   This leads to more generated carriers, higher substrate current, and increased probability of hot carrier effects.

**Practice Question 1:**

Explain how the substrate current in a short-channel MOSFET is generated and how it relates to impact ionization. What are the power dissipation implications of substrate current?

**Answer 1:**

Substrate current ($I_{sub}$) in a short-channel MOSFET is generated due to impact ionization. When the lateral electric field in the channel, particularly near the drain, becomes sufficiently high, carriers (electrons in nMOS) gain enough kinetic energy. Upon colliding with the silicon lattice, they can break covalent bonds, creating new electron-hole pairs. The holes generated by this process are typically swept towards the substrate terminal, contributing to the substrate current.

Power dissipation implications:
1.  **Voltage Drop:** The substrate current flowing through the substrate's finite resistance ($R_{sub}$) causes a voltage drop ($V_{sub} = I_{sub} \cdot R_{sub}$). This can affect the transistor's operating point and potentially lead to voltage-dependent threshold shifts.
2.  **Direct Power Dissipation:** The power dissipated in the substrate resistance is $P_{sub} = I_{sub}^2 \cdot R_{sub}$.
3.  **Indirect Power Dissipation:** Crucially, the carriers that constitute the substrate current are generated from the power supplied to the device. This means that impact ionization effectively increases the total current drawn from the power supply, thereby increasing the overall dynamic power consumption ($P_{dynamic} = V_{DD} \cdot I_{total}$).

**Practice Question 2:**

List at least three negative consequences of impact ionization on MOSFET device performance and reliability.

**Answer 2:**

1.  **Hot Carrier Effects:** Accelerated carriers (hot carriers) can inject into the gate oxide or create interface traps at the Si-SiO2 interface. This leads to:
    *   Threshold voltage shifts ($\Delta V_{th}$).
    *   Degradation of transconductance ($g_m$).
    *   Increased subthreshold leakage.
2.  **Avalanche Breakdown:** If the electric field is very high, carrier multiplication can lead to avalanche breakdown, causing a large current flow and potential device damage.
3.  **Increased Substrate Current:** The generated holes flowing to the substrate increase parasitic currents, leading to power dissipation and potential noise issues.
4.  **Light Emission (Electroluminescence):** A minor consequence, but energetic carriers emit photons, indicating high field operation.

**Practice Question 3:**

How does reducing the operating voltage ($V_{DD}$) help mitigate the effects of impact ionization?

**Answer 3:**

Reducing the operating voltage ($V_{DD}$) directly reduces the electric field within the MOSFET channel. The electric field is a primary driver for carrier acceleration. By lowering the electric field strength:

*   Carriers gain less kinetic energy.
*   The probability of exceeding the impact ionization threshold energy ($E_{th}$) decreases significantly.
*   The ionization coefficients ($\alpha$ and $\beta$) are reduced.
*   Consequently, the generation of new electron-hole pairs by impact ionization is suppressed.
*   This, in turn, reduces substrate current and the injection of hot carriers into the gate oxide/interface, thereby improving device reliability and reducing performance degradation.

### 7. Summary and Key Takeaways

*   **Impact ionization** is the process where high-energy carriers create new electron-hole pairs upon colliding with the crystal lattice.
*   It is primarily driven by **high electric fields** in the MOSFET channel, especially in short-channel devices.
*   Key phenomena include **substrate current generation**, **hot carrier effects** (interface trap generation, charge trapping), and potentially **avalange breakdown**.
*   **Hot carrier effects** are a major concern for device reliability, leading to performance degradation (threshold voltage shifts, $g_m$ reduction).
*   Impact ionization contributes to **dynamic power dissipation** by increasing the total current drawn from the supply.
*   Mitigation strategies focus on **device engineering** (e.g., LDD structures) and **circuit design** (e.g., voltage scaling, transistor stacking).
*   **Lowering $V_{DD}$** is a fundamental low-power technique that also significantly reduces impact ionization and hot carrier effects.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   Impact ionization is an **energy-dependent phenomenon**.
*   It becomes significant when electric fields exceed a **critical threshold**.
*   **Short channel lengths** are a major enabler of high electric fields.
*   The consequences are detrimental to both **performance** and **reliability**.
*   It is a critical factor to consider in the **scaling roadmap** of MOSFET technology for low-power designs.
