# Advanced Communication Theory: Module 1 - Entropy: Discrete Memoryless Sources

## Module Overview

This module introduces the fundamental concepts of information theory, starting with the notion of entropy as a measure of uncertainty or information content. We will focus on discrete memoryless sources (DMSs), which are the foundational building blocks for understanding more complex communication systems. This will lay the groundwork for source coding (data compression) and understanding the limits of communication.

Upon successful completion of this topic, you will be able to:

*   Define and calculate the entropy of a discrete random variable.
*   Understand the properties of entropy and its interpretation as average information content.
*   Introduce the concept of joint entropy and conditional entropy.
*   Relate entropy to data compression and the theoretical limits of compression.

## 1. Discrete Memoryless Sources (DMS)

### 1.1 Definition of a Source

A **source** in communication theory is a process that generates a sequence of symbols. These symbols can represent anything from letters in a text document to bits in a digital signal.

### 1.2 Definition of a Discrete Memoryless Source (DMS)

A **Discrete Memoryless Source (DMS)** is a source that generates symbols from a finite alphabet, and the generation of each symbol is independent of all previous symbols generated by the source.

**Key Characteristics:**

*   **Discrete Alphabet:** The set of possible symbols generated by the source is finite.
*   **Memoryless:** The probability distribution of the next symbol is independent of the past symbols generated. This means the source has no "memory" of its previous states or outputs.

**Mathematical Representation:**

A DMS can be characterized by:

*   An alphabet $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m\}$ representing the set of possible symbols.
*   A probability mass function (PMF) $P(x)$ for each symbol $x \in \mathcal{X}$, where $P(x_i) \ge 0$ for all $i$, and $\sum_{i=1}^m P(x_i) = 1$.

**Example:**

Consider a fair coin toss.
*   Alphabet: $\mathcal{X} = \{\text{Heads (H)}, \text{Tails (T)}\}$
*   PMF: $P(\text{H}) = 0.5$, $P(\text{T}) = 0.5$.
Since each coin toss is independent of previous tosses, this is a DMS.

Consider a biased coin toss where the probability of heads is 0.7.
*   Alphabet: $\mathcal{X} = \{\text{Heads (H)}, \text{Tails (T)}\}$
*   PMF: $P(\text{H}) = 0.7$, $P(\text{T}) = 0.3$.
This is also a DMS.

**Referenced Textbooks:**

*   **Elements of Information Theory by Cover & Thomas:** This is the primary reference for foundational information theory concepts like DMS, entropy, and their properties. The initial chapters will extensively cover these definitions.
*   **Wireless Communications by Goldsmith:** While focusing on wireless, Goldsmith often introduces information-theoretic concepts as building blocks for understanding channel capacity and data transmission limits.
*   **Communication Systems by Haykin:** Haykin's book will also introduce these concepts as they relate to the information-carrying capacity of communication channels.

## 2. Entropy

### 2.1 Information Content of a Symbol

The **information content** or **surprisal** of an event (symbol) $x$ is a measure of how surprising or unexpected that event is. It is inversely related to its probability. More probable events are less surprising, and less probable events are more surprising.

The information content of a symbol $x$ is defined as:

$I(x) = \log_b \left(\frac{1}{P(x)}\right) = -\log_b P(x)$

Where:
*   $P(x)$ is the probability of symbol $x$.
*   $b$ is the base of the logarithm.

**Units of Information:**

*   If $b=2$, the unit is **bits**.
*   If $b=e$, the unit is **nats**.
*   If $b=10$, the unit is **dits** or **hartleys**.

We will primarily use base-2 logarithms, so information will be measured in bits.

**Example:**

*   A fair coin toss: $P(\text{H}) = 0.5$, $P(\text{T}) = 0.5$.
    *   $I(\text{H}) = -\log_2(0.5) = -\log_2(2^{-1}) = 1$ bit.
    *   $I(\text{T}) = -\log_2(0.5) = -\log_2(2^{-1}) = 1$ bit.
    Each outcome of a fair coin toss conveys 1 bit of information.

*   A biased coin toss: $P(\text{H}) = 0.7$, $P(\text{T}) = 0.3$.
    *   $I(\text{H}) = -\log_2(0.7) \approx 0.515$ bits.
    *   $I(\text{T}) = -\log_2(0.3) \approx 1.737$ bits.
    The less probable outcome (Tails) carries more information.

### 2.2 Entropy of a Discrete Random Variable (Source)

**Entropy (H)** is the average information content per symbol generated by a DMS. It measures the uncertainty or randomness of the source.

For a discrete random variable $X$ with alphabet $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m\}$ and PMF $P(x_1), P(x_2), \dots, P(x_m)$, the entropy is defined as:

$H(X) = E[I(X)] = \sum_{i=1}^m P(x_i) I(x_i) = \sum_{i=1}^m P(x_i) (-\log_2 P(x_i))$

$H(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^m P(x_i) \log_2 P(x_i)$

**Interpretation of Entropy:**

*   **Average Uncertainty:** Entropy quantifies the average amount of uncertainty associated with the output of the source.
*   **Average Information:** It also represents the average number of bits required to represent each symbol from the source, assuming an optimal encoding strategy.
*   **Measure of Randomness:** A source with higher entropy is more random and less predictable.

**Important Points:**

*   **Non-negativity:** $H(X) \ge 0$.
*   **Maximum Entropy:** For a given alphabet size $m$, entropy is maximized when all symbols are equally likely (uniform distribution). In this case, $P(x_i) = 1/m$ for all $i$, and $H(X) = \log_2 m$.
*   **Minimum Entropy:** Entropy is zero if and only if one symbol has probability 1 and all others have probability 0 (i.e., the source is deterministic).

**Referenced Textbooks:**

*   **Elements of Information Theory by Cover & Thomas:** This is the definitive source for the definition and properties of entropy. They thoroughly explore its relationship with information and uncertainty.
*   **Communication Systems by Haykin:** Haykin will present entropy as a measure of information that forms the basis for understanding channel capacity.

### 2.3 Properties of Entropy

1.  **Non-negativity:** $H(X) \ge 0$.
    *   *Proof:* Since $0 \le P(x_i) \le 1$, $\log_2 P(x_i) \le 0$. Thus, $-\log_2 P(x_i) \ge 0$. The sum of non-negative terms is non-negative.

2.  **Maximum Entropy:** For an alphabet of size $m$, $H(X) \le \log_2 m$. Equality holds when $P(x_i) = 1/m$ for all $i$ (uniform distribution).
    *   *Intuition:* Maximum uncertainty occurs when all outcomes are equally likely.

3.  **Zero Entropy:** $H(X) = 0$ if and only if there exists an $i$ such that $P(x_i) = 1$ and $P(x_j) = 0$ for all $j \ne i$.
    *   *Proof:* If $P(x_i)=1$, then $-\log_2 P(x_i) = -\log_2 1 = 0$. The sum becomes $1 \times 0 = 0$.

4.  **Concavity:** Entropy is a concave function of the probability distribution. This is related to Jensen's inequality for concave functions.

**Example:**

Consider a source generating symbols from $\mathcal{X} = \{A, B, C\}$.

*   **Case 1: Uniform distribution**
    $P(A) = 1/3, P(B) = 1/3, P(C) = 1/3$
    $H(X) = -\left(\frac{1}{3}\log_2 \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3}\log_2 \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3}\log_2 \frac{1}{3}\right)$
    $H(X) = -3 \times \frac{1}{3} \log_2 \frac{1}{3} = -\log_2 \frac{1}{3} = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits.
    This is the maximum possible entropy for an alphabet of size 3.

*   **Case 2: Non-uniform distribution**
    $P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(C) = 0.1$
    $H(X) = -(0.5 \log_2 0.5 + 0.4 \log_2 0.4 + 0.1 \log_2 0.1)$
    $H(X) = -(0.5 \times (-1) + 0.4 \times (-1.322) + 0.1 \times (-3.322))$
    $H(X) = -(-0.5 - 0.529 - 0.332) = 1.361$ bits.
    This entropy is less than the maximum of $\log_2 3$.

**Highlighted Point:** The entropy quantifies the *average* uncertainty. Even if some symbols are very rare (high information content), if they occur with very low probability, they contribute less to the *average* uncertainty.

## 3. Joint Entropy and Conditional Entropy

These concepts extend the idea of entropy to multiple random variables and provide insights into the relationships between them. While not strictly part of a *memoryless* source's individual entropy calculation, they are crucial for understanding information flow in communication systems and are part of the broader information theory measures (CO1).

### 3.1 Joint Entropy

**Definition:** For two discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$, their joint entropy $H(X, Y)$ is the average amount of information required to specify the pair of outcomes $(X, Y)$.

$H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(x, y) \log_2 P(x, y)$

Where $P(x, y)$ is the joint probability mass function of $X$ and $Y$.

**Relationship to Individual Entropies:**

$H(X, Y) \le H(X) + H(Y)$

Equality holds if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

### 3.2 Conditional Entropy

**Definition:** The conditional entropy $H(Y|X)$ is the average amount of uncertainty remaining about $Y$ given that $X$ is known. It measures the uncertainty of $Y$ *after* $X$ has been observed.

$H(Y|X) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) H(Y|X=x)$

Where $H(Y|X=x) = -\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(y|x) \log_2 P(y|x)$ is the entropy of $Y$ given $X=x$.

The formula can also be written as:

$H(Y|X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(x, y) \log_2 P(y|x)$

**Chain Rule for Entropy:**

The joint entropy can be expressed in terms of individual and conditional entropies:

$H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X)$
$H(X, Y) = H(Y) + H(X|Y)$

**Important Properties:**

1.  **Non-negativity:** $H(Y|X) \ge 0$.
2.  **Relationship to Joint Entropy:** $H(Y|X) \le H(Y)$. This means knowing $X$ can only reduce or keep the uncertainty of $Y the same; it cannot increase it. Equality holds if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
3.  **Independence:** $H(Y|X) = H(Y)$ if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

**Example:**

Consider a source generating pairs of symbols $(X, Y)$ where $X$ is a fair coin flip (H/T, 0.5/0.5) and $Y$ is a symbol that is 'A' if $X$ is H, and 'B' if $X$ is T.

*   $P(X=H) = 0.5, P(X=T) = 0.5 \implies H(X) = 1$ bit.
*   $P(Y=A) = P(Y=A|X=H)P(X=H) + P(Y=A|X=T)P(X=T) = 1 \times 0.5 + 0 \times 0.5 = 0.5$.
*   $P(Y=B) = P(Y=B|X=H)P(X=H) + P(Y=B|X=T)P(X=T) = 0 \times 0.5 + 1 \times 0.5 = 0.5$.
*   $H(Y) = -(0.5 \log_2 0.5 + 0.5 \log_2 0.5) = 1$ bit.

Now, let's calculate conditional entropy $H(Y|X)$:

*   If $X=H$, then $Y$ must be 'A'. So $P(Y=A|X=H) = 1$, $P(Y=B|X=H) = 0$.
    $H(Y|X=H) = -(1 \log_2 1 + 0 \log_2 0) = 0$. (By convention, $0 \log_2 0 = 0$)
*   If $X=T$, then $Y$ must be 'B'. So $P(Y=A|X=T) = 0$, $P(Y=B|X=T) = 1$.
    $H(Y|X=T) = -(0 \log_2 0 + 1 \log_2 1) = 0$.

$H(Y|X) = P(X=H)H(Y|X=H) + P(X=T)H(Y|X=T)$
$H(Y|X) = 0.5 \times 0 + 0.5 \times 0 = 0$ bits.

In this case, $H(Y|X) = 0$, which makes sense because knowing $X$ completely determines $Y$.

**Referenced Textbooks:**

*   **Elements of Information Theory by Cover & Thomas:** These are fundamental concepts explored in detail in this text.
*   **Communication Systems by Haykin:** Haykin will discuss conditional entropy in the context of understanding the relationship between a transmitted signal and the received signal, especially in the presence of noise.

## 4. Entropy and Data Compression (Source Coding)

Entropy is the cornerstone of data compression. The **Source Coding Theorem** (Shannon's First Theorem) establishes a fundamental limit on how much a sequence of symbols from a DMS can be compressed.

### 4.1 The Source Coding Theorem

**Statement:** For a DMS $X$ with entropy $H(X)$ bits per symbol, it is possible to represent sequences of symbols from $X$ using an average number of bits per symbol arbitrarily close to $H(X)$. Conversely, it is impossible to compress the data to achieve an average number of bits per symbol less than $H(X)$.

**Implication:** $H(X)$ is the theoretical lower bound on the average code length for lossless data compression.

**How it works (Intuition):**

*   **Most Probable Symbols:** More frequent symbols should be assigned shorter codewords.
*   **Least Probable Symbols:** Less frequent symbols can be assigned longer codewords.
*   **Average Code Length:** The weighted average of codeword lengths (weighted by symbol probabilities) should approach $H(X)$.

**Example (Illustrative):**

Consider a DMS with the following probabilities:
$P(A) = 0.5$
$P(B) = 0.25$
$P(C) = 0.125$
$P(D) = 0.125$

$H(X) = -(0.5 \log_2 0.5 + 0.25 \log_2 0.25 + 0.125 \log_2 0.125 + 0.125 \log_2 0.125)$
$H(X) = -(0.5 \times -1 + 0.25 \times -2 + 0.125 \times -3 + 0.125 \times -3)$
$H(X) = -(-0.5 - 0.5 - 0.375 - 0.375) = 1.75$ bits.

A hypothetical optimal encoding could assign codewords:
*   A: 0 (length 1)
*   B: 10 (length 2)
*   C: 110 (length 3)
*   D: 111 (length 3)

Average code length $= 0.5 \times 1 + 0.25 \times 2 + 0.125 \times 3 + 0.125 \times 3$
Average code length $= 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75$ bits.
This matches the entropy, demonstrating the principle.

**Referenced Textbooks:**

*   **Elements of Information Theory by Cover & Thomas:** This book details the Source Coding Theorem and its implications for data compression.
*   **Wireless Communications by Goldsmith:** Information theory limits, including the source coding theorem, are often discussed as context for efficient data transmission.
*   **Communication Systems by Haykin:** Haykin will relate entropy to the fundamental limits of data compression.

**Course Outcome Addressed:**

*   **CO2:** Apply source coding theorem for data compression. (Knowledge Level: K3) - Understanding entropy is the first step to applying the source coding theorem.

## 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A discrete memoryless source generates symbols from the alphabet $\mathcal{X} = \{a, b, c, d\}$ with the following probabilities: $P(a) = 0.4$, $P(b) = 0.3$, $P(c) = 0.2$, $P(d) = 0.1$.
(a) Calculate the information content of each symbol.
(b) Calculate the entropy of the source.
(c) What is the maximum possible entropy for this source?

**Answer 1:**
(a)
$I(a) = -\log_2(0.4) \approx 1.32$ bits
$I(b) = -\log_2(0.3) \approx 1.74$ bits
$I(c) = -\log_2(0.2) \approx 2.32$ bits
$I(d) = -\log_2(0.1) \approx 3.32$ bits

(b)
$H(X) = -(0.4 \log_2 0.4 + 0.3 \log_2 0.3 + 0.2 \log_2 0.2 + 0.1 \log_2 0.1)$
$H(X) \approx -(0.4 \times -1.32 + 0.3 \times -1.74 + 0.2 \times -2.32 + 0.1 \times -3.32)$
$H(X) \approx -(-0.528 - 0.522 - 0.464 - 0.332) \approx 1.846$ bits.

(c)
The maximum possible entropy for a source with an alphabet of size 4 is $\log_2 4 = 2$ bits. This occurs when all symbols have a probability of $1/4 = 0.25$.

---

**Question 2:**
Let $X$ and $Y$ be two independent discrete random variables with $H(X) = 2$ bits and $H(Y) = 1.5$ bits.
(a) What is $H(X, Y)$?
(b) What is $H(Y|X)$?

**Answer 2:**
(a) Since $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y)$.
$H(X, Y) = 2 + 1.5 = 3.5$ bits.

(b) Since $X$ and $Y$ are independent, knowing $X$ does not reduce the uncertainty of $Y$. Therefore, $H(Y|X) = H(Y)$.
$H(Y|X) = 1.5$ bits.

---

**Question 3:**
Consider a DMS that outputs the letter 'E' with probability 0.7 and 'T' with probability 0.3.
(a) Calculate the entropy of this source.
(b) If we were to design an optimal prefix code for this source, what would be the approximate average codeword length?

**Answer 3:**
(a)
$H(X) = -(0.7 \log_2 0.7 + 0.3 \log_2 0.3)$
$H(X) \approx -(0.7 \times -0.515 + 0.3 \times -1.737)$
$H(X) \approx -(-0.3605 - 0.5211) \approx 0.8816$ bits.

(b)
According to the source coding theorem, the average codeword length for an optimal prefix code will be arbitrarily close to the entropy. Therefore, the approximate average codeword length would be $\approx 0.8816$ bits.

---

**Question 4 (Conceptual):**
Explain why a fair coin toss has higher entropy than a biased coin toss (e.g., P(H)=0.9, P(T)=0.1). Relate this to the concept of uncertainty.

**Answer 4:**
A fair coin toss has an entropy of 1 bit, while a biased coin toss has lower entropy (calculated as $-(0.9 \log_2 0.9 + 0.1 \log_2 0.1) \approx 0.469$ bits).

*   **Uncertainty:** The fair coin toss has maximum uncertainty because both outcomes (Heads and Tails) are equally likely. You have no more reason to believe one outcome will occur over the other.
*   **Bias:** The biased coin toss has lower uncertainty. You are much more likely to see Heads (90% chance). Therefore, the outcome of Heads is less surprising, and the outcome of Tails is more surprising. The overall average uncertainty about the next outcome is lower because the bias makes one outcome highly predictable. Higher probability leads to less information gained when that outcome occurs.

## Key Takeaways and Important Points to Remember

*   **DMS:** A source with a finite alphabet and independent symbol generation. Crucial for initial information theory modeling.
*   **Information Content:** $I(x) = -\log_2 P(x)$. Measures the "surprisal" of a symbol.
*   **Entropy $H(X)$:** The average information content per symbol. $H(X) = -\sum P(x_i) \log_2 P(x_i)$.
    *   Measures **uncertainty** or **randomness** of a source.
    *   Theoretical lower bound for **lossless data compression** (average bits per symbol).
*   **Maximum Entropy:** Occurs with uniform probability distributions ($H(X) = \log_2 m$ for $m$ symbols).
*   **Conditional Entropy $H(Y|X)$:** Uncertainty of $Y$ given $X$. $H(Y|X) \le H(Y)$.
*   **Source Coding Theorem:** Connects entropy to compression limits.
