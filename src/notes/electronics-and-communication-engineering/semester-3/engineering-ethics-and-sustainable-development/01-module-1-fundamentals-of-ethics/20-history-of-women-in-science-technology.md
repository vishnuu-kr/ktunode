# Engineering Ethics and Sustainable Development - Module 1: Fundamentals of Ethics

## Topic: History of Women in Science & Technology

This topic explores the often-overlooked contributions of women to science and technology throughout history, highlighting the systemic barriers they faced and their resilience. Understanding this history is crucial for fostering a more inclusive and equitable future in STEM, directly aligning with **Course Outcome 2 (CO2)**: "Develop the ability to exercise gender-sensitive practices in their professional lives."

---

### 1. Introduction: The Importance of Reclaiming Women's Histories in STEM

*   **The "Great Man" Theory of History:** Traditional historical narratives often focus on prominent male figures, inadvertently marginalizing or erasing the contributions of women and other underrepresented groups.
*   **Impact on Present and Future:** Ignoring women's contributions creates a skewed understanding of scientific progress and perpetuates biases that can hinder current and future generations of women in STEM.
*   **Ethical Imperative:** Recognizing and celebrating women's achievements is an ethical imperative, promoting fairness, diversity, and a more accurate representation of human endeavor. This connects to **CO1** by emphasizing ethical principles in professional life.
*   **Inspiration and Role Models:** Learning about women who overcame obstacles in STEM provides invaluable inspiration and role models for aspiring scientists and engineers, especially young women.

### 2. Early Contributions and Eras of Exclusion

#### 2.1 Ancient and Medieval Periods

*   **Limited Documentation:** Evidence of women's scientific and technological involvement in these periods is often scarce due to societal norms that restricted women's access to education and public life.
*   **Herbalism and Midwifery:** Women played significant roles as healers, midwives, and herbalists, contributing to early forms of medicine and pharmacology. These were often passed down through oral traditions.
*   **Mathematics and Astronomy:**
    *   **Hypatia of Alexandria (c. 350-415 AD):** A prominent philosopher, astronomer, and mathematician. She taught and lectured in Alexandria, and is credited with commentaries on mathematical and astronomical works.
        *   **Example:** Hypatia's work on conic sections is notable. She also improved astronomical instruments.
        *   **Reference:** While not explicitly detailed in the provided book list, historical accounts of science and philosophy would cover Hypatia. Her story exemplifies women engaging in intellectual pursuits despite societal constraints.
*   **The "Witch Hunts":** The persecution of women accused of witchcraft in later medieval and early modern periods often targeted those with knowledge of natural remedies and healing, further suppressing female expertise.

#### 2.2 The Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution

*   **Continued Barriers:** While the Scientific Revolution saw immense progress, women were largely excluded from formal scientific institutions and university education.
*   **Amateur and Domestic Science:** Women participated in scientific activities primarily within the domestic sphere or as assistants to male relatives.
*   **Maria Sibylla Merian (1647-1717):** A naturalist and scientific illustrator. She conducted groundbreaking research on insect metamorphosis, publishing detailed observations and illustrations.
    *   **Example:** Her book, *Metamorphosis insectorum Surinamensium* (The Metamorphosis of the Insects of Suriname), documented her expeditions and findings, revolutionizing entomology.
    *   **Reference:** Whitbeck's *Ethics in Engineering Practice and Research* might touch upon historical scientific practices and the challenges faced by individuals, providing context for Merian's work as a form of rigorous observation and documentation.
*   **Margaret Cavendish (1623-1673):** A philosopher and writer who wrote extensively on natural philosophy, challenging prevailing scientific ideas.
    *   **Example:** Her philosophical works debated scientific concepts of the time, offering a female intellectual perspective.

#### 2.3 The Enlightenment and Industrial Revolution

*   **"Salonnières" and Scientific Circles:** Some women hosted salons where scientific and philosophical discussions took place, acting as intellectual facilitators.
*   **Assisted Observation and Data Collection:** Women often served as assistants to male scientists, meticulously recording data and performing experiments, though their direct contributions were rarely acknowledged.
*   **Caroline Herschel (1750-1848):** An astronomer who worked alongside her brother, William Herschel. She discovered several comets and nebulae and cataloged stars.
    *   **Example:** She discovered eight comets and was the first woman to receive a salary as a scientific assistant from the Royal Society.
    *   **Reference:** The broad scope of ethics in science, as discussed in Whitbeck or Martin & Schinzinger, could implicitly address the societal structures that led to such limited recognition.

### 3. The 19th and Early 20th Centuries: Gaining Footholds

*   **Emergence of Women's Colleges and Universities:** The establishment of institutions dedicated to women's education began to open doors, albeit with separate and often unequal opportunities.
*   **"Computers" as Human Calculators:** Women were frequently employed as "computers" to perform complex calculations for astronomical and engineering projects.
    *   **Example:** The women who worked at NASA (then NACA) on early space missions, like the "human computers" of the Space Race (e.g., Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, Mary Jackson), made critical contributions to flight calculations. Their story, though from the mid-20th century, has roots in this earlier practice.
    *   **Reference:** Bakshi's *Sustainable Engineering Principles and Practice* or de Vries' *Sustainability Science* might touch upon the historical reliance on calculation and data analysis in large-scale projects, indirectly referencing the human effort involved, including that of women.
*   **Pioneering Scientists:**
    *   **Marie Curie (1867-1934):** A physicist and chemist who conducted pioneering research on radioactivity. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the first person and only woman to win the Nobel Prize twice, and the only person to win the Nobel Prize in two different scientific fields.
        *   **Example:** Her discovery of polonium and radium, and her work on isolating radioactive isotopes.
        *   **Reference:** Whitbeck and Martin & Schinzinger would likely discuss the ethical considerations of scientific research and discovery, and Curie's work is a prime example of groundbreaking scientific achievement and its societal impact.
    *   **Ada Lovelace (1815-1852):** Considered the first computer programmer for her work on Charles Babbage's proposed mechanical general-purpose computer, the Analytical Engine.
        *   **Example:** She wrote an algorithm intended to be processed by the machine, making her the first to recognize the full potential of computers beyond mere calculation.
        *   **Reference:** Discussions on the history of computing and technological innovation, potentially found in broader engineering ethics texts, would highlight Lovelace's foundational work.

### 4. Mid-20th Century Onwards: Breaking More Barriers

*   **Post-War Opportunities and Setbacks:** While World War II saw some women enter previously male-dominated roles, many were pushed back into domesticity afterward.
*   **Civil Rights and Feminist Movements:** These movements spurred greater awareness and advocacy for women's equality in education and employment, including in STEM.
*   **Increased Representation, Persistent Challenges:** While numbers improved, women continued to face challenges such as:
    *   **Glass Ceiling:** Invisible barriers preventing women from reaching top leadership positions.
    *   **Gender Pay Gap:** Earning less than male counterparts for equivalent work.
    *   **Stereotyping and Bias:** Assumptions about women's abilities and suitability for STEM fields.
    *   **Lack of Mentorship and Support Networks:** Fewer senior women to mentor and guide junior women.
*   **Key Figures:**
    *   **Grace Hopper (1906-1992):** A computer scientist and Rear Admiral in the U.S. Navy who pioneered the first compiler for a computer programming language and was instrumental in the development of COBOL.
        *   **Example:** Her development of the first compiler significantly advanced the field of computer programming.
        *   **Reference:** Discussions on technological innovation and the evolution of computing, which are relevant to engineering practice, would encompass Hopper's contributions.
    *   **Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, Mary Jackson:** The "human computers" at NASA, whose mathematical prowess was critical to calculating trajectories for Project Mercury and other missions. Their story was popularized by the book and film *Hidden Figures*.
        *   **Reference:** Bakshi's *Sustainable Engineering Principles and Practice* might include case studies of major engineering projects where accurate calculations were paramount, indirectly highlighting the importance of such contributions.
    *   **Dr. Shirley Ann Jackson:** A theoretical physicist and the first African-American woman to earn a doctorate from MIT. Her research has been instrumental in advancements in telecommunications, semiconductors, and energy.
        *   **Example:** Her work has had a profound impact on the development of technologies like caller ID, call waiting, and fiber optics.
        *   **Reference:** Her contributions directly relate to technological development, aligning with the broader themes of engineering ethics and societal impact discussed in texts like Martin & Schinzinger.

### 5. Contemporary Issues and the Path Forward

*   **Underrepresentation Persists:** Despite progress, women remain underrepresented in many STEM fields, particularly in leadership roles and in certain disciplines like engineering and computer science.
*   **Intersectionality:** The experiences of women in STEM are not monolithic. Race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, disability, and socioeconomic status intersect to create unique challenges.
*   **Promoting Inclusivity and Equity:**
    *   **STEM Education Initiatives:** Programs aimed at encouraging girls and young women to pursue STEM education from an early age.
    *   **Mentorship and Sponsorship Programs:** Creating formal and informal networks to support women in their careers.
    *   **Addressing Unconscious Bias:** Training and awareness programs to mitigate biases in hiring, promotion, and evaluation.
    *   **Flexible Work Policies:** Supporting work-life balance, which disproportionately affects women who often bear greater caregiving responsibilities.
    *   **Zero Tolerance for Harassment and Discrimination:** Creating safe and respectful work environments.
*   **Ethical Responsibilities of Engineers:**
    *   **CO1 (Apply principles of engineering ethics):** Engineers have an ethical duty to promote fairness and equity in their professional interactions and to ensure that all individuals have the opportunity to contribute their talents.
    *   **CO2 (Gender-sensitive practices):** Actively working to dismantle systemic barriers and promote inclusivity in STEM is a direct application of gender-sensitive practice. This includes challenging biased language, advocating for equal opportunities, and supporting colleagues.
    *   **CO3, CO4, CO5 (Sustainability and Climate Resilience):** Addressing global challenges like climate change requires the full spectrum of human talent. Excluding or marginalizing any group, including women, limits our collective capacity to innovate and find sustainable solutions. A diverse workforce brings varied perspectives essential for tackling complex problems.

### 6. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **STEM:** Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics.
*   **Gender Bias:** The tendency to favor one gender over another, often unconsciously.
*   **Glass Ceiling:** An invisible barrier that prevents certain demographic groups, particularly women, from advancing to senior leadership positions.
*   **Intersectionality:** The interconnected nature of social categorizations such as race, class, and gender, creating overlapping and interdependent systems of discrimination or disadvantage.
*   **Unconscious Bias:** Attitudes or stereotypes that affect our understanding, actions, and decisions in an unconscious manner.
*   **Equity:** Fairness and justice; providing resources and opportunities based on need to achieve equal outcomes.
*   **Equality:** The state of being equal, especially in status, rights, and opportunities.
*   **Mentorship:** A developmental relationship in which a more experienced or more knowledgeable person helps to guide a less experienced or less knowledgeable person.
*   **Sponsorship:** A relationship where a senior leader advocates for and actively promotes the career advancement of a protégé.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   The history of women in STEM is a history of overcoming significant societal and institutional barriers.
*   Contributions of women have often been overlooked or minimized.
*   Recognizing and rectifying these historical omissions is an ethical responsibility.
*   Diversity and inclusion in STEM are not just about fairness but are essential for innovation and effective problem-solving, especially in addressing global challenges like sustainability.
*   Engineers have a professional and ethical obligation to foster inclusive environments and practices.
*   Understanding this history helps us to better address current inequities and build a more equitable future.

### 8. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Discuss the historical context that led to the underrepresentation of women in STEM fields. Identify at least two specific barriers that women historically faced. (Aligns with CO2)

**Answer 1:**
Historically, women faced significant barriers to participation in STEM, including:
1.  **Limited Access to Education:** For centuries, formal education, especially higher education and scientific training, was largely inaccessible to women. They were often excluded from universities and scientific societies.
2.  **Societal Norms and Expectations:** Societal roles confined women primarily to domestic duties, childcare, and supportive roles. Scientific and technological pursuits were considered masculine endeavors.
3.  **Lack of Recognition and Credit:** Even when women contributed significantly, their work was often attributed to male colleagues or family members, or simply not documented.
4.  **Exclusion from Professional Networks:** Women were barred from informal networks where knowledge was shared and careers were advanced.

**Question 2:**
Choose one prominent woman in STEM history (e.g., Marie Curie, Ada Lovelace, Grace Hopper, Katherine Johnson) and explain her key contribution and the significance of her work. How does her story highlight the challenges faced by women in her time? (Aligns with CO2)

**Answer 2:**
Let's consider **Ada Lovelace**.
*   **Key Contribution:** Ada Lovelace is recognized as the first computer programmer for her work on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine. She wrote an algorithm intended to be processed by the machine, demonstrating an understanding of its potential beyond mere calculation – she foresaw its capacity for creative output.
*   **Significance:** Her work laid the conceptual foundation for modern computing and programming languages, making her a visionary in the nascent field of computer science.
*   **Challenges:** Lovelace's story highlights the challenges of intellectual women in the 19th century. Despite her intellectual prowess and access to education (uncommon for women of her era), her work was not fully appreciated or understood by many of her contemporaries. She had to navigate a society where women's intellectual contributions were often dismissed or patronized. Her own health issues also posed personal challenges.

**Question 3:**
How does promoting gender diversity and inclusion in STEM contribute to achieving sustainable development goals, particularly in areas like climate resilience and technological innovation? (Aligns with CO2, CO3, CO4)

**Answer 3:**
Promoting gender diversity and inclusion in STEM is crucial for sustainable development for several reasons:
1.  **Broader Talent Pool:** By including women, STEM fields tap into a wider range of talent, perspectives, and problem-solving approaches. This is vital for tackling complex global challenges like climate change, which require innovative and diverse solutions.
2.  **Varied Perspectives:** Women often bring different lived experiences and priorities, which can lead to the development of more inclusive and effective technologies and policies. For instance, in designing sustainable infrastructure or climate adaptation strategies, diverse input can ensure that solutions benefit all segments of society.
3.  **Innovation:** Research consistently shows that diverse teams are more innovative. Women's participation can spark new ideas and approaches in areas such as renewable energy, sustainable agriculture, and environmental monitoring.
4.  **Addressing Societal Needs:** A gender-balanced workforce is more likely to understand and address the needs of the entire population, including those disproportionately affected by environmental degradation or climate change.
5.  **Role Models and Future Generations:** Increased visibility of women in STEM inspires more girls to pursue these fields, creating a pipeline of future scientists and engineers committed to sustainability.

**Question 4 (Exercise):**
Reflect on a time you observed or experienced gender bias (subtle or overt) in an academic or professional setting related to science or technology. How could an engineering ethical framework, as discussed in CO1, guide actions to address such bias?

**Answer 4 (Self-Reflection/Discussion Prompt):**
This question is designed for personal reflection and discussion within a group. An engineering ethical framework, as outlined in texts like Whitbeck or Martin & Schinzinger, would emphasize principles such as:
*   **Honesty and Integrity:** Being truthful about the contributions of all individuals.
*   **Fairness and Justice:** Ensuring equal opportunities and treatment regardless of gender.
*   **Respect for Persons:** Valuing the dignity and contributions of every individual.
*   **Professional Responsibility:** Upholding the reputation of the profession by ensuring it is inclusive and equitable.

An engineer could act by:
*   **Speaking up:** Challenging biased remarks or practices.
*   **Mentoring and supporting:** Actively helping women advance their careers.
*   **Advocating for inclusive policies:** Supporting initiatives that promote diversity and address bias in hiring and promotion.
*   **Ensuring credit is given:** Publicly acknowledging the contributions of female colleagues.
