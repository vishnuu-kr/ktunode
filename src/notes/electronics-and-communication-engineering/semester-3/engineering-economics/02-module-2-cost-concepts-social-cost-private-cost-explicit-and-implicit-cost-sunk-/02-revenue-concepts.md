# Engineering Economics: Module 2 - Cost Concepts & Revenue Concepts

## Introduction to Module 2

This module delves into the fundamental building blocks of economic analysis in engineering decision-making: **Cost Concepts** and **Revenue Concepts**. Understanding these concepts is crucial for evaluating the profitability and economic viability of engineering projects and business decisions. We will explore different types of costs and revenues, their implications, and how they influence outcomes.

## 2.1 Cost Concepts (Review and Extension)

While the primary focus of this section is on revenue, a brief review and extension of cost concepts from previous discussions are essential for context.

### 2.1.1 Social Cost vs. Private Cost

*   **Private Cost:** The cost incurred by an individual or firm for producing or consuming a good or service. These are the costs that appear in the firm's accounting statements.
    *   **Example:** The cost of raw materials, labor wages, and factory rent paid by a manufacturing company.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** *Managerial Economics* by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015) highlights private costs as those borne directly by the decision-maker.

*   **Social Cost:** The total cost to society of producing or consuming a good or service. This includes private costs plus any external costs (externalities) imposed on third parties not directly involved in the transaction.
    *   **Externalities:** Costs or benefits that affect a party who did not choose to incur that cost or benefit.
        *   **Negative Externalities:** Costs imposed on others (e.g., pollution from a factory).
        *   **Positive Externalities:** Benefits conferred on others (e.g., a new public park built by a company).
    *   **Example:** The social cost of a factory's production includes its private costs *plus* the cost to the community from air pollution (healthcare costs, damage to property, reduced quality of life).
    *   **Textbook Reference:** While not explicitly detailed in the provided titles, understanding externalities is a core concept in broader economic principles discussed in engineering economics contexts, often related to environmental and societal impact assessments. *Contemporary Engineering Economics* by Chan S. Park (2001) might touch upon broader societal impacts.

### 2.1.2 Explicit Cost vs. Implicit Cost

*   **Explicit Cost (Out-of-Pocket Cost):** Costs that involve a direct, tangible payment of money. These are readily quantifiable and recorded in accounting records.
    *   **Example:** Wages paid to employees, rent for a factory, cost of raw materials, utility bills.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** *Engineering Economy* by R. Paneerselvam (2012) and *Engineering Economics* by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky (1966) both emphasize explicit costs as the direct financial outlays.

*   **Implicit Cost (Opportunity Cost):** Costs that represent the value of forgone opportunities or resources that are used without a direct monetary payment. They represent the value of the next best alternative use of a resource.
    *   **Example:**
        *   The salary an entrepreneur forgoes by starting their own business instead of working for someone else.
        *   The return on capital that an owner could have earned if they had invested their money elsewhere instead of in their business.
        *   The use of a building owned by the company instead of renting it out to another tenant.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** *Managerial Economics* by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015) extensively discusses implicit costs in the context of economic profit versus accounting profit.

### 2.1.3 Sunk Cost

*   **Sunk Cost:** A cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of future decisions. Sunk costs are irrelevant to future decision-making because they are unchangeable.
    *   **Example:**
        *   Money spent on research and development for a product that is ultimately not launched.
        *   The purchase price of specialized machinery that cannot be resold.
        *   Marketing expenses for a campaign that has already concluded.
    *   **Key Principle:** "Don't cry over spilled milk." Future decisions should be based on future costs and benefits, not past irrecoverable expenditures.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** *Engineering Economy* by R. Paneerselvam (2012) and *Engineering Economics and analysis* by Donald G. Newman, Jerome P. Lavelle (2002) both stress the importance of ignoring sunk costs in rational decision-making.

## 2.2 Revenue Concepts

Revenue is the income generated from the sale of goods or services. Understanding different types of revenue is crucial for assessing the financial performance and profitability of an engineering project or business venture. This section directly addresses **Learning Outcome 2: Develop decision making capability by applying concepts relating to costs and revenue**.

### 2.2.1 Total Revenue (TR)

*   **Definition:** The total income generated by a firm from the sale of a given quantity of a product or service. It is calculated by multiplying the price per unit by the number of units sold.
*   **Formula:**
    $TR = P \times Q$
    Where:
    *   $TR$ = Total Revenue
    *   $P$ = Price per unit
    *   $Q$ = Quantity of units sold

*   **Example:** A civil engineering firm charges \$5,000 per consultation for its services. If it completes 10 consultations in a month, its total revenue for the month is:
    $TR = \$5,000 \times 10 = \$50,000$

*   **Textbook Reference:** All textbooks listed (Geetika et al., Thuesen & Fabrycky, Paneerselvam) will cover Total Revenue as a fundamental concept. *Engineering Economy* by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky (1966) provides a strong foundation in this area.

### 2.2.2 Average Revenue (AR)

*   **Definition:** The revenue generated per unit of output sold. It is calculated by dividing total revenue by the quantity of units sold.
*   **Formula:**
    $AR = \frac{TR}{Q}$
    Since $TR = P \times Q$, we can also write:
    $AR = \frac{P \times Q}{Q} = P$
    Therefore, **Average Revenue is equal to the Price per unit**.

*   **Example:** Continuing the civil engineering firm example:
    $AR = \frac{\$50,000}{10} = \$5,000$
    This confirms that the average revenue per consultation is equal to the price charged for each consultation.

*   **Textbook Reference:** *Managerial Economics* by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015) and *Engineering Economy* by R. Paneerselvam (2012) will explain AR in relation to pricing strategies.

### 2.2.3 Marginal Revenue (MR)

*   **Definition:** The additional revenue gained from selling one more unit of a product or service. It represents the change in total revenue resulting from a one-unit increase in sales.
*   **Formula:**
    $MR = \frac{\Delta TR}{\Delta Q}$
    Where:
    *   $\Delta TR$ = Change in Total Revenue
    *   $\Delta Q$ = Change in Quantity (usually 1 unit)

*   **Example:** Consider a company selling customized software licenses.
    *   Selling 50 licenses at \$1,000 each yields $TR = \$50,000$.
    *   Selling 51 licenses at \$990 each (assuming a price reduction is needed to sell more) yields $TR = \$50,490$.
    *   The Marginal Revenue for the 51st license is:
        $MR = \frac{\$50,490 - \$50,000}{51 - 50} = \frac{\$490}{1} = \$490$

*   **Key Relationship:** In perfect competition, price is constant, so $MR = P = AR$. In imperfect markets (monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly), to sell more units, the firm usually has to lower the price for all units, causing $MR$ to be less than $AR$ and to decline at a faster rate than $AR$.
*   **Textbook Reference:** *Managerial Economics* by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015) and *Contemporary Engineering Economics* by Chan S. Park (2001) will discuss MR in the context of firm behavior and market structures.

### 2.2.4 Relationship between TR, AR, and MR

This relationship is fundamental to understanding pricing and output decisions.

*   **In Perfect Competition:**
    *   Price (P) is constant.
    *   $AR = P$
    *   $MR = P$
    *   Therefore, $TR = P \times Q$ (a straight line through the origin with a slope equal to P).
    *   $AR$ and $MR$ are horizontal lines at the market price.

*   **In Imperfect Competition (Downward-Sloping Demand Curve):**
    *   To sell more, the firm must lower the price for all units.
    *   $AR$ is the demand curve.
    *   $MR$ lies below the $AR$ curve.
    *   $MR$ is typically twice as steep as the $AR$ curve (when the demand curve is linear).
    *   $TR$ initially increases, reaches a maximum, and then decreases.
    *   $MR$ is positive when $TR$ is increasing, zero when $TR$ is at its maximum, and negative when $TR$ is decreasing.

### 2.2.5 Relationship to Course Outcomes

*   **CO1 (Demand, Supply, Elasticity):** Revenue concepts are directly linked to the demand curve. The elasticity of demand influences how total revenue changes with price changes, and consequently impacts marginal revenue.
*   **CO2 (Costs and Revenue, Firm Functioning):** Understanding revenue is as critical as understanding costs for developing decision-making capabilities regarding pricing, production levels, and overall firm performance in different market situations.
*   **CO4 (Break-Even Analysis):** Total Revenue is a key component of break-even analysis, where the point at which total revenue equals total cost determines profitability.

## 2.3 Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A manufacturing company produces specialized industrial pumps. It sells them at \$2,500 per pump. In a particular month, it sold 80 pumps.
a) Calculate the Total Revenue (TR) for the month.
b) Calculate the Average Revenue (AR) per pump.
c) If the company decides to sell 81 pumps and has to lower the price to \$2,480 per pump to do so, calculate the Marginal Revenue (MR) for the 81st pump.

**Answer 1:**
a) $TR = Price \times Quantity = \$2,500 \times 80 = \$200,000$
b) $AR = \frac{TR}{Quantity} = \frac{\$200,000}{80} = \$2,500$ (which is equal to the price)
c)
New Total Revenue ($TR_{81}$) = $81 \times \$2,480 = \$200,880$
Change in Total Revenue ($\Delta TR$) = $TR_{81} - TR_{80} = \$200,880 - \$200,000 = \$880$
Change in Quantity ($\Delta Q$) = $81 - 80 = 1$
$MR = \frac{\Delta TR}{\Delta Q} = \frac{\$880}{1} = \$880$

**Question 2:**
An engineering consulting firm is considering two projects.
Project A: They have already spent \$50,000 on initial feasibility studies. The project is now facing challenges, and they can either continue or abandon it.
Project B: They are considering investing \$100,000 in new software that will improve their design capabilities. This software is highly specialized and has no resale value.

Identify any sunk costs in the scenarios above and explain why they should be ignored in future decision-making.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Project A Sunk Cost:** The \$50,000 spent on initial feasibility studies is a sunk cost. It has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of whether they continue or abandon Project A. This cost should be ignored when deciding whether to proceed with the project. The decision should be based on the *future* costs and benefits of continuing.
*   **Project B Sunk Cost:** The \$100,000 investment in specialized software is also a sunk cost once it is purchased, as it has no resale value. If the decision is about *whether to purchase* the software, it's a future cost. However, if the question implies the software has *already been purchased*, then it's a sunk cost. Assuming the decision is to *buy* it, the \$100,000 is an explicit cost to be considered. If the question implies it's already bought and the firm is deciding *how to use it*, then its unrecoverable nature makes it a sunk cost in that *specific future usage decision context*.

**Question 3 (Conceptual):**
Explain the difference between private cost and social cost using an example related to the construction of a new highway.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Private Cost:** The costs incurred by the construction company building the highway. This includes the cost of land acquisition, materials (asphalt, concrete), labor wages, equipment rental, and financing costs.
*   **Social Cost:** The total cost to society. This includes the private costs *plus* externalities. For a highway construction:
    *   **Negative Externalities:**
        *   Disruption to nearby residents and businesses during construction (noise, dust, traffic delays).
        *   Environmental impact (habitat destruction, increased emissions from construction vehicles, potential water pollution).
        *   Long-term impact on local communities due to traffic and pollution after the highway is built.
    *   **Positive Externalities:**
        *   Increased accessibility and reduced travel times for the public.
        *   Economic development in areas served by the highway.

The social cost is higher than the private cost due to the inclusion of these negative externalities.

## 2.4 Important Points to Remember

*   **Decision-Making Focus:** Engineering economic decisions should always be based on relevant future costs and benefits, not sunk costs.
*   **Opportunity Cost is Key:** Implicit costs (opportunity costs) are crucial for accurate economic analysis, even though they don't involve direct cash outlays. They represent the true economic cost of using resources.
*   **Revenue and Profit Link:** While revenue is the income, profit is what remains after all costs (explicit and implicit) are deducted from revenue. Understanding the different revenue components is vital for maximizing profit.
*   **Market Structure Matters:** The relationship between Price, AR, and MR is heavily influenced by the market structure in which a firm operates (perfect competition vs. imperfect competition).
*   **Social vs. Private:** For many engineering projects with public impact, considering social costs and benefits alongside private ones provides a more complete picture for decision-making.
