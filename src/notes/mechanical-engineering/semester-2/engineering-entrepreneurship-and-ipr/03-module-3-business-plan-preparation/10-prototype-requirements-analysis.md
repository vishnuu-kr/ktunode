# Engineering Entrepreneurship and IPR: Module 3 - Business Plan Preparation
## Topic: Prototype Requirements Analysis

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into a crucial phase of our business planning journey: **Prototype Requirements Analysis**. Think of your business plan as the blueprint for your entire venture, and the prototype is the very first tangible manifestation of your product or service. Getting this right is absolutely vital, and it directly impacts how well you'll convince investors, customers, and even your own team about the viability of your idea.

This topic is where we bridge the gap between a good idea and a concrete offering. It's about answering the fundamental question: "What *exactly* does my product need to do, and how will it do it, to solve a real problem for a real customer?" This ties directly into **Course Outcome 4 (CO4)** – creating a comprehensive business plan – because the prototype requirements form a core section of that plan, detailing the product's functionality and development. It also heavily supports **CO3**, learning essential tools for understanding product-market fit and customer needs, as our analysis of prototype requirements is driven by those very needs.

### Understanding the "Why" Behind the Prototype

Before we even talk about features, let's revisit the importance of "Why." As Simon Sinek powerfully articulates in *Start with Why*, people don't buy *what* you do; they buy *why* you do it. Your prototype needs to embody that "why." It needs to demonstrate that you understand the problem you're solving and that your proposed solution, even in its nascent form, offers a compelling answer.

So, what is a prototype in this context? It's not necessarily a fully polished, market-ready product. Instead, it's an **early-stage representation** of your idea. It could be a sketch, a wireframe, a 3D model, a functional piece of hardware, or even a simulated software experience. The goal is to **test and validate key assumptions** about your product and its ability to meet customer needs.

### Connecting Prototype Requirements to Business Plan Success (CO4)

In your business plan, a well-defined prototype requirements section shows investors you've thought critically about the product. It demonstrates:

*   **Technical Feasibility:** Can this actually be built?
*   **Market Viability:** Does it address a genuine customer need?
*   **Scalability:** Can it grow?
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** Is it economically viable to develop and produce?

Without clear prototype requirements, your business plan might feel vague and unconvincing. Investors want to see that you have a roadmap, and the prototype is the first, most important stop on that roadmap.

### Identifying Core Functional and Non-Functional Requirements

When we talk about "requirements analysis" for a prototype, we're essentially breaking down the desired product into its essential components. We categorize these into two main types:

#### 1. Functional Requirements: "What does it do?"

These are the specific actions or functions your product must perform. Think of them as the verbs of your product's capabilities.

*   **What are the essential tasks the user needs to accomplish?** For example, if you're building a smart irrigation system, a core functional requirement might be "The system shall automatically water plants based on pre-set schedules." Another could be "The user shall be able to manually override the watering schedule via a mobile app."
*   **How does it interact with the user and the environment?** This includes inputs, outputs, and data processing. For our irrigation system, this might involve sensing soil moisture levels as an input.
*   **What are the critical workflows?** Trace the user's journey. For an e-commerce platform, a critical workflow would be "A user shall be able to search for a product, add it to the cart, and complete the checkout process."

**Real-world Analogy:** Imagine you're planning a trip. The functional requirements are "book flights," "reserve a hotel," and "pack essentials." These are the *actions* you must take to achieve your goal.

**Exam Tip:** When outlining functional requirements for your business plan, be specific and avoid ambiguity. Use clear action verbs. Think about the user's perspective. This directly supports **CO3** by forcing you to articulate exactly how your product will solve their problem.

#### 2. Non-Functional Requirements: "How well does it do it?"

These requirements don't describe *what* the product does, but rather *how* it performs its functions. They define qualities and constraints. These are often the "hidden" requirements that can make or break a product's success.

*   **Performance:** How fast, efficient, or responsive must it be? For an app, this could be "The app shall load within 3 seconds on a standard 4G connection."
*   **Usability:** How easy is it for the target audience to learn and use? This relates to user interface (UI) and user experience (UX). A requirement might be: "The onboarding process for new users shall be completable within 5 minutes without external assistance." This is directly linked to **CO3** – understanding customer needs and product-market fit. If your product is too hard to use, it won't fit the market, no matter how innovative it is.
*   **Reliability:** How often does it fail? What's the acceptable downtime? For a critical system, this might be "The system shall have 99.9% uptime."
*   **Security:** How is data protected? Are there authentication mechanisms? "User passwords shall be stored using industry-standard encryption."
*   **Maintainability:** How easy is it to update, fix, or enhance?
*   **Scalability:** Can the system handle an increasing load of users or data? This is crucial for long-term growth.
*   **Compatibility:** With which operating systems, devices, or other software must it work?

**Real-world Analogy:** Continuing the trip analogy, non-functional requirements might be: "The flight should be non-stop" (performance/convenience), "The hotel should have good reviews" (usability/quality), "I need to arrive before dawn" (time constraint/performance), and "I need my passport to be valid for at least six months" (security/regulatory).

**Textbook Insight:** *Business Model Generation* by Osterwalder and Pigneur emphasizes understanding customer segments and their needs. Your non-functional requirements must align with what your target customer *values*. For some, speed might be paramount; for others, simplicity and ease of use will trump raw performance.

**Exam Focus:** When detailing non-functional requirements in your business plan, think about how these will impact customer satisfaction and the overall cost of development and maintenance. Often, there's a trade-off between different non-functional requirements (e.g., high performance can increase development cost).

### The Prototype Requirements Analysis Process: A Guided Approach

Now, how do we actually *determine* these requirements? It's not a guessing game. It's a systematic process that leverages market research and customer feedback. This is where **Course Outcome 3 (CO3)** really comes into play, as we use tools to understand the market.

#### 1. Define the Problem Statement Clearly

This is foundational. What is the pain point you are addressing? Your prototype requirements should directly serve to validate that your solution effectively alleviates this pain.

*   **Ask:** What problem am I solving? For whom? What are the consequences of this problem not being solved?
*   **Example:** Instead of "We're building a better toaster," it should be "Busy professionals struggle to prepare healthy breakfast options due to lack of time, leading to poor dietary choices."

#### 2. Identify Your Target Users and Their Needs (CO3)

Who are you building this for? You need to know them intimately.

*   **User Personas:** Create semi-fictional representations of your ideal customers. Give them names, backgrounds, motivations, and pain points.
*   **User Stories:** Frame requirements from the user's perspective. A common format is: "As a [type of user], I want to [perform some task] so that [I can achieve some goal]."
    *   *Example (Smart Irrigation):* "As a busy homeowner, I want to set watering schedules remotely via my smartphone so that my garden stays healthy even when I'm away." This directly feeds into functional requirements.
*   **Customer Interviews & Surveys:** Talk to potential users! Ask open-ended questions about their current challenges and what they wish existed. This is invaluable for uncovering both functional and non-functional needs you might not have considered. Think of this as early-stage validation, as discussed in **CO3**.

#### 3. Brainstorm Potential Features and Functionality

Based on your understanding of the problem and user needs, start listing all possible features. Don't filter too much at this stage.

*   **Think Broadly:** What *could* your product do?

#### 4. Prioritize Features for the Prototype (MVP - Minimum Viable Product)

This is a crucial step, especially for a business plan and prototype. You can't build everything at once. You need to define the **Minimum Viable Product (MVP)**.

*   **What is an MVP?** It's the version of your product with just enough features to satisfy early customers and provide feedback for future development. It's about learning, not perfection.
*   **Prioritization Techniques:**
    *   **Must-Have:** Essential features that solve the core problem. Without these, the product is useless.
    *   **Should-Have:** Important features that add significant value but aren't strictly essential for initial launch.
    *   **Could-Have:** Nice-to-have features that enhance the user experience or competitiveness but can be deferred.
    *   **Won't-Have (for now):** Features that are out of scope for the initial prototype or MVP.
*   **The "So What?" Test:** For each feature, ask "So what?" What value does this add to the user? If the answer isn't compelling, reconsider its inclusion in the prototype.
*   **The Innovator's DNA Connection:** Clayton Christensen and Jeffrey Dyer in *The Innovator's DNA* highlight the importance of "associating" ideas and looking for opportunities. Prioritizing features for a prototype involves associating user needs with feasible technological solutions, and deciding which associations are most critical to validate first.

**Real-world Example:** Imagine developing a new social media app.
*   *Must-Have:* Ability to create a profile, post text/images, follow other users.
*   *Should-Have:* Direct messaging, video sharing.
*   *Could-Have:* Live streaming, augmented reality filters.
For the *initial prototype*, you'd focus on the "Must-Haves" to test the core concept of user interaction and content sharing.

**Exam Question Type:** "Explain the concept of an MVP and its importance in prototype development for a new venture, linking it to market validation."

#### 5. Define Technical and Design Constraints

Every project operates within constraints. Understanding these early is vital for realistic planning.

*   **Budget:** How much can you spend on developing the prototype?
*   **Timeline:** When do you need a working prototype?
*   **Technology Stack:** What programming languages, frameworks, or hardware will you use? Consider existing knowledge within your team or the feasibility of learning new technologies.
*   **Resources:** What expertise, equipment, and personnel are available?

#### 6. Document the Requirements

Once you've analyzed and prioritized, it's time to write it down. This becomes a critical part of your business plan (CO4).

*   **Clarity and Conciseness:** Use precise language.
*   **Testability:** Each requirement should be verifiable. How will you know if it's met?
*   **Completeness:** Cover all essential aspects.
*   **Consistency:** Requirements should not contradict each other.

### The Prototype Requirements Document: Your Business Plan's Foundation

The output of this analysis should be a clear, documented set of requirements. This document will form a significant portion of your product development section in the business plan. It might include:

*   **Introduction:** Briefly state the product's purpose and the problem it solves.
*   **Target Audience:** Describe your primary user personas.
*   **Functional Requirements:** A detailed list of what the prototype *must* do, often presented as user stories or specific feature descriptions.
*   **Non-Functional Requirements:** The quality attributes your prototype needs to possess.
*   **Constraints:** Budget, timeline, technology limitations.
*   **Success Metrics:** How will you measure if the prototype is successful in validating your assumptions? (e.g., user engagement rates, task completion success, positive feedback on usability).

**Why is this so important for your business plan?** It shows investors you're not just dreaming; you've done the homework. It gives them confidence that you have a tangible plan for bringing your idea to life. It also helps in estimating development costs and timelines, which are critical components of financial projections in your business plan. This directly addresses **CO4**.

### Prototyping as a Tool for Learning and Validation (CO5)

Remember, the purpose of this prototype isn't necessarily to sell. It's to **learn and validate**. As Saibal Gupta and Ashok Jhunjhunwala suggest in *The Engineering Entrepreneur*, rapid prototyping and iteration are key to success in the Indian context, where market dynamics can change quickly. You're testing hypotheses about your product and market.

*   **Validating assumptions about user behavior:** Do users interact with the interface as you expected?
*   **Testing the core value proposition:** Does the prototype truly solve the problem in a way users find valuable?
*   **Gathering feedback for iteration:** What do users like? What confuses them? What's missing?

This iterative process, fueled by prototype testing and feedback, directly supports **Course Outcome 5 (CO5)**, as it's a fundamental aspect of stakeholder engagement (getting feedback from users) and team collaboration (working together to build and refine the prototype).

### Intellectual Property Considerations (IPR) During Prototyping

While focusing on requirements, don't forget your Intellectual Property Rights (IPR). As highlighted by Prabuddha Ganguli in *Intellectual Property Rights*, early consideration of IPR is crucial for startups.

*   **Novelty and Inventive Step:** Are the features or the underlying technology novel? This is key for patentability (as discussed in P. Narayanan's *Indian Patent Law*).
*   **Trade Secrets:** Some aspects of your unique solution might be best kept as trade secrets rather than disclosed in a patent.
*   **User Interface Design:** Unique and aesthetically pleasing interfaces can be protected by design patents or copyright (as per B.L. Wadehra's *The Law of Copyright and Designs*).
*   **Confidentiality Agreements (NDAs):** When sharing your prototype or its details with external parties (e.g., potential investors, beta testers), ensure they sign Non-Disclosure Agreements to protect your confidential information.

**Exam Connection:** You might be asked to discuss how IPR considerations influence the design and documentation of prototype requirements. For instance, deciding whether to patent a novel algorithm might influence how much detail you reveal about its workings in your public business plan or even in the prototype itself.

### Conclusion: The Bridge to Market

In essence, **Prototype Requirements Analysis** is the bridge between a raw idea and a tangible, testable product. It's a critical exercise that informs your business plan, guides your development efforts, and helps you validate your assumptions about the market and your customers. By meticulously defining what your prototype needs to do and how well it needs to do it, you lay a robust foundation for your entrepreneurial journey. It's about translating user needs and business goals into concrete technical specifications, ensuring that your innovation has a clear path to market and, crucially, that it resonates with the people you aim to serve. Remember this: a well-defined prototype requirement set is a powerful tool for de-risking your venture and building investor confidence.

## Sample Questions and Answers

**Q1. What is the primary purpose of conducting a prototype requirements analysis as part of business plan preparation?**

**Answer:** The primary purpose is to translate the business idea and identified customer needs into a concrete, testable specification for an early-stage product or service. This analysis helps validate technical feasibility, market desirability, and provides essential details for the product development section of the business plan, thereby increasing investor confidence and guiding development efforts. It directly supports CO4 (Create a comprehensive business plan) and CO3 (Understand product-market fit and customer needs).

**Q2. Differentiate between functional and non-functional requirements with a relevant engineering example.**

**Answer:**
*   **Functional Requirements** describe *what* the system does.
    *   *Example (for a drone delivery system):* "The drone shall be able to carry a payload of up to 2 kg." or "The user shall be able to select a delivery location via a map interface."
*   **Non-Functional Requirements** describe *how well* the system performs its functions or the qualities it possesses.
    *   *Example (for the same drone delivery system):* "The drone shall have a flight endurance of at least 30 minutes on a single charge." (Performance) or "The flight control software shall be robust enough to recover from a minor sensor malfunction." (Reliability).

**Q3. Explain the concept of an MVP (Minimum Viable Product) and its role in prototype requirements analysis.**

**Answer:** An MVP is the simplest version of a product that can be released to early customers to gather feedback and validate core hypotheses. In prototype requirements analysis, defining the MVP is crucial for **prioritization**. Instead of listing every conceivable feature, you focus on the **"must-have"** features that are essential to demonstrate the core value proposition and test key assumptions about user needs and market fit. This pragmatic approach, as advocated by Christensen in *The Innovator's DNA*, allows for learning with minimal investment, reducing risk and informing future development cycles, thereby aligning with CO3 and CO5.

**Q4. How can intellectual property (IP) considerations influence the definition of prototype requirements? Provide an example.**

**Answer:** IP considerations can influence prototype requirements by dictating what aspects of the technology should be highlighted, tested, or kept confidential. For instance, if a novel algorithm is the core innovation intended for patenting (as discussed in *Indian Patent Law*), the prototype requirements might focus on demonstrating its unique output or efficiency, perhaps using a simplified or abstracted implementation, rather than revealing the exact underlying code. Conversely, if a unique user interface is the key differentiator, then usability and aesthetic requirements will be paramount. The decision to patent, copyright, or protect as a trade secret (as per Ganguli) will shape how detailed and specific certain functional or non-functional requirements are documented, especially in external-facing business plans. For example, a company might specify "efficient data processing" (general) in its business plan if the exact method is a trade secret, but specify "user-friendly drag-and-drop interface" (specific) if that design is to be protected via design rights. This directly connects to CO1 and supports the rationale behind specific requirements.

**Q5. Imagine you are developing a smart home thermostat. Outline two functional and two non-functional requirements for its initial prototype that would be included in a business plan.**

**Answer:**

*   **Functional Requirements:**
    1.  **Remote Temperature Adjustment:** "The user shall be able to adjust the set temperature of the thermostat remotely via a dedicated mobile application." (This tests the core connectivity and control feature.)
    2.  **Learning User Schedule:** "The thermostat shall record and learn the user's daily temperature adjustments over a one-week period to automatically create a basic heating/cooling schedule." (This tests the "smart" aspect of the device.)

*   **Non-Functional Requirements:**
    1.  **Usability (Onboarding):** "New users shall be able to connect the thermostat to their Wi-Fi network and complete the initial setup within 10 minutes using the mobile app." (Ensures ease of adoption, critical for market acceptance - CO3).
    2.  **Reliability (Temperature Sensing):** "The thermostat's internal temperature sensor shall maintain an accuracy of +/- 0.5 degrees Celsius compared to a calibrated reference thermometer under normal operating conditions." (Ensures the core function of temperature control is dependable).
