## Module 9: Draw Plan, Section and Elevation of an Industrial Building

### Topic: Drawing Plan, Section, and Elevation of an Industrial Building

Welcome, everyone, to another exciting session in our Civil Engineering Drafting Lab! Today, we’re going to tackle a crucial skill: **drawing the plan, section, and elevation of an industrial building.** Now, you might think, "Why industrial buildings? Aren't residential buildings enough?" Well, industrial buildings are the backbone of our economy, housing everything from manufacturing plants to warehouses. Understanding their spatial layout, structural elements, and functional aspects through drawings is vital for any civil engineer.

This module directly contributes to our course objectives:

*   **CO1: Illustrate ability to organize civil engineering drawings systematically and professionally.** We'll be focusing on the proper presentation and organization of these complex drawings.
*   **CO2: Illustrate the detailing of building components like doors, windows, roof trusses etc.** Industrial buildings have specific structural elements, and we’ll be looking at how to represent them accurately.
*   **CO3: Develop the sketch of plan, front elevation and sectional elevation from line diagram.** This is about interpreting basic layouts and transforming them into detailed views.
*   **CO4: Draft the plan elevation and sectional views of the residential buildings, industrial buildings, and framed structures using software.** While this module focuses on manual drafting principles that can be applied to software later, understanding the fundamentals is key to mastering any drafting tool.

Remember, a good drawing isn't just about lines and symbols; it's a *language* that communicates your design intent to everyone involved – from clients and contractors to other engineers. And for industrial buildings, precision and clarity are paramount.

### Understanding the Fundamentals: What are Plan, Section, and Elevation?

Before we dive into the specifics of industrial buildings, let’s quickly recap what these views represent.

*   **Plan:** Imagine slicing through a building horizontally at about sill-wall height (typically around 1 to 1.5 meters above the finished floor level) and looking down from above. That’s what a plan view shows you. It’s essentially a bird’s-eye view of each floor, revealing the layout of walls, rooms, doors, windows, stairs, and other features at that specific level. For an industrial building, the plan will show you the arrangement of factory floors, storage areas, offices, and any specialized zones. Think of it like looking at a map of the building's interior.

*   **Elevation:** Elevations are external views of the building, as if you were standing outside and looking directly at one of its faces. We typically have front elevation, rear elevation, and side elevations. These drawings show the external appearance of the building, including the height of walls, the placement and design of windows and doors, rooflines, and any decorative elements. For an industrial building, elevations will showcase the facade, loading docks, ventilation systems, and overall scale.

*   **Section:** Sections are like cutting the building vertically. Imagine slicing through the building with an imaginary plane and looking at the cut surface. This reveals the internal structure, heights of different components, and the relationship between floors, roofs, and foundations. Sectional views are critical for understanding how the building is put together, the thickness of walls, the type of floors, and the structure of the roof. In an industrial building, a section might show the height of the factory floor, the supporting columns, the roof trusses, and any machinery supports. It answers the question: "What does the inside structure *look* like?"

### Key Elements of an Industrial Building Drawing

Industrial buildings have their own set of unique features that need to be accurately represented. Let’s look at some of these:

#### 1. Layout and Zoning

Unlike residential buildings, industrial buildings are often designed for specific functions. The plan will reflect this.

*   **Factory Floor/Production Area:** This is the main operational space. The plan will show its dimensions, column grid, and access points for machinery and materials.
*   **Storage Areas/Warehouses:** These need to accommodate large volumes and often have specific racking systems or loading bay access. The plan will show clear pathways and storage zones.
*   **Office Spaces:** These are typically segregated from the production areas for administrative functions.
*   **Ancillary Spaces:** This can include restrooms, break rooms, control rooms, maintenance areas, loading docks, and truck bays.

Think of a biscuit factory. The plan will show the large open space for machinery, separate areas for raw material storage, packaging, and a loading dock to ship out the finished products. The office block will be clearly marked.

#### 2. Structural System

Industrial buildings often employ robust structural systems to support heavy loads and large spans.

*   **Columns and Beams:** The plan will show the grid of columns supporting the roof. The beams, connecting these columns, are crucial for load transfer. Elevations and sections will reveal their sizes and how they are connected.
*   **Roof Trusses:** For large, clear spans, roof trusses are common. The plan might indicate the spacing of these trusses, while sections will detail their geometry (how the triangles are arranged) and the type of purlins and sheeting used. Shah, Kale, and Patki's "Building Drawing With An Integrated Approach to Built Environment" often emphasizes how to represent these structural elements clearly, showing the connections at the joints.
*   **Foundations:** While not always shown in detail on the main plan, sections will often indicate the type of foundation (e.g., isolated footings, raft foundation) supporting the columns.

#### 3. Openings and Access

*   **Doors:** These will be shown on the plan with their swing direction. Elevations will show their design and height. In industrial buildings, you'll find large roller shutter doors for vehicles, personnel doors, and emergency exits.
*   **Windows:** Placed at appropriate heights for natural light and ventilation, especially in office areas.
*   **Loading Docks:** A key feature for logistics, often shown prominently on elevations and with access details on the plan.

#### 4. Services and Utilities

*   **Ventilation:** Industrial processes often require specialized ventilation systems, like fume extraction. Sections and elevations might show ducts, vents, or exhaust stacks.
*   **Lighting:** Natural and artificial lighting are crucial. Plans can indicate light fixtures.
*   **Drainage:** Industrial floors often require specific drainage systems.

### Drawing the Plan of an Industrial Building

When drawing the plan, remember our learning outcomes:

*   **Systematic Organization (CO1):** Start with the building outline, then position major internal walls and columns. Use clear lines for different elements.
*   **Detailing Components (CO2):** Show doors with their swing, windows with their frames, and any special openings like loading docks.
*   **Interpreting Layouts (CO3):** If you're given a line diagram or a basic layout, translate it accurately into a properly dimensioned plan.

**Steps:**

1.  **Establish the Grid:** Mark out the column grid as per the structural design. This forms the primary framework.
2.  **Outline External Walls:** Draw the outer boundary of the building.
3.  **Position Internal Walls:** Delineate walls for offices, restrooms, and other segregated areas.
4.  **Locate Openings:** Place doors and windows, indicating their types and swing direction on the plan. Mark any loading bay openings.
5.  **Add Labels and Dimensions:** Clearly label rooms (e.g., "Factory Floor," "Store Room," "Office") and provide essential dimensions for walls, bays, and openings.
6.  **Indicate Key Features:** Show any internal columns, drains, or service entry points.

**Example:** Imagine drawing the plan for a small workshop. You’d have a main open workshop area, a small office, and a storage room. The plan would show the column grid, the workshop floor space, the walls dividing the office and store, and a personnel door into the office and a large roller shutter door into the workshop.

### Drawing the Sectional Elevation of an Industrial Building

Sections are where we truly see the building’s internal structure and vertical relationships.

*   **Understanding Vertical Relationships (CO3):** A section clarifies how different levels connect and the vertical extent of components.
*   **Detailing Structural Elements (CO2):** This is where we show the true height of walls, the depth of foundations, the configuration of roof trusses, and the floor slab construction.
*   **Software Application (CO4):** While we’re focusing on the concepts, visualize how these sectional details would be constructed using CAD software.

**Steps:**

1.  **Choose the Cutting Plane:** Decide where to cut the building vertically. Typically, you cut through major structural elements like columns and load-bearing walls, and through openings like doors or windows.
2.  **Project Visible Elements:** All visible elements above and below the cutting plane are projected onto the cutting plane.
3.  **Show the Cut:** The cutting plane itself is shown with thicker lines. What is cut through is typically shown with hatching or section lining to indicate the material.
4.  **Represent Structural Members:** Draw the columns, beams, floor slabs, and foundation elements with their correct proportions.
5.  **Detail the Roof:** Show the roof structure, including trusses, purlins, and roof sheeting. Dr. Balagopal T.S. Prabhu’s "Building Drawing and Detailing" often provides excellent examples of how to detail roof structures, including the connections between members, which is vital for industrial buildings.
6.  **Add Levels and Dimensions:** Indicate finished floor levels (FFL), finished roof levels (FRL), and critical heights.
7.  **Label Components:** Label columns, beams, roof trusses, floor slabs, etc.

**Example:** A section through the factory floor might show a concrete slab on ground, supporting steel columns. The columns would be connected by steel beams at the roof level, onto which steel purlins are fixed. The roof sheeting would then be placed on the purlins. The section would clearly illustrate the height of the building, the type of roof structure, and how the loads are transferred down to the foundation.

### Drawing the Elevation of an Industrial Building

Elevations provide the external aesthetic and functional appearance.

*   **External Appearance (CO3):** They show what the building looks like from the outside.
*   **Incorporating Features (CO2):** Elevational details of doors, windows, loading docks, and external utilities are critical.
*   **Professional Presentation (CO1):** Elevations need to be neat, with consistent line weights and accurate representation of materials and features.

**Steps:**

1.  **Project from Plan:** Start by projecting the key external features of the building from the plan view (e.g., wall lines, window and door locations).
2.  **Establish Ground Line:** Draw a ground line at the base of the elevation.
3.  **Draw External Walls:** Represent the height of the external walls.
4.  **Show Openings:** Draw the doors and windows with their frames and glazing, according to their design and position. Loading docks are particularly important here.
5.  **Depict the Roofline:** Show the roof parapet, or the profile of the roof if it's visible from this angle.
6.  **Add Details:** Include any external architectural features, pipework, ventilation units, or other visible services.
7.  **Label and Shade (Optional):** Label the elevation (e.g., "Front Elevation"). Sometimes, simple shading can enhance clarity, showing depth or different materials.

**Example:** The front elevation of a warehouse might show a large roller shutter door for truck access, a few personnel doors, and high windows spaced along the wall for natural light. The building might have a simple, functional design, with the elevation reflecting this utilitarian purpose.

### Adhering to Codes and Standards

As civil engineers, we must always consider relevant codes and standards.

*   **National Building Code of India (NBC):** This is our primary reference for general building requirements, safety norms, and dimensional standards. While it covers residential extensively, it also provides guidelines applicable to industrial buildings regarding fire safety, ventilation, and accessibility.
*   **Kerala Panchayat Building Rules / Kerala Municipality Building Rules:** These local regulations provide specific requirements for construction within the respective jurisdictions, including setbacks, height restrictions, and material usage. Always refer to the latest updates!
*   **IS 962:1989 (Reaffirmed 2022): Indian Standard Code of Practice for Architectural and Building Drawings:** This standard is crucial for understanding the conventions used in architectural drawings, including line types, symbols, and dimensioning practices. It ensures that our drawings are universally understood.

These codes help ensure that our drawings are not only aesthetically pleasing but also structurally sound, safe, and compliant with legal requirements.

### Connecting Concepts to Exam Preparation

When you’re preparing for exams, remember to:

*   **Understand the Relationship:** Be able to explain how the plan, section, and elevation views relate to each other. For instance, a column shown on the plan must appear at the correct vertical position in the section and elevation.
*   **Dimensioning:** Accurate and clear dimensioning is always tested. Ensure you dimension critical elements like wall thicknesses, door/window sizes, and overall building dimensions.
*   **Line Conventions:** Use appropriate line weights and types as per IS 962. Visible lines are thick, hidden lines are dashed, center lines are chain-dotted, and section lines are usually thicker than visible lines.
*   **Hatching:** Correctly hatching sections to indicate different materials is a common requirement.
*   **Labeling:** Clear and concise labeling of rooms, structural elements, and levels is essential for understanding.

### Conclusion

Drawing the plan, section, and elevation of an industrial building is a comprehensive exercise that integrates your understanding of building layout, structural principles, and drafting conventions. By meticulously representing these views, you ensure that the complex functional and structural requirements of an industrial facility are communicated clearly and effectively. Always remember to refer to your textbooks like Prabhu, Shah et al., and Chitawadagi & Bhavikatti for detailed examples and techniques, and keep the relevant codes in mind. Practice is key, so let’s get drafting!

## Sample Questions with Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):** Explain the primary purpose of a sectional view in the context of an industrial building drawing.

**Answer:** The primary purpose of a sectional view in an industrial building drawing is to reveal the internal construction, structural composition, and vertical arrangement of building elements. It shows how the building is put together, including the heights of walls, floor slabs, roof structures (like trusses and purlins), and the connection of structural members. This is crucial for understanding load-bearing paths and construction methods, which is vital for safety and efficiency in industrial settings. Unlike the plan (horizontal layout) or elevation (external appearance), the section provides insight into the building's "insides" and structural integrity.

**Question 2 (Exam-Oriented):** A simple line diagram for an industrial shed shows the outer dimensions of 30m x 20m, with internal columns spaced at 6m x 5m. A roller shutter door is located on one 30m side, and a personnel door and two windows are on one 20m side. Draw the plan of this industrial shed. Assume a wall thickness of 230mm and indicate the column positions.

**Answer:**

*   **Drawing Steps:**
    1.  Draw the outer rectangle of 30m x 20m, representing the building's external walls.
    2.  Establish the column grid:
        *   Along the 30m side, mark columns at 0m, 6m, 12m, 18m, 24m, and 30m.
        *   Along the 20m side, mark columns at 0m, 5m, 10m, 15m, and 20m.
        *   Draw lines representing the columns at these intersection points. Typical column representation might be a square or circle showing its dimensions.
    3.  Draw the 230mm thick external walls connecting the outermost columns.
    4.  On one 30m side, indicate a large opening for the roller shutter door, showing its position and width (e.g., a 4m wide opening).
    5.  On one 20m side, indicate a personnel door opening (e.g., 1m wide) and two window openings at appropriate heights, showing their locations along the wall.
    6.  Label the dimensions (30m and 20m) and the column grid spacing (6m and 5m). Label the areas like "Factory Floor" and mention the types of openings (e.g., "Roller Shutter Door," "Personnel Door").

*   **Reasoning:** The plan needs to accurately reflect the given dimensions and the placement of structural elements (columns) and functional openings (doors, windows). The wall thickness must be accounted for, and the grid system provides the backbone for locating columns. The section or elevation would detail the *height* of these elements, but the plan focuses on the horizontal layout and distribution.

**Question 3 (Conceptual & Application):** How do the National Building Code of India (NBC) and IS 962:1989 influence the way we draw an industrial building’s plan?

**Answer:**
The NBC and IS 962:1989 are fundamental for creating accurate and compliant industrial building drawings.

*   **NBC Influence:** The NBC provides guidelines on essential aspects like minimum ventilation requirements (especially for factory floors and office spaces), fire safety measures (location of exits, fire-resistant construction details that might be indicated in sections), and general safety standards. While it might not dictate the exact drawing style, it informs *what* needs to be shown and considered in the layout (e.g., ensuring adequate space for emergency exits or specific operational clearances). It influences the functional planning that is represented on the drawing.

*   **IS 962:1989 Influence:** This standard directly dictates the *how* of drawing. It specifies:
    *   **Line Types and Weights:** For instance, it defines that visible lines are thick, hidden lines are dashed, and center lines are chain-dotted. This ensures clarity and differentiates elements.
    *   **Symbols:** Standardized symbols for doors, windows, stairs, and other building components are provided. Using these ensures that the drawing is universally understood by other professionals.
    *   **Dimensioning Practices:** It outlines how dimensions should be placed and represented on the drawing for clarity and ease of interpretation.
    *   **Section Lining (Hatching):** It provides conventions for hatching to represent different materials (e.g., concrete, steel, brickwork) when a section is drawn.

In essence, the NBC guides the design intent and functional requirements shown on the plan, while IS 962 provides the standardized visual language and conventions for accurately communicating that intent through the drawing itself. Not following these can lead to misinterpretations and non-compliance.
