# PRINCIPLES OF SAFETY MANAGEMENT

## Module 4: Code of Practice on Safety Audit

### Topic: Analytical Techniques for Safety Management

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into Module 4, which focuses on the **Code of Practice on Safety Audit**. While audits might sound like just checking boxes, they are a crucial part of ensuring a safe workplace. And to conduct effective audits, we need powerful analytical tools. This module introduces us to some of these, helping us move beyond simply identifying hazards to deeply understanding *how* and *why* things can go wrong, and how to prevent them systematically.

Our journey today will cover several key analytical techniques: **System Safety, Change Analysis, MORT (Management Oversight and Risk Tree), Multi-Events Sequencing, and TOR (Task Observation Record)**. These aren't just abstract concepts; they are practical tools that help us diagnose safety issues, predict potential failures, and build robust safety management systems. As we go through these, remember how they connect back to our Course Outcomes, particularly CO3 (estimating safety performance, producing permits) and CO4 (analyzing accidents, job safety, and compliance). These techniques are the bedrock for truly understanding and improving safety.

---

### Understanding the Need for Analytical Techniques

Before we jump into specific techniques, let's establish *why* we need them. Accidents, as Heinrich H.W. famously pointed out in his work "Industrial Accident Prevention," are rarely caused by a single factor. They are usually the result of a chain of events and conditions. Simply reacting to accidents isn't enough. We need to be proactive, to anticipate potential failures, and to understand the complex interactions within our systems.

Think about it: if a machine malfunctions, is it just the machine's fault? Or is it the maintenance schedule? The operator's training? The design of the machine? The work environment? These analytical techniques help us unpack these layers. They allow us to move from a superficial understanding to a deeper, systemic view of safety. This is essential for building effective safety management systems, as emphasized in works like Willie Hammer's "Occupational Safety Management and Engineering."

---

### 1. System Safety Analysis

Let's start with **System Safety**. What do we mean by a "system"? In safety, a system is anything that can cause harm. It's not just a single piece of equipment, but the entire network of people, processes, equipment, environment, and interactions that make up a work activity. System Safety is a disciplined approach to identifying, evaluating, and controlling hazards throughout the lifecycle of a system – from design to operation and even disposal.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Hazard:** A condition with the potential to cause harm.
*   **Risk:** The probability of a hazard occurring and the severity of the harm it can cause.
*   **System Life Cycle:** The entire journey of a system, from its inception (design, development) through operation, maintenance, modification, and eventual retirement.

**How it Works (and why it's important):**

System Safety aims to design safety *into* the system from the very beginning. It's like building a house with strong foundations and fire-resistant materials from the start, rather than trying to fix problems after the house is built. This proactive approach, often discussed in texts like "Loss Prevention in Process Industries" by Lees F.P., emphasizes integrating safety considerations at every stage.

Imagine a new chemical plant. System Safety would involve:
*   **Design Phase:** Analyzing the inherent hazards of the chemicals used, the potential for leaks, the safety features of the equipment, and the ergonomics of the control room.
*   **Construction Phase:** Ensuring safe construction practices, proper material handling, and quality control of safety-critical components.
*   **Operation Phase:** Developing safe operating procedures, training operators, establishing maintenance schedules, and planning for emergencies.
*   **Modification Phase:** Carefully analyzing the safety implications of any changes made to the plant.

**Connection to Course Outcomes:** System Safety directly supports CO4 by providing a framework for analyzing hazards and potential accidents within complex work environments. Understanding the system helps us predict where things might go wrong, which is crucial for both job safety analysis and accident analysis.

### 2. Change Analysis (also known as Management of Change - MOC)

Now, let's talk about **Change Analysis**, or more commonly in industry, **Management of Change (MOC)**. Nothing in a workplace stays static. We modify processes, introduce new equipment, change suppliers, or alter procedures. Any change, no matter how small it seems, can introduce new hazards or alter existing risk profiles. Change Analysis is a systematic process to ensure that these changes are implemented safely.

**The Core Idea:**

Think of a simple example: You decide to switch from an old, manual coffee grinder to a new, electric one. The *function* (grinding coffee beans) is the same, but the *method* has changed. The new grinder has moving parts, electricity, and potentially different safety guards (or lack thereof). If you just swap it out without thinking, you might not realize the new hazards associated with it, like the risk of getting your fingers caught in the blades or an electrical shock.

MOC is about systematically asking:
*   What is changing?
*   Why is it changing?
*   What are the potential hazards introduced by this change?
*   What are the risks associated with these new hazards?
*   What controls are needed to manage these risks?
*   Who needs to be informed or trained about this change?

**Reference:** This concept is fundamental to preventing accidents, as discussed in N.V. Krishnan's "Safety Management in Industry." Krishnan likely highlights how seemingly minor modifications can have significant safety implications if not properly managed.

**Practical Application:**

Let's say a factory decides to change the type of lubricant used for its conveyor belts.
*   **The Change:** New lubricant type.
*   **Potential Hazards:** Is the new lubricant flammable? Is it toxic if inhaled or comes into contact with skin? Does it degrade existing seals or materials? Does it affect the friction coefficient of the belt, leading to slippage?
*   **Risk Assessment:** If flammable, what's the risk of ignition from static discharge? If toxic, what PPE is needed?
*   **Controls:** Update MSDS (Material Safety Data Sheets), revise cleaning procedures, train maintenance staff on safe handling and disposal, issue updated PPE requirements.

**Connection to Course Outcomes:** MOC directly links to CO3 by requiring the production of updated work permits and procedures. It also supports CO4 by providing a structured way to analyze potential accidents that might arise from deviations in standard operations. It's a core component of compliance checks.

### 3. MORT (Management Oversight and Risk Tree)

Now, let's tackle **MORT – Management Oversight and Risk Tree**. This is a more sophisticated analytical tool, often used for in-depth investigations and for developing comprehensive safety management systems. MORT is essentially a structured troubleshooting tool that helps identify deficiencies in a management system by breaking down a large problem (like an accident) into smaller, manageable components.

**The Analogy:** Imagine you're trying to figure out why your car isn't starting. You could just try random things. Or, you could use a systematic approach: Check the battery, check the fuel, check the ignition system, check the starter motor. MORT is like that, but for safety management.

**How it's Structured:**

MORT uses a "tree" structure, starting with the undesirable event (the accident or incident) at the root. It then branches out to explore various "branches" of management system failure that could have contributed to the event. These branches often cover areas like:
*   **System Failure:** Was the safety policy clear? Were procedures adequate?
*   **Implementation Failure:** Were policies and procedures actually followed? Was training effective?
*   **Hardware Failure:** Did equipment malfunction?
*   **Human Factors:** Were there errors in judgment, fatigue, or lack of skill?

**Key Features:**

*   **Comprehensive:** It aims to cover all potential areas of management system failure.
*   **Systematic:** It provides a structured way to think about causes.
*   **Diagnostic:** It helps identify the *root causes* within the management system, not just the immediate cause.

**Origin and Use:** MORT was developed by Dr. William W. Johnson and colleagues at Sandia Laboratories for the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission. It's a powerful tool for analyzing the effectiveness of an entire safety program. While perhaps more detailed than what's typically required for a basic safety audit checklist, understanding its principles helps us appreciate the depth of analysis possible. Ronald Blake's "Industrial Safety" might offer perspectives on the hierarchical and systematic nature of safety management that aligns with MORT's philosophy.

**Connection to Course Outcomes:** MORT is a prime example of how we analyze accidents (CO4). It provides a framework for deep-dive analysis to understand how management decisions and oversights (or lack thereof) contributed to an incident. It helps answer the "why" behind the "what."

### 4. Multi-Events Sequencing

Next up is **Multi-Events Sequencing**. This technique focuses on the chronological order of events that lead to an accident. It's about understanding the "story" of how an incident unfolded, identifying the critical points where intervention could have prevented it. This is closely related to accident causation models, which we touch upon in CO1 and CO4.

**The Concept:**

Think of a domino effect. If you want to stop the last domino from falling, you need to know which domino knocked it over, and which one knocked *that* one over, and so on. Multi-Events Sequencing maps out this sequence.

**How it's Done:**

It involves collecting all available information about the incident and then arranging the contributing factors and actions in chronological order. This might include:
*   The unsafe act or condition.
*   The precursor events.
*   The immediate cause.
*   The supervisory failures.
*   The management system deficiencies.

**Example:**

Consider a minor fire in a workshop:
1.  **Event:** A welding operation was ongoing.
2.  **Precursor Event:** Nearby combustible material (rags soaked in oil) was not moved away.
3.  **Contributing Factor:** The welding procedure did not explicitly require the removal of all nearby combustibles, or this step was overlooked during the permit-to-work process.
4.  **Immediate Cause:** A spark from the welding ignited the oily rags.
5.  **Consequence:** A small fire started.

By sequencing these events, we can see that if the rags had been moved (step 2 or 3), the ignition (step 4) might not have occurred, or if a fire extinguisher was immediately accessible and known to operators (a management system control, not an event in itself, but a contributing factor to limiting consequences), the damage would be minimized.

**Textbook Connection:** Ferry's "Modern Accident Investigation and Analysis" is a great resource for understanding such sequential event analysis and how to reconstruct the timeline of an incident.

**Connection to Course Outcomes:** Multi-Events Sequencing is a direct application for CO4 – analyzing accidents. It helps us understand the causal chain, which is fundamental for preventing recurrence. It also implicitly supports CO3 by highlighting areas where work permit procedures might need to be more rigorous.

### 5. TOR (Task Observation Record)

Finally, let's look at the **Task Observation Record (TOR)**. This is a more direct, hands-on technique used to assess the safety of how a specific task is being performed by an individual or a team. It’s about observing actual work practices and comparing them against safe procedures. This is a very practical tool for ensuring that what's written in the procedures is actually what's happening on the shop floor.

**What it is:**

A TOR is essentially a checklist or a structured observation form used by supervisors or safety personnel to watch employees performing their jobs. The observer notes whether the employee is following safe work practices, using required PPE, and adhering to established procedures.

**Key Elements of a TOR:**

*   **Task Identification:** What specific task is being observed? (e.g., operating a press, handling chemicals, working at height).
*   **Safe Procedures:** What are the documented safe ways to perform this task?
*   **Observation Points:** Specific behaviors or conditions to look for.
*   **Actual Practice:** What the worker is actually doing.
*   **Compliance Status:** Does the actual practice match the safe procedure? (Compliant, Non-Compliant, Partial Compliance).
*   **Corrective Action:** What needs to be done if non-compliance is observed?

**Example:**

Observing a worker changing a light bulb at height:
*   **Task:** Replacing a light bulb on a mezzanine floor.
*   **Safe Procedure:** Use a designated, stable ladder; ensure the ladder is secured at the base and top; maintain three points of contact when climbing; wear safety shoes.
*   **Observation:** The observer sees the worker using a shaky, unsecured stool.
*   **Compliance:** Non-compliant.
*   **Corrective Action:** Stop the worker immediately, explain the hazards of using the stool, provide the correct ladder, and reinforce ladder safety procedures.

**The "Teaching" Aspect:** A good TOR isn't just about catching people doing things wrong. It's a coaching opportunity. When a supervisor or safety officer uses a TOR, they should aim to provide immediate feedback and guidance. This reinforces safe behavior and corrects unsafe practices *before* an accident happens. This aligns perfectly with the idea of safety psychology and motivational theories mentioned in CO2, as positive reinforcement and constructive feedback are key motivators.

**Reference:** The concept of observing and correcting work practices is implicitly covered in many general safety management texts, including perhaps Grimaldi and Simonds' "Safety Management," which would emphasize the role of supervision.

**Connection to Course Outcomes:** TOR directly supports CO3 by providing a tool to estimate safety performance (e.g., percentage of tasks observed performed safely) and is a practical way to check compliance with safety regulations and work permits. It also feeds into CO4 by providing data for job safety analysis and identifying areas where training or procedures might need improvement.

### Connecting the Techniques: A Holistic Approach

It's crucial to see these techniques not as isolated tools, but as parts of a larger, integrated safety management system.

*   **System Safety** gives us the big picture, ensuring hazards are managed from design through operation.
*   **Change Analysis (MOC)** ensures that as the system evolves, new hazards aren't introduced unknowingly.
*   **MORT** provides a framework for auditing and improving the management system itself.
*   **Multi-Events Sequencing** helps us reconstruct and understand accidents when they do occur.
*   **TOR** is our eyes and ears on the ground, observing day-to-day work practices.

Together, these analytical techniques allow us to move from a reactive to a truly proactive and systematic approach to safety. They help us answer questions like: Are our procedures effective? Are our people following them? What happens if something changes? And, if something goes wrong, how can we learn from it to prevent it from happening again? This is the essence of effective safety management and what these analytical tools enable us to do.

Remember this: A strong safety audit is built upon a foundation of thorough analysis. These techniques are your building blocks.

## Sample Questions and Answers

**Q1. Briefly explain the purpose of System Safety analysis in the context of a manufacturing plant.**

**Answer:**
System Safety analysis in a manufacturing plant aims to identify, evaluate, and control hazards throughout the entire lifecycle of its operations, from the design of machinery and processes to their daily operation, maintenance, and eventual decommissioning. Its purpose is to proactively integrate safety into all aspects of the plant, ensuring that potential harm from hazards associated with people, equipment, procedures, and the environment is minimized from the outset, rather than trying to fix problems after they arise. This aligns with the proactive nature of safety management and helps prevent accidents by building safety into the system's core.

**Q2. Why is Change Analysis (Management of Change - MOC) considered a critical component of a safety audit?**

**Answer:**
Change Analysis (MOC) is critical because any alteration to a workplace – whether it's a new piece of equipment, a modified process, a change in raw materials, or even a revised work procedure – can introduce new hazards or alter existing risk levels. Without a systematic process to identify, assess, and control these potential new risks before and during implementation, accidents can occur. A safety audit needs to verify that an organization has robust MOC procedures in place to ensure that changes are managed safely, preventing unintended consequences and maintaining the overall safety integrity of the workplace. This directly supports CO3 by ensuring procedures and permits are updated accordingly.

**Q3. Differentiate between the focus of Multi-Events Sequencing and Task Observation Record (TOR) in accident analysis.**

**Answer:**
*   **Multi-Events Sequencing** focuses on reconstructing the **chronological order of events and contributing factors** that led to an accident or incident. It's about understanding the causal chain, mapping out the sequence from initial conditions to the final outcome, to identify critical points where intervention could have prevented the event. This aligns with CO4's emphasis on accident analysis models.
*   **Task Observation Record (TOR)**, on the other hand, focuses on **observing the actual performance of a specific task** by individuals in real-time. Its purpose is to assess compliance with established safe work procedures and identify unsafe behaviours or conditions during the execution of routine or non-routine tasks. This is more about verifying ongoing compliance and providing immediate feedback, supporting CO3 and CO4 by ensuring safe job practices are followed.

**Q4. Explain the core principle behind MORT (Management Oversight and Risk Tree) and how it contributes to understanding safety system effectiveness.**

**Answer:**
The core principle of MORT is to provide a comprehensive and systematic framework for analyzing the effectiveness of a safety management system by breaking down a large problem (like an accident) into its constituent parts. It uses a "tree" structure where the undesirable event is the root, and branches explore potential failures across various management system elements (e.g., policy, organization, planning, implementation, review). By systematically questioning whether each aspect of the management system functioned as intended, MORT helps identify deficiencies and root causes of failures within the management structure itself, rather than just the immediate causes of an incident. This diagnostic capability is crucial for improving overall safety system performance and compliance, directly addressing the analytical needs of CO4.

**Q5. Imagine a scenario where a new chemical is introduced into a factory. Which analytical technique would be most crucial for proactively identifying potential hazards, and why?**

**Answer:**
The most crucial analytical technique for proactively identifying potential hazards when introducing a new chemical would be **System Safety Analysis**, coupled with a robust **Change Analysis (MOC)** process.
*   **System Safety Analysis** would ensure that the inherent hazards of the chemical (toxicity, flammability, reactivity), its interaction with existing processes and equipment, and safe handling/storage requirements are considered from the design phase of its introduction.
*   **Change Analysis (MOC)** would then systematically assess the specific impact of introducing *this particular* chemical into the *existing* factory system. This would involve reviewing Safety Data Sheets (SDS), determining necessary PPE, updating operating procedures, training personnel, and ensuring proper ventilation and emergency response plans are in place.

Together, these techniques ensure that the introduction of the new chemical is managed safely, preventing the creation of new, uncontrolled risks. This supports CO4 by enabling the analysis of job safety and potential accidents related to the new chemical.
