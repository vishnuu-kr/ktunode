# PRINCIPLES OF SAFETY MANAGEMENT

## Module 4: Code of Practice on Safety Audit

### Topic: Risk Management – Introduction to ISO 31000 & Accident Investigation

Welcome, everyone! In this module, we're going to delve into two critically important areas of safety management: **Risk Management** and **Accident Investigation**. Understanding these concepts is fundamental to creating a safe working environment. Think of it this way: safety isn't just about preventing accidents; it's about proactively identifying what could go wrong and then systematically figuring out how to stop it, or at least minimize its impact. And when, despite our best efforts, something *does* go wrong, we need to understand *why* so we can prevent it from happening again.

This topic directly supports our course outcomes. By the end of this session, you'll be better equipped to explain safety concepts (CO1), understand accident prevention methods (CO2), and crucially, analyze accidents and produce reports (CO4). You’ll also gain insights that inform your understanding of safety performance (CO3).

### Part 1: Risk Management – An Introduction to ISO 31000

#### What is Risk? And Why Manage It?

Let's start with the core concept: **Risk**. In simple terms, risk is the **effect of uncertainty on objectives**. This means it's about things that *might* happen, and how those things could affect what we're trying to achieve. In a safety context, our primary objective is, of course, the health and well-being of our people, but it also extends to protecting assets, the environment, and the organization's reputation.

Think about your daily commute. You want to get to class on time (your objective). What are the risks? A flat tire, traffic jams, an unexpected road closure, or even just getting lost. Each of these is an effect of uncertainty on your objective. You manage these risks by having a spare tire, planning your route, leaving a bit early, or having a GPS. Safety management applies this same thinking to the workplace.

#### The Need for a Standard: Introducing ISO 31000

Now, managing risk can feel a bit chaotic if everyone does it differently. That's where standards come in. **ISO 31000** is a globally recognized guideline for risk management. It doesn't tell you *what* specific risks to manage, but rather *how* to set up a robust **risk management framework** and a **process** for managing risks. It’s like a recipe for good risk management; it gives you the ingredients and the steps, but you decide what dish you’re making.

ISO 31000 helps organizations integrate risk management into their governance, strategy, and operations. It promotes a consistent and systematic approach, which is essential for effective safety management. If you're aiming for excellence in safety, aligning with ISO 31000 principles is a smart move. It helps ensure you're not just reacting to problems, but proactively building a culture of safety and resilience.

#### The Core Components of Risk Management (as per ISO 31000 principles)

While ISO 31000 is a comprehensive standard, we can break down its essence into a few key ideas that are vital for our understanding:

1.  **The Risk Management Framework:** This is the overarching structure within your organization that enables risk management. It includes policies, procedures, and the culture that supports risk-aware decision-making. Think of it as the "house" where all your risk management activities live. It needs to be built on a strong foundation of leadership commitment and a clear understanding of the organization's context.

2.  **The Risk Management Process:** This is the step-by-step method for identifying, analyzing, evaluating, treating, monitoring, and communicating risks. This is the “doing” part of risk management.

    *   **Establishing the Context:** Before you can manage risks, you need to understand your organization, its objectives, its environment, and the criteria for what constitutes an acceptable level of risk. What are we trying to achieve, and what are the boundaries?
    *   **Risk Assessment:** This is the heart of the process. It involves:
        *   **Risk Identification:** What could go wrong? This is about brainstorming potential hazards and undesirable events.
        *   **Risk Analysis:** How likely is it to happen, and what would be the consequences? This is where we look at probabilities and impacts.
        *   **Risk Evaluation:** Based on the analysis, how significant is this risk? Does it need immediate attention? This helps us prioritize.
    *   **Risk Treatment:** What are we going to do about the risks? This involves selecting and implementing options to modify the risk. This is where we develop control measures – like safety procedures, engineering controls, or personal protective equipment (PPE).
    *   **Monitoring and Review:** Is our risk management plan working? Are the controls effective? We need to continuously check and update our approach.
    *   **Communication and Consultation:** This is crucial throughout the entire process. We need to talk to people – employees, stakeholders, experts – to gather information, share findings, and ensure buy-in.

**Remember this:** ISO 31000 provides a structured way to think about and manage uncertainty. It’s about being prepared and making informed decisions to protect your people and your operations. It directly contributes to CO1 by providing a framework for understanding safety concepts.

### Part 2: Accident Investigation – Understanding the "Why, When, Where, Who, and How"

Now, let’s shift gears to what happens when things go wrong – **Accident Investigation**. This is not about assigning blame; it's about learning and preventing recurrence. It's a fundamental aspect of improving safety and directly supports CO4, where you'll learn to produce reports and analyze accidents.

#### Why Investigate Accidents?

This is the most important question to start with. Why do we bother?
The primary reasons are:

1.  **To Determine the Cause(s):** This is the most obvious reason. We need to understand the root cause(s), not just the immediate cause. For example, a cut finger is the immediate cause of injury. But *why* did the cut happen? Was it a dull knife, improper training, a faulty machine guard, or rushing?
2.  **To Prevent Recurrence:** Once we know *why* it happened, we can implement controls to stop it from happening again. This is the proactive element.
3.  **To Identify Hazards:** Investigations often uncover hazards that were previously unknown or not adequately controlled.
4.  **To Assess the Effectiveness of Existing Controls:** Did our current safety measures fail? If so, why?
5.  **To Comply with Legal and Regulatory Requirements:** Many laws mandate the investigation of accidents and incidents.
6.  **To Improve Safety Performance:** By learning from mistakes, we continuously improve our safety systems and culture.
7.  **To Gather Information for Legal or Insurance Purposes:** While not the primary goal, an investigation provides factual documentation.

**Think about it:** If your child falls off a swing, you don't just scold them and move on. You check if they’re okay, understand *how* they fell (did they slip, jump off incorrectly?), and then you might adjust the swing's height, add softer landing material, or talk to them about how to use it safely. Accident investigation is the professional version of that. It aligns with CO1 by explaining a core safety function.

#### When to Investigate?

Not every minor scrape requires a full-blown investigation. However, certain events warrant immediate attention:

*   **Fatalities:** Absolutely.
*   **Serious Injuries:** Injuries requiring significant medical treatment, hospitalization, or resulting in permanent disability.
*   **Near Misses (or "Near Hit"):** These are events that *could have* resulted in an accident but didn't, often by chance. These are golden opportunities for learning! A pipe bursting but not spraying anyone is a near miss. Investigating it can prevent a future spray that *does* injure someone.
*   **Major Property Damage or Environmental Releases:** Even if no one is hurt, these events often point to underlying systemic weaknesses.
*   **Any event that poses a significant risk if it were to happen again.**

**Key takeaway:** Don't wait for a catastrophe. Investigate near misses and minor incidents; they are often precursors to major events. This understanding is crucial for CO2 (accident prevention methods) and CO4 (analyzing accidents).

#### Where to Investigate?

The investigation usually begins at the **scene of the incident**. This is where the physical evidence is located. However, it's important to remember that the "where" can extend beyond the immediate accident site. It might involve:

*   **The location where equipment was last maintained.**
*   **The location where the worker received training.**
*   **The location where procedures were developed or reviewed.**
*   **Off-site facilities if relevant (e.g., supplier issues).**

The goal is to gather all relevant information, which means going where the evidence and context lie.

#### Who Should Investigate?

The investigation team composition is critical for a thorough and unbiased review. Typically, it should involve:

*   **A Trained Investigator:** Someone with knowledge of investigation techniques.
*   **Supervisor of the Affected Area:** They understand the operational context.
*   **Workers (Peers):** They can provide insights into the actual work practices and conditions.
*   **Safety Personnel:** They bring expertise in safety principles and regulations.
*   **Technical Experts (if needed):** Engineers, metallurgists, ergonomists, etc., depending on the nature of the incident.
*   **Management Representative:** To ensure resources are allocated and findings are acted upon.

**Crucially, the team should be impartial.** Avoid having individuals deeply involved in the immediate operation or potentially responsible for the incident lead the investigation. This ensures objectivity, which is vital for identifying the true root causes and not just surface-level explanations.

#### How to Investigate? The Process

The "how" is about the systematic process. While specific methodologies vary, the core steps are consistent:

1.  **Immediate Response and Notification:** Ensure the scene is safe, provide first aid, and notify relevant parties.
2.  **Securing the Scene:** Prevent disturbance of evidence. This is like a police cordon.
3.  **Data Collection:** This is the most intensive phase. It involves:
    *   **Examining the Scene:** Looking for physical evidence – damaged equipment, misplaced tools, spilled materials, environmental conditions.
    *   **Interviewing Witnesses:** This is a delicate but vital part.
    *   **Collecting Documents:** Work permits, maintenance logs, training records, operating procedures, safety data sheets (SDS), risk assessments.
    *   **Taking Photographs/Videos:** Documenting the scene and evidence.
    *   **Gathering Samples:** If chemical exposure or material failure is suspected.
4.  **Analysis:** This is where you make sense of the data.
    *   **Reconstruction of the Event:** Chronologically piecing together what happened.
    *   **Root Cause Analysis (RCA):** This is the most critical step. It’s about digging deeper than the immediate cause to find the underlying system failures. Methods like the "5 Whys" (ask "why" repeatedly until you reach a fundamental cause) or Fault Tree Analysis can be used. For example, if a machine guard was removed, *why*? (To clear a jam). *Why* was there a jam? (Poor maintenance). *Why* was maintenance poor? (Lack of trained personnel). *Why* were there no trained personnel? (Inadequate training program). *Why* was there an inadequate training program? (Lack of budget/priority). The budget/priority issue might be a root cause.
5.  **Developing Recommendations:** Based on the analysis, what needs to be done? These should be specific, actionable, and aimed at preventing recurrence.
6.  **Writing the Report:** Documenting findings, causes, and recommendations. This links directly to CO4.
7.  **Implementing Recommendations:** This is the crucial follow-through. An investigation is useless if nothing changes.
8.  **Follow-up and Review:** Checking if the implemented recommendations are effective.

#### Basics: Man- Environment & Systems

When investigating, it’s helpful to consider the contributing factors through these three lenses:

*   **Man (Human Factors):** This includes worker skills, knowledge, training, fatigue, stress, attitudes, behavior, and physiological or psychological states. Did the worker have the right training? Were they fatigued? Was the task too complex?
*   **Environment:** This covers the physical surroundings – lighting, noise, temperature, ventilation, housekeeping, layout, weather conditions, and the presence of hazardous substances. Was the floor slippery? Was the area too crowded? Was there adequate ventilation?
*   **Systems:** This refers to the organizational structures, procedures, policies, supervision, maintenance, design of equipment, management commitment, and safety culture. Were there clear procedures? Was supervision adequate? Was the equipment properly designed and maintained?

Most accidents are not caused by a single factor but a combination from these areas, often stemming from system failures. Heinrich’s accident pyramid (though not explicitly mentioned, it’s a core concept in accident causation theories taught in CO1) suggests that for every major accident, there are many minor accidents, and even more near misses, all stemming from unsafe acts and conditions, which are often rooted in system deficiencies.

#### Tools and Techniques for Investigation

*   **Checklists:** Useful for ensuring all aspects are considered during data collection.
*   **Photographs/Video:** Visual evidence is powerful.
*   **Diagrams/Sketches:** To illustrate the scene and event sequence.
*   **Root Cause Analysis (RCA) Tools:**
    *   **5 Whys:** Simple, effective for straightforward problems.
    *   **Fishbone Diagram (Ishikawa Diagram):** Helps categorize potential causes (Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Environment).
    *   **Fault Tree Analysis:** A top-down deductive analysis.
*   **Incident Investigation Software:** Can help manage data and reporting.

#### Data Collection: The Foundation of a Good Investigation

This is where you gather the raw material. Be thorough and objective.

*   **Scene Examination:** Be meticulous. Look for anything out of place.
*   **Interviews:** This is a critical skill.
    *   **Handling Witnesses:**
        *   **Be Prompt:** Interview witnesses as soon as possible while memories are fresh.
        *   **Be Empathetic and Reassuring:** Assure them they are not in trouble (unless they are withholding information, which is a separate issue). Their goal is to help understand what happened.
        *   **Interview Separately:** To prevent them from influencing each other's accounts.
        *   **Start with Open-Ended Questions:** "Tell me what you saw/heard."
        *   **Listen More Than You Talk:** Let them tell their story.
        *   **Clarify and Probe:** Ask specific questions based on their initial account. "You mentioned a noise. Can you describe that noise?"
        *   **Avoid Leading Questions:** "You saw the worker remove the guard, didn't you?" (This suggests the answer).
        *   **Be Neutral:** Don't show shock or judgment about their account.
        *   **Thank Them:** Acknowledge their contribution.
    *   **Documentation:** Take detailed notes during interviews.
*   **Record Review:** Ensure all relevant records are obtained.

#### Case Study Example:

Let's imagine a scenario:

**Incident:** A maintenance worker, John, tripped over a loose cable on the factory floor and fractured his ankle.

**Investigation:**

1.  **Immediate Response:** First aid was given, John was transported to the hospital. The area was cordoned off.
2.  **Scene Examination:** A loose electrical cable was found trailing across a walkway. It wasn't secured, and no warning signs were present. The walkway was well-lit, but this specific area had a lot of foot traffic.
3.  **Witness Interviews:**
    *   **John (once able):** Said he was rushing to complete a task and didn't see the cable as he was looking ahead for the machine he needed to service. He mentioned that cables are often left unsecured in that area.
    *   **Another worker:** Saw the cable there earlier in the day and had to step over it but didn't think to report it.
    *   **John’s Supervisor:** Confirmed John was behind schedule and under pressure to finish. He also admitted that cable management in that walkway had been an ongoing minor issue, but not prioritized for immediate fix.
    *   **Electrician responsible for cable:** Stated the cable was temporary for a recent machine test and was scheduled to be removed next week. They typically use cable ties or conduits for permanent installations, but for temporary setups, they sometimes left them loose if they weren't directly in a main thoroughfare.
4.  **Document Review:** John’s training records showed he was competent. The task he was rushing for was critical. The risk assessment for the walkway did not specifically highlight the hazard of trailing cables, though it mentioned general housekeeping.
5.  **Analysis (Root Cause):**
    *   **Immediate Cause:** Tripped over unsecured cable.
    *   **Why 1?** Cable was left unsecured.
    *   **Why 2?** Temporary setup, not prioritized for management.
    *   **Why 3?** Existing procedures for temporary cable management were not robust enough or not followed for this specific situation.
    *   **Why 4?** Lack of specific risk assessment for temporary cable hazards in high-traffic areas.
    *   **Why 5?** Management had not prioritized improving temporary cable management practices despite the ongoing "minor issue."

    *   **Contributing Factors:** John was rushing due to schedule pressure. Witness did not report a minor hazard.
6.  **Recommendations:**
    *   Implement a mandatory procedure for securing all temporary cables, especially in walkways, using temporary cable ramps or tape, regardless of perceived traffic.
    *   Conduct a specific risk assessment for trailing cables in all high-traffic areas.
    *   Review scheduling pressures for maintenance tasks and ensure realistic timelines are set.
    *   Reinforce a "report any hazard" culture among all employees, even minor ones.
7.  **Report:** A detailed report documenting these findings and recommendations would be prepared.
8.  **Implementation:** The procedure for temporary cables would be updated, and the walkway hazard addressed. Training would be provided.

This case study demonstrates how an investigation moves from the immediate event to the underlying systemic issues, supporting CO4’s objective of analyzing accidents and producing reports.

### Sample Questions and Answers:

**Q1: According to ISO 31000 principles, what is the fundamental purpose of establishing the context before conducting a risk assessment?**

**Answer:** Establishing the context (or "Context of the Organization") is crucial in ISO 31000 because it defines the objectives, stakeholders, organizational environment, and risk criteria. This foundational understanding ensures that the risk assessment and subsequent risk treatments are aligned with the organization's goals, capabilities, and its specific operating environment. Without proper context, risks might be identified and managed in isolation, leading to ineffective or irrelevant controls, and potentially failing to meet the organization's actual safety objectives. It helps answer: "What are we trying to achieve, and what are the boundaries for managing risk?"

**Q2: A near-miss incident occurs where a heavy object falls from a height but lands without injuring anyone. Why is investigating this near-miss critical, even though no one was harmed?**

**Answer:** Investigating a near-miss incident is critical because it presents a valuable learning opportunity *before* a serious injury or fatality occurs. The fact that the object fell indicates a failure in the control measures designed to prevent it from falling in the first place. By investigating, the organization can:
    1.  **Identify the root cause:** Was it improper storage, equipment failure, inadequate securing mechanisms, or procedural breakdown?
    2.  **Prevent recurrence:** Implement corrective actions to fix the underlying issue and prevent the object from falling again, thereby preventing a future injury.
    3.  **Uncover systemic weaknesses:** Near misses often reveal underlying systemic flaws that might not be apparent in day-to-day operations.
    Ignoring near misses is akin to ignoring warning signs, which significantly increases the probability of a more severe event occurring in the future.

**Q3: When conducting witness interviews during an accident investigation, why is it important to interview each witness separately?**

**Answer:** Interviewing witnesses separately is vital for ensuring the accuracy and integrity of the information gathered. If witnesses are interviewed together, there is a risk of:
    1.  **Confabulation/Information Contamination:** One witness might inadvertently influence another's memory or account, either by suggesting details they hadn't initially recalled or by collectively developing a narrative that isn't entirely factual.
    2.  **Peer Pressure/Fear:** A more assertive witness might dominate the discussion, or less confident witnesses might hesitate to share their true observations for fear of contradicting the majority or appearing incorrect.
    3.  **Unnecessary Delay:** A group interview can be less efficient in allowing each individual to fully express their account.
    By interviewing separately, investigators can obtain individual, uninfluenced perspectives, which allows for better cross-referencing of facts and a more reliable reconstruction of the event sequence.

**Q4: Briefly explain the "5 Whys" technique in root cause analysis and provide a simple example related to workplace safety.**

**Answer:** The "5 Whys" is a simple, iterative interrogative technique used to explore the cause-and-effect relationships underlying a particular problem. The primary goal is to determine the root cause of a defect or problem by repeatedly asking the question "Why?". Typically, the question is asked at least five times, but it can be more or less depending on when the fundamental cause is identified.

**Example:**

*   **Problem:** A machine operator suffered a minor cut to their hand.
*   **Why 1?** The operator got too close to the rotating blades while clearing a jam.
*   **Why 2?** The safety guard was not in place.
*   **Why 3?** The operator removed the guard to clear a recurring jam.
*   **Why 4?** The machine frequently jams because the material feed is inconsistent.
*   **Why 5?** The material feed mechanism is worn out and requires replacement (This is a potential root cause – a mechanical failure due to lack of maintenance).

This simple example shows how asking "Why?" repeatedly helps uncover the mechanical issue (worn part) behind the immediate cause (guard removed), allowing for a more effective solution (repairing the machine) rather than just telling operators to keep guards on.
