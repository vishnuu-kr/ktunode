# MODULE 1: MONOMERS AND POLYMERS – STRUCTURE AND DEFINITIONS

Welcome, everyone, to our journey into the fascinating world of polymers! In this first module, we're going to lay the foundational stones, understanding the very building blocks of these incredible materials that shape our everyday lives. Think about it: the plastic in your phone, the fabric of your clothes, the tires on your car – all are polymers! So, let's dive in and get a clear picture of what makes them tick.

---

## 1.1 The Building Blocks: Monomers and Repeat Units

Before we can talk about a giant polymer chain, we need to understand its smallest repeating part.

### What is a Monomer?

Imagine you're building with LEGOs. Each individual LEGO brick is like a **monomer**. A monomer is a small molecule that can react with other identical or similar molecules to form a larger molecule, a polymer. The key here is its ability to *react* and *link up*.

*   **Relationship to Course Outcomes:** This definition directly addresses **CO1: Explain the structural requirements of polymerisation.** A monomer *must* have the correct chemical structure, usually with specific functional groups, to be able to link up and polymerize. Without the right "LEGO brick," you can't build the structure.

### What is a Repeat Unit?

Now, when those LEGO bricks link together, they form a chain. The specific structure that gets repeated along that chain is called the **repeat unit**. It's what's left of the monomer after it has joined the polymer chain, typically after losing a small molecule like water or HCl during the polymerization process.

Think of a train: the engine and carriages are distinct parts, but the entire train is made up of many repeating sections of carriage and connecting couplings. The repeat unit is like the essential structure of one carriage and its connection point.

*   **Example:** If you take ethylene (CH₂=CH₂), which is a monomer, and polymerize it, it forms polyethylene. The monomer ethylene opens up its double bond to link with others. What ends up in the chain is a `-CH₂-CH₂-` unit. That’s the repeat unit.

    *   **Monomer:** Ethylene (CH₂=CH₂)
    *   **Repeat Unit:** `-CH₂-CH₂-`

*   **Relationship to Course Outcomes:** Understanding the repeat unit is crucial for **CO2: Illustrate structural formula of simple plastic and rubber materials.** When we draw a polymer's structure, we're usually drawing this repeating segment.

### The Monomer vs. The Repeat Unit – A Critical Distinction

It's *vital* to distinguish between the monomer and the repeat unit. The monomer is the *starting material*, often containing a double bond or reactive end groups that are consumed during polymerization. The repeat unit is the *segment that makes up the polymer chain*.

*   **Common Pitfall:** Students sometimes get confused and draw the monomer structure when they mean the repeat unit. Remember, the repeat unit is *part of the polymer chain*, showing how the monomers have linked.

---

## 1.2 Assembling the Chain: Oligomers and Polymers

Let's continue building our understanding of how these monomers come together.

### What is an Oligomer?

Before you get a huge polymer chain, you often have shorter chains. An **oligomer** is a molecule composed of a relatively small, *fixed* number of repeat units, typically between 2 and 10-20. Think of it as a short LEGO train – maybe just 3 or 4 bricks linked together. They are not yet "long enough" to exhibit the characteristic properties of a true polymer.

*   **Example:** A dimer (2 repeat units), trimer (3 repeat units), tetramer (4 repeat units), and so on, are all oligomers.

### What is a Polymer?

Now, a **polymer** is a large molecule, often called a macromolecule, formed by the covalent bonding of many repeating units (monomers). The key here is "many" and the fact that the number of repeat units is not fixed and can vary significantly. It’s our long LEGO train – so long that it starts behaving differently than a short one.

*   **Analogy:** Think of spaghetti. A single strand of spaghetti is a monomer. A few strands stuck together are oligomers. A pot full of long, intertwined spaghetti strands represents a polymer – they have collective properties (like holding their shape in a bowl) that individual strands don't.

*   **Relationship to Course Outcomes:** This definition is central to the entire module and course, supporting **CO1** and introducing the concept of molecular size which is foundational for **CO3: Explain the basic concepts of average molecular weights.**

### Degree of Polymerization (DP)

How long is our polymer chain? That's where the **degree of polymerization (DP)** comes in. It's simply the number of repeat units in a polymer chain.

*   **Formula:** DP = Number of repeat units

For a polymer made from a single type of monomer (a homopolymer), if the repeat unit is derived from a monomer M, the polymer chain can be represented as `-[M]n-`, where 'n' is the degree of polymerization.

*   **Importance:** The DP is directly related to the polymer's molecular weight and significantly influences its physical properties, like strength, viscosity, and melting point. A higher DP generally means a stronger, tougher material.

*   **Exam Tip:** Questions might ask you to calculate DP if you know the molecular weight of the polymer and the repeat unit. You'll need to find the molecular weight of the repeat unit and divide the polymer's molecular weight by it.

### Representation of a Polymer

How do we draw these long chains? We use brackets!

*   **General Representation:** A polymer is typically represented by enclosing its repeat unit within brackets and indicating that it repeats by adding a subscript 'n' outside the brackets. For example, polyethylene: `[-CH₂-CH₂-]n`.

This 'n' signifies that this `-CH₂-CH₂-` segment is repeated many, many times along the chain. It doesn't mean every chain has exactly 'n' units; it implies a distribution of chain lengths, and 'n' represents the average number of repeat units.

## 1.3 Monomer Functionality: The Key to Linking

Now, let's look at the monomer itself. What allows it to link up and form these chains? It's its **functionality**.

### What is Functionality?

**Functionality** of a monomer refers to the number of reactive sites or linkages that a monomer molecule can form. These reactive sites are where the monomer can bond with other monomers or growing polymer chains.

*   **Analogy:** Think of building with magnetic LEGOs. If a brick has magnets on two sides, it has a functionality of 2. It can connect to two other bricks. If it had magnets on three sides, its functionality would be 3.

*   **Relationship to Course Outcomes:** This is *critical* for **CO1: Explain the structural requirements of polymerisation.** A monomer needs to have a functionality of at least 2 to form a linear chain. Monomers with functionality greater than 2 can lead to branched or cross-linked polymer structures.

    *   **Monofunctional monomer (f=1):** Cannot polymerize to form chains. They act as chain stoppers.
    *   **Bifunctional monomer (f=2):** Can form linear polymer chains. This is the most common case for many plastics.
    *   **Trifunctional or higher (f>2):** Can lead to branched or cross-linked (3D network) polymers.

## 1.4 Exploring Specific Monomers, Repeat Units, and Polymers

Let's get concrete! We'll now examine the structures of common monomers, their resulting repeat units, and the overall polymer for a range of important materials. This section directly addresses **CO2: Illustrate structural formula of simple plastic and rubber materials.**

For each, we'll show:
*   **Monomer:** The starting molecule.
*   **Repeat Unit:** The segment that repeats in the polymer chain.
*   **Polymer:** The resulting macromolecule, represented using the repeat unit notation.

---

### 1.4.1 Polyethylene (PE)

One of the most common plastics in the world! Think of your plastic bags, milk jugs, and films.

*   **Monomer:** Ethylene
    *   Structure: CH₂=CH₂
    *   Note the double bond – this is the reactive site that allows polymerization.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Ethylene unit
    *   Structure: `[-CH₂-CH₂-]`
    *   The double bond in ethylene opens up to form single bonds with adjacent units.

*   **Polymer:** Polyethylene
    *   Representation: `[-CH₂-CH₂-]n`

---

### 1.4.2 Polypropylene (PP)

Used in containers, automotive parts, and textiles. It’s similar to polyethylene but has a methyl group attached.

*   **Monomer:** Propylene (or Propene)
    *   Structure: CH₂=CH-CH₃
    *   Note the methyl group (CH₃) attached to one of the carbons in the double bond.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Propylene unit
    *   Structure: `[-CH₂-CH(CH₃)-]`
    *   The double bond opens up. The methyl group can be positioned in different ways relative to the main chain (we'll discuss stereoregularity later, but for now, just the basic connectivity).

*   **Polymer:** Polypropylene
    *   Representation: `[-CH₂-CH(CH₃)-]n`

---

### 1.4.3 Polystyrene (PS)

Think of disposable cups, CD cases, and foam packaging (Styrofoam).

*   **Monomer:** Styrene
    *   Structure: CH₂=CH-C₆H₅ (where C₆H₅ is a phenyl or benzene ring)
    *   It's ethylene with a phenyl group attached.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Styrene unit
    *   Structure: `[-CH₂-CH(C₆H₅)-]`
    *   Again, the double bond opens. The bulky phenyl ring hangs off the main chain.

*   **Polymer:** Polystyrene
    *   Representation: `[-CH₂-CH(C₆H₅)-]n`

---

### 1.4.4 Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC)

Found in pipes, window frames, flooring, and electrical insulation.

*   **Monomer:** Vinyl Chloride
    *   Structure: CH₂=CH-Cl
    *   It's ethylene with a chlorine atom attached.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Vinyl Chloride unit
    *   Structure: `[-CH₂-CH(Cl)-]`
    *   The chlorine atom is attached to the polymer backbone.

*   **Polymer:** Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC)
    *   Representation: `[-CH₂-CH(Cl)-]n`

---

### 1.4.5 Polyacrylonitrile (PAN)

Used in synthetic fibers (like acrylics for clothing) and carbon fiber precursors.

*   **Monomer:** Acrylonitrile
    *   Structure: CH₂=CH-C≡N
    *   It has a nitrile group (-C≡N) attached.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Acrylonitrile unit
    *   Structure: `[-CH₂-CH(C≡N)-]`
    *   The nitrile group is polar and contributes to the material's properties.

*   **Polymer:** Polyacrylonitrile (PAN)
    *   Representation: `[-CH₂-CH(C≡N)-]n`

---

### 1.4.6 Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)

Known as acrylic glass, plexiglass, or Lucite. Clear, shatter-resistant alternative to glass.

*   **Monomer:** Methyl Methacrylate (MMA)
    *   Structure: CH₂=C(CH₃)-COOCH₃
    *   This is a bit more complex. It's an ester of methacrylic acid. Note the double bond and the ester group.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Methyl Methacrylate unit
    *   Structure: `[-CH₂-C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)-]`
    *   The double bond opens, and the ester group is pendant to the main chain.

*   **Polymer:** Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)
    *   Representation: `[-CH₂-C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)-]n`

---

### 1.4.7 Polycarbonate (PC)

Used for safety glasses, CDs, DVDs, and durable, transparent components.

Polycarbonates are often formed by condensation polymerization, which involves the release of a small molecule.

*   **Monomers:**
    1.  **Bisphenol A (BPA):** This is a diol (has two -OH groups).
        *   Structure: HO-C₆H₄-C(CH₃)₂-C₆H₄-OH
    2.  **Phosgene (COCl₂):** Or a related carbonate precursor.

*   **Repeat Unit:**
    *   Structure: `[-O-C₆H₄-C(CH₃)₂-C₆H₄-O-CO-]`
    *   Here, the polymerization occurs between the -OH groups of Bisphenol A and phosgene, releasing HCl. The `-CO-` unit bridges the aromatic rings via carbonate linkages (`-O-CO-O-`).

*   **Polymer:** Polycarbonate
    *   Representation: `[-O-C₆H₄-C(CH₃)₂-C₆H₄-O-CO-]n`

---

### 1.4.8 Phenol-Formaldehyde Resin (PF Resin / Bakelite)

One of the first synthetic plastics, known for its electrical insulation and heat resistance. Used in handles for cookware, electrical components.

This is a **thermosetting** polymer, often formed by condensation.

*   **Monomers:**
    1.  **Phenol:** C₆H₅OH (has reactive sites on the benzene ring).
    2.  **Formaldehyde:** CH₂O.

*   **Repeat Unit (Simplified):** The structure can be complex due to branching and cross-linking, but a simplified view involves linking units derived from phenol and formaldehyde, forming methylene bridges (-CH₂-).
    *   A repeating segment might look something like: `[-C₆H₄-CH₂-]` or more complex networked structures. The phenol rings are linked by methylene groups.

*   **Polymer:** Phenol-Formaldehyde Resin (PF Resin)
    *   Representation: Due to its cross-linked nature, a simple linear representation isn't fully accurate. It forms a 3D network.

---

### 1.4.9 Melamine-Formaldehyde Resin (MF Resin)

Used in laminates (like Formica), dinnerware, and coatings due to its hardness and scratch resistance.

Similar to PF resin, this is a thermosetting polymer.

*   **Monomers:**
    1.  **Melamine:** C₃N₃(NH₂)₃ (a triazine ring with amino groups).
    2.  **Formaldehyde:** CH₂O.

*   **Repeat Unit (Simplified):** The amino groups of melamine react with formaldehyde, creating cross-links and forming a rigid network. Simplified segments show melamine rings linked by methylene (-CH₂-) or methoxy (-CH₂-O-) bridges.

*   **Polymer:** Melamine-Formaldehyde Resin (MF Resin)
    *   Representation: Again, a 3D network.

---

### 1.4.10 Nylon 6

A synthetic polyamide used in textiles, carpets, and fishing nets.

*   **Monomer:** Caprolactam
    *   Structure: A cyclic amide with 6 carbon atoms in the ring.
    *   This is a bit special: the *monomer itself* is a ring that opens up.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Amide unit
    *   Structure: `[-NH-(CH₂)₅-CO-]`
    *   The ring opens at the amide bond, forming an amide linkage in the polymer chain.

*   **Polymer:** Nylon 6
    *   Representation: `[-NH-(CH₂)₅-CO-]n`

---

### 1.4.11 Nylon 6,6

Another very common nylon, known for its strength and abrasion resistance. Used in fabrics, ropes, and engineering plastics.

This is a **step-growth polymerization** (specifically, condensation) from two different monomers.

*   **Monomers:**
    1.  **Hexamethylenediamine:** H₂N-(CH₂)₆-NH₂ (a diamine – has two amine groups).
    2.  **Adipic Acid:** HOOC-(CH₂)₄-COOH (a dicarboxylic acid – has two carboxylic acid groups).

*   **Repeat Unit:** Formed by the reaction between an amine group (-NH₂) and a carboxylic acid group (-COOH) to form an amide linkage (-NH-CO-), with the loss of a water molecule (H₂O).
    *   Structure: `[-NH-(CH₂)₆-NH-CO-(CH₂)₄-CO-]`

*   **Polymer:** Nylon 6,6
    *   Representation: `[-NH-(CH₂)₆-NH-CO-(CH₂)₄-CO-]n`

---

### 1.4.12 Poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET)

Familiar as the material for plastic bottles (soft drinks) and polyester fibers (clothing, sleeping bags).

Another condensation polymer.

*   **Monomers:**
    1.  **Ethylene Glycol:** HO-CH₂-CH₂-OH (a diol).
    2.  **Terephthalic Acid:** HOOC-C₆H₄-COOH (a dicarboxylic acid).

*   **Repeat Unit:** Formed by esterification reaction between the alcohol group and the carboxylic acid group, forming an ester linkage (-COO-), with the loss of a water molecule (H₂O).
    *   Structure: `[-O-CH₂-CH₂-O-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]`

*   **Polymer:** Poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET)
    *   Representation: `[-O-CH₂-CH₂-O-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]n`

---

### 1.4.13 Epoxy Polymer

Used as adhesives, coatings, and in composite materials due to their excellent adhesion and strength.

Epoxy resins are thermosetting polymers formed from the reaction of an epoxy resin (containing epoxide groups) and a hardener (often a diamine or polyamine).

*   **Monomers (Simplified Example):**
    1.  **Bisphenol A Diglycidyl Ether (DGEBA) - The Resin:** Contains epoxide rings.
    2.  **A Diamine (e.g., Hexamethylenediamine) - The Hardener:** Contains reactive -NH₂ groups.

*   **Repeat Unit:** The epoxide ring opens and reacts with the amine groups, forming hydroxyl groups (-OH) and amine linkages. The cross-linked network is complex. A repeating segment within the network could show the reaction product of the resin and hardener. For instance, after reaction, an epoxide ring is opened to form `-CH(OH)-CH₂-`.

*   **Polymer:** Epoxy Polymer
    *   Representation: Typically a highly cross-linked 3D network, not easily represented by a simple linear `[-X-]n` formula.

---

### 1.4.14 Kevlar®

A high-strength synthetic fiber used in bulletproof vests, tires, and aerospace applications. It's a type of aramid (aromatic polyamide).

*   **Monomers:**
    1.  **p-Phenylenediamine:** H₂N-C₆H₄-NH₂ (a diamine with amine groups at opposite ends of a benzene ring).
    2.  **Terephthaloyl Chloride:** ClOC-C₆H₄-COCl (a diacyl chloride).

*   **Repeat Unit:** Formed by condensation reaction between the amine and acyl chloride groups, forming an amide linkage (-NH-CO-) and releasing HCl.
    *   Structure: `[-NH-C₆H₄-NH-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]`
    *   Note the presence of rigid aromatic rings in the backbone, contributing to Kevlar's strength.

*   **Polymer:** Kevlar
    *   Representation: `[-NH-C₆H₄-NH-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]n`

---

### 1.4.15 Natural Rubber

The polymer found in rubber trees, known for its elasticity. Used in tires, gloves, and seals.

*   **Monomer:** Isoprene (2-methyl-1,3-butadiene)
    *   Structure: CH₂=C(CH₃)-CH=CH₂
    *   This monomer has *two* double bonds, but only one is involved in the polymerization reaction in the typical way.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Isoprene unit
    *   Structure: `[-CH₂-C(CH₃)=CH-CH₂-]`
    *   The polymerization typically occurs via 1,4-addition, where the double bonds at positions 1 and 4 react, leaving a double bond within the repeat unit.

*   **Polymer:** Natural Rubber (Polyisoprene)
    *   Representation: `[-CH₂-C(CH₃)=CH-CH₂-]n`
    *   The double bond within the repeat unit is crucial for vulcanization (cross-linking with sulfur), which dramatically improves its elasticity and strength.

---

### 1.4.16 Silicone Rubber

Flexible, heat-resistant, and electrically insulating. Used in sealants, cookware, and medical implants.

*   **Monomer (Simplified Concept):** While various silicones exist, a common type is derived from silanes. Let's consider a simple example related to dimethylsiloxane. The key is the silicon-oxygen backbone.

*   **Repeat Unit:** Siloxane unit
    *   Structure: `[-Si(CH₃)₂-O-]`
    *   The backbone consists of alternating silicon and oxygen atoms, with organic groups (like methyl, -CH₃) attached to the silicon atoms.

*   **Polymer:** Silicone Rubber (Polydimethylsiloxane - PDMS)
    *   Representation: `[-Si(CH₃)₂-O-]n`

## 1.5 Functionality and Polymer Architecture

Remember we talked about functionality? It's not just about forming linear chains!

*   **Bifunctional monomers (f=2):** Typically lead to linear or branched polymers.
    *   **Linear polymers:** Like polyethylene or PET, where monomers link end-to-end in a single chain.
    *   **Branched polymers:** If a monomer has a side group that can also react, or if there's a small amount of trifunctional monomer present, branches can form off the main chain.

*   **Monofunctional monomers (f=1):** Act as **chain stoppers**. They can attach to a growing chain end but cannot extend it further, thus controlling molecular weight.

*   **Trifunctional and higher (f>2):** These monomers are responsible for creating **cross-linked** or **network polymers**.
    *   Think of PF and MF resins, or cured epoxy. The monomers link in three dimensions, forming an intricate, rigid network. This is why thermosetting plastics don't melt or soften significantly upon heating; the strong covalent bonds of the network resist flow.

*   **Relationship to Course Outcomes:** This directly reinforces **CO1: Explain the structural requirements of polymerisation** by showing how monomer functionality dictates the final polymer architecture (linear, branched, or network).

## 1.6 Connecting Concepts: The Big Picture

Let's tie these ideas together and see how they relate to our learning objectives.

*   **CO1 (Structural Requirements):** We’ve seen that monomers need reactive sites (functionality) to link. The type of linkage (addition vs. condensation) and the functionality of the monomers dictate whether we get linear, branched, or cross-linked polymers.
*   **CO2 (Illustrating Structures):** We've drawn out the repeat units for many common plastics and rubbers. This is fundamental to recognizing and understanding these materials.
*   **CO3 (Average Molecular Weights):** While we haven't calculated them yet, the concept of 'n' (DP) implies that polymers don't have a single, sharp molecular weight. They exist as a distribution of chain lengths. This is a crucial concept for understanding polymer properties, and we'll delve into it more in later modules.
*   **CO4 (Polymerization Principles):** By looking at structures like polyethylene (addition) and PET/Nylon (condensation), we've seen the basic outcomes of different polymerization types. Stereoregularity (how groups are arranged on the backbone, like in polypropylene) is a key structural aspect influencing properties, which we'll explore later.
*   **CO5 (Comparing Polymerization Techniques):** While we've only touched upon the *results* of polymerization here, understanding the monomer and repeat unit structures is the first step in comparing techniques. For example, knowing that ethylene forms polyethylene via addition polymerization and that Nylon 6,6 forms via condensation polymerization helps us differentiate the processes.

## Sample Questions with Answers

**Q1. What is the difference between a monomer and a repeat unit? Give an example.**

**Answer:** A monomer is the small molecule that undergoes polymerization. The repeat unit is the structural segment that is repeated along the polymer chain, typically formed from the monomer after it has reacted and potentially lost a small molecule.

*   **Example:** For polyethylene, the monomer is ethylene (CH₂=CH₂), which has a double bond. The repeat unit is `[-CH₂-CH₂-]`, formed by the opening of the double bond as ethylene units link together.

**Q2. Which of the following monomers would likely lead to a cross-linked polymer if polymerized?**
    a) CH₂=CH₂ (Ethylene)
    b) H₂N-(CH₂)₆-NH₂ (Hexamethylenediamine)
    c) CH₂=CH-CH(OH)-CH₃
    d) CH₂(OH)-CH(OH) (Ethylene Glycol)

**Answer:** The correct answer is **(c) CH₂=CH-CH(OH)-CH₃**.
*   **Reasoning:** Monomers leading to cross-linked polymers typically have a functionality greater than 2.
    *   (a) Ethylene is bifunctional (via the double bond) and forms linear polyethylene.
    *   (b) Hexamethylenediamine is bifunctional (two amine groups) and is used in step-growth polymerization to form linear polymers like Nylon 6,6 (when reacted with a dicarboxylic acid).
    *   (c) CH₂=CH-CH(OH)-CH₃ has a double bond (potential for addition polymerization) *and* a hydroxyl group (-OH). If polymerization occurs via the double bond, the -OH group on the side chain can potentially react with other chains or monomers, leading to cross-linking. This molecule can be considered trifunctional in a broader sense depending on the reaction conditions.
    *   (d) Ethylene glycol is bifunctional (two hydroxyl groups) and is used in condensation polymerization (e.g., to form PET), leading to linear polymers.

**Q3. What is the degree of polymerization (DP) of a sample of PET with a molecular weight of 20,000 g/mol? (Atomic weights: C=12, H=1, O=16)**

**Answer:**
First, we need the structure of the repeat unit for PET: `[-O-CH₂-CH₂-O-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]`
Let's calculate the molecular weight of this repeat unit:
*   O: 16 g/mol * 3 = 48 g/mol
*   CH₂: 14 g/mol * 2 = 28 g/mol
*   C: 12 g/mol * 9 = 108 g/mol
*   H: 1 g/mol * 8 = 8 g/mol
*   CO: 12 g/mol + 16 g/mol = 28 g/mol * 2 = 56 g/mol
*   Benzene ring C₆H₄: (12 * 6) + (1 * 4) = 72 + 4 = 76 g/mol

Molecular Weight of Repeat Unit = 48 + 28 + 108 + 8 + 56 + 76 = **324 g/mol** (approximately, without considering precise benzene structure for simplification here).
Let's re-calculate the repeat unit molecular weight precisely:
`[-O-CH₂-CH₂-O-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]`
O = 16 (x3) = 48
CH₂ = 14 (x2) = 28
C = 12 (x9) = 108
H = 1 (x8) = 8
CO = 28 (x2) = 56
Total = 48 + 28 + 108 + 8 + 56 = **248 g/mol** (Correct calculation for the atoms in the repeat unit, excluding the aromatic carbons which are part of C6H4).

Let's redo the repeat unit and calculation:
`[-O-CH₂-CH₂-O-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]`
The repeat unit is `-(O-CH₂-CH₂-O-CO-C₆H₄-CO)-`
Atoms: 3 Oxygen, 4 Carbon (in the two CH2 and two CO), 8 Hydrogen, 6 Carbon and 4 Hydrogen (in the benzene ring).
Total C: 4 + 6 = 10
Total H: 8 + 4 = 12
Total O: 3
Molecular Weight of Repeat Unit = (10 * 12) + (12 * 1) + (3 * 16) = 120 + 12 + 48 = **180 g/mol**.
This is the molecular weight of the unit `-(O-CH2-CH2-O-CO-CO)-` if it were terephthalic acid reacted with ethylene glycol without the phenyl ring!

Let's re-examine the repeat unit structure again carefully from typical sources:
PET repeat unit: `[-O-CH₂-CH₂-O-CO-C₆H₄-CO-]`
MW Calculation:
*   O: 3 * 16 = 48
*   CH₂: 2 * 14 = 28
*   CO: 2 * 28 = 56
*   C₆H₄: (6 * 12) + (4 * 1) = 72 + 4 = 76
Total MW of repeat unit = 48 + 28 + 56 + 76 = **208 g/mol**.

Now, Degree of Polymerization (DP) = (Molecular Weight of Polymer) / (Molecular Weight of Repeat Unit)
DP = 20,000 g/mol / 208 g/mol ≈ **96.15**

Since DP must be an integer (number of repeat units), we would typically round this or state it as an average DP. In exams, if the numbers don't work out perfectly, use the closest whole number or the given average. Let's assume for exam purposes the repeat unit MW is precisely calculated to give a whole number DP, or the question implies an average. For our calculation, DP ≈ 96.

**Q4. Explain why the presence of a phenyl group in polystyrene makes it more rigid than polyethylene.**

**Answer:** The bulky phenyl group (C₆H₅) attached to the polymer backbone of polystyrene hinders the free rotation of the polymer chains. Unlike the flexible polyethylene chains which can easily coil and uncoil, the phenyl groups in polystyrene create steric hindrance, causing the chains to pack less efficiently and restricting their movement. This increased restriction in chain movement leads to a higher glass transition temperature (Tg) and makes the material more rigid and brittle compared to the more flexible polyethylene. This relates to **CO1** as the structure of the repeat unit (presence of the bulky group) dictates the polymer's properties.
