# Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order - Topic: $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$

Welcome back, everyone! In this module, we're diving deeper into the fascinating world of differential equations, specifically focusing on **homogeneous linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) of the second order**. These equations are fundamental to understanding many physical phenomena and electrical systems, from the oscillation of a spring to the transient behavior of an electrical circuit.

Our primary goal in this module is to equip you with the skills to solve these equations systematically. As stated in our Course Outcomes, particularly **CO2: Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equation with constant coefficients**, and even touching upon **CO3: Compute Laplace transform and apply it to solve ODEs arising in engineering**, we are building a toolkit for analyzing dynamic systems.

Today, we're going to tackle a very specific, yet incredibly important, type of solution for these ODEs: **solutions of the form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$**. You might be thinking, "Why this specific form?" Well, as you'll see throughout your studies, many real-world systems exhibit oscillatory behavior that can be described by sine and cosine functions, often modulated by an exponential term. Think of a damped pendulum slowly coming to rest, or the voltage across a capacitor in an RLC circuit as it settles down. The $e^{\alpha x}$ part often represents damping or growth, while $\sin(\omega x)$ captures the oscillatory nature.

## Understanding the Core: The Characteristic Equation

Before we get to our specific form, let's briefly recap where these solutions come from. For a homogeneous linear ODE of the second order with constant coefficients, the general form is:

$ay'' + by' + cy = 0$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants.

The magic happens when we assume a solution of the form $y = e^{rx}$. Substituting this into the ODE, we get:

$a(r^2e^{rx}) + b(re^{rx}) + c(e^{rx}) = 0$

Since $e^{rx}$ is never zero, we can divide it out, leaving us with the **characteristic equation** (or auxiliary equation):

$ar^2 + br + c = 0$

The roots of this quadratic equation, $r_1$ and $r_2$, dictate the form of our general solution.

## When Roots Lead to Oscillations: Complex Roots

Now, what happens when the roots of the characteristic equation are **complex**? This is precisely where our target solution form, $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$, comes into play.

Recall from algebra that a quadratic equation has complex roots when the discriminant ($b^2 - 4ac$) is negative. When the roots are complex, they appear in conjugate pairs. Let these roots be:

$r = \alpha \pm i\beta$

where $\alpha$ is the real part and $\beta$ is the imaginary part (with $\beta > 0$).

If we directly use these complex roots, we'd get solutions like $y_1 = e^{(\alpha + i\beta)x}$ and $y_2 = e^{(\alpha - i\beta)x}$. Using Euler's formula, $e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i\sin(\theta)$, we can rewrite these:

$e^{(\alpha + i\beta)x} = e^{\alpha x}e^{i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x))$
$e^{(\alpha - i\beta)x} = e^{\alpha x}e^{-i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x))$

Now, linear combinations of these two solutions are also solutions. We can form new, real-valued solutions by adding and subtracting these:

$y_{real1} = \frac{1}{2}(y_1 + y_2) = \frac{1}{2}e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x) + \cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x))$
$y_{real1} = \frac{1}{2}e^{\alpha x}(2\cos(\beta x)) = e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x)$

And

$y_{real2} = \frac{1}{2i}(y_1 - y_2) = \frac{1}{2i}e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x) - (\cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x)))$
$y_{real2} = \frac{1}{2i}e^{\alpha x}(2i\sin(\beta x)) = e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$

So, when the roots of the characteristic equation are $\alpha \pm i\beta$, the general solution to $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ is:

$y(x) = c_1e^{\alpha x}\cos(\beta x) + c_2e^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$

This can be factored as:

$y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos(\beta x) + c_2\sin(\beta x))$

This is the foundation for solutions involving sines and cosines, modulated by an exponential.

## Our Specific Focus: The Form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$

The topic $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ refers to *one particular type of term* that can appear within the general solution when roots are complex. In our general solution form $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos(\beta x) + c_2\sin(\beta x))$, if we were to choose specific values for $c_1$ and $c_2$, we could isolate such terms.

For example, if $c_1 = 0$ and $c_2 = k$ (where $k$ is a constant), we get $y(x) = ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\beta x)$. Notice that our topic uses $\omega$ for the angular frequency, so let's adapt that: if the characteristic roots are $\alpha \pm i\omega$, then terms of the form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ are part of the solution.

So, when do we encounter this specific form? It arises when the characteristic equation has **complex conjugate roots** of the form $\alpha \pm i\omega$. The value $\alpha$ dictates the exponential growth or decay, and $\omega$ dictates the frequency of the oscillation.

**Remember this:** Complex roots in the characteristic equation are the gateway to oscillatory solutions!

## Example: A Simple Harmonic Oscillator with Damping

Let's consider a physical system: a mass-spring system with a damping force. The equation of motion can be modeled by:

$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + c\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0$

where $m$ is mass, $c$ is the damping coefficient, and $k$ is the spring constant. For simplicity, let $m=1$, $c=2$, and $k=5$. The ODE becomes:

$x'' + 2x' + 5x = 0$

Let's find the characteristic equation:

$r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0$

Using the quadratic formula, $r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$:

$r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(5)}}{2(1)}$
$r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2}$
$r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2}$
$r = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2}$
$r = -1 \pm 2i$

Here, we have complex roots! Comparing this to $r = \alpha \pm i\omega$:

*   $\alpha = -1$ (the real part)
*   $\omega = 2$ (the imaginary part, the angular frequency)

So, the general solution is:

$x(t) = e^{-1t}(c_1\cos(2t) + c_2\sin(2t))$
$x(t) = e^{-t}(c_1\cos(2t) + c_2\sin(2t))$

Now, if we were interested in a specific behavior, for instance, if the initial conditions dictated that $c_1=0$ and $c_2$ was some value, say $A$, then a *particular solution term* would be $x(t) = Ae^{-t}\sin(2t)$. This is exactly the form $ke^{\alpha t}\sin(\omega t)$ we're discussing!

**Analogy:** Imagine pushing a swing.
*   If there's no air resistance or friction, it swings forever (undamped oscillation).
*   If there's a little friction, it gradually slows down and stops (damped oscillation). The $e^{\alpha t}$ term (with $\alpha < 0$) describes this slowing down.
*   The $\sin(\omega t)$ or $\cos(\omega t)$ term describes the back-and-forth motion of the swing. The $\omega$ determines how fast it swings.
*   Our form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ represents a swing that is gradually slowing down due to friction (or perhaps being driven in a way that causes a specific type of decay in its amplitude).

**Textbook Connection:** Kreyszig's "Advanced Engineering Mathematics" (Chapter 2, Section 2.3 on Differential Equations with Constant Coefficients) thoroughly covers the cases for real and complex roots, directly leading to the forms we are discussing. Bird's "Higher Engineering Mathematics" also provides excellent coverage of these topics with numerous examples relevant to engineering applications.

## Interpretation and Significance

The form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ tells us several things:

1.  **Oscillation:** The $\sin(\omega x)$ term signifies a periodic, wave-like behavior. The $\omega$ parameter is the angular frequency, determining how rapidly the oscillations occur.
2.  **Exponential Modulation:** The $e^{\alpha x}$ term acts as a multiplier, modulating the amplitude of the oscillation.
    *   If $\alpha > 0$, the amplitude grows exponentially with $x$. This is like a system that becomes increasingly unstable or is being driven to produce larger and larger oscillations.
    *   If $\alpha < 0$, the amplitude decays exponentially with $x$. This is the most common scenario in physical systems, representing damping – energy is dissipated over time, and the oscillations eventually die out.
    *   If $\alpha = 0$, the amplitude remains constant, leading to pure sinusoidal oscillations (like an ideal undamped spring).
3.  **Constant $k$:** This constant is determined by initial or boundary conditions, scaling the magnitude of this specific oscillatory component.

**In essence, $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ describes a decaying (or growing) sinusoid.**

### Example: Electrical Circuits (RLC Series Circuit)

Consider an RLC series circuit with a voltage source. The governing equation can be a second-order ODE. If the source is removed (or we analyze the natural response), the behavior of the current or voltage can be oscillatory and damped. For instance, the voltage across the capacitor in a series RLC circuit can be described by an equation whose solution involves terms like $e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega t)$, representing the voltage decaying over time while oscillating due to the interplay of the resistor, inductor, and capacitor.

The roots of the characteristic equation for such circuits ($\lambda^2 + \frac{R}{L}\lambda + \frac{1}{LC} = 0$) can be real, repeated, or complex. The complex root case leads directly to these oscillatory behaviors.

## Finding Solutions of the Form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$

To find if a solution of this specific form exists for a given ODE $ay''+by'+cy=0$, you need to:

1.  **Form the characteristic equation:** $ar^2 + br + c = 0$.
2.  **Solve for the roots:** Use the quadratic formula or factoring.
3.  **Check the nature of the roots:**
    *   If the roots are complex conjugates, say $r = \alpha \pm i\omega$, then solutions of the form $e^{\alpha x}\cos(\omega x)$ and $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ exist.
    *   The general solution will be a linear combination of these: $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos(\omega x) + c_2\sin(\omega x))$.
    *   A specific term of the form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ would be a part of this general solution if the coefficient $c_1$ happened to be zero for specific initial conditions.

**Common Pitfall:** Students sometimes get confused between the general solution and a specific *term* within the solution. The topic $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ highlights one such term. When asked for the *general solution*, you must include both cosine and sine terms with arbitrary constants $c_1$ and $c_2$.

**Exam Tip:** Problems often ask you to find the general solution when roots are complex. Be sure to state it in the form $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos(\omega x) + c_2\sin(\omega x))$. If specific initial conditions (like $y(0)$ and $y'(0)$) are given, you'll need to solve for $c_1$ and $c_2$ to find a particular solution, which might then simplify to a form like $Ae^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ if $c_1$ turns out to be zero.

## Summary of Key Concepts

*   **Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order:** $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$.
*   **Characteristic Equation:** $ar^2 + br + c = 0$.
*   **Complex Conjugate Roots:** $r = \alpha \pm i\omega$.
*   **General Solution for Complex Roots:** $y(x) = e^{\alpha x}(c_1\cos(\omega x) + c_2\sin(\omega x))$.
*   **Form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$:** Represents a single oscillatory term with exponentially decaying (or growing) amplitude. This occurs when, in the general solution, $c_1=0$ and $c_2=k$ for specific initial/boundary conditions.

## Sample Questions and Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):** What is the key characteristic of the roots of the characteristic equation that leads to solutions involving $e^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$?

**Answer:** The characteristic equation must have **complex conjugate roots** of the form $r = \alpha \pm i\omega$. The real part $\alpha$ determines the exponential factor, and the imaginary part $\omega$ determines the frequency of the oscillation.

**Question 2 (Application):** Find the general solution for the ODE $y'' - 4y' + 13y = 0$.

**Answer:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:** $r^2 - 4r + 13 = 0$.
2.  **Solve for roots:** Using the quadratic formula:
    $r = \frac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2(1)}$
    $r = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2}$
    $r = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2}$
    $r = 2 \pm 3i$
3.  **Identify $\alpha$ and $\omega$:** Here, $\alpha = 2$ and $\omega = 3$.
4.  **General Solution:** The general solution is $y(x) = e^{2x}(c_1\cos(3x) + c_2\sin(3x))$.

**Question 3 (Connecting to the specific form):** For the ODE $y'' + 4y = 0$, if the initial conditions are $y(0)=1$ and $y'(0)=0$, what is the particular solution, and does it involve the form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$?

**Answer:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:** $r^2 + 4 = 0 \implies r^2 = -4 \implies r = \pm 2i$.
2.  **Identify $\alpha$ and $\omega$:** Here, $\alpha = 0$ (since there's no $e^0$ term) and $\omega = 2$.
3.  **General Solution:** $y(x) = e^{0x}(c_1\cos(2x) + c_2\sin(2x)) = c_1\cos(2x) + c_2\sin(2x)$.
4.  **Apply initial conditions:**
    *   $y(0) = 1 \implies c_1\cos(0) + c_2\sin(0) = 1 \implies c_1(1) + c_2(0) = 1 \implies c_1 = 1$.
    *   Now we have $y(x) = \cos(2x) + c_2\sin(2x)$.
    *   Find the derivative: $y'(x) = -2\sin(2x) + 2c_2\cos(2x)$.
    *   $y'(0) = 0 \implies -2\sin(0) + 2c_2\cos(0) = 0 \implies -2(0) + 2c_2(1) = 0 \implies 2c_2 = 0 \implies c_2 = 0$.
5.  **Particular Solution:** $y(x) = \cos(2x)$.
6.  **Does it involve the form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$?** No, the particular solution here is $y(x) = 1 \cdot \cos(2x)$. It does not contain a $\sin(\omega x)$ term with a non-zero coefficient, nor does it have an $e^{\alpha x}$ term with $\alpha \neq 0$. If the initial conditions had led to $c_1=0$ and $c_2=k$, then it would have been of the form $ke^{\alpha x}\sin(\omega x)$ (with $\alpha=0$ in this case, giving $y(x) = k\sin(\omega x)$).
