## Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order - Topic: The $k \sin(\omega x)$ Term

Welcome, everyone! In this session, we're going to delve into a specific type of input forcing function that often appears in our second-order homogeneous linear ODEs: the **$k \sin(\omega x)$** term. This kind of function is incredibly common in electrical engineering, particularly when dealing with oscillating circuits like RLC circuits driven by an AC voltage source, or in mechanical systems with forced oscillations. Think of a swing being pushed at a regular interval, or the alternating current in your home. These are precisely the kinds of phenomena that can be modeled using this type of forcing function.

Our goal today is to understand how to find the **particular solution** ($y_p$) when the non-homogeneous part of our ODE takes the form $f(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$. This will be crucial for our overall understanding of solving non-homogeneous linear ODEs with constant coefficients, directly contributing to **Course Outcome 2: Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equation with constant coefficients.**

### Understanding the Structure of the Problem

Remember, we're dealing with a second-order linear ODE with constant coefficients, which generally looks like this:

$ay'' + by' + cy = f(x)$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants, and $f(x)$ is our forcing function. We've already covered how to find the **complementary solution** ($y_c$) by solving the associated homogeneous equation ($ay'' + by' + cy = 0$). The total solution to the non-homogeneous ODE is then the sum of the complementary and particular solutions:

$y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x)$

Today's focus is solely on that $y_p(x)$ when $f(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$.

### The Method of Undetermined Coefficients for $k \sin(\omega x)$

The most straightforward way to find the particular solution $y_p$ for this type of forcing function is the **Method of Undetermined Coefficients**. The core idea here, as discussed in texts like Kreyszig's "Advanced Engineering Mathematics" and Bird's "Higher Engineering Mathematics," is to make an educated guess about the form of $y_p$ based on the form of $f(x)$.

When $f(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$, our guess for $y_p$ needs to be able to produce a $\sin(\omega x)$ term when plugged into the ODE. The simplest function that can do this is a linear combination of $\sin(\omega x)$ and $\cos(\omega x)$. Why both? Because when you differentiate $\sin(\omega x)$, you get $\cos(\omega x)$, and differentiating $\cos(\omega x)$ brings you back to $\sin(\omega x)$ (with a sign change). So, to capture all possible terms that might arise from differentiation, we need to include both.

Therefore, our initial guess for $y_p$ is:

$y_p(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$

where $A$ and $B$ are constants we need to determine.

### The Crucial "Case" Analysis: Avoiding Annihilation

Now, here's a critical point, and this is where many students can get tripped up. The method of undetermined coefficients relies on the assumption that our guess for $y_p$ is *not* already part of the complementary solution $y_c$. If our guess for $y_p$ is, in fact, a solution to the homogeneous equation, then plugging it into the left-hand side of the ODE will result in zero, and we won't be able to match the forcing function $k \sin(\omega x)$.

This leads us to a three-case analysis, which is a standard procedure for this method, covered in detail in Anton's "Calculus" and also emphasized in Haykin's "Signals and Systems" when discussing system responses.

**Case 1: $\omega$ is NOT a root of the characteristic equation.**

The characteristic equation for $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ is $ar^2 + br + c = 0$. The roots of this equation are $r_1$ and $r_2$.

If neither $r_1$ nor $r_2$ is equal to $i\omega$ or $-i\omega$ (remember, for sinusoidal forcing functions, we're concerned with *complex* roots that lead to oscillatory behavior in the complementary solution), then our initial guess is perfectly fine.

Our guess: $y_p(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$

We then need to find the first and second derivatives of $y_p$:

$y_p'(x) = -A\omega \sin(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x)$
$y_p''(x) = -A\omega^2 \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 \sin(\omega x)$

Now, substitute these into the original ODE $ay'' + by' + cy = k \sin(\omega x)$:

$a(-A\omega^2 \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 \sin(\omega x)) + b(-A\omega \sin(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x)) + c(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)) = k \sin(\omega x)$

Let's group terms involving $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$:

$(\text{terms with } \cos(\omega x)) + (\text{terms with } \sin(\omega x)) = k \sin(\omega x)$

For $\cos(\omega x)$:
$-aA\omega^2 + bB\omega + cA = 0$  (This is the coefficient of $\cos(\omega x)$)

For $\sin(\omega x)$:
$-aB\omega^2 - bA\omega + cB = k$  (This is the coefficient of $\sin(\omega x)$)

This gives us a system of two linear equations in $A$ and $B$:

1. $(c - a\omega^2)A + b\omega B = 0$
2. $-b\omega A + (c - a\omega^2)B = k$

We can solve this system for $A$ and $B$. The key is that since $i\omega$ and $-i\omega$ are not roots of the characteristic equation, the determinant of the coefficient matrix, which is $(c - a\omega^2)^2 + (b\omega)^2$, will be non-zero. This guarantees a unique solution for $A$ and $B$.

**Example:** Consider the ODE $y'' + 4y = 5 \sin(2x)$.
Here, $a=1$, $b=0$, $c=4$, and $f(x) = 5 \sin(2x)$, so $\omega=2$.
The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 4 = 0$, which has roots $r = \pm 2i$.
Notice that $\omega = 2$, so $i\omega = 2i$. This means our forcing frequency $\omega$ *is* related to the natural frequency of the system. Uh oh! This falls into Case 2. Let's try a different example for Case 1.

**Revised Example for Case 1:** Consider $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 10 \sin(x)$.
Here, $a=1$, $b=2$, $c=5$, and $f(x) = 10 \sin(x)$, so $\omega=1$.
The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0$.
Using the quadratic formula, $r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(5)}}{2(1)} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$.
The roots are $r_1 = -1 + 2i$ and $r_2 = -1 - 2i$.
Since $\omega=1$, $i\omega = i$. Our roots are $-1 \pm 2i$. Neither of these is $\pm i$. So, we are in Case 1.

Our guess is $y_p(x) = A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)$.
$y_p'(x) = -A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)$
$y_p''(x) = -A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)$

Substitute into $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 10 \sin(x)$:
$(-A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)) + 2(-A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)) + 5(A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)) = 10 \sin(x)$

Group terms:
$\cos(x)(-A + 2B + 5A) + \sin(x)(-B - 2A + 5B) = 10 \sin(x)$
$\cos(x)(4A + 2B) + \sin(x)(-2A + 4B) = 10 \sin(x)$

Equating coefficients:
For $\cos(x)$: $4A + 2B = 0 \implies B = -2A$
For $\sin(x)$: $-2A + 4B = 10$

Substitute $B = -2A$ into the second equation:
$-2A + 4(-2A) = 10$
$-2A - 8A = 10$
$-10A = 10 \implies A = -1$

Then $B = -2A = -2(-1) = 2$.

So, our particular solution is $y_p(x) = - \cos(x) + 2 \sin(x)$.

**Case 2: $\omega$ IS a root of the characteristic equation (complex conjugate pair).**

This is the "resonance" case. This happens when the roots of the characteristic equation are purely imaginary, i.e., $r = \pm i\omega$. This is precisely the situation when the forcing frequency $\omega$ matches the natural frequency of the system. Think of pushing a swing at its natural rhythm – the amplitude grows.

In this scenario, the roots are $r_1 = i\omega$ and $r_2 = -i\omega$. The complementary solution will be of the form $y_c(x) = C_1 \cos(\omega x) + C_2 \sin(\omega x)$.

If we tried our initial guess $y_p(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$, every term would be annihilated when plugged into the ODE, leading to $0 = k \sin(\omega x)$, which is impossible.

So, what do we do? We multiply our guess by $x$. This is a standard technique, as found in Kreyszig and Ramana's "Higher Engineering Mathematics." Our new guess becomes:

$y_p(x) = x(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)) = Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x)$

Now, we need to find the derivatives of this new $y_p$:

$y_p'(x) = A(\cos(\omega x) - \omega x \sin(\omega x)) + B(\sin(\omega x) + \omega x \cos(\omega x))$
$y_p'(x) = (A + B\omega x) \cos(\omega x) + (B - A\omega x) \sin(\omega x)$

Now for the second derivative. This requires the product rule and chain rule carefully:

$y_p''(x) = [B\omega \cos(\omega x) - A\omega \sin(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x) - B\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x)]$  (from differentiating the first term of $y_p'$)
         $+ [B\omega \sin(\omega x) + A\omega \cos(\omega x) + B\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x)]$  (from differentiating the second term of $y_p'$)

Let's simplify $y_p''(x)$ by collecting terms:
$\cos(\omega x)$ terms: $B\omega - B\omega^2 x + A\omega + B\omega^2 x = A\omega + B\omega$
$\sin(\omega x)$ terms: $-A\omega - A\omega^2 x + B\omega + A\omega^2 x = B\omega - A\omega$

So, $y_p''(x) = (A\omega + B\omega) \cos(\omega x) + (B\omega - A\omega) \sin(\omega x)$ ... Wait, this simplification is wrong. Let's re-do the second derivative carefully.

$y_p'(x) = A \cos(\omega x) - A \omega x \sin(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x) + B \omega x \cos(\omega x)$

$y_p''(x) =$
$A(-\omega \sin(\omega x))$  (derivative of $A \cos(\omega x)$)
$- A[\omega \sin(\omega x) + \omega^2 x \cos(\omega x)]$ (derivative of $- A \omega x \sin(\omega x)$)
$+ B(\omega \cos(\omega x))$ (derivative of $B \sin(\omega x)$)
$+ B[\omega \cos(\omega x) - \omega^2 x \sin(\omega x)]$ (derivative of $B \omega x \cos(\omega x)$)

Combining terms:
$\cos(\omega x)$: $-A\omega^2 x + B\omega + B\omega = 2B\omega - A\omega^2 x$
$\sin(\omega x)$: $-A\omega - A\omega - A\omega^2 x = -2A\omega - A\omega^2 x$

Let's group them more systematically:
$y_p''(x) = (-2A\omega \sin(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x)) + (2B\omega \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x) + B\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x))$

Collecting coefficients for $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$:

$y_p''(x) = [\cos(\omega x) \times (-A\omega^2 x - B\omega^2 x + 2B\omega)] + [\sin(\omega x) \times (-2A\omega - A\omega^2 x)]$
$y_p''(x) = \cos(\omega x) (2B\omega - A\omega^2 x) + \sin(\omega x) (-2A\omega - A\omega^2 x)$ ... Still not right. Let's be methodical with the sum.

$y_p'(x) = A \cos(\omega x) - A\omega x \sin(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x) + B\omega x \cos(\omega x)$

$y_p''(x) = \frac{d}{dx} [A \cos(\omega x) - A\omega x \sin(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x) + B\omega x \cos(\omega x)]$

Derivative of $A \cos(\omega x)$: $-A\omega \sin(\omega x)$
Derivative of $-A\omega x \sin(\omega x)$: $-A\omega [\sin(\omega x) + x \cos(\omega x) \cdot \omega] = -A\omega \sin(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x)$
Derivative of $B \sin(\omega x)$: $B\omega \cos(\omega x)$
Derivative of $B\omega x \cos(\omega x)$: $B\omega [\cos(\omega x) + x (-\sin(\omega x)) \cdot \omega] = B\omega \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x)$

Summing these up:
$y_p''(x) = -A\omega \sin(\omega x) - A\omega \sin(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x) + B\omega \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x)$

Group by $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$:
$y_p''(x) = \cos(\omega x) [B\omega + B\omega - A\omega^2 x] + \sin(\omega x) [-A\omega - A\omega - B\omega^2 x]$
$y_p''(x) = \cos(\omega x) [2B\omega - A\omega^2 x] + \sin(\omega x) [-2A\omega - B\omega^2 x]$

Okay, this feels more correct. Now let's plug into $ay'' + by' + cy = k \sin(\omega x)$.
Given $r^2 + \omega^2 = 0$ as the characteristic equation (for Case 2, where roots are $\pm i\omega$), it implies $a=1, b=0, c=\omega^2$. So the ODE is $y'' + \omega^2 y = k \sin(\omega x)$.

Substitute $y_p = Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x)$ and its derivatives into $y'' + \omega^2 y = k \sin(\omega x)$.
With $a=1, b=0, c=\omega^2$:
$y_p''(x) + \omega^2 y_p(x) = k \sin(\omega x)$

Let's use the simplified derivatives for $y_p''$ when $b=0$:
$y_p'(x) = A(\cos(\omega x) - \omega x \sin(\omega x)) + B(\sin(\omega x) + \omega x \cos(\omega x))$
$y_p''(x) = \frac{d}{dx} y_p'(x)$
This is where it gets messy. Let's try to be smart.

If $r = \pm i\omega$ are roots of $ar^2+br+c=0$, then $a(i\omega)^2 + b(i\omega) + c = 0$, which means $-a\omega^2 + ib\omega + c = 0$.
This implies $c-a\omega^2 = 0$ and $b\omega = 0$. Since $\omega \neq 0$, $b$ must be $0$.
So, Case 2 occurs when $b=0$ and $c=a\omega^2$. The ODE is $ay'' + a\omega^2 y = k \sin(\omega x)$, or $y'' + \omega^2 y = \frac{k}{a} \sin(\omega x)$. Let $K = k/a$.
The ODE is $y'' + \omega^2 y = K \sin(\omega x)$.

Let's re-calculate $y_p''$ for $y_p = Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x)$:
$y_p' = A(\cos(\omega x) - \omega x \sin(\omega x)) + B(\sin(\omega x) + \omega x \cos(\omega x))$
$y_p'' = A(-\omega \sin(\omega x)) - A(\omega \sin(\omega x) + \omega^2 x \cos(\omega x)) + B(\omega \cos(\omega x)) + B(\omega \cos(\omega x) - \omega^2 x \sin(\omega x))$
$y_p'' = -2A\omega \sin(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x) + 2B\omega \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x)$

Now substitute into $y'' + \omega^2 y = K \sin(\omega x)$:
$[-2A\omega \sin(\omega x) - A\omega^2 x \cos(\omega x) + 2B\omega \cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2 x \sin(\omega x)] + \omega^2 [Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x)] = K \sin(\omega x)$

Let's group terms:
$\cos(\omega x)$: $-A\omega^2 x + 2B\omega + A\omega^2 x = 2B\omega$
$\sin(\omega x)$: $-2A\omega - B\omega^2 x + B\omega^2 x = -2A\omega$

So, $y_p'' + \omega^2 y_p = (2B\omega) \cos(\omega x) + (-2A\omega) \sin(\omega x)$.

Equating this to $K \sin(\omega x)$:
$2B\omega \cos(\omega x) - 2A\omega \sin(\omega x) = K \sin(\omega x)$

This means:
Coefficient of $\cos(\omega x)$: $2B\omega = 0 \implies B = 0$ (since $\omega \neq 0$)
Coefficient of $\sin(\omega x)$: $-2A\omega = K \implies A = -K/(2\omega)$

So, for the ODE $y'' + \omega^2 y = K \sin(\omega x)$, the particular solution is:
$y_p(x) = Ax \cos(\omega x) + Bx \sin(\omega x) = -\frac{K}{2\omega} x \cos(\omega x) + 0 \cdot x \sin(\omega x)$
$y_p(x) = -\frac{K}{2\omega} x \cos(\omega x)$

**Example:** Consider $y'' + 9y = 2 \sin(3x)$.
Here $a=1, b=0, c=9$. Forcing function is $2 \sin(3x)$, so $k=2$ and $\omega=3$.
The characteristic equation is $r^2 + 9 = 0$, so $r^2 = -9$, $r = \pm 3i$.
Since $\omega=3$ and the roots are $\pm 3i$, this is Case 2.
The ODE is $y'' + \omega^2 y = K \sin(\omega x)$ with $\omega=3$ and $K=2$.

Our guess is $y_p(x) = x(A \cos(3x) + B \sin(3x))$.
Using the formula derived above, $B=0$ and $A = -K/(2\omega) = -2/(2 \cdot 3) = -1/3$.

So, $y_p(x) = -\frac{1}{3} x \cos(3x)$.

**How to check this?**
$y_p = -\frac{1}{3} x \cos(3x)$
$y_p' = -\frac{1}{3} [\cos(3x) + x(-\sin(3x) \cdot 3)] = -\frac{1}{3} \cos(3x) + x \sin(3x)$
$y_p'' = -\frac{1}{3} (-\sin(3x) \cdot 3) + [\sin(3x) + x \cos(3x) \cdot 3]$
$y_p'' = \sin(3x) + \sin(3x) + 3x \cos(3x) = 2 \sin(3x) + 3x \cos(3x)$

Now substitute into $y'' + 9y$:
$(2 \sin(3x) + 3x \cos(3x)) + 9 (-\frac{1}{3} x \cos(3x))$
$= 2 \sin(3x) + 3x \cos(3x) - 3x \cos(3x)$
$= 2 \sin(3x)$. This matches the forcing function $k \sin(\omega x)$ with $k=2, \omega=3$. Success!

**Case 3: $\omega$ is related to a repeated real root of the characteristic equation.**

This case isn't directly applicable to $k \sin(\omega x)$ because sinusoidal functions arise from complex roots, not real roots. However, it's good to be aware of it for completeness in the Method of Undetermined Coefficients. If the roots were $r_1 = r_2 = \alpha$, then $y_c = (C_1 + C_2 x)e^{\alpha x}$. If the forcing function was $kx e^{\alpha x}$, our guess would be $x^2(A + Bx)e^{\alpha x}$.

**Important Note on Case 2:** What if the ODE is $y'' + by' + cy = k \sin(\omega x)$ and the roots of $ar^2+br+c=0$ are $r = \pm i\omega$, but $b \neq 0$? This scenario implies that $b\omega \neq 0$, contradicting the condition for roots being purely imaginary $ib\omega$. So, Case 2, as described above, specifically happens when $b=0$ and $c = a\omega^2$.

However, it's also possible to have complex roots $r = \alpha \pm i\beta$ where $\beta = \omega$, but $\alpha \neq 0$. In this situation, $y_c$ involves terms like $e^{\alpha x} \cos(\omega x)$ and $e^{\alpha x} \sin(\omega x)$. If our forcing function were $k x \sin(\omega x)$, we'd need a modified guess. But for just $k \sin(\omega x)$, if the roots are $\alpha \pm i\omega$ with $\alpha \neq 0$, our initial guess $y_p = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$ is still valid (Case 1).

**The key distinction for Case 2 with $\sin(\omega x)$ is when the roots are *purely imaginary* and equal to $\pm i\omega$.**

### What About $k \cos(\omega x)$?

If the forcing function is $f(x) = k \cos(\omega x)$, the process is identical. Our guess for $y_p$ would still be $y_p(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$ (or $x$ times this in Case 2). When we differentiate and substitute, we'll end up with a system of equations similar to before, but the right-hand side will have a $\cos(\omega x)$ term.

Equating coefficients:
$y_p''(x) + \omega^2 y_p(x) = k \cos(\omega x)$
$(2B\omega) \cos(\omega x) + (-2A\omega) \sin(\omega x) = k \cos(\omega x)$

$2B\omega = k \implies B = k/(2\omega)$
$-2A\omega = 0 \implies A = 0$

So, for $y'' + \omega^2 y = k \cos(\omega x)$, the particular solution is $y_p(x) = \frac{k}{2\omega} x \sin(\omega x)$.

### Superposition Principle: What if $f(x) = k_1 \sin(\omega_1 x) + k_2 \cos(\omega_2 x)$?

The beauty of linear ODEs is the **Superposition Principle**. If $f(x)$ is a sum of multiple forcing functions, we can find the particular solution for each component separately and then add them up.

For example, if $y'' + 4y = 3 \sin(2x) + 5 \cos(x)$:
1. Solve for $y_p$ for $y'' + 4y = 3 \sin(2x)$. Here $\omega=2$. Characteristic equation $r^2+4=0 \implies r = \pm 2i$. This is Case 2 with $\omega=2$. ODE form is $y'' + \omega^2 y = K \sin(\omega x)$, with $K=3$.
   $y_{p1}(x) = -\frac{K}{2\omega} x \cos(\omega x) = -\frac{3}{2 \cdot 2} x \cos(2x) = -\frac{3}{4} x \cos(2x)$.

2. Solve for $y_p$ for $y'' + 4y = 5 \cos(x)$. Here $\omega=1$. Characteristic equation $r^2+4=0 \implies r = \pm 2i$. This is Case 1, as $\omega=1$ is not related to $\pm 2i$. ODE form is $y'' + \omega^2 y = K \cos(\omega x)$, with $K=5$.
   Guess: $y_{p2}(x) = A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)$.
   $y_{p2}' = -A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)$
   $y_{p2}'' = -A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)$
   $(-A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)) + 4(A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)) = 5 \cos(x)$
   $\cos(x)(-A+4A) + \sin(x)(-B+4B) = 5 \cos(x)$
   $3A \cos(x) + 3B \sin(x) = 5 \cos(x)$
   $3A = 5 \implies A = 5/3$.
   $3B = 0 \implies B = 0$.
   So, $y_{p2}(x) = \frac{5}{3} \cos(x)$.

3. The total particular solution is $y_p(x) = y_{p1}(x) + y_{p2}(x) = -\frac{3}{4} x \cos(2x) + \frac{5}{3} \cos(x)$.

This ability to combine solutions for different forcing functions is a cornerstone of linear systems, directly relating to **Course Outcome 2** and the general understanding of how systems respond to various inputs.

Understanding both methods deepens your grasp on solving ODEs in engineering contexts.

### Exam Tips and Common Pitfalls

1.  **Always check the characteristic equation roots FIRST.** Before even writing down your guess for $y_p$, find the roots of the auxiliary equation. This determines which case you are in.
2.  **Be meticulous with derivatives.** Especially in Case 2, the derivatives of $x \cos(\omega x)$ and $x \sin(\omega x)$ are lengthy. Double-check your calculations. A small error here propagates.
3.  **Don't forget the $\cos(\omega x)$ term in your guess.** Even if the forcing function is purely $\sin(\omega x)$, you need to include $A \cos(\omega x)$ in your guess to cover all possibilities arising from differentiation, unless you are in Case 2 where $B$ often turns out to be zero.
4.  **The multiplier $x$ in Case 2 is essential.** If the roots are $\pm i\omega$ and you forget to multiply by $x$, you'll get $0=k \sin(\omega x)$ and be stuck.
5.  **Pay attention to the constant $a$ in $ay'' + by' + cy$.** If $a \neq 1$, remember to divide the entire ODE by $a$ to get it into the standard form $y'' + (b/a)y' + (c/a)y = f(x)/a$ before analyzing roots or applying formulas. The $K$ in our derived formulas is often $f(x)/a$.

### Summary of the Method for $k \sin(\omega x)$

Given $ay'' + by' + cy = k \sin(\omega x)$:

1.  **Find the roots of the characteristic equation:** $ar^2 + br + c = 0$. Let them be $r_1, r_2$.
2.  **Determine the case:**
    *   **Case 1:** If neither $r_1$ nor $r_2$ is $\pm i\omega$.
        *   Guess: $y_p(x) = A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x)$.
        *   Calculate $y_p'$ and $y_p''$, substitute into the ODE, and equate coefficients of $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ to find $A$ and $B$.
    *   **Case 2:** If $r_1 = i\omega$ and $r_2 = -i\omega$. (This implies $b=0$ and $c=a\omega^2$).
        *   Guess: $y_p(x) = x(A \cos(\omega x) + B \sin(\omega x))$.
        *   Calculate $y_p'$ and $y_p''$, substitute into the ODE, and equate coefficients of $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ to find $A$ and $B$. (Often $B=0$ if $b=0$ from the start). For the specific form $y''+\omega^2 y = K \sin(\omega x)$, we found $y_p(x) = -\frac{K}{2\omega} x \cos(\omega x)$.

### Sample Questions and Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
Why do we include both $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ in our guess for the particular solution when the forcing function is $k \sin(\omega x)$?

**Answer:**
We include both $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ because differentiation of $\sin(\omega x)$ yields a $\cos(\omega x)$ term, and differentiation of $\cos(\omega x)$ yields a $\sin(\omega x)$ term. For the left-hand side of the ODE ($ay'' + by' + cy$) to be able to match the $\sin(\omega x)$ forcing function, it must be able to produce both sine and cosine terms through differentiation. If we only guessed $A \sin(\omega x)$, we could never generate a $\cos(\omega x)$ term to cancel out any $\cos(\omega x)$ that might arise from the derivatives of $y_p'$.

**Question 2 (Exam-Oriented - Case 1):**
Find the particular solution for the ODE $y'' - y' - 2y = 3 \sin(x)$.

**Answer:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:** $r^2 - r - 2 = 0 \implies (r-2)(r+1) = 0$. Roots are $r_1 = 2, r_2 = -1$.
2.  **Forcing Function:** $f(x) = 3 \sin(x)$. So, $\omega = 1$.
3.  **Case Analysis:** The roots are $2$ and $-1$. Neither root is $\pm i(1)$. Therefore, this is Case 1.
4.  **Guess for $y_p$:** $y_p(x) = A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)$.
5.  **Derivatives:**
    $y_p'(x) = -A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)$
    $y_p''(x) = -A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)$
6.  **Substitution into ODE:**
    $(-A \cos(x) - B \sin(x)) - (-A \sin(x) + B \cos(x)) - 2(A \cos(x) + B \sin(x)) = 3 \sin(x)$
7.  **Group Coefficients:**
    $\cos(x)[-A - B - 2A] + \sin(x)[-B + A - 2B] = 3 \sin(x)$
    $\cos(x)[-3A - B] + \sin(x)[A - 3B] = 3 \sin(x)$
8.  **Equate Coefficients:**
    For $\cos(x)$: $-3A - B = 0 \implies B = -3A$
    For $\sin(x)$: $A - 3B = 3$
9.  **Solve for A and B:** Substitute $B = -3A$ into the second equation:
    $A - 3(-3A) = 3$
    $A + 9A = 3$
    $10A = 3 \implies A = 3/10$
    Then $B = -3A = -3(3/10) = -9/10$.
10. **Particular Solution:** $y_p(x) = \frac{3}{10} \cos(x) - \frac{9}{10} \sin(x)$.

**Question 3 (Exam-Oriented - Case 2):**
Find the particular solution for the ODE $y'' + 16y = 5 \cos(4x)$.

**Answer:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:** $r^2 + 16 = 0 \implies r^2 = -16 \implies r = \pm 4i$.
2.  **Forcing Function:** $f(x) = 5 \cos(4x)$. So, $\omega = 4$.
3.  **Case Analysis:** The roots are $\pm 4i$. Since $\omega=4$ matches the imaginary part of the roots, this is Case 2. The ODE is of the form $y'' + \omega^2 y = K \cos(\omega x)$ with $K=5, \omega=4$.
4.  **Guess for $y_p$:** Based on the analysis for $k \cos(\omega x)$ in Case 2 ($y''+\omega^2 y = K \cos(\omega x)$), our guess is $y_p(x) = Ax \sin(\omega x)$. (We found $A=K/(2\omega)$ and $B=0$).
    So, $y_p(x) = Ax \sin(4x)$.
5.  **Derivatives:**
    $y_p'(x) = A(\sin(4x) + x \cos(4x) \cdot 4) = A \sin(4x) + 4Ax \cos(4x)$
    $y_p''(x) = A(4 \cos(4x)) + [4A \cos(4x) + 4Ax (-\sin(4x) \cdot 4)]$
    $y_p''(x) = 4A \cos(4x) + 4A \cos(4x) - 16Ax \sin(4x)$
    $y_p''(x) = 8A \cos(4x) - 16Ax \sin(4x)$
6.  **Substitution into ODE:**
    $(8A \cos(4x) - 16Ax \sin(4x)) + 16(Ax \sin(4x)) = 5 \cos(4x)$
7.  **Simplify:**
    $8A \cos(4x) - 16Ax \sin(4x) + 16Ax \sin(4x) = 5 \cos(4x)$
    $8A \cos(4x) = 5 \cos(4x)$
8.  **Equate Coefficients:**
    $8A = 5 \implies A = 5/8$.
9.  **Particular Solution:** $y_p(x) = \frac{5}{8} x \sin(4x)$.

Keep practicing these steps, and you'll become very proficient at handling sinusoidal forcing functions in your ODEs!
