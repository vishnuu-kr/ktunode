## Module 2: Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order - Topic: $kx^n$

Welcome, everyone! In this module, we’re delving into the fascinating world of second-order homogeneous linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with constant coefficients. These equations are the bedrock for understanding many phenomena in electrical science and physical science – think about how current oscillates in a circuit, or how a mass on a spring moves. They describe systems that, when disturbed from their equilibrium, tend to return to it in a predictable, often oscillatory, manner.

Today, we're going to focus on a specific type of homogeneous linear ODE of second order, but before we dive into the "kx^n" part, let's quickly recap what we mean by these terms.

*   **Second-Order ODE:** This means the highest derivative in the equation is the second derivative. We’ll be looking at equations of the form $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, where $y$ is our unknown function (often representing displacement, voltage, or charge), $y'$ is its first derivative, and $y''$ is its second derivative. The coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants.
*   **Homogeneous:** The "0" on the right-hand side is crucial. It means there's no external forcing function or driving term. The system's behavior is solely determined by its internal dynamics.
*   **Linear:** The dependent variable $y$ and its derivatives ($y'$, $y''$) appear only to the first power and are not multiplied together. This linearity is what allows us to use powerful techniques like superposition.

Our goal in this section is to understand how to find the general solution for equations of the form $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$. This is directly related to **Course Outcome 2 (CO2): Solve homogeneous and non-homogeneous linear differential equations with constant coefficients.** By mastering the homogeneous case, we lay the groundwork for tackling non-homogeneous ones later.

### The Characteristic Equation: The Key to Unlocking Solutions

So, how do we find solutions for $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$? A clever technique, often introduced in texts like Kreyszig's "Advanced Engineering Mathematics," involves assuming a solution of the form $y = e^{\lambda x}$. Why this form? Well, think about exponential functions. Their derivatives are just scaled versions of themselves. This property makes them ideal candidates for equations where derivatives are linearly combined.

Let's try it:
If $y = e^{\lambda x}$, then:
$y' = \lambda e^{\lambda x}$
$y'' = \lambda^2 e^{\lambda x}$

Now, substitute these into our general ODE:
$a(\lambda^2 e^{\lambda x}) + b(\lambda e^{\lambda x}) + c(e^{\lambda x}) = 0$

Notice that $e^{\lambda x}$ is a common factor. Since $e^{\lambda x}$ is never zero, we can divide it out:
$a\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c = 0$

This is it! This beautiful quadratic equation is called the **characteristic equation** (or auxiliary equation). The roots of this equation, $\lambda$, will tell us everything we need to know about the form of our solutions $y(x)$. This is a fundamental concept, and understanding it is key to solving these ODEs.

### Cases of Roots and Their Implications for Solutions

The nature of the roots of the characteristic equation dictates the form of the general solution. Let's consider the discriminant, $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$.

#### Case 1: Distinct Real Roots ($\Delta > 0$)

If $b^2 - 4ac > 0$, the characteristic equation has two distinct real roots, say $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$.
In this case, we have two independent solutions: $y_1 = e^{\lambda_1 x}$ and $y_2 = e^{\lambda_2 x}$.

Because our ODE is linear and homogeneous, the **principle of superposition** (a concept also important in linear algebra, relating to CO1) tells us that any linear combination of these solutions is also a solution. Therefore, the **general solution** is:

$y(x) = C_1 e^{\lambda_1 x} + C_2 e^{\lambda_2 x}$

where $C_1$ and $C_2$ are arbitrary constants.

**Analogy:** Imagine you have two independent ways to escape a maze, each with a specific speed of exit. The general way to escape is a combination of taking either path, with you deciding how much time you spend on each path.

**Example:** Consider the ODE $y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0$.
The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 2 = 0$.
Factoring this, we get $(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 2) = 0$.
The roots are $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.
These are distinct real roots.
So, the general solution is $y(x) = C_1 e^x + C_2 e^{2x}$.

**Exam Tip:** Always check the discriminant first. If it's positive, you know you're in this distinct real roots case.

#### Case 2: Repeated Real Roots ($\Delta = 0$)

If $b^2 - 4ac = 0$, the characteristic equation has one real root of multiplicity two, say $\lambda_0$. This means $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda_0$.
If we naively applied our previous formula, we'd get $y(x) = C_1 e^{\lambda_0 x} + C_2 e^{\lambda_0 x} = (C_1 + C_2)e^{\lambda_0 x} = C e^{\lambda_0 x}$. This only gives us one independent solution! We need two independent solutions to form the general solution.

This is where a bit of ingenuity comes in, a technique often attributed to the mathematician d'Alembert and discussed in detail in calculus texts like Anton, Bivens, Davis. If $e^{\lambda_0 x}$ is a solution, we try a solution of the form $y(x) = v(x) e^{\lambda_0 x}$, where $v(x)$ is some function of $x$.
If we substitute this into $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, after some algebraic manipulation (which you can follow in your textbooks), we find that $v(x)$ must be a linear function, specifically $v(x) = C_1 + C_2 x$.

So, when we have repeated real roots $\lambda_0$, the two linearly independent solutions are $y_1 = e^{\lambda_0 x}$ and $y_2 = x e^{\lambda_0 x}$.
The **general solution** is:

$y(x) = C_1 e^{\lambda_0 x} + C_2 x e^{\lambda_0 x} = (C_1 + C_2 x) e^{\lambda_0 x}$

**Analogy:** Imagine trying to escape a maze, but there's only one path. If you go down it, you might get stuck. But if you go down it and realize you can always choose to walk a little further or a little less each time you pass a certain point (represented by the 'x' multiplier), you can explore all possibilities along that single path.

**Example:** Consider the ODE $y'' - 4y' + 4y = 0$.
The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 4 = 0$.
This factors as $(\lambda - 2)^2 = 0$.
So, we have a repeated real root $\lambda_0 = 2$.
The general solution is $y(x) = (C_1 + C_2 x) e^{2x}$.

**Exam Tip:** If you see a perfect square in the characteristic equation, you're in this case. Don't forget the crucial '$x$' multiplying one of the exponential terms.

#### Case 3: Complex Conjugate Roots ($\Delta < 0$)

If $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, the characteristic equation has two complex conjugate roots. Let them be $\lambda = \alpha \pm i\beta$, where $\alpha = -b/(2a)$ and $\beta = \sqrt{4ac - b^2}/(2a)$.

Using Euler's formula, $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, we can derive real-valued solutions from the complex exponential solutions.
We have $e^{(\alpha + i\beta)x} = e^{\alpha x} e^{i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) + i\sin(\beta x))$
and $e^{(\alpha - i\beta)x} = e^{\alpha x} e^{-i\beta x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos(\beta x) - i\sin(\beta x))$.

By taking linear combinations of these two complex solutions, we can obtain two real, linearly independent solutions:
$y_1 = e^{\alpha x} \cos(\beta x)$
$y_2 = e^{\alpha x} \sin(\beta x)$

The **general solution** is then:

$y(x) = C_1 e^{\alpha x} \cos(\beta x) + C_2 e^{\alpha x} \sin(\beta x)$
$y(x) = e^{\alpha x} (C_1 \cos(\beta x) + C_2 \sin(\beta x))$

**Analogy:** Imagine a pendulum. When it swings, its motion can be described by a combination of cosine and sine waves. The $e^{\alpha x}$ term acts as a damping or growth factor on this oscillation. If $\alpha > 0$, the oscillations grow; if $\alpha < 0$, they decay (like a damped spring).

**Example:** Consider the ODE $y'' + 4y = 0$.
The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 + 4 = 0$.
This gives $\lambda^2 = -4$, so $\lambda = \pm \sqrt{-4} = \pm 2i$.
Here, $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 2$.
The general solution is $y(x) = e^{0x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x)) = C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x)$.
This describes pure oscillation, as expected for a system with no damping.

**Example 2:** Consider $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$.
The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 5 = 0$.
Using the quadratic formula, $\lambda = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(5)}}{2(1)} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$.
Here, $\alpha = -1$ and $\beta = 2$.
The general solution is $y(x) = e^{-x}(C_1 \cos(2x) + C_2 \sin(2x))$. This describes damped oscillations.

**Exam Tip:** If you encounter complex roots, remember to convert them into the $e^{\alpha x}(C_1 \cos(\beta x) + C_2 \sin(\beta x))$ form. The real part ($\alpha$) goes with the exponential, and the imaginary part ($\beta$) goes with the sine and cosine.

### The "kx^n" Aspect - Where Does It Fit?

You might be wondering, "Professor, where is the $kx^n$ in all of this?" The topic title, "$kx^n$", might seem a bit disconnected from $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$. This is a common point of confusion!

The "$kx^n$" form doesn't directly refer to the *homogeneous* linear ODE of second order itself, but rather to a **specific type of non-homogeneous term** that we would encounter if we were dealing with equations like $ay'' + by' + cy = kx^n$. However, in the context of *homogeneous* equations, this notation might be a slight misdirection or perhaps an indication of how initial conditions or boundary conditions might be expressed in some problems, or it might hint at **Euler-Cauchy equations** which are *not* of constant coefficients, but have terms like $x^2 y''$, $xy'$, etc.

For *this specific topic within the module focused on Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order with CONSTANT COEFFICIENTS*, we are primarily concerned with the structure $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$. The $kx^n$ notation *might* appear in some problem statements as part of an initial or boundary condition (e.g., $y(0) = k \cdot 0^n = 0$ for $n>0$) or it could be a precursor to a more general class of ODEs (like Euler-Cauchy) that are not covered in this *specific* section on *constant coefficients*.

**Crucially, for the scope of solving $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$, you do NOT substitute $y = kx^n$ into the equation.** That is a method used for a different class of ODEs (Euler-Cauchy, where coefficients are functions of $x$, like $x^2 y''$).

**So, for this section, focus on the $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ form and the characteristic equation method.** The "$kx^n$" might be:
1.  A poorly phrased topic title that doesn't accurately reflect the content of solving constant-coefficient homogeneous ODEs.
2.  An indicator of functions that might *arise* in related problems (like non-homogeneous parts or specific boundary conditions) which are not the core of this particular learning objective.
3.  A hint that future topics might explore ODEs with variable coefficients, where $kx^n$ *is* relevant as a potential solution form for specific types of equations.

**For the learning outcomes listed (CO1, CO2, CO3, CO4) and the focus on Homogeneous Linear ODEs of Second Order with CONSTANT COEFFICIENTS, the characteristic equation method is your primary tool.**

### Summary of Key Points

*   Homogeneous linear ODEs with constant coefficients are of the form $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$.
*   Solutions are found by solving the **characteristic equation**: $a\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c = 0$.
*   **Three cases** arise based on the roots of the characteristic equation:
    1.  **Distinct Real Roots ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2$)**: $y(x) = C_1 e^{\lambda_1 x} + C_2 e^{\lambda_2 x}$
    2.  **Repeated Real Roots ($\lambda_0$)**: $y(x) = (C_1 + C_2 x) e^{\lambda_0 x}$
    3.  **Complex Conjugate Roots ($\alpha \pm i\beta$)**: $y(x) = e^{\alpha x} (C_1 \cos(\beta x) + C_2 \sin(\beta x))$

Remember these forms well. They are fundamental to understanding the behavior of many physical and electrical systems. The method is systematic, and once you master the characteristic equation, you've conquered a huge chunk of second-order ODEs!

### Sample Questions with Answers

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
What is the role of the characteristic equation in solving homogeneous linear ODEs of second order with constant coefficients?

**Answer:**
The characteristic equation ($a\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c = 0$) is derived by assuming a solution of the form $y = e^{\lambda x}$. The roots of this quadratic equation, $\lambda$, directly determine the form of the general solution for the ODE. Each type of root (distinct real, repeated real, complex conjugate) corresponds to a specific functional form (exponential, exponential with polynomial multiplier, or damped/growing sinusoids) that describes the system's behavior. It's the central tool for finding the solution.

**Question 2 (Exam-Oriented - Case 1):**
Find the general solution of the differential equation $y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0$.

**Answer:**
This is a homogeneous linear ODE of second order with constant coefficients.
1.  **Characteristic Equation:** Assume $y = e^{\lambda x}$. Substituting into the ODE gives the characteristic equation:
    $\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6 = 0$
2.  **Solve for Roots:** Factor the quadratic equation:
    $(\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 3) = 0$
    The roots are $\lambda_1 = 2$ and $\lambda_2 = 3$.
3.  **Identify Case:** These are distinct real roots.
4.  **General Solution:** For distinct real roots $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, the general solution is $y(x) = C_1 e^{\lambda_1 x} + C_2 e^{\lambda_2 x}$.
    Therefore, the general solution is $y(x) = C_1 e^{2x} + C_2 e^{3x}$.

**Question 3 (Exam-Oriented - Case 2):**
Solve the differential equation $y'' + 6y' + 9y = 0$.

**Answer:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:**
    $\lambda^2 + 6\lambda + 9 = 0$
2.  **Solve for Roots:** This is a perfect square:
    $(\lambda + 3)^2 = 0$
    The root is $\lambda_0 = -3$ (a repeated real root).
3.  **Identify Case:** Repeated real root.
4.  **General Solution:** For a repeated real root $\lambda_0$, the general solution is $y(x) = (C_1 + C_2 x) e^{\lambda_0 x}$.
    Therefore, the general solution is $y(x) = (C_1 + C_2 x) e^{-3x}$.

**Question 4 (Exam-Oriented - Case 3):**
Find the general solution for $y'' + 2y' + 10y = 0$.

**Answer:**
1.  **Characteristic Equation:**
    $\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 10 = 0$
2.  **Solve for Roots:** Using the quadratic formula:
    $\lambda = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(1)(10)}}{2(1)} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 40}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm 6i}{2} = -1 \pm 3i$
3.  **Identify Case:** Complex conjugate roots, where $\alpha = -1$ and $\beta = 3$.
4.  **General Solution:** For complex conjugate roots $\alpha \pm i\beta$, the general solution is $y(x) = e^{\alpha x} (C_1 \cos(\beta x) + C_2 \sin(\beta x))$.
    Therefore, the general solution is $y(x) = e^{-x} (C_1 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x))$.

**Question 5 (Conceptual/Connection to CO2):**
How do the different types of solutions for homogeneous linear ODEs (exponential, oscillatory) relate to the behavior of physical systems, and why is this important for CO2?

**Answer:**
The different types of solutions directly model the behavior of physical systems.
*   **Distinct Real Roots:** Often represent systems that decay to equilibrium without oscillation, or grow unbounded if the roots are positive. Think of a critically damped shock absorber or an unstable electronic circuit.
*   **Repeated Real Roots:** Represent a transition between oscillatory and non-oscillatory behavior, often indicating critical damping where the system returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible without overshooting.
*   **Complex Conjugate Roots:** Represent oscillatory behavior. The real part ($\alpha$) of the roots determines if the oscillations are damped ($\alpha < 0$, like a pendulum with air resistance), sustained ($\alpha = 0$, ideal harmonic oscillator), or growing ($\alpha > 0$, like feedback in an amplifier causing oscillation).

This understanding is crucial for CO2 because it allows us to predict and analyze the dynamic response of systems described by these ODEs. Knowing the solution form tells us whether a system will stabilize, oscillate, or become unstable, which is fundamental in electrical and physical science engineering.
