# Product Design and Innovation - Module 3: Design for Manufacturing and Assembly

## Topic: Value Engineering - Definition

---

### **Introduction**

This section introduces the concept of **Value Engineering (VE)**, a systematic, organized approach to providing the necessary functions in a project at the lowest possible cost without sacrificing quality, performance, or reliability. It is a crucial methodology within Design for Manufacturing and Assembly (DFMA) that aims to optimize the value of a product or service. Understanding VE is essential for achieving product success by ensuring that the final product is both functional and cost-effective to produce and assemble.

---

### **1. Core Concepts and Definitions**

**Value Engineering (VE)**:

*   **Definition:** VE is a systematic and creative approach to improving the value of products, systems, or services. It focuses on identifying and eliminating unnecessary costs by analyzing the **function** of a product and achieving that function at the lowest possible cost, without compromising essential performance, quality, reliability, or safety.
*   **Key Principle:** Value is defined as the ratio of **function** to **cost**.
    *   **Value = Function / Cost**
*   **Goal:** To increase value by:
    *   Improving the function for the same cost.
    *   Reducing the cost for the same function.
    *   Improving the function and reducing the cost simultaneously.
*   **Origin:** VE was developed during World War II by Lawrence D. Miles at General Electric.
*   **Distinction from Cost Reduction:** While VE can lead to cost reduction, its primary focus is on improving the overall *value* by understanding and optimizing functions. Simple cost reduction might cut corners, compromising quality, whereas VE ensures essential functions are maintained or enhanced.

**Function:**

*   **Definition:** A function is the action or purpose a product or its components perform. Functions are typically described by a verb and a measurable noun (e.g., "supports weight," "conducts electricity," "cuts material").
*   **Types of Functions:**
    *   **Primary Functions:** Essential functions that the product must perform for its intended use.
    *   **Secondary Functions:** Functions that are not essential to the product's primary purpose but might enhance its usability or aesthetic appeal.
    *   **Basic Functions:** The fundamental reason for a product's existence.
    *   **Esteem Functions:** Functions related to appearance, style, or perceived quality that contribute to customer satisfaction but are not essential for the basic operation.

**Cost:**

*   **Definition:** The total cost associated with a product, including design, materials, manufacturing, assembly, distribution, and potentially end-of-life disposal.

### **3. Key Concepts and Definitions (In-depth)**

**The VE Process (Overview for foundational understanding):**

While the full VE process involves distinct phases (Information, Function Analysis, Creative, Evaluation, Development, Presentation), understanding the **definition** means grasping its underlying philosophy and purpose.

*   **Focus on Function:** The central tenet of VE is the rigorous analysis of functions. By asking "What does this part/product do?", and "Is there a cheaper way to achieve this function?", VE drives innovation.
*   **Systematic Approach:** VE is not haphazard. It follows a structured methodology to ensure all aspects are considered.
*   **Team-Based:** Effective VE is often conducted by a multidisciplinary team, bringing diverse perspectives to identify opportunities.
*   **Creativity and Innovation:** VE encourages creative problem-solving to find alternative ways to achieve functions.

**Examples Where Relevant (Illustrative):**

*   **Automotive Industry:**
    *   **Original Design:** A car seat might have a complex metal frame requiring welding and multiple fasteners.
    *   **VE Application:** A VE study might question the need for such a robust frame if a lighter, injection-molded plastic component can perform the same "support passenger" function at a lower cost and with simpler assembly. The focus is on the *function* of supporting the passenger, not necessarily the material or complexity of the original design.
*   **Consumer Electronics:**
    *   **Original Design:** A product enclosure might have numerous small screws to hold it together.
    *   **VE Application:** VE could identify that a snap-fit or clip mechanism can achieve the "secure enclosure" function, eliminating the need for screws, reducing assembly time and parts.
*   **Packaging:**
    *   **Original Design:** A product might be over-packaged with multiple layers of protective material.
    *   **VE Application:** VE could analyze the "protect product during transit" function and determine if a simpler, less material-intensive packaging design can achieve the same level of protection, thereby reducing material cost and waste.

### **4. Textual References and Content Integration**

**From Ulrich & Eppinger (Product Design & Development, 2003):**

*   While Ulrich & Eppinger's primary focus might be on the broader product development process, the principles of **Design for Manufacturing (DFM)** and **Design for Assembly (DFA)** inherently overlap with VE. The book emphasizes reducing manufacturing and assembly costs by simplifying product design.
*   **Relevance to VE Definition:** The book's discussions on designing for ease of manufacturing (e.g., using standard components, minimizing part count, designing for automated assembly) are directly aligned with the VE goal of achieving functions at lower costs. Understanding the impact of design choices on production efficiency, as discussed by Ulrich & Eppinger, provides context for *why* VE is important and *how* it can be applied through DFMA principles. For instance, their discussions on reducing part count directly contribute to VE's objective of lowering cost by simplifying assembly and material usage.

**From Otto & Wood (Product Design: “Techniques in Reverse Engineering and new Product Development.”, 2000):**

*   Otto & Wood's work often delves into detailed techniques for product redesign and improvement. They discuss methodologies that are closely related to VE, particularly in how to analyze existing products to identify areas for cost reduction and functional improvement.
*   **Relevance to VE Definition:** Their focus on **reverse engineering** (disassembling and analyzing existing products) is a foundational step in many VE studies. By understanding how a product is currently made and how its functions are achieved, teams can then apply VE principles to find better ways. The book's emphasis on identifying and eliminating unnecessary features or complexity in existing designs directly mirrors the core aim of VE.

### **5. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Value = Function / Cost:** This is the fundamental equation of Value Engineering.
*   **Focus is on Function:** Never lose sight of what the product or component *does*.
*   **Systematic & Creative:** VE requires both disciplined analysis and innovative thinking.
*   **Multidisciplinary Effort:** The best results come from diverse team inputs.
*   **Not Just Cost Cutting:** VE is about optimizing value, which can include enhancing function.
*   **DFMA Alignment:** VE is a powerful tool within the broader DFMA framework.

### **6. Practice Questions or Exercises**

**Question 1:**

Define Value Engineering (VE) in your own words, using the fundamental formula that describes value.

**Answer 1:**
Value Engineering (VE) is a systematic and creative methodology focused on improving the value of a product, system, or service. It achieves this by thoroughly analyzing the functions performed by the product and identifying ways to achieve those functions at the lowest possible cost, without compromising quality, performance, or reliability. The fundamental formula is: **Value = Function / Cost**. This means VE aims to increase value by improving function, reducing cost, or both.

**Question 2:**

Imagine you are tasked with designing a simple chair. What would be the primary function of the chair? How might a Value Engineering approach challenge the initial design to improve its value? (Focus on the definition aspect of VE).

**Answer 2:**
The primary function of a chair is to **"support a person in a seated position."**

A Value Engineering approach would challenge the initial design by asking:
*   What are the essential components needed to perform this "support" function?
*   Are there any parts or design features that do not directly contribute to this primary function (secondary or esteem functions) and could be eliminated or simplified?
*   Are there alternative materials or manufacturing processes that can achieve the same "support" function at a lower cost?
*   Could the number of parts be reduced to simplify assembly and material requirements?

For example, an initial design might involve a complex wooden frame. VE might explore using a single molded plastic shell, metal tubing with simpler joinery, or even a design that uses fewer fasteners, all while ensuring the primary function of supporting a person is met, but at a potentially lower cost and with easier manufacturing/assembly. The focus remains on the *function* and its cost-effectiveness.

**Question 3:**

According to the definition of Value Engineering, what are the two primary ways to increase the value of a product?

**Answer 3:**
The two primary ways to increase the value of a product, according to the definition of Value Engineering, are:
1.  **Improving the function for the same cost.**
2.  **Reducing the cost for the same function.**
(A third, related way is to improve the function while simultaneously reducing the cost).
