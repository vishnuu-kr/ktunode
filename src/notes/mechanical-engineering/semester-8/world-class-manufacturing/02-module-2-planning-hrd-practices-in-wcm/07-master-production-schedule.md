### 1. Introduction to Master Production Schedule (MPS)

The Master Production Schedule (MPS) is a fundamental element in manufacturing planning and control. It translates the sales and operations plan (S&OP) into a detailed production plan for specific products over a defined period. In the context of World Class Manufacturing (WCM), a well-executed MPS is crucial for achieving operational efficiency, responsiveness, and customer satisfaction.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Master Production Schedule (MPS):** A statement of what products will be produced, how many units, and when. It is a crucial input for Material Requirements Planning (MRP).
*   **Time Horizon:** The period for which the MPS is developed, typically covering several weeks or months.
*   **Time Buckets:** The MPS is usually broken down into discrete time periods (e.g., daily, weekly).
*   **Forecast:** An estimate of future demand for a product.
*   **Order Promising:** The process of committing to delivery dates for customer orders based on the MPS and available inventory.
*   **Available-to-Promise (ATP):** The quantity of a product that can be promised to customers, considering current inventory, scheduled receipts, and the MPS.

### 2. The Role of MPS in the Planning Hierarchy

The MPS sits at a critical juncture in the manufacturing planning hierarchy, bridging strategic decisions with operational execution.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP):** A cross-functional process to balance demand and supply at a aggregate level, typically over a medium-term horizon.
*   **Production Plan:** The aggregate plan for production levels over a longer period, usually derived from S&OP.
*   **Material Requirements Planning (MRP):** A system that calculates the materials and components needed to produce the finished goods specified in the MPS.
*   **Capacity Requirements Planning (CRP):** A process to determine if sufficient production capacity exists to execute the MPS.

**The Planning Hierarchy (Simplified):**

1.  **Strategic Planning:** Long-term goals, market positioning, competitive strategy.
2.  **Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP):** Aggregate demand and supply balancing, monthly.
3.  **Master Production Schedule (MPS):** Specific product-level production plan, weekly/daily.
4.  **Material Requirements Planning (MRP):** Component and raw material needs.
5.  **Shop Floor Control:** Day-to-day execution of production activities.

**Example:**

A furniture manufacturer uses S&OP to determine that, in the next quarter, they need to produce 500 dining tables and 300 coffee tables. The MPS then breaks this down weekly, specifying:

*   Week 1: 50 dining tables, 20 coffee tables
*   Week 2: 60 dining tables, 25 coffee tables
*   ...and so on.

This MPS then feeds into MRP, which determines the specific legs, tabletops, and screws needed for each table type in each week.

### 3. Developing the Master Production Schedule

Creating an effective MPS requires careful consideration of demand, capacity, and company objectives.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Demand Management:** Forecasting future demand and managing incoming orders.
*   **Capacity Management:** Ensuring that the production capacity is available to meet the MPS.
*   **Available-to-Promise (ATP) Calculation:** A critical output of the MPS that assures customers of delivery dates.
*   **Pegging:** The ability to trace the demand for a component back to the specific MPS order that generated it.
*   **Time Fences:** Periods within the MPS where changes are restricted to maintain stability.
    *   **Frozen Zone:** Changes to the MPS are not allowed.
    *   **Slushy Zone (or Shaky Zone):** Limited changes may be allowed, often requiring higher approval.
    *   **Liquid Zone:** Most flexibility exists for changes.

**Steps in MPS Development:**

1.  **Review the S&OP Plan:** Understand the aggregate demand and production targets.
2.  **Forecast Demand:** Use statistical forecasting and input from sales and marketing.
3.  **Incorporate Firm Orders:** Add specific customer orders already received.
4.  **Calculate Available-to-Promise (ATP):** Determine what can be promised based on current inventory, scheduled receipts, and the planned MPS quantities.
5.  **Tentative MPS:** Develop an initial production schedule.
6.  **Capacity Check:** Evaluate if the tentative MPS is feasible with current or planned capacity (using CRP).
7.  **Revise MPS:** Adjust the schedule based on capacity constraints, inventory levels, and customer commitments.
8.  **Communicate MPS:** Share the approved schedule with relevant departments (e.g., production, procurement).

**Example of ATP Calculation:**

Product: Bicycle
Period: Week 1

*   Beginning Inventory: 100 units
*   Scheduled Receipts (orders due in Week 1): 50 units
*   Total Available: 150 units
*   Customer Orders due in Week 1: 60 units
*   MPS Quantity for Week 1: 40 units

**ATP for Week 1 = Beginning Inventory + Scheduled Receipts - Customer Orders due *before* MPS quantities are produced.**

Let's refine the ATP calculation for a specific week:

**Week 1:**
*   Beginning Inventory: 50
*   Scheduled Receipts (already planned and arriving in Week 1): 30
*   Total Available at start of Week 1 = 50 + 30 = 80
*   Orders to fulfill in Week 1 = 70
*   MPS for Week 1 = 20 (produced during Week 1)

**ATP for Week 1:**
*   Start with beginning inventory: 50
*   Add scheduled receipts: 50 + 30 = 80
*   Subtract committed orders (orders placed *before* this week for delivery *this* week): Let's say 60 units were ordered last week for delivery this week. 80 - 60 = 20. This 20 is available *before* the MPS quantity for this week is produced.
*   The MPS quantity for Week 1 is 20. So, ATP at end of Week 1 is 20 (from beginning inv + scheduled receipts - orders) + 20 (MPS) = 40.

A more common way to represent ATP is:

**ATP (Beginning of Week 1) = Beginning Inventory + Scheduled Receipts (in Week 1) - Sum of Customer Orders due in Week 1.**
*   ATP (Week 1) = 50 + 30 - 70 = 10. (This means 10 units are available to promise for *future* weeks based on this week's start).

**ATP (End of Week 1) = ATP (Beginning of Week 1) - MPS quantity for Week 1.** This is incorrect.

The correct way to think about ATP for a specific period is what's available to promise for that period and beyond.

**Correct ATP Calculation Example:**

Let's say we are planning for Week 1.
*   Beginning Inventory (before Week 1 starts): 50
*   Scheduled Receipts (arriving in Week 1): 30
*   Customer Orders due in Week 1: 70
*   MPS for Week 1: 20

**ATP for Week 1 = Beginning Inventory + Scheduled Receipts - Sum of orders due *before* the MPS is available for delivery in that week.**

If the MPS is produced mid-week:
*   Available to promise at the start of Week 1 for orders due *in* Week 1: 50 (inventory) + 30 (receipts) = 80.
*   We have 70 orders due in Week 1.
*   So, the first 70 units of the 80 available are committed to the orders due in Week 1.
*   This leaves 80 - 70 = 10 units from inventory/receipts.
*   The MPS for Week 1 is 20 units.
*   Total available to promise *during* Week 1 = 10 (from previous) + 20 (MPS) = 30 units.

**Important Point to Remember:** ATP is calculated as: **Beginning Inventory + Scheduled Receipts - Customer Orders (due before the MPS quantity is available) - Previous ATP quantities.**

Let's simplify for the purpose of a note:

**ATP (for a period) = Beginning Inventory + Scheduled Receipts (in that period) - Orders committed for that period (that are fulfilled from beginning inventory/scheduled receipts before the MPS quantity is ready).**

**Time Fences:**
*   **Frozen:** Protects the production schedule from frequent changes, ensuring stability and allowing production to run efficiently. Essential for WCM's focus on smooth flow.
*   **Slushy:** Allows for limited, controlled adjustments, often for urgent customer needs or significant forecast changes.
*   **Liquid:** Provides flexibility to respond to market dynamics, but requires careful management to avoid disrupting other production runs.

### 4. MPS in the Context of WCM Principles

WCM is characterized by continuous improvement, waste reduction, customer focus, and employee involvement. The MPS plays a vital role in supporting these principles.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Just-In-Time (JIT):** Producing goods only when they are needed, in the exact quantity needed. A well-defined MPS is essential for JIT.
*   **Lean Manufacturing:** A philosophy focused on eliminating waste. MPS helps reduce waste related to overproduction, inventory, and rework.
*   **Demand-Pull:** Production is triggered by actual demand rather than forecasts alone. MPS helps to signal this demand to production.
*   **Continuous Improvement (Kaizen):** Regularly reviewing and improving processes. MPS performance is a key area for improvement.
*   **Employee Involvement (HRD):** Employees actively participate in problem-solving and process improvement. Employees responsible for executing the MPS can provide feedback for its improvement.

**How MPS supports WCM:**

*   **Reduces Overproduction:** By scheduling specific quantities, MPS prevents the wasteful production of items that may not be immediately needed.
*   **Minimizes Inventory:** A stable MPS allows for better planning of raw materials and work-in-progress, reducing holding costs and obsolescence.
*   **Improves Flow:** By providing a clear production roadmap, MPS facilitates a smoother flow of materials and products through the manufacturing process.
*   **Enhances Responsiveness:** By managing ATP effectively, companies can provide accurate delivery dates, improving customer satisfaction and responsiveness.
*   **Supports JIT:** The predictable flow created by MPS is a prerequisite for effective JIT implementation.
*   **Facilitates Problem Identification:** Deviations from the MPS highlight production issues or forecast inaccuracies, triggering continuous improvement efforts.

**Example:**

A WCM automotive plant uses MPS to schedule daily production of specific car models and configurations. This precise schedule:
*   Ensures that the right components arrive from suppliers *just-in-time*.
*   Minimizes the need for large buffer stocks of finished cars.
*   Allows assembly lines to operate at a steady, optimized pace.
*   If there's a consistent underproduction of a specific model indicated by MPS performance, it triggers a Kaizen event to investigate the root cause on the shop floor.

### 5. Challenges and Best Practices for MPS in WCM

Implementing and maintaining an effective MPS can present challenges, but adopting best practices ensures its success within a WCM framework.

**Key Challenges:**

*   **Inaccurate Forecasts:** Over-reliance on unreliable demand predictions.
*   **Capacity Bottlenecks:** Scheduling more than can be produced.
*   **Lack of Coordination:** Poor communication between sales, marketing, and production.
*   **Frequent Schedule Changes:** Reacting too quickly to minor fluctuations, disrupting flow.
*   **Data Inaccuracy:** Errors in bills of materials, inventory records, or lead times.
*   **Resistance to Change:** Employees not adhering to the new scheduling discipline.

**Best Practices for MPS in WCM:**

*   **Strong S&OP Process:** Ensure the MPS is directly aligned with aggregate sales and operations plans.
*   **Collaborative Forecasting:** Involve sales, marketing, and operations in demand planning.
*   **Realistic Capacity Planning:** Integrate capacity constraints into MPS development.
*   **Stable Time Fences:** Implement and adhere to time fences to maintain schedule stability.
*   **Accurate Data:** Maintain clean and up-to-date data in the ERP/MRP system.
*   **Regular Review and Revision:** Periodically review MPS performance and make necessary adjustments.
*   **Visibility and Communication:** Ensure the MPS is visible to all relevant stakeholders.
*   **Utilize Technology:** Leverage advanced planning and scheduling (APS) systems for sophisticated MPS generation.
*   **Empower Employees:** Train and empower shop-floor personnel to understand the MPS and provide feedback for continuous improvement.

**Example:**

A WCM company implements a "daily production meeting" where the MPS for the next 24 hours is reviewed. Operators, supervisors, and planners discuss potential issues, material availability, and machine status. This collaborative approach helps identify and resolve minor problems before they impact the schedule, embodying employee involvement and continuous improvement.

### Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
What is the primary purpose of a Master Production Schedule (MPS) in World Class Manufacturing?
A) To forecast future demand for raw materials.
B) To dictate the specific end products to be manufactured, their quantities, and the timing of their production.
C) To manage overall financial budgeting for the manufacturing division.
D) To determine the optimal location for new factories.

**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** The MPS translates the broader sales and operations plan into specific production plans for finished goods. This direct link to what and when to produce is its primary function, crucial for WCM's focus on efficiency and responsiveness.

**Question 2:**
Which of the following is NOT typically considered a "Time Fence" in MPS management?
A) Frozen Zone
B) Liquid Zone
C) Planning Zone
D) Slushy Zone

**Answer:** C
**Explanation:** The typical time fences are Frozen, Slushy (or Shaky), and Liquid. The Planning Zone is not a standard term for a time fence.

**Question 3:**
How does a well-managed MPS contribute to the Just-In-Time (JIT) philosophy, a key WCM principle?
A) By encouraging large batch production to reduce setup times.
B) By scheduling production precisely based on demand, thus minimizing excess inventory and ensuring materials arrive only when needed.
C) By focusing solely on product quality and ignoring production timing.
D) By allowing for frequent, unplanned changes to production schedules.

**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** JIT relies on predictable demand signals and smooth production flow. An accurate MPS provides these signals, allowing for production only when needed and facilitating the timely arrival of materials, thereby reducing waste and inventory.

**Question 4:**
What does the term "Available-to-Promise" (ATP) represent in relation to the MPS?
A) The total inventory available in the warehouse.
B) The quantity of a product that can be promised to customers, considering current inventory, scheduled receipts, and the MPS.
C) The amount of production capacity available for unscheduled tasks.
D) The forecast of future demand for a specific product.

**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** ATP is a critical output of the MPS process that reassures customers of delivery timelines by accounting for existing resources and planned production.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **MPS is a Bridge:** It connects strategic planning (S&OP) with tactical execution (MRP, shop floor).
*   **Specificity is Key:** The MPS details *what* and *when*, unlike the aggregate S&OP.
*   **ATP is Crucial for Customer Commitments:** It provides realistic promises.
*   **Time Fences are for Stability:** They protect the production schedule from constant disruption, essential for WCM's smooth flow.
*   **Data Accuracy is Paramount:** Errors in inventory, BOMs, or lead times will render the MPS ineffective.
*   **WCM Alignment:** A good MPS supports lean principles, reduces waste, improves flow, and can incorporate employee insights.
*   **Flexibility vs. Stability:** Time fences help balance the need for responsiveness with the need for a stable production environment.
