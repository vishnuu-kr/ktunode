# Aircraft Design: Module 3 - Aircraft Performance and Fuel Fraction Estimates

## Topic: Special Performance Requirements - Accelerated Flight

**Module Description:** This module delves into various aspects of aircraft performance and the critical process of estimating fuel fractions. Understanding these elements is vital for determining an aircraft's capabilities and operational efficiency. This topic specifically focuses on the performance requirements associated with accelerated flight, a critical phase in many aircraft operations.

**Learning Outcomes:**
*   Understand the fundamental principles of accelerated flight in aircraft.
*   Analyze the forces acting on an aircraft during acceleration.
*   Calculate the acceleration of an aircraft.
*   Determine the time and distance required for acceleration to a target speed.
*   Assess the impact of propeller/jet engine characteristics on acceleration.
*   Understand the role of wing loading and thrust-to-weight ratio in acceleration.

### 1. Introduction to Accelerated Flight

**Key Concept:** Accelerated flight occurs when the net force acting on an aircraft is not zero, resulting in a change in its velocity (i.e., acceleration or deceleration).

**Definition:**
*   **Acceleration:** The rate of change of velocity with respect to time. In aircraft performance, it's the increase in speed.
*   **Deceleration:** The rate of decrease of velocity with respect to time.

**Relevance to Aircraft Design:**
*   **Takeoff Performance:** Crucial for achieving takeoff speed within a given runway length.
*   **Climb Performance:** Many climbs involve continuous acceleration to reach higher speeds as altitude increases.
*   **Maneuvering:** Combat aircraft and aerobatic aircraft require rapid acceleration for tactical maneuvers.
*   **Maneuverability:** High acceleration capability contributes to an aircraft's ability to change speed and direction quickly.

**Reference:**
*   **Raymer (2012), Chapter 9 - Performance:** Discusses the fundamental equations of motion for aircraft, including acceleration.
*   **Eshelby (2000), Chapter 4 - Straight and Level Flight:** While focusing on level flight, it lays the groundwork for understanding forces that contribute to or oppose acceleration.

### 2. Forces Acting on an Aircraft During Acceleration

**Key Concept:** Newton's Second Law of Motion ($F_{net} = ma$) is the foundation for understanding accelerated flight. The net force acting on the aircraft is the vector sum of all forces.

**Forces Involved:**
*   **Thrust ($T$):** The forward force generated by the engines (propeller or jet).
*   **Drag ($D$):** The aerodynamic force resisting the aircraft's motion.
*   **Lift ($L$):** The aerodynamic force perpendicular to the relative wind, primarily supporting the aircraft.
*   **Weight ($W$):** The force of gravity acting on the aircraft.

**Equation of Motion for Accelerated Flight:**

In the direction of motion (horizontal acceleration, assuming no pitching):

$F_{net\_horizontal} = T - D = m \cdot a_x$

Where:
*   $T$ = Thrust
*   $D$ = Drag
*   $m$ = Mass of the aircraft ($W/g$)
*   $a_x$ = Horizontal acceleration

**Important Considerations:**

*   **Thrust Variation:** Thrust can vary with altitude, airspeed, and throttle setting.
*   **Drag Variation:** Drag is a function of airspeed, altitude, aircraft configuration (flaps, gear), and lift.
*   **Angle of Attack (AoA):** During acceleration, the pilot will typically adjust the AoA to maintain a desired flight path or altitude. This affects both lift and drag.
*   **Weight Variation:** As fuel is consumed, the aircraft's weight decreases, which can affect acceleration.

**Reference:**
*   **Raymer (2012), Chapter 9 - Performance:** Provides detailed equations for thrust and drag, and their application to accelerated flight.
*   **Eshelby (2000), Chapter 10 - Accelerated Flight:** Specifically covers the principles and calculations for accelerated flight.

### 3. Calculating Aircraft Acceleration

**Key Concept:** Acceleration is determined by the net force acting on the aircraft and its mass.

**Formula for Acceleration:**

$a_x = \frac{T - D}{m}$

Where:
*   $a_x$ = Horizontal acceleration
*   $T$ = Thrust
*   $D$ = Drag
*   $m$ = Mass of the aircraft ($W/g$)

**Steps for Calculation:**

1.  **Determine Thrust ($T$):** This depends on the engine type, altitude, and throttle setting. For jet engines, it's often given as a function of Mach number and altitude. For propeller engines, it's related to engine power, propeller efficiency, and airspeed.
2.  **Determine Drag ($D$):** This is calculated using the drag equation:
    $D = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_D$
    Where:
    *   $\rho$ = Air density
    *   $V$ = Airspeed
    *   $S$ = Wing reference area
    *   $C_D$ = Drag coefficient, which is a function of $C_L$ and other factors (e.g., $C_D = C_{D0} + K C_L^2$).
3.  **Determine Lift ($L$):** During climbing or level flight with acceleration, the aircraft must maintain sufficient lift to counteract its weight. In a steady climb or during acceleration in level flight, lift is typically close to weight ($L \approx W$). However, during maneuvers or during takeoff roll, lift can be lower than weight.
4.  **Calculate Mass ($m$):** $m = W/g$. The weight ($W$) will change as fuel is consumed. For acceleration calculations, it's crucial to use the *current* mass of the aircraft at that point in the flight.
5.  **Calculate Acceleration ($a_x$):** Plug the values of $T$, $D$, and $m$ into the formula.

**Example Scenario (Takeoff Roll):**

Consider an aircraft accelerating on the runway.
*   **Thrust ($T$):** Engine takeoff thrust.
*   **Drag ($D$):** Primarily parasite drag and induced drag (though induced drag is low at low AoA on the runway). A significant factor is rolling resistance.
*   **Lift ($L$):** As speed increases, lift also increases, reducing the normal force on the wheels and thus reducing rolling resistance.
*   **Weight ($W$):** Initial takeoff weight.

**Reference:**
*   **Eshelby (2000), Chapter 10 - Accelerated Flight:** Provides detailed derivations and examples of calculating acceleration for various flight conditions.
*   **Fielding (2017), Chapter 5 - Aircraft Performance:** Explains the fundamental equations and their application to different flight phases.

### 4. Time and Distance for Acceleration

**Key Concept:** Once the acceleration ($a_x$) is known, the time and distance required to reach a target speed can be calculated using kinematic equations.

**Assumptions:**
*   Constant acceleration (this is an approximation, as $T$ and $D$ can change with speed).
*   Starting from an initial velocity ($V_0$).

**Kinematic Equations:**

*   **Velocity as a function of time:** $V = V_0 + a_x t$
*   **Distance as a function of time:** $s = V_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a_x t^2$
*   **Velocity as a function of distance:** $V^2 = V_0^2 + 2 a_x s$

**Calculations:**

*   **Time to reach a target speed ($V_{target}$):**
    $t_{accel} = \frac{V_{target} - V_0}{a_x}$

*   **Distance to reach a target speed ($V_{target}$):**
    $s_{accel} = \frac{V_{target}^2 - V_0^2}{2 a_x}$

**Important Considerations:**

*   **Variable Acceleration:** In reality, acceleration is rarely constant. Thrust might be limited, and drag increases with speed. For accurate calculations, a step-by-step integration method (numerical integration) is often used, dividing the acceleration process into small time or speed increments.
*   **Takeoff Roll:** This is a critical application where calculating the distance to reach the takeoff decision speed ($V_1$) and takeoff rotation speed ($V_R$) is paramount.

**Reference:**
*   **Raymer (2012), Chapter 9 - Performance:** Demonstrates how to calculate takeoff distance, which relies heavily on acceleration calculations.
*   **Eshelby (2000), Chapter 10 - Accelerated Flight:** Provides methods for handling variable acceleration and performing numerical integrations.

### 5. Impact of Aircraft Design Parameters on Acceleration

**Key Concept:** Various design choices directly influence an aircraft's acceleration capability.

**Key Design Parameters:**

*   **Thrust-to-Weight Ratio ($T/W$):**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of total engine thrust to the aircraft's weight.
    *   **Impact:** A higher $T/W$ ratio directly leads to higher acceleration, as $a_x = (T - D)/W \times g$. A $T/W$ greater than 1.0 would theoretically allow vertical acceleration (though thrust is typically less than weight in level flight). For takeoff and climb, a higher $T/W$ is highly desirable.
    *   **Design Implications:** Choosing more powerful engines or reducing aircraft weight (e.g., through lightweight materials) improves $T/W$.
    *   **Example:** Fighter jets have very high $T/W$ ratios (often $> 1.0$) for rapid acceleration and maneuvering. Transport aircraft have lower $T/W$ ratios.

*   **Wing Loading ($W/S$):**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of aircraft weight to wing area.
    *   **Impact:** Higher wing loading means the aircraft needs a higher speed to generate sufficient lift to equal its weight. This increases the takeoff speed and requires more thrust to overcome drag at that speed, thus reducing acceleration capability at lower speeds. Lower wing loading generally leads to better acceleration at lower speeds (e.g., during takeoff).
    *   **Design Implications:** Increasing wing area for a given weight reduces wing loading. This can be achieved with larger wings or by reducing the aircraft's weight.

*   **Aerodynamic Efficiency ($L/D$):**
    *   **Definition:** The ratio of lift to drag.
    *   **Impact:** While primarily related to endurance and range, a higher $L/D$ means lower drag for a given amount of lift. This reduces the drag component that opposes acceleration, thus improving it.
    *   **Design Implications:** Streamlined fuselage, efficient wing design, and reduced interference drag contribute to higher $L/D$.

*   **Propeller/Jet Engine Characteristics:**
    *   **Propeller Efficiency:** Affects the thrust produced at different airspeeds.
    *   **Jet Engine Thrust Specific Fuel Consumption (TSFC):** While more related to fuel fraction, efficient engines can provide more thrust for a given fuel flow.
    *   **Thrust Curve:** The variation of thrust with airspeed and altitude is critical. Some engines have a flatter thrust curve, maintaining thrust better at higher speeds.

**Reference:**
*   **Raymer (2012), Chapter 3 - Forces and Performance Principles:** Introduces $T/W$ and $W/S$ and their fundamental impact on performance.
*   **Eshelby (2000), Chapter 10 - Accelerated Flight:** Analyzes how these parameters specifically affect acceleration performance.
*   **Booth (2018), Chapter 2 - Aircraft Design Process:** Touches upon how performance requirements drive the selection of engines and wing configurations.

### 6. Accelerated Maneuvers (Brief Overview)

**Key Concept:** Accelerated flight is not limited to straight-line motion. Turns, climbs, and dives often involve significant acceleration.

**Turning:**
*   **Bank Angle:** In a turn, the lift vector is tilted. The horizontal component of lift provides the centripetal force required for turning.
*   **Load Factor ($n$):** The ratio of the total aerodynamic force (lift + drag) to the aircraft's weight. In a turn, $n > 1$.
    $n = \frac{L_{total}}{W}$
*   **Impact on Acceleration:** To maintain a turn with a bank angle $\phi$, the pilot must increase the angle of attack, which increases both lift and drag. The increased lift required is $L = W \cos\phi$ (vertical component) and the centripetal force is $F_c = W \tan\phi$.
*   **Sustained Turn:** A turn where the aircraft maintains altitude and speed. This requires sufficient thrust to overcome the increased drag at the higher lift coefficient.
*   **Accelerated Turn:** A turn where the pilot intentionally increases speed or altitude simultaneously.

**Climbs:**
*   **Climb Gradient:** The ratio of altitude gained to distance traveled.
*   **Rate of Climb:** The vertical speed.
*   **Accelerated Climb:** A climb where the aircraft is gaining speed. This usually occurs at lower altitudes where engine power is higher.

**Reference:**
*   **Eshelby (2000), Chapter 11 - Turning and Co-ordinated Turns:** Discusses the forces and performance during turning maneuvers.
*   **Raymer (2012), Chapter 9 - Performance:** Covers climb performance and the impact of acceleration on climb rates.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Newton's Second Law ($F_{net} = ma$) is central to understanding accelerated flight.**
*   **Acceleration is driven by the excess thrust ($T - D$).**
*   **The mass ($m$) of the aircraft is a critical factor; consider how fuel burn affects mass.**
*   **Thrust-to-Weight ($T/W$) and Wing Loading ($W/S$) are key design parameters that directly influence acceleration.**
*   **Takeoff roll calculations are a primary application of accelerated flight principles.**
*   **For accurate calculations, variable acceleration must be considered, often requiring numerical integration.**
*   **Accelerated flight is crucial for mission effectiveness in various aircraft roles (e.g., combat, transport, general aviation).**

### 8. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
An aircraft has a weight of 100,000 N and a wing area of 30 m². At a certain point during its takeoff roll, the airspeed is 50 m/s. At this condition, the total drag is 15,000 N, and the engine thrust is 25,000 N. Assuming the thrust and drag remain constant for the next 5 seconds, calculate:
a) The aircraft's mass.
b) The horizontal acceleration of the aircraft.
c) The new speed after 5 seconds.
d) The distance covered during these 5 seconds.

**Answer 1:**
a) Mass ($m$) = Weight ($W$) / $g$
$g \approx 9.81$ m/s² (standard gravity)
$m = 100,000 N / 9.81 m/s² \approx 10,194 kg$

b) Horizontal acceleration ($a_x$) = (Thrust ($T$) - Drag ($D$)) / Mass ($m$)
$a_x = (25,000 N - 15,000 N) / 10,194 kg$
$a_x = 10,000 N / 10,194 kg \approx 0.981 m/s²$

c) New speed after 5 seconds ($V_{final}$):
$V_{final} = V_{initial} + a_x \cdot t$
$V_{final} = 50 m/s + (0.981 m/s²) \cdot (5 s)$
$V_{final} = 50 m/s + 4.905 m/s \approx 54.91 m/s$

d) Distance covered during these 5 seconds ($s$):
$s = V_{initial} \cdot t + \frac{1}{2} a_x \cdot t²$
$s = (50 m/s) \cdot (5 s) + \frac{1}{2} (0.981 m/s²) \cdot (5 s)²$
$s = 250 m + \frac{1}{2} (0.981 m/s²) \cdot (25 s²)$
$s = 250 m + 12.26 m \approx 262.26 m$

**Question 2:**
An aircraft has a Thrust-to-Weight ratio ($T/W$) of 0.3 and a wing loading ($W/S$) of 4000 N/m². If the drag coefficient at a certain condition is $C_D = 0.04$ and the lift coefficient required for level flight is $C_L = 0.4$, and assuming air density $\rho = 1.0 kg/m³$, and wing area $S = 50 m²$:
a) Calculate the aircraft's weight.
b) Calculate the total drag ($D$).
c) Calculate the aircraft's acceleration in level flight at this condition.
d) How would increasing the wing area (while keeping weight and engine thrust the same) affect the acceleration?

**Answer 2:**
a) Aircraft's weight ($W$):
Given $W/S = 4000 N/m²$ and $S = 50 m²$
$W = (W/S) \times S = 4000 N/m² \times 50 m² = 200,000 N$

b) Total drag ($D$):
First, calculate $C_D$. Assuming $C_D = C_{D0} + KC_L^2$ and $C_{D0} = 0.02, K = 0.05$ (typical values):
$C_D = 0.02 + 0.05 \times (0.4)^2 = 0.02 + 0.05 \times 0.16 = 0.02 + 0.008 = 0.028$

Now, calculate drag: $D = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_D$
We need the airspeed $V$. In level flight, Lift = Weight, so $L = W = 200,000 N$.
$L = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_L$
$200,000 N = \frac{1}{2} (1.0 kg/m³) V^2 (50 m²) (0.4)$
$200,000 N = 10 V^2$
$V^2 = 20,000$
$V = \sqrt{20,000} \approx 141.4 m/s$

Now, calculate drag:
$D = \frac{1}{2} (1.0 kg/m³) (141.4 m/s)² (50 m²) (0.028)$
$D = \frac{1}{2} (1.0) (20,000) (50) (0.028)$
$D = 10,000 \times 50 \times 0.028 = 500,000 \times 0.028 = 14,000 N$

c) Aircraft's acceleration in level flight ($a_x$):
$a_x = \frac{T - D}{m}$
We know $T/W = 0.3$, so $T = 0.3 \times W = 0.3 \times 200,000 N = 60,000 N$.
Mass $m = W/g = 200,000 N / 9.81 m/s² \approx 20,387 kg$.
$a_x = (60,000 N - 14,000 N) / 20,387 kg$
$a_x = 46,000 N / 20,387 kg \approx 2.26 m/s²$

d) How would increasing the wing area (while keeping weight and engine thrust the same) affect the acceleration?
If wing area ($S$) increases, wing loading ($W/S$) decreases. To maintain level flight (Lift = Weight), the aircraft would need to fly at a lower speed ($V^2 \propto 1/(S \cdot C_L)$ if $C_L$ remains constant or $V^2 \propto 1/S$ if $C_L$ is adjusted such that $L/S$ is constant).
Crucially, at lower speeds, drag ($D = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_D$) tends to be lower (assuming $C_D$ doesn't increase drastically due to higher $C_L$).
However, $T/W$ remains the same. The acceleration is $a_x = g(T/W - D/W)$.
If drag ($D$) decreases at the new flight condition due to lower speed, then $D/W$ decreases, leading to a higher acceleration ($a_x$).
So, **increasing wing area (decreasing wing loading) while keeping thrust and weight constant would generally lead to better acceleration at lower speeds**, as the aircraft requires less speed to generate lift and experiences lower drag.
