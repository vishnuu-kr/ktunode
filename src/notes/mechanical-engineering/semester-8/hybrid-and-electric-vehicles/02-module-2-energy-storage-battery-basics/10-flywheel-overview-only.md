# HYBRID AND ELECTRIC VEHICLES - Module 2: Energy Storage – Battery Basics

**Knowledge Level:** K2 (Descriptive)

**Context:** This topic provides an overview of flywheels as an energy storage mechanism in the broader context of electric and hybrid vehicle energy storage. While the module focuses on batteries, understanding other potential energy storage technologies, even at a high level, offers valuable comparative insight.

### 1. Learning Outcomes Covered:

*   **General understanding of energy storage in HEVs/EVs:** This section will introduce flywheels as an alternative or supplementary energy storage technology, contrasting it with batteries in terms of operational principles.

### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Flywheel:** A rotating mechanical device that stores rotational kinetic energy. Its primary purpose is to smooth out power fluctuations and absorb energy during periods of high demand and release it during periods of low demand.
*   **Rotational Kinetic Energy:** The energy an object possesses due to its rotation. It is directly proportional to the moment of inertia of the object and the square of its angular velocity.
    *   Formula: $KE_{rotational} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$
        *   $I$: Moment of inertia (a measure of an object's resistance to changes in its rotation)
        *   $\omega$: Angular velocity (rotational speed)
*   **Moment of Inertia (I):** A property of a rotating body that describes how its mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation. A larger moment of inertia means more energy is required to change its rotational speed.
*   **Angular Velocity ($\omega$):** The rate of change of angular displacement, typically measured in radians per second or revolutions per minute (RPM).
*   **Energy Density:** The amount of energy stored per unit volume or mass. Flywheels generally have lower energy density compared to batteries.
*   **Power Density:** The amount of power delivered per unit volume or mass. Flywheels can offer very high power density.
*   **Efficiency:** The ratio of output energy to input energy. Flywheel systems can achieve high round-trip efficiencies, especially in well-designed systems.
*   **Mechanical Losses:** Energy dissipated due to friction (in bearings) and aerodynamic drag.
*   **Containment:** Critical for safety, as a failing flywheel can disintegrate and cause significant damage.

### 3. Overview of Flywheel Operation in HEVs/EVs:

*   **How it works:**
    *   A flywheel stores energy by accelerating a rotating mass (the rotor) to high speeds.
    *   Energy is charged by motorizing the flywheel, converting electrical energy into kinetic energy.
    *   Energy is discharged by using the spinning flywheel to drive a generator, converting kinetic energy back into electrical energy.
*   **Components of a Flywheel Energy Storage System (FESS):**
    *   **Rotor:** The rotating mass, typically made of high-strength materials like steel, composites (carbon fiber), or alloys to withstand high rotational speeds. The shape and material are crucial for maximizing energy storage for a given weight and volume.
    *   **Bearings:** Support the rotor and allow it to spin with minimal friction. Advanced magnetic bearings are often used to reduce friction and allow for higher speeds.
    *   **Motor/Generator:** An electric motor that accelerates the rotor and a generator that extracts energy from the rotor. These are often integrated into a single unit (e.g., a permanent magnet synchronous motor/generator).
    *   **Containment System:** A robust housing designed to safely contain the rotor in case of failure.
    *   **Vacuum Enclosure (Optional but common):** To minimize aerodynamic drag and thus reduce energy losses, flywheels are often housed in a vacuum chamber.
    *   **Power Electronics:** To manage the charging and discharging of the flywheel, interfacing with the vehicle's electrical system.

### 4. Flywheels vs. Batteries in HEVs/EVs:

| Feature           | Flywheel                                                                | Battery                                                                         |
| :---------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Energy Storage** | Rotational kinetic energy                                               | Chemical potential energy                                                       |
| **Energy Density** | Lower (especially volumetric)                                           | Higher (especially volumetric)                                                  |
| **Power Density**  | Very high (can deliver large bursts of power quickly)                   | Moderate to high, but can be limited by internal resistance during rapid discharge |
| **Charge/Discharge Rate** | Very high (limited by mechanical power handling)                        | Limited by internal chemistry and thermal management                             |
| **Lifespan**       | Very long (millions of charge/discharge cycles)                         | Limited by chemical degradation (thousands of cycles)                           |
| **Efficiency**     | High round-trip efficiency (can be >90% in optimized systems)           | Good, but can vary with depth of discharge and temperature                    |
| **Maintenance**    | Relatively low (especially with magnetic bearings)                      | Requires battery management system (BMS) for health, balancing, and safety     |
| **Safety Concerns**| Rotor disintegration at high speeds (requiring robust containment)       | Thermal runaway, chemical hazards, electrical short circuits                   |
| **Cost**           | Can be high due to specialized materials and precision manufacturing    | Varies significantly with battery chemistry and scale of production           |
| **Typical Application** | Regenerative braking energy capture, power smoothing, quick bursts of acceleration | Primary energy source for propulsion, extended range                           |

### 5. Applications and Potential in HEVs/EVs:

*   **Regenerative Braking:** Flywheels excel at capturing the kinetic energy from braking, which is often in short, high-power bursts. This energy can then be re-used for acceleration.
*   **Power Smoothing:** In hybrid vehicles, flywheels can absorb power fluctuations from the engine or during transitions between engine and electric motor, improving fuel efficiency and ride comfort.
*   **Peak Power Assist:** Providing rapid bursts of power during acceleration, supplementing the primary energy source.
*   **Supercapacitors vs. Flywheels:** Both offer high power density and fast charge/discharge rates. Flywheels store mechanical energy, while supercapacitors store electrostatic energy. Flywheels typically store more energy for their weight/volume when operating at very high speeds.

### 6. Important Points to Remember:

*   Flywheels store energy kinetically, not chemically.
*   Their strength lies in high power delivery and rapid charge/discharge, making them excellent for regenerative braking and power smoothing.
*   Energy storage capacity is proportional to the square of the rotational speed and the moment of inertia.
*   Safety and containment are paramount due to the high speeds involved.
*   They are often used in conjunction with batteries or other energy storage devices rather than as the sole energy source for vehicles due to their lower energy density.

### 9. Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:**
What is the primary principle by which a flywheel stores energy?
    a) Chemical reactions
    b) Electrostatic charge
    c) Rotational kinetic energy
    d) Thermal energy

**Question 2:**
Flywheel energy storage systems are particularly well-suited for which of the following vehicle functions?
    a) Providing long-range driving range
    b) Capturing regenerative braking energy
    c) Storing energy from the grid for later use
    d) Reducing the weight of the vehicle

**Question 3:**
The formula for rotational kinetic energy is $KE_{rotational} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$. If a flywheel's speed ($\omega$) is doubled while its moment of inertia ($I$) remains constant, by what factor does its stored energy increase?
    a) 2
    b) 3
    c) 4
    d) 8

**Question 4:**
List at least two key components of a typical flywheel energy storage system.

**Question 5:**
Compare and contrast the energy density and power density of flywheels with those of batteries.

---

### Answers:

**Answer 1:**
c) Rotational kinetic energy

**Answer 2:**
b) Capturing regenerative braking energy
    *   **Explanation:** Flywheels excel at absorbing and releasing energy in rapid bursts, making them ideal for capturing the kinetic energy lost during braking. Batteries are generally preferred for sustained energy storage for driving range.

**Answer 3:**
c) 4
    *   **Explanation:** Since energy is proportional to the square of the angular velocity ($\omega^2$), doubling the speed means the energy increases by $2^2 = 4$ times.

**Answer 4:**
Two key components of a flywheel energy storage system are:
    *   Rotor (the rotating mass)
    *   Motor/Generator
    *   Bearings
    *   Containment System
    *   Power Electronics

**Answer 5:**
*   **Energy Density:** Flywheels generally have *lower* energy density (energy stored per unit volume or mass) compared to batteries. This means for the same amount of stored energy, a flywheel system might be larger or heavier than a battery system.
*   **Power Density:** Flywheels typically have *very high* power density, meaning they can deliver or absorb energy at a much faster rate (higher power) than batteries. This is their significant advantage.
