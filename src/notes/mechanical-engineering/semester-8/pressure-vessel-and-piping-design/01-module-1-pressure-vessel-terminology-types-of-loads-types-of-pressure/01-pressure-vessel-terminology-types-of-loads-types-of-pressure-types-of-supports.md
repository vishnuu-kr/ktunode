### 1. Introduction to Pressure Vessels

**Definition:**
A pressure vessel is a closed container designed to hold fluids (liquids or gases) at a pressure substantially different from the ambient pressure. This pressure can be internal, external, or both. They are crucial components in various industries, including chemical processing, oil and gas, power generation, and food and beverage.

**Importance of Design:**
The design of pressure vessels is critical for safety and operational efficiency. Failure can lead to catastrophic consequences, including explosions, fires, and environmental damage. Codes and standards (e.g., ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code) provide guidelines and rules for ensuring safe design, fabrication, inspection, and testing.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Harvey, John F. *Theory and Design of Pressure Vessels***: This textbook provides a foundational understanding of pressure vessel mechanics, stressing the importance of material properties, stress analysis, and design codes.
*   **Brownell, L. E., and Young, E. H. *Process Equipment Design***: Focuses on the practical aspects of designing process equipment, including pressure vessels, with considerations for various operating conditions and materials.

### 2. Pressure Vessel Terminology

Understanding the terminology is fundamental to discussing and designing pressure vessels.

*   **Shell:** The main body of the vessel, typically a cylinder, sphere, or a combination thereof, designed to withstand pressure.
    *   **Cylindrical Shell:** A common geometry, characterized by its radius, length, and wall thickness.
    *   **Spherical Shell:** Offers superior strength-to-weight ratio for containing internal pressure.
*   **Head/End Closure:** The component that closes the ends of a cylindrical shell.
    *   **Hemispherical Head:** The strongest type of head, with a thickness approximately half that of a hemispherical shell of the same radius and pressure.
    *   **Elliptical Head:** A common type, offering a good balance between strength and ease of fabrication. Often referred to as a "2:1" elliptical head, where the major axis is twice the minor axis.
    *   **Torispherical Head (Dish-end):** Consists of a crown radius, knuckle radius, and straight cylindrical skirt. Less efficient than hemispherical or elliptical heads but easier to form.
    *   **Flat Head:** Used for low-pressure applications or where space is limited. It experiences significant bending stresses and is generally less efficient for pressure containment.
*   **Nozzle:** A pipe or opening in the vessel shell used for inlet, outlet, sampling, or instrumentation.
    *   **Nozzle Neck:** The cylindrical part of the nozzle.
    *   **Flange:** Connects the nozzle to external piping or equipment.
    *   **Reinforcing Pad:** A plate welded around a nozzle opening to compensate for the material removed, reducing stress concentration.
*   **Manway:** A large opening in the vessel shell to allow personnel access for inspection, cleaning, or maintenance.
*   **Stiffener/Stiffening Ring:** An external or internal ring used to reinforce the shell against buckling, especially for external pressure or long cylindrical shells under internal pressure.
*   **Support:** Components attached to the vessel to provide structural integrity and transfer loads to the foundation. (Discussed in detail later).
*   **Weld Joint:** The connection between two pieces of metal.
    *   **Longitudinal Weld:** A weld running along the length of a cylindrical shell.
    *   **Circumferential Weld:** A weld running around the circumference of a cylindrical shell.
*   **Minimum Required Thickness:** The minimum wall thickness calculated based on design pressure, material properties, and allowable stress.
*   **Corrosion Allowance:** An additional thickness added to the minimum required thickness to account for material loss due to corrosion over the vessel's service life.
*   **Joint Efficiency:** A factor representing the quality of a weld joint, affecting the allowable stress. It's typically less than 1 for welded joints compared to seamless material.

**Important Point to Remember:**
Proper terminology is essential for accurate communication and design calculations according to industry codes.

### 3. Types of Loads on Pressure Vessels

Pressure vessels are subjected to various loads that must be considered during the design phase to ensure structural integrity.

*   **Internal Pressure:** The primary load in most pressure vessels, acting to expand the vessel and cause tensile stresses in the shell.
    *   **Design Pressure:** The maximum gauge pressure permissible at the top of the vessel in its normal operating condition. This is the basis for pressure part thickness calculations.
    *   **Maximum Allowable Working Pressure (MAWP):** The maximum gauge pressure at the bottom of the vessel at any allowable temperature.
*   **External Pressure:** Pressure acting on the outside of the vessel, tending to cause compressive stresses and potential buckling. Common in vacuum services or vessels operating at sub-atmospheric pressures.
*   **Weight Loads:**
    *   **Weight of Shell and Internals:** The self-weight of the vessel and its components (e.g., trays, packing, insulation).
    *   **Weight of Contents:** The weight of the fluid (liquid or gas) contained within the vessel. This can be significant for large vessels.
    *   **Weight of Insulation and Cladding:** If applicable.
*   **Thermal Loads:**
    *   **Thermal Expansion/Contraction:** Differential temperatures between parts of the vessel or between the vessel and connected piping can induce stresses due to expansion or contraction. This is particularly important in systems with significant temperature variations.
    *   **Thermal Gradients:** Uneven heating or cooling can create temperature differences across the wall thickness, leading to bending stresses.
*   **Mechanical Loads:**
    *   **Nozzle Loads:** Forces and moments transmitted from connected piping to the vessel nozzles. These are critical and often require localized reinforcement and stress analysis.
    *   **Wind Loads:** Applied to the external surface of the vessel, especially for elevated or exposed vessels. These cause bending and shear stresses.
    *   **Seismic Loads:** Forces generated due to earthquake ground motion. These are important for vessels in earthquake-prone regions.
    *   **Live Loads:** Intermittent loads such as the weight of personnel on platforms or ladders.
    *   **Support Loads:** Loads transferred from the vessel to its supports.
*   **Fatigue Loads:** Cyclic variations in pressure or temperature can lead to fatigue failure over time, requiring special considerations in the design.

**Example:**
A large vertical storage tank filled with a dense liquid will have significant weight loads. A vessel operating under vacuum will have dominant external pressure loads. A vessel in a chemical plant experiencing frequent start-ups and shutdowns with varying temperatures will be subject to significant thermal loads and potentially fatigue.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Mosss, Dennis R. *Pressure Vessel Design Manual***: This manual offers detailed explanations of various load types and their impact on vessel integrity, including methods for calculating their effects.
*   **Megyesy, Eugene F. *Pressure Vessel Design Handbook***: Provides analytical methods for calculating stresses due to various loads, emphasizing the importance of combining different load cases for a comprehensive design.

### 4. Types of Pressure

Understanding the types of pressure is crucial for correctly applying design codes and calculating stresses.

*   **Internal Pressure:**
    *   **Operating Pressure:** The typical pressure at which the vessel operates.
    *   **Design Pressure:** The maximum gauge pressure that the vessel is designed to withstand during normal operation, including allowances for upset conditions. It is usually set higher than the operating pressure to provide a safety margin.
    *   **Test Pressure:** The pressure applied during hydrostatic or pneumatic testing. It is typically higher than the design pressure to ensure the vessel's integrity. For hydrostatic testing, it is usually 1.31 times the MAWP multiplied by the ratio of the allowable stress at test temperature to the allowable stress at design temperature.
*   **External Pressure:**
    *   **Vacuum:** A pressure below atmospheric pressure. Vessels designed for vacuum service must be able to withstand the external atmospheric pressure.
    *   **External Operating Pressure:** The pressure on the outside of the vessel during operation.
    *   **External Design Pressure:** The maximum external pressure the vessel is designed to withstand. This is a critical consideration for preventing buckling.

**Key Concept:**
The design pressure (internal) and external design pressure are the primary parameters used in shell thickness calculations.

**Calculation of Stresses due to Pressure (Basic Concepts):**

*   **Thin Cylindrical Shells (Internal Pressure):**
    *   **Hoop Stress ($\sigma_h$):** Tensile stress acting circumferentially.
        $\sigma_h = \frac{PD}{2t}$
        where:
        $P$ = Internal pressure
        $D$ = Inside diameter
        $t$ = Wall thickness
    *   **Longitudinal Stress ($\sigma_l$):** Tensile stress acting along the axis of the cylinder.
        $\sigma_l = \frac{PD}{4t}$
        *Observation:* Hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress in a thin cylindrical shell.

*   **Thin Spherical Shells (Internal Pressure):**
    *   **Membrane Stress ($\sigma_s$):** Tensile stress acting equally in all directions tangential to the shell surface.
        $\sigma_s = \frac{PD}{4t}$
        where:
        $P$ = Internal pressure
        $D$ = Inside diameter (or $2R$ where $R$ is inside radius)
        $t$ = Wall thickness

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Chathopadhyay, Somnath. *Pressure Vessels Design and Practice***: This book delves into the theoretical aspects of stress distribution in pressure vessels under various pressure conditions, providing analytical solutions and design methodologies.
*   **Harvey, John F. *Theory and Design of Pressure Vessels***: Offers detailed derivations of stress formulas for thin and thick shells under internal and external pressures.

### 5. Types of Supports for Pressure Vessels

Supports are crucial for transferring the loads from the vessel to the foundation and ensuring the vessel's stability and integrity. The type of support depends on the vessel's orientation (vertical or horizontal), size, weight distribution, and operating conditions.

#### 5.1. Supports for Vertical Pressure Vessels

Vertical vessels are typically supported from the bottom.

*   **Skirt Support:**
    *   **Description:** A cylindrical shell or band attached to the bottom of the vessel shell. It extends downwards and is attached to a foundation.
    *   **Load Transfer:** Transfers the vessel's weight, internal/external pressure loads, and any nozzle loads to the foundation.
    *   **Design Considerations:**
        *   **Circumferential Stiffening:** The skirt itself acts as a stiffener for the bottom of the vessel shell.
        *   **Buckling Analysis:** The skirt must be designed to resist buckling under compressive loads, especially the combined loads of vessel weight and external pressure.
        *   **Attachment Welds:** The welds connecting the skirt to the vessel and to the base plate must be adequately designed.
        *   **Foundation Connection:** The skirt connects to a base ring or lugs which are then anchored to the concrete foundation.
    *   **Textbook Reference:**
        *   **Brownell, L. E., and Young, E. H. *Process Equipment Design***: Discusses skirt design in detail, including considerations for wind and seismic loads.
        *   **Megyesy, Eugene F. *Pressure Vessel Design Handbook***: Provides methodologies for analyzing stresses and buckling in skirt supports.

*   **Lug Supports:**
    *   **Description:** Saddle-like structures welded to the vessel shell, typically at the bottom. They are often used for smaller or lighter vessels.
    *   **Load Transfer:** Transfer loads to supporting structures (e.g., concrete piers, steel beams).
    *   **Design Considerations:**
        *   **Stress Concentration:** Lugs can induce significant bending and shear stresses in the vessel shell. These must be carefully analyzed and minimized.
        *   **Attachment Details:** The design of the lugs and their attachment to the shell is critical to avoid shell distortion and cracking.
        *   **Reinforcement:** The shell area beneath the lugs might require reinforcement.
    *   **Example:** A small process vessel might be supported by two or more lugs resting on a concrete foundation.
    *   **Textbook Reference:**
        *   **Harvey, John F. *Theory and Design of Pressure Vessels***: Covers the analysis of stresses induced by lug attachments.

*   **Saddle Supports (for some vertical applications):**
    *   While more common for horizontal vessels, saddles can sometimes be used for vertical vessels, particularly if they are supported at multiple elevations.

#### 5.2. Supports for Horizontal Pressure Vessels

Horizontal vessels are typically supported by saddles or legs.

*   **Saddle Supports:**
    *   **Description:** Two or more saddle-shaped members placed under the vessel, usually at approximately one-third points along the length of the cylindrical shell. The saddle forms a groove with a specific radius that contacts the vessel.
    *   **Load Transfer:** Transfers the vessel's weight, contents weight, and other loads to the foundation.
    *   **Design Considerations:**
        *   **Contact Area:** The radius of the saddle's contact surface is usually larger than the vessel's radius to reduce pressure on the shell.
        *   **Shell Stresses:** Saddles induce localized bending and compressive stresses in the shell at the points of contact. These stresses must be analyzed and managed.
        *   **Buckling:** The shell can be susceptible to buckling under the compressive forces from the saddles, especially if the saddles are too narrow or the shell is thin. Stiffening rings may be required.
        *   **Saddle Design:** The saddle itself must be strong enough to support the vessel and transfer the loads to the foundation.
    *   **Types of Saddles:**
        *   **Fixed Saddle:** Restricts the longitudinal movement of the vessel.
        *   **Sliding Saddle:** Allows for longitudinal thermal expansion.
    *   **Textbook Reference:**
        *   **Brownell, L. E., and Young, E. H. *Process Equipment Design***: Provides extensive details on saddle support design, including the calculation of contact pressures and stresses.
        *   **Mosss, Dennis R. *Pressure Vessel Design Manual***: Offers practical guidelines and charts for saddle support design, focusing on preventing shell buckling.

*   **Leg Supports:**
    *   **Description:** Vertical members attached to the vessel shell, extending to the foundation. They are used for vessels requiring elevation above the ground.
    *   **Load Transfer:** Directly transfers loads to the foundation.
    *   **Design Considerations:**
        *   **Attachment to Shell:** Similar to lugs, leg attachments can induce significant stresses and require careful design and reinforcement.
        *   **Lateral Loads:** Legs must also be designed to resist lateral loads such as wind and seismic forces.
        *   **Thermal Expansion:** If legs are rigidly attached, they can restrict thermal expansion, leading to stresses. Sliding attachments or expansion joints might be needed.
    *   **Example:** A horizontal storage tank might have four legs lifting it off the ground.

*   **Ring Supports:**
    *   **Description:** A continuous ring around the circumference of the vessel, resting on a foundation. Less common for primary support but can be used for specific applications.

### 6. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
A cylindrical pressure vessel has an internal diameter of 1 meter and is designed to withstand an internal pressure of 5 MPa. The vessel is made of carbon steel with an allowable stress of 150 MPa. Calculate the minimum required thickness for the cylindrical shell, neglecting corrosion allowance. Assume the vessel is thin-walled.
*(Hint: Use hoop stress formula $\sigma_h = \frac{PD}{2t}$ and solve for $t$)*

**Answer 1:**
Given:
Internal Pressure, $P = 5 \text{ MPa}$
Inside Diameter, $D = 1 \text{ m} = 1000 \text{ mm}$
Allowable Stress, $\sigma_h = 150 \text{ MPa}$

From the hoop stress formula for a thin cylindrical shell:
$\sigma_h = \frac{PD}{2t}$

Rearranging to solve for thickness $t$:
$t = \frac{PD}{2\sigma_h}$

Substituting the given values:
$t = \frac{(5 \text{ MPa}) \times (1000 \text{ mm})}{2 \times (150 \text{ MPa})}$
$t = \frac{5000}{300} \text{ mm}$
$t = 16.67 \text{ mm}$

Therefore, the minimum required thickness for the cylindrical shell is approximately 16.67 mm.

**Question 2:**
Describe the primary advantage of a hemispherical head compared to a torispherical head in a pressure vessel.

**Answer 2:**
The primary advantage of a hemispherical head over a torispherical head is its superior strength and efficiency in containing internal pressure. A hemispherical head experiences uniform tensile membrane stress around its entire surface, which is half the hoop stress in a cylinder of the same diameter. This means a hemispherical head requires approximately half the thickness of a torispherical head for the same pressure and diameter, making it more material-efficient.

**Question 3:**
Explain why stiffening rings are often required for pressure vessels subjected to external pressure.

**Answer 3:**
Pressure vessels subjected to external pressure are primarily at risk of **buckling**. Unlike internal pressure which causes tensile stresses that tend to expand the vessel, external pressure causes compressive stresses that tend to collapse the vessel. Thin cylindrical shells are inherently unstable under compression and can buckle even at relatively low pressures. Stiffening rings (either external or internal) are added to increase the shell's resistance to buckling by providing additional rigidity and reducing the effective slenderness ratio of the shell. They effectively divide the shell into smaller, more stable segments.

**Question 4:**
What is the purpose of a reinforcing pad around a nozzle opening?

**Answer 4:**
The purpose of a reinforcing pad around a nozzle opening is to **compensate for the material removed** from the vessel shell to create the opening. Cutting a hole in the shell weakens it and creates stress concentrations. The reinforcing pad is a thicker plate welded around the nozzle, which effectively increases the cross-sectional area of the shell in that region. This redistribution of material helps to reduce localized stresses and prevent premature failure, ensuring that the opening can withstand the design pressure.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Safety First:** Pressure vessel design is governed by stringent codes (e.g., ASME BPVC) to ensure safety.
*   **Load Identification:** Accurately identify and quantify all potential loads acting on the vessel.
*   **Stress Analysis:** Understand how different loads induce stresses in the vessel shell and its components.
*   **Thin vs. Thick Shells:** The design formulas differ significantly for thin-walled (thickness less than 1/10th of the diameter) and thick-walled vessels.
*   **Buckling is Critical:** External pressure and compressive loads are primary causes of buckling, requiring specific design considerations and stiffening.
*   **Support Function:** Supports are vital for structural integrity and must be designed to safely transfer all applicable loads to the foundation.
*   **Material Properties:** The choice of material and its allowable stress are fundamental to thickness calculations.
*   **Corrosion Allowance:** Always factor in corrosion allowance for vessels operating in corrosive environments.
