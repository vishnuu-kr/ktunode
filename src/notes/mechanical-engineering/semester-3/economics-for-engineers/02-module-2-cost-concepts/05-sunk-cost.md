# Economics for Engineers: Module 2 - Cost Concepts

## Topic: Sunk Cost

This topic delves into the nature of sunk costs and their critical importance in rational decision-making within engineering and business contexts. Understanding sunk costs helps engineers avoid common behavioral biases and make economically sound choices.

---

### 1. Introduction to Sunk Costs

**Definition:** A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. It is a past expenditure that is irrelevant to future decisions.

**Key Characteristics:**

*   **Already Incurred:** The money has been spent and is gone.
*   **Unrecoverable:** There is no way to get the money back, regardless of future actions.
*   **Irrelevant to Future Decisions:** Sunk costs should not influence choices about whether to continue or abandon a project, or which alternative to pursue.

**Common Misconception:** Sunk costs are often incorrectly considered when making decisions. This is known as the **sunk cost fallacy** or **escalation of commitment**.

---

### 2. Why Sunk Costs are Irrelevant for Decision-Making

**Rational Decision-Making Principle:** Decisions should be based on the *incremental* costs and benefits associated with each future alternative. Sunk costs, by definition, are not incremental; they are constant regardless of the decision made.

**Cognitive Biases & The Sunk Cost Fallacy:**

*   **Psychological Attachment:** People tend to feel a sense of ownership or investment in projects where they have already spent significant resources (time, money, effort).
*   **Desire to Avoid "Wasting" Money:** The feeling that abandoning a project would mean "throwing away" the money already spent.
*   **Commitment and Consistency:** A desire to appear consistent and committed to a chosen course of action, even if it is no longer optimal.

**Example (Illustrating the Fallacy):**

Imagine you bought a non-refundable ticket for a concert for $100. On the day of the concert, you are feeling very unwell and the weather is terrible. You have two options:

1.  **Go to the concert:** You will incur additional costs (transportation, food) and endure discomfort.
2.  **Stay home:** You will save on additional costs and feel more comfortable.

The $100 ticket price is a **sunk cost**. It's already spent and cannot be recovered whether you go to the concert or stay home. A rational decision would weigh the *additional* benefits of going (enjoyment of music, company) against the *additional* costs and discomfort. Focusing on the $100 already spent to justify going despite feeling sick is the sunk cost fallacy. The correct approach is to ask: "Given I have already spent $100, what is the best use of my time and resources *from this point forward*?"

### 3. Sunk Costs in Engineering Projects

Sunk costs are ubiquitous in engineering, from initial research and development to the construction and operation of projects.

**Examples in Engineering:**

*   **Research and Development (R&D) Costs:** Money spent on feasibility studies, prototype development, or initial testing for a new product or process. If the project is later deemed not viable, these R&D costs are sunk.
*   **Equipment Already Purchased:** The purchase price of machinery or specialized tools that have been acquired for a project.
*   **Training Costs:** Expenses incurred to train personnel on a specific technology or process.
*   **Initial Infrastructure Investment:** Costs of building a factory, laying foundations, or setting up initial utility connections for a project.
*   **Marketing and Advertising:** Money spent on launching a product or service.

**Decision Point:** When faced with a project that is underperforming or has encountered unforeseen issues, engineers must analyze whether to continue investing in it or to abandon it.

**Key Question:** "What are the future costs and benefits of continuing this project, assuming the past expenditures are irrelevant?"

---

### 4. Differentiating Sunk Costs from Other Cost Concepts

It's crucial to distinguish sunk costs from other types of costs relevant to engineering economics.

*   **Incremental Costs (or Marginal Costs):** The additional cost incurred by producing one more unit of a good or service, or by taking one more step in a project. **These are the costs that matter for decisions.**
*   **Opportunity Costs:** The value of the next best alternative that must be forgone when a choice is made. This is also a future-oriented cost.
*   **Fixed Costs:** Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run (e.g., rent, salaries). While some fixed costs might have been incurred in the past, the concept of "sunk" emphasizes irrecoverability.
*   **Variable Costs:** Costs that change directly with the level of output (e.g., raw materials, direct labor).

**Textbook Cross-Reference:**

*   **Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015):** This text likely emphasizes the distinction between relevant (future, incremental) costs and irrelevant (past, sunk) costs in managerial decision-making. They would explain how focusing on sunk costs leads to suboptimal outcomes.
*   **Thuesen, Fabrycky (1966) & Paneerselvam (2012):** These engineering economy texts would frame this within the context of project evaluation and capital budgeting, where decisions are made about allocating scarce resources to future investments. Sunk costs are explicitly excluded from the analysis of project alternatives.
*   **Blank & Tarquin (7th Ed.):** They would likely present sunk costs as a fundamental concept to be avoided in economic analysis, stressing the importance of forward-looking decision-making.
*   **Park (2001):** Similar to others, Park would highlight the behavioural aspect of the sunk cost fallacy and its detrimental effects on engineering project management.

### 5. Practical Application and Decision-Making Framework

When evaluating a project or a set of alternatives, follow this framework:

1.  **Identify all relevant costs and benefits** for each alternative.
2.  **Exclude all sunk costs** from the analysis.
3.  **Focus on future, incremental costs and benefits.**
4.  **Compare the net present value (NPV)** or other appropriate metrics for each alternative.

**Example Scenario:**

A company invested $1 million in developing a new software product. After 2 years of development, it has spent $800,000. The remaining budget for completion is $300,000. Market analysis now indicates that the projected revenue will only cover the *remaining* development costs and initial operating expenses, but will not recoup the initial $800,000 spent.

**Decision:** Should the company complete the software or abandon the project?

**Analysis:**

*   **Sunk Cost:** $800,000 already spent. This cannot be recovered and is irrelevant to the decision of whether to continue.
*   **Future Costs:** $300,000 to complete the project.
*   **Future Benefits:** Projected revenue from the software.

The decision should be based on whether the projected future revenues justify the *future* expenditure of $300,000. If the future revenue from completing the project is less than $300,000, or if there are better investment opportunities for that $300,000 (opportunity cost), the project should be abandoned. The fact that $800,000 was already spent is emotionally compelling but economically irrelevant to this specific go/no-go decision *now*.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO1 (K2):** Understanding the definition and nature of sunk costs contributes to the fundamental understanding of economic concepts.
*   **CO2 (K3):** Applying the concept of sunk costs to ignore past expenditures and focus on future costs and benefits is crucial for developing decision-making capabilities related to costs and revenues. This directly impacts the functioning of firms.
*   **CO4 (K3):** In cost-benefit analysis and capital budgeting, correctly identifying and excluding sunk costs is paramount for making sound investment decisions and solving business problems.

### 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A civil engineering firm spent $50,000 on site surveys and initial design blueprints for a proposed bridge. Further geological testing has revealed that construction costs will be significantly higher than initially estimated. The remaining construction costs are now projected to be $5 million, and the estimated revenue from tolls will only cover ongoing maintenance and operational costs, not recouping the initial $50,000 or the construction costs. Is the $50,000 spent on surveys and blueprints a sunk cost? Why or why not? Should this $50,000 influence the decision to proceed with the bridge construction?

**Answer 1:**
Yes, the $50,000 spent on site surveys and initial design blueprints is a sunk cost. It has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of whether the bridge is built or not. This $50,000 should *not* influence the decision to proceed with construction. The decision should be based solely on whether the future projected revenues justify the future projected costs of construction and operation.

**Question 2:**
An automotive manufacturer has invested $10 million in developing a new electric vehicle. They have a working prototype. However, a competitor has just released a significantly superior electric vehicle that is priced lower. The manufacturer is considering whether to proceed with mass production of their own vehicle. The remaining costs to bring their vehicle to market are estimated at $5 million. If they proceed, they expect to make a profit of $2 million over the product's lifecycle. If they abandon the project, they can salvage some components for $500,000.

What is the sunk cost in this scenario? What is the relevant cost for the decision to proceed with production? Should they proceed with production?

**Answer 2:**
*   **Sunk Cost:** The $10 million already invested in development is a sunk cost.
*   **Relevant Costs for Decision:**
    *   If proceeding with production: $5 million (future cost)
    *   If abandoning: $0 (as the $500,000 salvage value is a future benefit, not a cost).
*   **Decision:** To decide whether to proceed, compare the future profit ($2 million) with the future cost ($5 million). The net future outcome if proceeding is $2 million profit - $5 million cost = -$3 million (a loss). Since the expected future profit is not enough to cover the future costs (and the alternative of salvaging for $500,000 is better than proceeding), they should abandon the project. The $10 million sunk cost is irrelevant.

**Question 3 (Conceptual):**
Explain the phrase: "Don't throw good money after bad," in the context of sunk costs.

**Answer 3:**
This idiom perfectly captures the essence of avoiding the sunk cost fallacy. It advises against continuing to invest resources (money, time, effort) into a failing or suboptimal project simply because significant resources have already been committed. The "good money" being considered for further investment will likely also be "lost" or yield poor returns if the underlying problem or poor decision is not addressed. The focus should always be on the future prospects and net benefits of continued investment, not on the past investment.

---

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Sunk costs are history.** They have been incurred and cannot be recovered.
*   **Rational decisions are forward-looking.** They must only consider future costs and benefits.
*   **The sunk cost fallacy** is the error of letting past, unrecoverable costs influence present decisions.
*   **Always ask:** "What are the future costs and benefits of each option *from this point forward*?"
*   In engineering economics, correctly identifying and **ignoring sunk costs** is fundamental to accurate project evaluation and capital budgeting.
