# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - MODULE 4: VALUE ANALYSIS AND VALUE ENGINEERING

## TOPIC: VALUE ENGINEERING PROCEDURE

### INTRODUCTION

Value Engineering (VE) is a systematic, organized approach to providing the necessary functions in a project at the lowest possible cost without sacrificing quality, performance, or reliability. It is a creative, organized effort which analyzes the functions of products, systems, or processes for the purpose of achieving improvements in performance, reliability, maintainability, safety, and aesthetics, and reducing costs.

This procedure is a structured methodology to achieve value improvement, aligning with **CO4** by enabling the application of value analysis and engineering principles to solve business problems.

### 1. THE VE JOB PLAN: A SYSTEMATIC APPROACH

The VE procedure is typically executed through a structured plan known as the **VE Job Plan**. While variations exist, a common and effective VE Job Plan consists of the following phases:

*   **Information Phase**
*   **Function Analysis Phase**
*   **Creative Phase**
*   **Evaluation Phase**
*   **Development Phase**
*   **Presentation Phase**
*   **Implementation Phase**
*   **Audit Phase**

---

### 2. DETAILED BREAKDOWN OF THE VE JOB PLAN PHASES

#### 2.1. INFORMATION PHASE

**Objective:** To gather all relevant information about the project, product, system, or process being analyzed. This phase focuses on understanding *what* is being done and *why*.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Define the Scope:** Clearly identify the boundaries of the VE study. What specific product, system, or process will be analyzed?
*   **Gather Product/Project Data:** Collect comprehensive data, including:
    *   **Drawings and Specifications:** Detailed technical descriptions.
    *   **Cost Data:** Material costs, labor costs, manufacturing overhead, design costs, etc. (Refer to **Paneerselvam, 2012** for cost concepts).
    *   **Performance Data:** How the product/system functions, its efficiency, durability, reliability.
    *   **Usage Data:** How and where it is used, user feedback.
    *   **Manufacturing Processes:** Detailed steps involved in production.
    *   **Market Information:** Competitor analysis, customer requirements, pricing.
    *   **Historical Data:** Past performance, previous issues, and improvements.
*   **Understand the "Why":** Determine the original design intent and the current operational goals. What problem is it solving? What needs is it fulfilling?
*   **Identify Key Stakeholders:** Who are the designers, manufacturers, users, and customers?

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Scope Definition:** Crucial for focusing the VE effort.
*   **Data Collection:** Accuracy and completeness are paramount.
*   **Understanding Function:** Laying the groundwork for the next phase.

**Example:** If analyzing a chair, the information phase would involve gathering its design drawings, material costs (wood, screws, fabric), manufacturing labor hours, weight, load-bearing capacity, ergonomic studies, user feedback on comfort, and its intended use (office chair, dining chair, etc.).

---

#### 2.2. FUNCTION ANALYSIS PHASE

**Objective:** To identify and analyze the functions performed by the product, system, or process and their associated costs. This phase focuses on *how* it is being done and the *value* of those functions.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Define Functions:** Identify all functions performed by the entity being studied. Functions can be classified as:
    *   **Primary Functions:** Essential functions that the product/system must perform to fulfill its basic purpose (e.g., a chair's primary function is to support a person).
    *   **Secondary Functions:** Functions that are not essential to the basic purpose but contribute to usability, aesthetics, or convenience (e.g., a chair's secondary function might be being stackable or having armrests).
*   **Functional Cost Analysis:** Assign costs to each identified function. This involves breaking down the total cost into costs associated with each function.
*   **Functional Worth Analysis:** Determine the "worth" of each function, independent of its cost. Worth is the lowest cost to achieve that function reliably. This often involves benchmarking against alternative solutions.
*   **Pareto Analysis (80/20 Rule):** Identify functions that consume the most cost or are least worthy. This helps prioritize areas for improvement.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Functions:** What a product/system *does*.
*   **Worth vs. Cost:** Worth is what the function *should* cost, while cost is what it *does* cost. The difference indicates potential for value improvement.
*   **FAST Diagramming (Function Analysis Systems Technique):** A graphical tool to visualize the relationships between functions, often used in this phase.

**Example:** For the chair, the primary function is "support person." Secondary functions might include "provide comfort," "be aesthetically pleasing," "be movable." Functional cost analysis would allocate costs: support person (frame, legs, seat) costs X, comfort (cushioning) costs Y, aesthetics (finish, style) costs Z. Functional worth analysis might determine that the "support person" function *should* cost less, indicating an opportunity for cost reduction in the structure.

---

#### 2.3. CREATIVE PHASE (IDEATION)

**Objective:** To generate a wide range of alternative ideas for achieving the required functions at lower cost or with improved performance. This is the brainstorming phase.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Brainstorming:** Encourage free-flowing ideas without immediate judgment. Techniques include:
    *   **Brainstorming:** Group members contribute ideas spontaneously.
    *   **Brainwriting:** Participants write down ideas individually before sharing.
    *   **Attribute Listing:** Break down the product/system into its attributes and brainstorm improvements for each.
    *   **SCAMPER Technique:** Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse – prompts for idea generation.
*   **Focus on Functions:** Generate ideas specifically aimed at improving the functions identified in the previous phase.
*   **No Idea is Bad:** Emphasize quantity over quality initially.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Creativity and Innovation:** The core of VE.
*   **Quantity of Ideas:** More ideas increase the probability of finding good ones.
*   **Suspension of Judgment:** Crucial for encouraging open thinking.

**Example:** For the chair's "support person" function, creative ideas might include:
*   Using a different, cheaper material for the frame (e.g., plastic instead of wood).
*   Redesigning the frame for fewer components.
*   Using a molded seat instead of a separate cushion and frame.
*   Exploring different manufacturing techniques (e.g., injection molding).

---

#### 2.4. EVALUATION PHASE

**Objective:** To screen and evaluate the generated ideas based on feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and potential value improvement.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Criteria Development:** Establish clear criteria for evaluating ideas, such as:
    *   **Feasibility:** Technical, operational, and logistical viability.
    *   **Cost Savings:** Potential reduction in costs.
    *   **Performance Improvement:** Potential enhancement in quality, reliability, etc.
    *   **Risk Assessment:** Potential downsides or challenges.
    *   **Customer Acceptance:** Will users or customers accept the change?
*   **Screening:** Eliminate unfeasible or low-potential ideas.
*   **Ranking:** Rank the remaining ideas based on the established criteria.
*   **Cost-Benefit Analysis:** Quantify the potential benefits (cost savings, performance improvements) against the costs of implementing the idea. This aligns with **CO4**.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Evaluation Metrics:** Objective measures for decision-making.
*   **Feasibility Study:** Assessing the practicality of an idea.
*   **Cost-Benefit Analysis:** A key decision-making tool in economics for engineers. (Refer to **Paneerselvam, 2012** or **Thuesen & Fabrycky, 1966** for cost-benefit analysis principles).

**Example:** Evaluating the chair ideas:
*   "Plastic frame": Feasible, potentially cheaper material, but check strength and aesthetics. Cost savings moderate.
*   "Molded seat": Feasible for mass production, high initial tooling cost, but could integrate support and comfort, potentially lower assembly cost. Cost savings potentially high.
*   "Fewer frame components": Feasible, likely cost savings in assembly and materials, but need to ensure structural integrity.

---

#### 2.5. DEVELOPMENT PHASE

**Objective:** To refine the selected ideas into detailed proposals, including technical specifications, cost estimates, and implementation plans.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Detailed Design:** Develop detailed drawings, specifications, and prototypes for the chosen ideas.
*   **Cost Estimation:** Conduct thorough cost analysis of the proposed solution, including manufacturing, implementation, and potential rework costs.
*   **Performance Verification:** Test and validate the performance of the proposed solution.
*   **Risk Mitigation:** Develop plans to address any identified risks.
*   **Develop an Implementation Plan:** Outline the steps, timeline, and resources required for implementation.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Prototyping:** Creating a preliminary model to test design.
*   **Life Cycle Costing:** Considering costs throughout the entire life of the product/system. (Refer to **Thuesen & Fabrycky, 1966** for life cycle cost concepts).
*   **Project Planning:** Essential for successful implementation.

**Example:** For the "molded seat" idea for the chair, this phase would involve:
*   Designing the mold and the chair structure.
*   Selecting the specific plastic material and estimating its cost.
*   Estimating the cost of tooling for the mold.
*   Building a prototype of the molded chair and testing its load capacity, comfort, and durability.
*   Developing a plan for how to transition from the old manufacturing process to the new one.

---

#### 2.6. PRESENTATION PHASE

**Objective:** To present the VE proposal to decision-makers, advocating for its adoption.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Prepare a Comprehensive Proposal:** Include the problem statement, analysis performed, proposed solution, benefits (cost savings, performance improvements), risks, costs, and implementation plan.
*   **Clear and Concise Communication:** Present the information in a compelling and understandable manner. Use visuals and data to support the proposal.
*   **Address Concerns:** Be prepared to answer questions and address any concerns from stakeholders.
*   **Quantify Value:** Clearly articulate the expected value improvement, often expressed as a return on investment (ROI).

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Persuasion and Communication Skills:** Essential for gaining approval.
*   **Data-Driven Presentation:** Using facts and figures to support arguments.
*   **Return on Investment (ROI):** A key financial metric for project justification.

**Example:** Presenting the molded chair proposal to management, highlighting the projected cost savings per unit, improved production efficiency, and potential for enhanced customer satisfaction due to comfort and aesthetics, supported by prototype test results.

---

#### 2.7. IMPLEMENTATION PHASE

**Objective:** To put the approved VE proposal into action.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Execute the Implementation Plan:** Carry out the planned steps for redesign, manufacturing changes, procurement, training, etc.
*   **Project Management:** Oversee the project to ensure it stays on track, within budget, and meets objectives.
*   **Change Management:** Address any resistance to change and ensure smooth transition.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Project Execution:** The actual doing of the work.
*   **Resource Management:** Allocating and managing personnel, materials, and equipment.
*   **Stakeholder Management:** Keeping all involved parties informed and engaged.

**Example:** Implementing the molded chair design involves procuring the necessary plastic materials, setting up or modifying the injection molding machinery, training operators, and phasing out the old chair production.

---

#### 2.8. AUDIT PHASE

**Objective:** To verify that the implemented changes have achieved the expected benefits and to capture lessons learned.

**Key Activities:**

*   **Performance Measurement:** Compare the actual results (costs, performance, quality) with the projected benefits.
*   **Identify Discrepancies:** Investigate any deviations from the plan or expected outcomes.
*   **Document Lessons Learned:** Record what worked well and what could be improved for future VE projects.
*   **Formalize Savings:** Officially recognize and document the achieved savings.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Verification and Validation:** Confirming that the solution works as intended.
*   **Continuous Improvement:** Using the audit results to enhance future processes.
*   **Performance Tracking:** Monitoring long-term benefits.

**Example:** After a period of manufacturing and selling the molded chairs, an audit would be conducted to verify the actual cost savings per unit against the original estimates, check customer satisfaction levels for the new design, and assess the overall success of the VE project.

---

### 3. VALUE ENGINEERING VS. VALUE ANALYSIS

While often used interchangeably, there's a subtle distinction:

*   **Value Analysis (VA):** Typically performed *after* a product has been designed and is in production. It focuses on existing products to reduce costs without affecting functionality. (Refer to **Geetika, Ghosh & Chodhury, 2015** for insights on cost reduction strategies).
*   **Value Engineering (VE):** Typically performed *during* the design and development phase of a new product or system. It aims to achieve the desired functions at the lowest cost from the outset.

The **VE Job Plan** described above is applicable to both VA and VE, with adjustments in the information gathering and implementation phases based on whether the project is existing or new.

---

### 4. IMPORTANT POINTS TO REMEMBER

*   **VE is Function-Oriented:** Always start by defining and analyzing functions.
*   **Creativity is Key:** Encourage a broad range of ideas in the creative phase.
*   **Quantify Everything:** Use data for cost, worth, and benefit analysis.
*   **Teamwork:** VE is most effective when conducted by a multidisciplinary team.
*   **Customer Focus:** Always consider the customer's needs and satisfaction.
*   **Systematic Approach:** Follow the structured VE Job Plan for maximum effectiveness.
*   **Continuous Improvement:** VE should be an ongoing process.
*   **Alignment with CO4:** VE directly supports the ability to use possibilities of value analysis and engineering to solve business problems.

### 5. PRACTICE QUESTIONS AND EXERCISES

**Question 1:**
List the main phases of the Value Engineering Job Plan and briefly describe the objective of each phase.

**Answer 1:**
The main phases are:
1.  **Information Phase:** Gather all relevant data about the product/system.
2.  **Function Analysis Phase:** Identify and analyze functions and their costs/worth.
3.  **Creative Phase:** Generate a wide range of alternative ideas.
4.  **Evaluation Phase:** Screen and evaluate ideas based on feasibility and cost-effectiveness.
5.  **Development Phase:** Refine selected ideas into detailed proposals.
6.  **Presentation Phase:** Present the VE proposal to decision-makers.
7.  **Implementation Phase:** Put the approved proposal into action.
8.  **Audit Phase:** Verify the achieved benefits and capture lessons learned.

**Question 2:**
A company is producing a simple plastic bracket that costs $2 per unit to manufacture and sells for $5. The bracket performs the function of "mounting device." An engineer has identified a new material that could reduce the material cost by 40% and a simpler manufacturing process that could reduce labor cost by 30%. The original design has 5 components, while the new design has 3.

a)  Calculate the potential cost savings per unit if both proposed changes are implemented.
b)  What phase of the VE Job Plan would the engineer be in when identifying these changes?
c)  What phase would involve developing the detailed specifications for the new bracket?

**Answer 2:**
Assume original costs: Material = $1.00, Labor = $0.80, Overhead/Other = $0.20. Total = $2.00.

a)  **Potential Cost Savings:**
    *   New Material Cost: $1.00 * (1 - 0.40) = $0.60
    *   New Labor Cost: $0.80 * (1 - 0.30) = $0.56
    *   Assume Overhead remains similar relative to labor/material or conservatively keep it at $0.20.
    *   New Total Cost: $0.60 (material) + $0.56 (labor) + $0.20 (overhead) = $1.36
    *   Cost Savings per Unit: $2.00 - $1.36 = $0.64

b)  The engineer identifying these changes is in the **Creative Phase** (generating alternative ideas).

c)  Developing detailed specifications would be part of the **Development Phase**.

**Question 3 (Conceptual):**
Explain the difference between "Function" and "Worth" in the context of Value Engineering. Provide an example.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Function:** What an item or system *does*. It describes its purpose or performance. Functions are typically verbs.
*   **Worth:** The lowest expected cost to achieve a *specific function reliably*. It's a theoretical cost, not necessarily the actual cost.

**Example:**
Consider a simple car windshield wiper.
*   **Function:** To "remove water from glass."
*   **Worth:** The minimum cost to achieve the "remove water from glass" function. This might be achieved by a simple squeegee or a basic rubber blade mechanism. The current motorized arm, motor, and complex blade assembly might cost significantly more than its worth, indicating an opportunity for VE. (Refer to **Park, 2001** for concepts on functional analysis and worth).
