### 1. Introduction to Elasticity

Elasticity is a fundamental economic concept that measures the responsiveness of one economic variable to a change in another economic variable. In simpler terms, it tells us "how much" one thing changes when another thing changes. For engineers, understanding elasticity is crucial for forecasting demand, setting prices, analyzing the impact of policy changes, and making informed investment decisions.

**Key Concept:**

*   **Responsiveness:** The degree to which a quantity (like demand or supply) changes in relation to a change in a factor (like price, income, or price of related goods).

### 2. Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

**Definition:**

**Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)** measures the percentage change in the quantity demanded of a good or service in response to a percentage change in its price, assuming all other factors remain constant.

**Formula:**

$$
PED = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\% \text{ Change in Price}}
$$

*   **Percentage Change in Quantity Demanded:**
    $$
    \% \Delta Q_d = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_1} \times 100
    $$
*   **Percentage Change in Price:**
    $$
    \% \Delta P = \frac{P_2 - P_1}{P_1} \times 100
    $$

Therefore,
$$
PED = \frac{\frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_1} \times 100}{\frac{P_2 - P_1}{P_1} \times 100} = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_1} \times \frac{P_1}{P_2 - P_1}
$$

**Important Note:** The value of PED is usually negative because of the law of demand (as price increases, quantity demanded decreases). However, economists often refer to the *absolute value* of PED when classifying its elasticity.

**Types of Price Elasticity of Demand:**

| Elasticity Value | Terminology          | Meaning                                                                   | Impact on Total Revenue (TR) when Price Increases | Demand Curve Shape (approximate) |
| :--------------- | :------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------- |
| $|PED| > 1$        | **Elastic Demand**   | Quantity demanded changes more than proportionally to the price change. | TR decreases                                      | Relatively flat                  |
| $|PED| < 1$        | **Inelastic Demand** | Quantity demanded changes less than proportionally to the price change.   | TR increases                                      | Relatively steep                 |
| $|PED| = 1$        | **Unit Elastic**     | Quantity demanded changes proportionally to the price change.           | TR remains constant                               | Rectangular hyperbola            |
| $|PED| = 0$        | **Perfectly Inelastic** | Quantity demanded does not change at all, regardless of price changes. | TR increases                                      | Vertical                         |
| $|PED| = \infty$   | **Perfectly Elastic** | Any increase in price leads to zero quantity demanded.                    | TR decreases to zero                              | Horizontal                       |

**Examples:**

1.  **Elastic Demand:** If the price of a branded smartphone increases by 10%, and the quantity demanded falls by 20%, then:
    $$
    PED = \frac{-20\%}{10\%} = -2
    $$
    The absolute value is 2, which is greater than 1, so demand is elastic. A price increase leads to a decrease in total revenue.

2.  **Inelastic Demand:** If the price of essential medicine increases by 10%, and the quantity demanded falls by only 2%, then:
    $$
    PED = \frac{-2\%}{10\%} = -0.2
    $$
    The absolute value is 0.2, which is less than 1, so demand is inelastic. A price increase leads to an increase in total revenue.

3.  **Unit Elastic:** If the price of a product increases by 5%, and the quantity demanded falls by 5%, then:
    $$
    PED = \frac{-5\%}{5\%} = -1
    $$
    The absolute value is 1, so demand is unit elastic. Total revenue remains unchanged.

4.  **Perfectly Inelastic:** If the price of life-saving equipment doubles, but the quantity needed remains the same, then:
    $$
    PED = \frac{0\%}{\text{any % change}} = 0
    $$
    Demand is perfectly inelastic.

5.  **Perfectly Elastic:** If a company selling a standard commodity (like wheat in a perfectly competitive market) tries to charge even slightly above the market price, no one will buy from them. Any price increase leads to zero demand.
    $$
    PED = \frac{\text{% change in quantity (large negative)}}{\text{% change in price (small positive)}} = -\infty
    $$
    Demand is perfectly elastic.

**Factors Affecting Price Elasticity of Demand:**

*   **Availability of Substitutes:** The more substitutes available, the more elastic the demand. (e.g., if the price of Coca-Cola rises, consumers can switch to Pepsi).
*   **Necessity vs. Luxury:** Necessities tend to have inelastic demand, while luxuries have elastic demand. (e.g., demand for basic food is inelastic, while demand for a sports car is elastic).
*   **Proportion of Income Spent:** Goods that constitute a large proportion of a consumer's income tend to have more elastic demand. (e.g., a rise in the price of rent is more impactful than a rise in the price of chewing gum).
*   **Time Horizon:** Demand tends to be more elastic in the long run than in the short run, as consumers have more time to adjust their consumption patterns and find substitutes.
*   **Definition of the Market:** Broadly defined markets (e.g., "food") have more inelastic demand than narrowly defined markets (e.g., "specific brand of ice cream").

**Application for Engineers:**

*   **Pricing Decisions:** Companies can use PED to predict how price changes will affect sales volume and total revenue. For instance, if a product has elastic demand, a price increase could lead to a significant drop in sales, potentially reducing revenue.
*   **Forecasting:** Understanding the elasticity of demand for a product helps engineers forecast sales based on expected price changes or market conditions.
*   **Government Policies:** Engineers involved in infrastructure projects or public utilities might need to assess the elasticity of demand for services like electricity or water to advise on pricing and capacity planning. For example, understanding the PED of electricity can help in planning for demand response programs.

*   **CO1 (K2):** Understands the concept of elasticity and how it measures responsiveness.

### 3. Income Elasticity of Demand (IED)

**Definition:**

**Income Elasticity of Demand (IED)** measures the percentage change in the quantity demanded of a good or service in response to a percentage change in consumers' income, assuming all other factors remain constant.

**Formula:**

$$
IED = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\% \text{ Change in Income}}
$$

$$
IED = \frac{\frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_1} \times 100}{\frac{Y_2 - Y_1}{Y_1} \times 100} = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_1} \times \frac{Y_1}{Y_2 - Y_1}
$$
Where $Y$ represents income.

**Types of Income Elasticity of Demand:**

| Elasticity Value | Classification      | Meaning                                                                                             | Example                                       |
| :--------------- | :------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- |
| $IED > 0$        | **Normal Good**     | As income rises, the quantity demanded rises.                                                       | Luxury goods, electronics, cars               |
| $0 < IED < 1$    | **Inferior Good**   | As income rises, the quantity demanded falls. Consumers switch to higher-quality goods.             | Generic brands, second-hand goods             |
| $IED < 0$        | **Necessity Good**  | As income rises, the quantity demanded rises, but less than proportionally to the income rise.      | Basic food items, utilities                   |
| $IED = 0$        | **No Relationship** | Quantity demanded does not change with income.                                                      | (Rare; perhaps very specific, non-essential items) |

**Examples:**

1.  **Normal Good (Luxury):** If consumer income increases by 10%, and the demand for new cars increases by 15%, then:
    $$
    IED = \frac{15\%}{10\%} = 1.5
    $$
    Since $IED > 1$, cars are considered luxury goods.

2.  **Normal Good (Necessity):** If consumer income increases by 10%, and the demand for basic food items increases by 5%, then:
    $$
    IED = \frac{5\%}{10\%} = 0.5
    $$
    Since $0 < IED < 1$, basic food items are considered necessities.

3.  **Inferior Good:** If consumer income increases by 10%, and the demand for generic brand rice decreases by 5%, then:
    $$
    IED = \frac{-5\%}{10\%} = -0.5
    $$
    Since $IED < 0$, generic brand rice is an inferior good.

**Application for Engineers:**

*   **Product Development and Forecasting:** Understanding IED helps engineers identify which products will experience increased demand during economic booms (luxury goods) and which might see declining demand as incomes rise (inferior goods). This is vital for planning production capacity and R&D investments.
*   **Market Analysis:** For engineers working on products or services that cater to specific income segments, IED analysis helps in understanding market growth potential. For example, predicting demand for high-end construction materials in a region with rising incomes.
*   **Economic Cycles:** Engineers can anticipate shifts in demand for their company's products based on macroeconomic forecasts of income growth or recession.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO1 (K2):** Understands how income changes affect demand.
*   **CO2 (K3):** Can analyze how IED influences revenue and business strategy.

### 4. Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED)

**Definition:**

**Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED)** measures the percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good in response to a percentage change in the price of another good.

**Formula:**

$$
XED_{AB} = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Demanded of Good A}}{\% \text{ Change in Price of Good B}}
$$

$$
XED_{AB} = \frac{\frac{\Delta Q_A}{Q_A} \times 100}{\frac{\Delta P_B}{P_B} \times 100} = \frac{\Delta Q_A}{Q_A} \times \frac{P_B}{\Delta P_B}
$$
Where A and B are two different goods.

**Types of Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand:**

| Elasticity Value | Relationship          | Meaning                                                                               | Example                                      |
| :--------------- | :-------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------- |
| $XED > 0$        | **Substitute Goods**  | If the price of Good B increases, the quantity demanded of Good A increases.          | Butter and Margarine; Coffee and Tea       |
| $XED < 0$        | **Complementary Goods** | If the price of Good B increases, the quantity demanded of Good A decreases.          | Cars and Gasoline; Printers and Ink Cartridges |
| $XED = 0$        | **Unrelated Goods**   | Changes in the price of Good B have no effect on the quantity demanded of Good A. | Cars and Bread; Steel and Bananas          |

**Examples:**

1.  **Substitute Goods:** If the price of coffee increases by 10%, and the demand for tea increases by 5%, then:
    $$
    XED_{\text{Tea, Coffee}} = \frac{5\%}{10\%} = 0.5
    $$
    Since $XED > 0$, tea and coffee are substitutes.

2.  **Complementary Goods:** If the price of gasoline increases by 10%, and the demand for cars decreases by 8%, then:
    $$
    XED_{\text{Cars, Gasoline}} = \frac{-8\%}{10\%} = -0.8
    $$
    Since $XED < 0$, cars and gasoline are complements.

3.  **Unrelated Goods:** If the price of steel increases by 10%, and the demand for bananas remains unchanged, then:
    $$
    XED_{\text{Bananas, Steel}} = \frac{0\%}{10\%} = 0
    $$
    Since $XED = 0$, steel and bananas are unrelated.

**Application for Engineers:**

*   **Product Strategy:** Engineers need to understand how the demand for their company's products is affected by the prices of competing or complementary products. This informs decisions about product bundling, pricing of related products, and competitive analysis.
*   **New Product Introduction:** When introducing a new product, engineers can analyze potential cross-price elasticities with existing products in the market to gauge adoption rates and potential cannibalization or synergy effects.
*   **System Design:** In integrated systems, understanding complementary relationships is vital. For example, if an engineer designs a printing system, they must consider the price elasticity of ink cartridges (a complement) when setting prices for printers or cartridges.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO1 (K2):** Understands how the price of one good affects the demand for another.
*   **CO2 (K3):** Can use XED to analyze competitive landscapes and strategic pricing of related products.

### 5. Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

**Definition:**

**Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)** measures the percentage change in the quantity supplied of a good or service in response to a percentage change in its price, assuming all other factors remain constant.

**Formula:**

$$
PES = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Supplied}}{\% \text{ Change in Price}}
$$

$$
PES = \frac{\frac{Q_s2 - Q_s1}{Q_s1} \times 100}{\frac{P_2 - P_1}{P_1} \times 100} = \frac{Q_s2 - Q_s1}{Q_s1} \times \frac{P_1}{P_2 - P_1}
$$
Where $Q_s$ represents quantity supplied.

**Note:** PES is typically positive because of the law of supply (as price increases, quantity supplied increases).

**Types of Price Elasticity of Supply:**

| Elasticity Value | Terminology         | Meaning                                                                    | Demand Curve Shape (approximate) |
| :--------------- | :------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| $PES > 1$        | **Elastic Supply**  | Quantity supplied changes more than proportionally to the price change.    | Relatively flat                  |
| $PES < 1$        | **Inelastic Supply**| Quantity supplied changes less than proportionally to the price change.    | Relatively steep                 |
| $PES = 1$        | **Unit Elastic**    | Quantity supplied changes proportionally to the price change.              | Straight line through origin     |
| $PES = 0$        | **Perfectly Inelastic** | Quantity supplied does not change at all, regardless of price changes. | Vertical                         |
| $PES = \infty$   | **Perfectly Elastic** | Any price increase leads to infinite quantity supplied (or zero if below). | Horizontal                       |

**Factors Affecting Price Elasticity of Supply:**

*   **Time Horizon:** Supply is generally more elastic in the long run than in the short run. Producers have more time to adjust production levels, build new capacity, or enter/exit the market.
*   **Availability of Inputs:** If inputs (labor, raw materials) are readily available and easy to obtain, supply tends to be more elastic. If inputs are scarce or specialized, supply is more inelastic.
*   **Mobility of Factors of Production:** If factors of production can be easily shifted from one use to another, supply will be more elastic.
*   **Inventory Levels:** Firms with high inventory can respond more quickly to price changes, leading to more elastic supply.
*   **Production Capacity:** If firms are operating below full capacity, they can increase output more easily in response to price increases, leading to more elastic supply. If they are at full capacity, supply will be more inelastic in the short term.

**Examples:**

1.  **Elastic Supply:** Consider a manufacturer of custom furniture. If the price of custom furniture increases, they can easily shift production from standard furniture lines or hire more workers, leading to a significant increase in the quantity supplied.
2.  **Inelastic Supply:** Consider the supply of agricultural products like wheat. In the short term, farmers cannot easily increase the amount of wheat planted or harvested, even if prices rise substantially. The supply is inelastic. However, over several seasons, farmers can adjust acreage, making supply more elastic in the long run.
3.  **Perfectly Inelastic Supply:** A unique piece of art or a limited edition collectible might have perfectly inelastic supply, as the quantity available is fixed regardless of price.

**Application for Engineers:**

*   **Production Planning:** Engineers involved in operations and manufacturing can use PES to understand how changes in market prices will affect their ability to meet demand. If supply is inelastic, price increases might be necessary to incentivize higher production.
*   **Capacity Decisions:** Understanding PES helps engineers justify investments in expanding production capacity. If demand is expected to grow and supply is inelastic, then investing in more capacity can be profitable.
*   **Supply Chain Management:** For components or raw materials, understanding the PES of suppliers helps in planning for potential price volatility and ensuring supply chain resilience. For instance, if a critical component has inelastic supply, engineers might explore alternative materials or develop in-house production capabilities.
*   **Project Management:** In construction or large engineering projects, the availability and cost of specialized labor and materials can impact the elasticity of the project's completion timeline or cost in response to changes in demand or regulatory requirements.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam (2012):** This textbook likely links elasticity concepts to cost analysis, production, and investment decisions, providing practical examples for engineers.
*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky (1966):** While older, this foundational text might discuss the impact of price and demand on engineering project feasibility and resource allocation.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO1 (K2):** Understands the concept of PES and how supply responds to price.
*   **CO2 (K3):** Can use PES to inform production planning and capacity decisions.

### 6. Elasticity and Total Revenue

The relationship between price elasticity of demand and total revenue (TR) is a critical concept for business decisions. Total Revenue is calculated as Price (P) × Quantity Demanded (Q).

*   **Elastic Demand ($|PED| > 1$):**
    *   If price increases, quantity demanded falls by a larger percentage.
    *   **Result:** Total Revenue decreases.
    *   If price decreases, quantity demanded rises by a larger percentage.
    *   **Result:** Total Revenue increases.
    *   *Strategy:* To increase TR, lower prices.

*   **Inelastic Demand ($|PED| < 1$):**
    *   If price increases, quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage.
    *   **Result:** Total Revenue increases.
    *   If price decreases, quantity demanded rises by a smaller percentage.
    *   **Result:** Total Revenue decreases.
    *   *Strategy:* To increase TR, raise prices.

*   **Unit Elastic Demand ($|PED| = 1$):**
    *   If price changes, quantity demanded changes by the same percentage.
    *   **Result:** Total Revenue remains constant.
    *   *Strategy:* Price changes do not affect TR.

**Important Point to Remember:** For engineers involved in sales, marketing, or product management, understanding this relationship is key to optimizing pricing strategies to maximize revenue.

**Example:**

Imagine a software product with the following scenarios:

1.  **Scenario 1 (Elastic Demand):** Price = $100, Quantity = 1000 units. TR = $100,000.
    If price increases to $110 (+10%), and demand falls to 800 units (-20%). New TR = $110 * 800 = $88,000. TR decreased. ($|PED| = |-20%/10%| = 2$)
2.  **Scenario 2 (Inelastic Demand):** Price = $100, Quantity = 1000 units. TR = $100,000.
    If price increases to $110 (+10%), and demand falls to 950 units (-5%). New TR = $110 * 950 = $104,500. TR increased. ($|PED| = |-5%/10%| = 0.5$)

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO2 (K3):** This directly addresses the ability to develop decision-making capabilities by applying concepts relating to costs and revenue, specifically how elasticity impacts revenue.

### 7. Other Forms of Elasticity (Briefly)

While PED, IED, and XED are the most common, other elasticities exist and can be relevant.

*   **Advertising Elasticity of Demand:** Measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to changes in advertising expenditure.
    *   Formula: $(\% \Delta Q_d) / (\% \Delta \text{Advertising Expenditure})$
    *   Useful for marketing and product launch decisions.
*   **Elasticity of Production:** Measures the responsiveness of output to a change in a specific input (e.g., labor or capital). This is a core concept in production functions and is closely related to concepts like marginal product.
    *   Formula: $(\% \Delta \text{Output}) / (\% \Delta \text{Input})$
    *   Crucial for production engineers and process optimization.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO1 (K2):** Broadens the understanding of elasticity beyond just price and income.

### 8. Application of Elasticity in Engineering Contexts

*   **Infrastructure Pricing:** For public utilities like water, electricity, or public transport, understanding the elasticity of demand helps in setting user charges that balance revenue needs with social equity and demand management. For example, if electricity demand is inelastic, utility companies might use tiered pricing to encourage conservation.
*   **Product Lifecycle Management:** As a product matures, its demand elasticity might change. Engineers need to be aware of this to adjust pricing and production strategies.
*   **Technology Adoption:** The elasticity of demand for new technologies can be influenced by factors like price, perceived utility, availability of substitutes, and network effects. Engineers involved in new product development need to assess these.
*   **Material Science and Engineering:** The PES of raw materials can affect manufacturing costs and the feasibility of scaling up production. Engineers might need to source materials with more elastic supply to ensure stable production.
*   **Policy Analysis:** Engineers working in areas like environmental policy or energy regulation might need to analyze the elasticity of demand for fossil fuels or renewable energy sources to evaluate the impact of taxes, subsidies, or regulations.

### Summary and Key Takeaways

*   **Elasticity** is about responsiveness – how much one variable changes when another changes.
*   **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)**: Measures how quantity demanded changes with price. Critical for pricing decisions and revenue management.
    *   $|PED| > 1$: Elastic (price up, TR down)
    *   $|PED| < 1$: Inelastic (price up, TR up)
    *   $|PED| = 1$: Unit Elastic (TR constant)
*   **Income Elasticity of Demand (IED)**: Measures how quantity demanded changes with income. Helps classify goods (normal, inferior, luxury) and forecast demand during economic cycles.
*   **Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED)**: Measures how the demand for one good changes with the price of another. Identifies substitutes ($XED > 0$) and complements ($XED < 0$). Crucial for competitive strategy.
*   **Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)**: Measures how quantity supplied changes with price. Important for production planning, capacity decisions, and understanding market supply dynamics.
    *   More elastic supply means quicker and larger responses to price changes.
    *   Time horizon, input availability, and capacity are key determinants of PES.
*   **Factors Influencing Elasticities:** Availability of substitutes, necessity vs. luxury, proportion of income, time horizon, and production constraints are crucial considerations.
*   **Engineering Relevance:** Elasticity is not just an economist's tool; it's vital for engineers in pricing, forecasting, production planning, capacity management, technology adoption, and policy analysis.

### Practice Questions

1.  **Question:** A company is considering increasing the price of its engineering software from $200 to $220. They estimate that this will reduce the number of units sold from 10,000 to 9,000.
    a) Calculate the Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) for the software.
    b) Based on your calculation, is the demand for the software elastic or inelastic?
    c) What effect will the price increase have on the company's total revenue?

2.  **Question:** During a recession, consumer income falls by 5%. The demand for economy cars increases by 2%, while the demand for luxury electric vehicles decreases by 8%.
    a) Calculate the Income Elasticity of Demand (IED) for economy cars and luxury electric vehicles.
    b) Classify economy cars and luxury electric vehicles based on your IED calculations.

3.  **Question:** The price of a specific type of sensor used in industrial automation increases by 15%. As a result, the demand for complementary control systems decreases by 10%.
    a) Calculate the Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED) between the sensors and the control systems.
    b) Are sensors and control systems complements or substitutes?

4.  **Question:** A manufacturing plant can increase its production of a particular component by 20% in response to a 10% price increase. What is the Price Elasticity of Supply (PES) for this component? Is the supply elastic or inelastic?

5.  **Question:** A civil engineering firm is analyzing the demand for its design services. They believe demand is highly elastic. If they want to increase their total revenue, should they increase or decrease their prices? Explain your reasoning.

---

### Answers to Practice Questions

1.  **Answer:**
    a) **PED Calculation:**
    $\% \Delta P = \frac{220 - 200}{200} \times 100 = \frac{20}{200} \times 100 = 10\%$
    $\% \Delta Q_d = \frac{9000 - 10000}{10000} \times 100 = \frac{-1000}{10000} \times 100 = -10\%$
    $PED = \frac{-10\%}{10\%} = -1$

    b) **Elasticity Classification:** The absolute value of PED is $|-1| = 1$. This means the demand is **unit elastic**.

    c) **Effect on Total Revenue:** When demand is unit elastic, a change in price leads to an equal and opposite percentage change in quantity demanded. Therefore, the **total revenue will remain approximately the same**.
    *   Initial TR = $200 * 10,000 = $2,000,000$
    *   New TR = $220 * 9,000 = $1,980,000$
    (Note: The change is minimal, indicating unit elasticity. In a real-world scenario, exact unit elasticity is rare, and the calculation might show a slight increase or decrease due to rounding or the discrete nature of the change).

2.  **Answer:**
    a) **IED Calculations:**
    *   **Economy Cars:**
        $\% \Delta \text{Income} = -5\%$
        $\% \Delta Q_d (\text{Economy Cars}) = 2\%$
        $IED_{\text{Economy Cars}} = \frac{2\%}{-5\%} = -0.4$

    *   **Luxury Electric Vehicles:**
        $\% \Delta \text{Income} = -5\%$
        $\% \Delta Q_d (\text{Luxury EVs}) = -8\%$
        $IED_{\text{Luxury EVs}} = \frac{-8\%}{-5\%} = 1.6$

    b) **Classification:**
    *   **Economy Cars:** Since $IED < 0$, economy cars are **inferior goods**. People buy more of them when incomes fall (or less when incomes rise).
    *   **Luxury Electric Vehicles:** Since $IED > 1$, luxury EVs are **luxury goods** (a type of normal good). People buy significantly more of them when incomes rise.

3.  **Answer:**
    a) **XED Calculation:**
    $\% \Delta P_{\text{Sensors}} = 15\%$
    $\% \Delta Q_d (\text{Control Systems}) = -10\%$
    $XED_{\text{Sensors, Control Systems}} = \frac{-10\%}{15\%} = -0.67$ (approximately)

    b) **Relationship:** Since $XED < 0$, sensors and control systems are **complementary goods**. This means they are used together, and an increase in the price of one leads to a decrease in the demand for the other.

4.  **Answer:**
    **PES Calculation:**
    $\% \Delta Q_s = 20\%$
    $\% \Delta P = 10\%$
    $PES = \frac{20\%}{10\%} = 2$

    The supply is **elastic** because $PES > 1$. The quantity supplied changes more than proportionally to the change in price.

5.  **Answer:** If the firm believes demand is highly elastic, and they want to increase their total revenue, they should **decrease their prices**.
    **Reasoning:** For elastic demand ($|PED| > 1$), a decrease in price leads to a proportionally larger increase in quantity demanded. This results in an increase in total revenue. (e.g., If price drops by 10% and demand increases by 20%, total revenue will increase).
