# Materials Testing Lab: Module 2 - Compression Test on Ductile Materials

## Topic: Conduct Compression Test on Ductile Materials (Mild Steel, Tor-steel, or High-Strength Steel) using a UTM with Extensometer

This module focuses on understanding the behavior of ductile materials under compression. We will explore the experimental procedure, data analysis, and the interpretation of results obtained from a Universal Testing Machine (UTM) equipped with an extensometer.

---

### 1. Introduction to Compression Testing

**Definition:** Compression testing is a method used to determine a material's resistance to compressive forces. It measures how a material deforms and fails when subjected to a pushing load.

**Importance:**
*   Understanding material behavior under axial compressive loads is crucial for designing structural components, machine parts, and materials used in various engineering applications (e.g., columns, shafts, fasteners, packaging).
*   While tensile testing is more common for ductile materials, compression testing provides vital information about their behavior in scenarios where tensile failure might not occur.

**Relevance to Ductile Materials (Mild Steel, Tor-steel, High-Strength Steel):**
Ductile materials can undergo significant plastic deformation before fracture. In compression, they typically exhibit yielding and barreling (bulging) rather than brittle fracture. Understanding these behaviors is key for their application.

### 3. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Compression:** An axial force that tends to shorten or squeeze an object.
*   **Stress (σ):** Force per unit area. In compression, $\sigma_c = \frac{P}{A_0}$, where $P$ is the applied compressive load and $A_0$ is the original cross-sectional area.
*   **Strain (ε):** The deformation of a material in response to stress. In compression, $\varepsilon_c = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0}$, where $\Delta L$ is the change in length and $L_0$ is the original length.
*   **Yield Strength (Compression):** The stress at which a material begins to deform plastically. For ductile materials, it's often determined from the stress-strain curve. (Callister, Ch 6; Kuhn & Medlin, Ch 3)
*   **Ultimate Compressive Strength:** The maximum compressive stress a material can withstand before it begins to flow or exhibit significant barreling. This is often less defined for ductile materials compared to their ultimate tensile strength because they tend to bulge rather than fracture.
*   **Modulus of Elasticity (Young's Modulus, E):** The ratio of stress to strain in the elastic region. $E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$ (in the elastic region). In compression, it's often very similar to the Young's Modulus in tension for many materials. (Callister, Ch 6)
*   **Ductility (in Compression):** The ability of a material to deform plastically under compression. This is often characterized by the amount of strain the material can withstand before fracture or significant barreling occurs.
*   **Barreling:** The outward bulging of the sides of a cylindrical specimen under compression due to friction at the loading platens. This is a common phenomenon in compression tests. (Kuhn & Medlin, Ch 3)
*   **Universal Testing Machine (UTM):** A machine capable of performing various mechanical tests, including tensile, compression, bending, and shear, by applying controlled forces and measuring deformation.
*   **Extensometer:** An instrument used to accurately measure the strain (deformation) of a specimen during a mechanical test. In compression, it typically clips onto the specimen to measure elongation or contraction. (Kuhn & Medlin, Ch 2)
*   **Stress-Strain Curve (Compression):** A graphical representation of the relationship between compressive stress and compressive strain for a material. It provides information about elastic behavior, yielding, plastic deformation, and ultimate strength.

### 4. Materials Used in Compression Testing

*   **Mild Steel:** Low carbon steel, known for its ductility and moderate strength. Widely used in construction and manufacturing. Exhibits significant plastic deformation before fracture.
*   **Tor-steel (Reinforcing Bar):** Typically has a higher yield strength than mild steel and often exhibits a distinct yield point, followed by significant plastic deformation. Its surface texture can also influence behavior.
*   **High-Strength Steel:** Steel alloys designed for superior strength, often achieved through heat treatments and alloying elements. May exhibit less ductility than mild steel but can withstand higher compressive loads.

**Callister (Ch 6) and Kuhn & Medlin (Ch 3) discuss the mechanical properties and testing of steels, providing context for the behavior of these materials.**

### 5. Universal Testing Machine (UTM) and Extensometer

**Universal Testing Machine (UTM):**
*   **Components:**
    *   **Load Frame:** Provides structural support and houses the loading mechanism.
    *   **Loading Actuator/Ram:** Applies the force (hydraulic or electro-mechanical).
    *   **Load Cell:** Measures the applied force accurately.
    *   **Crosshead:** Moves up or down to apply the load.
    *   **Control System:** Manages the test parameters (load rate, displacement rate, data acquisition).
    *   **Grips/Fixtures:** Hold the specimen. For compression, these are typically flat, rigid platens.
*   **Operation:** The UTM applies a controlled load (either displacement-controlled or load-controlled) to the specimen placed between the platens. The load cell continuously records the applied force.

**Extensometer for Compression:**
*   **Purpose:** To precisely measure the change in length (strain) of the specimen during the test.
*   **Types:**
    *   **Clip-on Extensometers:** Attach directly to the specimen. They have precise measurement capabilities and are ideal for capturing the elastic and plastic deformation accurately. For compression, they are typically designed to measure the reduction in length.
    *   **LVDTs (Linear Variable Differential Transformers):** Can be mounted to measure displacement between two points on the specimen or on the machine's crosshead.
*   **Measurement:** The extensometer measures the change in length over a specific gauge length on the specimen. This measured displacement, when divided by the original gauge length, gives the strain.

**Kuhn & Medlin (Ch 2) provides detailed information on various extensometer types and their applications.**

### 6. Specimen Preparation for Compression Test

*   **Geometry:** Cylindrical specimens are most common. The aspect ratio (height-to-diameter ratio) is crucial.
    *   **Common Ratios:** Often 2:1 or 1:1 for height:diameter. A higher aspect ratio can lead to buckling. (ASTM E9 standard recommendations)
    *   **Standardization:** Refer to relevant ASTM standards (e.g., ASTM E9 - Standard Test Methods of Compression Testing of Metallic Materials at Room Temperature) for precise dimensions and tolerances.
*   **End Faces:** The ends of the specimen must be flat, parallel, and perpendicular to the specimen axis. This ensures uniform stress distribution. Machining or grinding is required.
*   **Surface Finish:** A smooth surface finish is desirable to minimize stress concentrations and ensure good contact with the platens.
*   **Dimensions:** Accurate measurement of the original diameter and height of the specimen is critical for calculating stress and strain. Use a micrometer.

**Example:** For a 10 mm diameter cylindrical specimen, a height of 20 mm (2:1 ratio) or 10 mm (1:1 ratio) would be typical.

### 7. Performing the Compression Test

1.  **Machine Setup:**
    *   Select the appropriate compression platens for the UTM.
    *   Ensure the UTM is calibrated and in good working condition.
    *   Set up the data acquisition system.
2.  **Specimen Placement:**
    *   Measure and record the original dimensions (diameter, height) of the specimen accurately.
    *   Place the specimen centrally between the UTM's compression platens.
    *   Ensure the specimen is aligned perpendicular to the platens.
3.  **Extensometer Attachment:**
    *   If using a clip-on extensometer, attach it securely to the specimen, ensuring it spans the desired gauge length. For compression, the extensometer will measure the reduction in length.
    *   Ensure the extensometer is properly zeroed before the test begins.
4.  **Test Execution:**
    *   Start the UTM at a controlled displacement or load rate according to the standard or lab protocol.
    *   The UTM will apply the compressive load, and the extensometer will record the corresponding strain.
    *   The data acquisition system will record load and displacement (from the extensometer) simultaneously.
5.  **Monitoring:**
    *   Observe the specimen's behavior as the load increases. Note any signs of deformation (barreling).
    *   Continue the test until fracture (rare in ductile materials) or until significant barreling and plastic flow occur, or to a predetermined strain level.
6.  **Data Recording:**
    *   Save the recorded data (load vs. extensometer displacement) from the UTM.
    *   Record any observations made during the test.

### 8. Data Analysis and Interpretation

1.  **Calculate Stress and Strain:**
    *   **Engineering Stress ($\sigma_c$):** $\sigma_c = \frac{P}{A_0}$ (where $P$ is the measured load and $A_0$ is the original cross-sectional area).
    *   **Engineering Strain ($\varepsilon_c$):** $\varepsilon_c = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0}$ (where $\Delta L$ is the change in length measured by the extensometer and $L_0$ is the original gauge length).

2.  **Plot the Stress-Strain Curve:**
    *   Plot engineering stress ($\sigma_c$) on the y-axis and engineering strain ($\varepsilon_c$) on the x-axis.
    *   **Initial Elastic Region:** The initial linear portion of the curve represents elastic deformation. The slope of this region is the Modulus of Elasticity (E). $E = \frac{\Delta \sigma_c}{\Delta \varepsilon_c}$ (in the elastic region).
    *   **Yielding:** Identify the point where the curve deviates from linearity. This indicates the onset of plastic deformation. For materials with a distinct yield point, this will be a clear plateau. For others, the 0.2% offset method (similar to tension) can be used, although it's less common in compression for ductile materials due to barreling.
    *   **Plastic Deformation:** The region beyond yielding shows permanent deformation. Ductile materials will continue to deform plastically.
    *   **Barreling:** The curve might show a decrease in the rate of stress increase as barreling becomes significant. The stress might not reach a clear "ultimate compressive strength" in the same way as tensile strength because the material continues to flow.
    *   **Fracture (Rare in Compression for Ductile Materials):** Ductile materials usually exhibit flow or barreling rather than fracture under pure compression.

3.  **Determine Mechanical Properties:**
    *   **Modulus of Elasticity (E):** Calculate from the slope of the initial linear portion of the stress-strain curve.
    *   **Yield Strength (Compression):** The stress at which significant plastic deformation begins. This might be the proportional limit or a specified offset yield strength.
    *   **Ultimate Compressive Strength:** For ductile materials in compression, this is often considered the stress at the point of maximum load before significant barreling, or the stress at a specified strain if no distinct maximum occurs. It's crucial to note that this value might be influenced by barreling.

**Example:**
If a specimen has an initial diameter of 10 mm and a gauge length of 20 mm, and during the test, the load reaches 50 kN when the extensometer records a strain of 0.005, then:
*   Original Area ($A_0$) = $\pi (\frac{10 \text{ mm}}{2})^2 = \pi (5 \text{ mm})^2 = 25\pi \text{ mm}^2 \approx 78.54 \text{ mm}^2$
*   Stress ($\sigma_c$) = $\frac{50 \text{ kN}}{78.54 \text{ mm}^2} = \frac{50000 \text{ N}}{78.54 \text{ mm}^2} \approx 636.6 \text{ MPa}$
*   Strain ($\varepsilon_c$) = 0.005

**Callister (Ch 6) and Gere & Goodno (Ch 2) provide the theoretical basis for stress-strain relationships and elastic/plastic behavior.**

### 9. Understanding Barreling

*   **Cause:** Barreling occurs due to friction between the specimen's ends and the compression platens. The material at the center can expand radially more freely than the material at the edges, which is restrained by friction.
*   **Effect on Test Results:**
    *   Increases the apparent compressive strength as the specimen effectively becomes shorter and wider.
    *   Can lead to non-uniform stress distribution within the specimen.
    *   Makes it difficult to define a precise "ultimate compressive strength" or fracture point for ductile materials.
*   **Mitigation:**
    *   **Lubrication:** Using lubricants (e.g., graphite, molybdenum disulfide) between the specimen and platens can reduce friction.
    *   **Specialized Platens:** Using pressure-lubricated platens or rounded ends on specimens can help, but ASTM E9 primarily specifies flat ends for standard tests.
    *   **Appropriate Aspect Ratio:** Using specimens with a smaller height-to-diameter ratio can reduce the tendency to buckle, but doesn't eliminate barreling.

**Kuhn & Medlin (Ch 3) extensively discusses factors affecting compression tests, including barreling.**

### 10. Sources of Error and Best Practices

**Potential Sources of Error:**
*   **Specimen Preparation:** Non-parallel or rough end faces, inaccurate dimensions, presence of surface defects.
*   **Alignment:** Misalignment of the specimen in the UTM can induce bending stresses.
*   **Extensometer Attachment/Calibration:** Incorrectly attached or calibrated extensometer leads to erroneous strain readings.
*   **Barreling:** As discussed, this affects stress distribution and the interpretation of results.
*   **Machine Compliance:** The flexibility of the UTM frame can slightly affect strain readings, especially for stiffer materials.
*   **Temperature Variations:** Though usually conducted at room temperature, significant temperature changes can affect material properties.
*   **Loading Rate:** Too high a loading rate can sometimes lead to dynamic effects or rate sensitivity of the material.

**Best Practices:**
*   **Follow Standards:** Adhere strictly to relevant ASTM (e.g., E9) or ISO standards for specimen dimensions, testing procedures, and data reporting.
*   **Accurate Measurement:** Precisely measure specimen dimensions before the test.
*   **Proper Alignment:** Ensure the specimen is perfectly centered and perpendicular to the platens.
*   **Careful Extensometer Use:** Attach the extensometer securely and ensure it's calibrated and functioning correctly.
*   **Controlled Loading Rate:** Use a consistent and appropriate loading rate.
*   **Record Observations:** Note any unusual behavior of the specimen or machine.
*   **Multiple Specimens:** Test multiple specimens (typically 3-5) to ensure reproducibility and obtain statistically reliable results.
*   **Lubrication (if allowed/specified):** Use appropriate lubricants to minimize barreling, if the test protocol permits.
*   **Post-Test Analysis:** Examine the tested specimen for any signs of premature failure or non-uniform deformation.

### 11. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** What is the primary purpose of using an extensometer in a compression test?
**Answer:** To accurately measure the change in length (strain) of the specimen during the application of load.

**Question 2:** Explain why barreling occurs in the compression testing of ductile materials.
**Answer:** Barreling occurs due to friction between the specimen's ends and the compression platens. The material at the center can deform radially more than the material at the edges, causing it to bulge outwards.

**Question 3:** If a cylindrical specimen of mild steel with an initial diameter of 12.7 mm and an initial gauge length of 25.4 mm undergoes a load of 80 kN at a strain of 0.002, what is the calculated engineering stress?
**Answer:**
*   Original Area ($A_0$) = $\pi (\frac{12.7 \text{ mm}}{2})^2 = \pi (6.35 \text{ mm})^2 \approx 126.68 \text{ mm}^2$
*   Stress ($\sigma_c$) = $\frac{80 \text{ kN}}{126.68 \text{ mm}^2} = \frac{80000 \text{ N}}{126.68 \text{ mm}^2} \approx 631.5 \text{ MPa}$

**Question 4:** Besides mild steel, name two other ductile materials commonly tested in compression.
**Answer:** Tor-steel, Aluminium alloys, Copper alloys, certain plastics.

**Question 5:** What is a significant difference in failure modes between ductile materials tested in tension versus compression?
**Answer:** In tension, ductile materials typically neck down and fracture. In compression, they tend to yield, deform plastically, and exhibit barreling or extrusion rather than a sharp fracture.
