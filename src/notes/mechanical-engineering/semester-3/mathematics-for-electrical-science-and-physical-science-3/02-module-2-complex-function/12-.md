### Introduction

The exponential function $w = e^z$ is a fundamental complex function with wide-ranging applications in electrical engineering, signal processing, and physics. Understanding its properties is crucial for analyzing oscillations, wave phenomena, and solving differential equations. This topic delves into the definition, properties, and geometric interpretation of the complex exponential function.

---

### 1. Definition of the Complex Exponential Function

#### Key Concept: Euler's Formula

The complex exponential function $e^z$ is defined for any complex number $z = x + iy$ using Euler's formula:

$e^z = e^{x + iy} = e^x e^{iy}$

where:
*   $e^x$ is the real exponential function, representing the magnitude.
*   $e^{iy} = \cos(y) + i \sin(y)$ is the complex exponential with purely imaginary argument, representing the phase.

Therefore, the definition is:

**$w = e^z = e^x (\cos(y) + i \sin(y))$**

Here, $x = \text{Re}(z)$ is the real part of $z$, and $y = \text{Im}(z)$ is the imaginary part of $z$.

#### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Real part of $w$:** $\text{Re}(w) = e^x \cos(y)$
*   **Imaginary part of $w$:** $\text{Im}(w) = e^x \sin(y)$
*   **Magnitude of $w$:** $|w| = |e^x (\cos(y) + i \sin(y))| = |e^x| |\cos(y) + i \sin(y)| = e^x \cdot 1 = e^x = e^{\text{Re}(z)}$
*   **Argument of $w$:** $\text{Arg}(w) = y = \text{Im}(z)$ (This is the principal argument if $y$ is chosen in $(-\pi, \pi]$).

### 2. Properties of the Complex Exponential Function

The complex exponential function shares many properties with its real counterpart, but with important nuances due to its complex nature.

#### 2.1 Additive Property (Crucial!)

For any two complex numbers $z_1$ and $z_2$:

**$e^{z_1 + z_2} = e^{z_1} e^{z_2}$**

*   **Proof:**
    Let $z_1 = x_1 + iy_1$ and $z_2 = x_2 + iy_2$.
    $e^{z_1 + z_2} = e^{(x_1+x_2) + i(y_1+y_2)} = e^{x_1+x_2} e^{i(y_1+y_2)}$
    Using Euler's formula: $e^{x_1+x_2} (\cos(y_1+y_2) + i \sin(y_1+y_2))$
    Using trigonometric sum formulas: $e^{x_1+x_2} (\cos y_1 \cos y_2 - \sin y_1 \sin y_2 + i(\sin y_1 \cos y_2 + \cos y_1 \sin y_2))$
    Rearranging terms: $(e^{x_1} \cos y_1 + i e^{x_1} \sin y_1) (e^{x_2} \cos y_2 + i e^{x_2} \sin y_2)$
    This is $e^{z_1} e^{z_2}$.

#### 2.2 Periodicity

The complex exponential function is periodic with a period of $2\pi i$.

**$e^{z + 2\pi i k} = e^z$ for any integer $k$**

*   **Proof:**
    $e^{z + 2\pi i k} = e^z e^{2\pi i k}$
    $e^{2\pi i k} = \cos(2\pi k) + i \sin(2\pi k) = 1 + i \cdot 0 = 1$
    Therefore, $e^{z + 2\pi i k} = e^z \cdot 1 = e^z$.

**Important Point:** Unlike the real exponential, the complex exponential function is *not* one-to-one. Different values of $z$ can map to the same value of $w$.

#### 2.3 Derivative

The derivative of $e^z$ with respect to $z$ is itself.

**$\frac{d}{dz} e^z = e^z$**

This is a fundamental property and implies that $e^z$ is an entire function (analytic everywhere in the complex plane).

*   **Proof (using Cauchy-Riemann equations):**
    Let $f(z) = e^z = e^{x+iy} = e^x (\cos y + i \sin y)$.
    So, $u(x,y) = e^x \cos y$ and $v(x,y) = e^x \sin y$.
    $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = e^x \cos y$, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -e^x \sin y$
    $\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = e^x \sin y$, $\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = e^x \cos y$
    We see that $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$ and $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$. The Cauchy-Riemann equations are satisfied.
    The derivative is $f'(z) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = e^x \cos y + i e^x \sin y = e^x (\cos y + i \sin y) = e^z$.

#### 2.4 Integral

The integral of $e^z$ is also itself (up to a constant of integration).

**$\int e^z dz = e^z + C$**

#### 2.5 Non-zero Property

**$e^z \neq 0$ for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$**

*   **Proof:**
    $|e^z| = |e^{x+iy}| = |e^x e^{iy}| = e^x$. Since $e^x > 0$ for all real $x$, $|e^z|$ is always positive, implying $e^z$ is never zero.

#### 2.6 Relationship with Trigonometric Functions

From Euler's formula: $e^{iy} = \cos y + i \sin y$
Also, $e^{-iy} = \cos(-y) + i \sin(-y) = \cos y - i \sin y$.

Adding these two: $e^{iy} + e^{-iy} = 2 \cos y \implies \cos y = \frac{e^{iy} + e^{-iy}}{2}$
Subtracting the second from the first: $e^{iy} - e^{-iy} = 2i \sin y \implies \sin y = \frac{e^{iy} - e^{-iy}}{2i}$

These relationships are fundamental for expressing trigonometric functions in terms of complex exponentials, which is crucial for Fourier analysis (CO1).

### 3. Geometric Interpretation of $w = e^z$

The mapping $w = e^z$ transforms the complex plane into itself. Understanding how regions are mapped is key to conformal mapping (CO2).

#### Key Concept: Mapping of Lines and Regions

*   **Mapping of a Vertical Line:** Consider $z = x_0 + iy$, where $x_0$ is constant and $y$ varies.
    $w = e^{x_0 + iy} = e^{x_0} e^{iy} = e^{x_0} (\cos y + i \sin y)$
    Let $R = e^{x_0}$ (constant magnitude) and $\theta = y$ (argument).
    So, $w = R(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)$. This represents a circle of radius $R = e^{x_0}$ centered at the origin in the $w$-plane.
    As $y$ varies from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, $w$ traverses this circle infinitely many times.

*   **Mapping of a Horizontal Line:** Consider $z = x + iy_0$, where $y_0$ is constant and $x$ varies.
    $w = e^{x + iy_0} = e^x e^{iy_0} = e^x (\cos y_0 + i \sin y_0)$
    The argument of $w$ is fixed at $\theta = y_0$ (or $y_0 + 2\pi k$). The magnitude $|w| = e^x$ varies as $x$ varies.
    This represents a ray starting from the origin in the $w$-plane, with a fixed angle $y_0$ and extending to infinity.
    If $y_0$ is a multiple of $2\pi$, the ray is along the positive real axis.

*   **Mapping of a Rectangular Region:** Consider a rectangle defined by $x_1 \le x \le x_2$ and $y_1 \le y \le y_2$.
    *   The vertical boundaries $x = x_1$ and $x = x_2$ map to circles of radius $e^{x_1}$ and $e^{x_2}$ respectively.
    *   The horizontal boundaries $y = y_1$ and $y = y_2$ map to rays starting from the origin with angles $y_1$ and $y_2$.
    *   The rectangular region is mapped to a **sector of an annulus** (a region between two concentric circles bounded by two rays from the origin).

*   **Mapping of the Entire Plane:**
    *   The imaginary axis ($x=0$) maps to the unit circle $|w|=1$.
    *   The right half-plane ($x > 0$) maps to the exterior of the unit circle ($|w|>1$).
    *   The left half-plane ($x < 0$) maps to the interior of the unit circle ($|w|<1$).
    *   The upper half-plane ($y > 0$) maps to the upper half of the $w$-plane (argument between 0 and $\pi$).
    *   The strip $0 < y < \pi$ maps to the upper half of the $w$-plane.

**Important Point:** The function $w = e^z$ is conformal everywhere because its derivative $e^z$ is never zero. This means it preserves angles between intersecting curves.

### 4. Learning Outcomes Alignment

*   **CO1: Fourier Transforms:** The relationship $e^{iy} = \cos y + i \sin y$ is foundational for understanding how complex exponentials represent sinusoidal signals. Fourier analysis relies heavily on the property $e^{i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + i \sin(\omega t)$ to decompose signals into frequency components. The additive property $e^{z_1+z_2} = e^{z_1}e^{z_2}$ is also useful in transform manipulations.
*   **CO2: Analyticity and Conformal Mapping:** $w=e^z$ is analytic everywhere in $\mathbb{C}$ because its derivative $e^z$ exists and is continuous for all $z$. Since $f'(z) = e^z \neq 0$ for all $z$, the mapping $w=e^z$ is conformal everywhere. This allows us to map geometric shapes from the $z$-plane to the $w$-plane while preserving angles, a crucial tool in solving boundary value problems in physics and engineering.

### 5. Examples and Practice Questions

#### Example 1: Evaluating $e^z$

Find the value of $e^{1 + i\pi/2}$.
$z = 1 + i\pi/2$. So $x=1$ and $y=\pi/2$.
$e^z = e^1 (\cos(\pi/2) + i \sin(\pi/2)) = e (0 + i \cdot 1) = ie$.

#### Example 2: Finding $z$ given $e^z$

Find all values of $z$ such that $e^z = 1$.
Let $z = x + iy$.
$e^z = e^x (\cos y + i \sin y) = 1$.
This means $e^x \cos y = 1$ and $e^x \sin y = 0$.
From $e^x \sin y = 0$, since $e^x \neq 0$, we must have $\sin y = 0$. This implies $y = n\pi$ for integer $n$.
Substitute this into the first equation: $e^x \cos(n\pi) = 1$.
If $n$ is even, $n=2k$, then $\cos(2k\pi) = 1$. So $e^x \cdot 1 = 1$, which means $e^x = 1$, so $x=0$.
If $n$ is odd, $n=2k+1$, then $\cos((2k+1)\pi) = -1$. So $e^x \cdot (-1) = 1$, which means $e^x = -1$. This has no real solution for $x$.
Therefore, $x=0$ and $y=2k\pi$ for integer $k$.
So, $z = 0 + i(2k\pi) = 2\pi i k$.
This confirms the periodicity of $e^z$.

#### Example 3: Geometric Mapping

What region in the $w$-plane corresponds to the rectangular region $0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le \pi/2$ in the $z$-plane under the mapping $w = e^z$?

*   The vertical lines $x=0$ and $x=1$ map to circles $|w| = e^0 = 1$ and $|w| = e^1 = e$.
*   The horizontal lines $y=0$ and $y=\pi/2$ map to rays starting from the origin with angles $\theta=0$ (positive real axis) and $\theta=\pi/2$ (positive imaginary axis).

Thus, the region is a **sector of an annulus** bounded by circles of radius 1 and $e$, and rays along the positive real and imaginary axes. Specifically, it's the portion of the annulus $\{w : 1 \le |w| \le e\}$ in the first quadrant of the $w$-plane.

#### Practice Questions:

1.  Evaluate $e^{-1 + i\pi}$.
    *   **Answer:** $e^{-1}(\cos \pi + i \sin \pi) = e^{-1}(-1 + 0i) = -e^{-1} = -1/e$.
2.  Find the magnitude and argument of $e^{3+4i}$.
    *   **Answer:** Magnitude: $|e^{3+4i}| = e^3$. Argument: $\text{Arg}(e^{3+4i}) = 4$ radians.
3.  Show that $e^{2z} = (e^z)^2$.
    *   **Answer:** $e^{2z} = e^{z+z} = e^z e^z = (e^z)^2$.
4.  Find all values of $z$ such that $e^z = -1$.
    *   **Answer:** $e^z = e^x(\cos y + i \sin y) = -1 = 1(\cos \pi + i \sin \pi)$.
        $e^x = 1 \implies x=0$.
        $y = \pi + 2k\pi$ for integer $k$.
        So $z = i(\pi + 2k\pi)$.
5.  Describe the image of the strip $\frac{\pi}{4} < y < \frac{3\pi}{4}$ under the mapping $w = e^z$.
    *   **Answer:** This strip maps to a sector of the $w$-plane between angles $\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$, extending infinitely from the origin.
6.  Determine if the function $w = e^z$ is conformal at $z = \pi i$. Justify your answer.
    *   **Answer:** Yes, $w = e^z$ is conformal at $z = \pi i$ because $f'(z) = e^z$, and $f'(\pi i) = e^{\pi i} = \cos \pi + i \sin \pi = -1 \neq 0$.

### 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Definition:** $e^{x+iy} = e^x(\cos y + i \sin y)$.
*   **Magnitude:** $|e^{x+iy}| = e^x$.
*   **Argument:** $\text{Arg}(e^{x+iy}) = y$.
*   **Additive Property:** $e^{z_1+z_2} = e^{z_1}e^{z_2}$. This is the most crucial property.
*   **Periodicity:** $e^{z + 2\pi i k} = e^z$ for any integer $k$. This means $e^z$ is not one-to-one.
*   **Derivative:** $\frac{d}{dz}e^z = e^z$. This means $e^z$ is an entire function.
*   **Non-zero:** $e^z \neq 0$ for all $z$.
*   **Geometric Mapping:** Vertical lines map to circles, horizontal lines map to rays. Rectangles map to sectors of annuli.
*   **Conformality:** $w=e^z$ is conformal everywhere because its derivative is never zero.

### 7. Further Study and Connections to Course Outcomes

*   **CO1 (Fourier Transforms):** The representation of $e^{i\omega t}$ as $\cos(\omega t) + i \sin(\omega t)$ is fundamental. Understanding $w=e^z$ is the first step towards understanding the Fourier series and Fourier transform, which are used to analyze signals and systems in electrical science. The property $e^{a+b} = e^a e^b$ is used extensively in manipulating exponential terms in transform calculus.
*   **CO2 (Analyticity and Conformal Mapping):** The fact that $e^z$ is analytic and has a non-zero derivative ensures it's a conformal mapping. This property is vital for transforming complex domains to simpler ones, which is used to solve various physical problems, like fluid flow or heat distribution, using techniques like the Schwarz-Christoffel transformation (which builds upon basic mappings like $e^z$).
*   **CO3 & CO4 (Complex Integrals and Series):** While not directly used in $w=e^z$ itself, the techniques for evaluating complex integrals (Cauchy's Integral Theorem/Formula) and understanding series expansions (like Taylor and Laurent series) are applied to other complex functions, building upon the foundational understanding of analyticity and function properties established here.
