# Manufacturing Processes: Module 2: Welding - Gas Welding

## Topic: Oxyacetylene Welding - Chemistry, Types of Flame, and Applications

### Introduction to Gas Welding

Gas welding is a fusion welding process where heat is generated by the combustion of a fuel gas with oxygen. The molten metal from the filler rod (if used) and the base metal combine to form a weld joint.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Fusion Welding:** Welding processes that melt the base metals to form the joint.
*   **Fuel Gas:** A gas that burns with oxygen to produce heat (e.g., acetylene, propane, natural gas).
*   **Filler Rod:** A rod of compatible metal used to add material to the weld joint and facilitate fusion.
*   **Flux:** A chemical agent that removes oxides and prevents further oxidation of the molten metal.

### Oxyacetylene Welding (OAW)

Oxyacetylene welding is the most common and versatile type of gas welding. It utilizes the combustion of acetylene gas with oxygen to produce the hottest and most intense flame among common fuel gases.

**Reference:** *Manufacturing Science* by Ghosh and Mallik (2nd Edition) discusses gas welding as a fundamental fusion welding process. *Manufacturing Engineering and Technology* by Kalpakjian and Schmid provides a comprehensive overview of various welding processes, including OAW.

**Learning Outcomes Covered:**

*   **CO3:** Categorize welding processes according to welding principles and materials. (Knowledge Level: K2) - OAW is a fusion welding process using a gas flame.

### Chemistry of Oxyacetylene Welding

The combustion reaction between acetylene and oxygen is the core of OAW.

**Acetylene Formula:** C₂H₂

**Oxygen Formula:** O₂

**The Ideal Combustion Reaction:**

2 C₂H₂ + 5 O₂ → 4 CO₂ + 2 H₂O + Heat

**Explanation of the Reaction:**

1.  **Initial Combustion (Inner Cone):** In the initial stage, acetylene reacts with a limited supply of oxygen directly at the tip of the welding torch. This produces carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrogen (H₂), along with immense heat.
    C₂H₂ + O₂ → 2 CO + H₂ + Heat

2.  **Secondary Combustion (Outer Envelope):** The carbon monoxide and hydrogen produced in the first stage then react with the surrounding atmospheric oxygen (or additional oxygen supplied through the torch) in a second combustion zone. This produces carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water vapor (H₂O), releasing further heat.
    2 CO + 1.5 O₂ → 2 CO₂ + Heat
    H₂ + 0.5 O₂ → H₂O + Heat

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The ratio of oxygen to acetylene significantly influences the flame's characteristics and the weld quality.
*   Complete combustion (achieved with the correct gas ratio) produces the highest flame temperature.
*   Incomplete combustion (due to insufficient oxygen) can lead to the presence of unburnt carbon, which can embrittle the weld.

### Types of Oxyacetylene Flames

The type of oxyacetylene flame is determined by the ratio of oxygen to acetylene supplied to the torch. This ratio controls the combustion process and the resulting flame characteristics.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Flame Temperature:** The maximum temperature achieved by the flame.
*   **Flame Characteristics:** The appearance and composition of the flame, influencing its effect on the molten metal.

#### 1. Neutral Flame

*   **Acetylene to Oxygen Ratio:** Approximately 1:1
*   **Chemical Reaction:** Represents complete combustion, where all acetylene and oxygen are consumed.
*   **Appearance:**
    *   **Inner Cone:** Bright white, sharply defined, pointed cone.
    *   **Outer Envelope:** Clear, pale blue, feather-like.
*   **Temperature:** Approximately 3100°C to 3500°C.
*   **Effect on Metal:** Does not oxidize or carburize the molten metal. It is the most common flame used for welding most metals, especially mild steel, stainless steel, and aluminum alloys.
*   **Applications:**
    *   Welding of steel (mild steel, stainless steel).
    *   Welding of aluminum and its alloys.
    *   Brazing and soldering.
    *   Cutting steel (when used with a cutting oxygen jet).

#### 2. Oxidizing Flame

*   **Acetylene to Oxygen Ratio:** Less than 1:1 (excess oxygen).
*   **Chemical Reaction:** More oxygen than required for complete combustion.
*   **Appearance:**
    *   **Inner Cone:** Shorter, blunter, and greenish tinge at the tip.
    *   **Outer Envelope:** Whistling sound, shorter and more pointed than a neutral flame.
*   **Temperature:** Slightly higher than a neutral flame (can reach up to 3500°C).
*   **Effect on Metal:** Tends to oxidize (burn) the molten metal, leading to brittleness and poor weld quality.
*   **Applications:**
    *   Welding of brass and bronze (oxyacetylene braze welding).
    *   Applications where oxidation is beneficial or tolerable. **Generally avoided for welding ferrous metals due to the risk of embrittlement.**

#### 3. Carburizing (or Reducing) Flame

*   **Acetylene to Oxygen Ratio:** Greater than 1:1 (excess acetylene).
*   **Chemical Reaction:** Incomplete combustion due to insufficient oxygen. Excess unburnt carbon is present.
*   **Appearance:**
    *   **Inner Cone:** White, feathery, with an inner acetylene feather or cone.
    *   **Outer Envelope:** Larger and longer than a neutral flame.
*   **Temperature:** Lower than a neutral flame (around 3000°C).
*   **Effect on Metal:** Tends to deposit carbon into the molten metal (carburization), making the weld brittle.
*   **Applications:**
    *   Welding of materials that tend to absorb carbon, such as cast iron (though specific procedures are followed).
    *   Hard-facing with certain alloys.
    *   **Generally avoided for welding most steels, as it leads to embrittlement.**

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The inner cone of the flame is the hottest part.
*   The type of flame is adjusted by manipulating the oxygen and acetylene valves on the welding torch.
*   For most welding applications, a neutral flame is preferred.

### Applications of Oxyacetylene Welding

Oxyacetylene welding has a wide range of applications due to its portability, versatility, and relatively low equipment cost.

**Reference:** *Foundry Technology* by Peter Beeley might touch upon the pre-heating or post-heating applications of gas flames in casting repair. *Tool Design* by Donaldson, LeCain, Goold, Ghose might mention the use of gas welding for tool repair or fabrication.

**Key Applications:**

1.  **Welding of Thin Sheet Metals:** OAW is excellent for joining thin sheets of steel, stainless steel, aluminum, copper, and brass.
    *   *Example:* Manufacturing of automotive exhaust systems, ductwork, and sheet metal enclosures.

2.  **Joining Dissimilar Metals:** With appropriate filler rods and flux, OAW can join dissimilar metals like steel to copper or brass.
    *   *Example:* Joining copper pipes to steel fittings.

3.  **Brazing and Soldering:** OAW provides the heat source for brazing and soldering operations, which are lower-temperature joining processes.
    *   *Example:* Joining of plumbing components, electrical connections.

4.  **Repair Work:** It is widely used for repairing machinery parts, tools, and molds.
    *   *Example:* Repairing cracked engine blocks (cast iron), filling worn-out tool edges with hard-facing material.

5.  **Cutting (Oxy-fuel Cutting):** While not strictly welding, OAW equipment with a cutting attachment is used for cutting ferrous metals. A pre-heating flame ignites the metal, and a high-pressure stream of pure oxygen then rapidly oxidizes and blows away the molten metal.
    *   *Example:* Cutting steel plates for fabrication or demolition.

6.  **Heating:** The OAW torch can be used for preheating or post-heating metal parts before or after welding or forming operations.
    *   *Example:* Preheating thick steel sections before welding to reduce stress.

7.  **Welding of Non-ferrous Metals:** OAW is suitable for welding aluminum, magnesium, copper, and their alloys, often requiring specific fluxes.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The choice of filler rod and flux is crucial for the success of OAW, especially for specific metals and alloys.
*   OAW is less efficient for very thick materials compared to arc welding processes due to its lower heat input rate.

### Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** What are the primary gases used in oxyacetylene welding?
**Answer:** Acetylene (C₂H₂) and Oxygen (O₂).

**Question 2:** Describe the appearance of a neutral oxyacetylene flame.
**Answer:** A neutral flame has a sharply defined, bright white inner cone and a clear, pale blue, feather-like outer envelope.

**Question 3:** What is the primary characteristic of an oxidizing flame?
**Answer:** An oxidizing flame has excess oxygen, a shorter and blunter inner cone with a greenish tinge, and tends to oxidize (burn) the molten metal.

**Question 4:** Why is a carburizing flame generally avoided for welding steel?
**Answer:** A carburizing flame has excess acetylene and incomplete combustion. It deposits carbon into the molten steel, making the weld brittle (carburization).

**Question 5:** List three common applications of oxyacetylene welding.
**Answer:**
1.  Welding of thin sheet metals.
2.  Brazing and soldering.
3.  Repair work and joining dissimilar metals.
4.  Heating and cutting (with attachment).

**Question 6:** Which type of flame is ideal for welding mild steel?
**Answer:** A neutral flame.

**Question 7:** What is the approximate maximum temperature of an oxyacetylene flame?
**Answer:** Approximately 3100°C to 3500°C.

**Question 8:** Explain the two stages of combustion in oxyacetylene welding.
**Answer:**
1.  **Initial Combustion:** Acetylene reacts with limited oxygen at the torch tip to produce CO, H₂, and heat.
2.  **Secondary Combustion:** The CO and H₂ then react with surrounding oxygen to produce CO₂, H₂O, and additional heat.

### Summary of Key Concepts

*   Oxyacetylene welding (OAW) is a fusion welding process using acetylene and oxygen.
*   The flame is generated by the combustion reaction: 2 C₂H₂ + 5 O₂ → 4 CO₂ + 2 H₂O + Heat.
*   Three main types of OAW flames exist: Neutral (1:1 ratio), Oxidizing (>1:1 O₂), and Carburizing (<1:1 C₂H₂).
*   **Neutral flame:** Ideal for most welding (steel, aluminum), balanced combustion.
*   **Oxidizing flame:** Excess oxygen, leads to metal oxidation, used sparingly for brass/bronze.
*   **Carburizing flame:** Excess acetylene, deposits carbon, can embrittle welds, used cautiously for cast iron.
*   OAW is versatile for thin metals, repair, brazing, soldering, and with attachments, cutting and heating.
