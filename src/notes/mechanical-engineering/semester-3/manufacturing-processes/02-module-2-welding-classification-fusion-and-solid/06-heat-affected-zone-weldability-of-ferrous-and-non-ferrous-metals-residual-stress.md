## 1. Heat Affected Zone (HAZ)

### **1.1. Definition:**

The **Heat Affected Zone (HAZ)** is the region of the base metal that is adjacent to the fusion zone (weld metal) and has not been melted, but whose microstructure or mechanical properties have been altered by the heat of the welding process.

### **1.2. Formation of HAZ:**

When welding, a significant amount of heat is introduced into the base metal. This heat flows away from the weld pool into the surrounding base metal through conduction. This heat transfer results in a temperature gradient across the base metal, creating distinct zones with varying microstructural and mechanical properties.

### **1.3. Zones within the HAZ (from fusion zone outwards):**

*   **Zone of Coarse Grain Structure:** This zone is closest to the fusion zone. It experiences the highest temperatures below the melting point, leading to grain growth. This can result in increased toughness but reduced ductility and potential for embrittlement.
*   **Zone of Fine Grain Structure (Recrystallization Zone):** This zone has experienced temperatures that cause recrystallization, forming new, smaller grains. This generally leads to improved strength and toughness compared to the unaffected base metal.
*   **Zone of Tempering:** This zone is encountered in heat-treated materials (e.g., hardened steels). The welding heat can cause tempering of the hardened microstructure, leading to a decrease in hardness and strength but an increase in ductility.
*   **Zone of Incomplete Penetration/No Metallurgical Change:** The outermost region of the HAZ, where the temperature rise is insufficient to cause significant microstructural changes.

### **1.4. Factors Affecting HAZ Size and Properties:**

*   **Heat Input (Welding Parameters):** Higher heat input (lower welding speed, higher current/voltage) leads to a larger HAZ.
*   **Material Properties:** Thermal conductivity, specific heat, and melting point of the base metal significantly influence heat dissipation and thus HAZ size. Materials with lower thermal conductivity tend to have larger HAZs.
*   **Joint Design and Thickness:** Thicker materials and certain joint designs can act as heat sinks, influencing heat flow and HAZ size.
*   **Preheating:** Preheating the base metal reduces the temperature gradient, generally leading to a finer HAZ and reduced susceptibility to defects.

### **1.5. Effects of HAZ:**

*   **Microstructural Changes:** Grain growth, recrystallization, phase transformations.
*   **Mechanical Property Changes:** Variations in hardness, strength, toughness, ductility.
*   **Corrosion Resistance:** Changes in microstructure can affect corrosion resistance, especially in stainless steels.

### **1.6. Importance:**

The properties of the HAZ can often be the weakest link in a welded joint. Understanding and controlling the HAZ is critical for ensuring the structural integrity and performance of welded components.

## 2. Weldability of Ferrous Metals

### **2.1. Definition of Weldability:**

**Weldability** refers to the ease with which a metal or alloy can be welded without impairing its properties or utility. It involves the ability to produce a sound, pressure-tight joint with acceptable mechanical and physical properties, and with minimal distortion and residual stresses.

### **2.2. Factors Affecting Weldability of Ferrous Metals:**

*   **Carbon Content (in Steels):** This is the most critical factor.
    *   **Low Carbon Steels (< 0.25% C):** Generally excellent weldability. Low susceptibility to hydrogen-induced cracking (HIC) and cold cracking.
    *   **Medium Carbon Steels (0.25% - 0.50% C):** Moderate weldability. Increased risk of HIC and cold cracking due to formation of martensite in the HAZ. Preheating and post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) are often required.
    *   **High Carbon Steels (> 0.50% C):** Poor weldability. Highly susceptible to cracking due to martensite formation, high cooling rates, and thermal stresses. Welding is often avoided or requires extensive preheating, slow cooling, and PWHT.
*   **Alloying Elements:**
    *   **Hardenable Alloying Elements (e.g., Cr, Mo, V, Ni):** Increase hardenability, making the HAZ more susceptible to martensite formation and cracking. Higher preheat and PWHT are necessary.
    *   **Austenitizing Elements (e.g., Ni):** Can promote grain growth.
    *   **Carbide-Forming Elements (e.g., Cr, Mo, V):** Can lead to carbide precipitation in the HAZ, affecting properties.
*   **Presence of Impurities:**
    *   **Sulphur (S):** Causes hot cracking due to formation of low melting point iron sulfides at grain boundaries.
    *   **Phosphorus (P):** Causes embrittlement and can contribute to hot cracking.
    *   **Nitrogen (N):** Can cause porosity and embrittlement in some steels.
*   **Grain Size:** Finer grains are generally preferred for better toughness. Coarse grains in the HAZ can reduce toughness.
*   **Thermal Expansion and Contraction:** Differential expansion and contraction during heating and cooling lead to residual stresses and distortion.
*   **Phase Transformations:** The austenite-to-martensite transformation in steels is a major concern for cracking.

### **2.3. Weldability of Specific Ferrous Metals:**

*   **Carbon Steels:**
    *   **Low Carbon Steels:** Easily welded, minimal precautions needed.
    *   **Medium Carbon Steels:** Require preheating (e.g., 100-250°C) and sometimes PWHT to prevent cracking. Use low hydrogen electrodes.
    *   **High Carbon Steels:** Very difficult to weld. Require high preheat (e.g., 300-600°C), slow cooling, and PWHT. Often require special welding consumables and techniques.
*   **Alloy Steels:** Weldability depends on the specific alloying elements and their concentration. Hardenability is a key factor. Consult welding procedure specifications (WPS) for specific alloys.
*   **Stainless Steels:**
    *   **Austenitic Stainless Steels (e.g., 304, 316):** Generally have excellent weldability. Low carbon content and face-centered cubic (FCC) structure prevent martensite formation. Susceptible to sensitization (carbide precipitation at grain boundaries leading to intergranular corrosion) if cooled slowly through the 500-850°C range, especially in higher carbon grades (e.g., 304L is preferred over 304 for welding).
    *   **Ferritic Stainless Steels (e.g., 430):** Can become brittle due to grain growth and embrittlement (sigma phase formation) in the HAZ. Susceptible to intergranular corrosion. Low carbon and nitrogen grades are preferred.
    *   **Martensitic Stainless Steels (e.g., 410):** High carbon content leads to hardening and potential cracking. Require preheating and PWHT to prevent cracking and achieve desired properties.
    *   **Duplex Stainless Steels:** Exhibit properties of both austenitic and ferritic steels. Require careful control of heat input to maintain the desired phase balance in the weld and HAZ.

### **2.4. Methods to Improve Weldability of Ferrous Metals:**

*   **Preheating:** Reduces cooling rates, minimizes thermal stresses, and reduces the risk of hydrogen-induced cracking.
*   **Post-Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT):** Stress relieving (reducing residual stresses), tempering (softening hardened zones), and normalizing.
*   **Low Hydrogen Electrodes/Consumables:** Minimizes the risk of hydrogen embrittlement and HIC.
*   **Controlled Cooling Rates:** Slow cooling from welding temperature.
*   **Shielding Gas:** Proper shielding gas selection in processes like TIG and MIG to prevent contamination.
*   **Filler Metal Selection:** Using filler metals with controlled chemistry to match base metal properties and avoid undesirable reactions.

## 3. Weldability of Non-Ferrous Metals

### **3.1. General Considerations:**

Non-ferrous metals and alloys have diverse properties, and their weldability varies significantly. Key considerations include:

*   **Oxide Films:** Many non-ferrous metals (e.g., Aluminum, Magnesium, Titanium) form tenacious, high-melting-point oxide films that need to be removed before welding and protected during welding.
*   **High Thermal Conductivity:** Metals like Aluminum and Copper have high thermal conductivity, which dissipates heat rapidly. This requires higher heat input and faster welding speeds to achieve fusion, and can lead to wider HAZs with less pronounced microstructural changes.
*   **Low Melting Points:** Some non-ferrous metals have lower melting points, requiring careful control to avoid over-melting.
*   **Solidification and Hot Cracking:** The presence of certain alloying elements can lead to segregation and the formation of low-melting-point constituents, promoting hot cracking during solidification.
*   **Gas Solubility:** Some non-ferrous metals can absorb large amounts of gases (e.g., Hydrogen in Aluminum, Nitrogen and Oxygen in Titanium) during welding, leading to porosity and embrittlement.

### **3.2. Weldability of Specific Non-Ferrous Metals:**

*   **Aluminum Alloys:**
    *   **Characteristics:** High thermal conductivity, low melting point, tenacious oxide film (Al₂O₃).
    *   **Weldability:** Generally good, but requires special techniques.
    *   **Oxide Film:** Must be mechanically removed (wire brushing) and is usually disrupted by AC welding (e.g., AC TIG) due to the cathodic cleaning action. Inert gas shielding is essential.
    *   **Porosity:** Hydrogen is readily absorbed, leading to porosity. Dryness of the filler metal and shielding gas is crucial.
    *   **Hot Cracking:** High-strength aluminum alloys (e.g., 2xxx and 7xxx series) are prone to hot cracking due to the presence of elements like Cu and Zn. Using filler metals with adjusted composition can mitigate this.
    *   **Distortion:** High thermal expansion and conductivity contribute to significant distortion.
    *   **Welding Processes:** TIG (GTAW) and MIG (GMAW) are commonly used.
*   **Copper Alloys (e.g., Pure Copper, Brass, Bronze):**
    *   **Characteristics:** High thermal and electrical conductivity, various melting points and alloying effects.
    *   **Weldability:** Varies by alloy.
    *   **Pure Copper:** High thermal conductivity makes welding difficult. Requires high heat input. Oxygen-free copper (OFHC) is easier to weld than deoxidized copper, which can suffer from embrittlement due to oxide formation.
    *   **Brass (Copper-Zinc):** High zinc content leads to dezincification (loss of zinc) and potential vaporisation of zinc, causing porosity and fumes. Requires careful control of filler metal composition and welding parameters.
    *   **Bronze (Copper-Tin):** Generally weldable, but tin can cause hot cracking. Use of deoxidizing filler metals is recommended.
    *   **Distortion:** High thermal expansion can cause distortion.
*   **Nickel Alloys (e.g., Inconel, Monel):**
    *   **Characteristics:** Excellent high-temperature strength and corrosion resistance.
    *   **Weldability:** Generally good, but can be susceptible to certain defects.
    *   **Oxide Films:** Form stable oxide films that require good shielding.
    *   **Hot Cracking:** Some nickel alloys can be susceptible to hot cracking if impurities like sulfur are present.
    *   **Intergranular Cracking:** Certain nickel alloys can be susceptible to intergranular cracking in the HAZ due to precipitation of intermetallic phases.
    *   **Welding Processes:** TIG (GTAW) and MIG (GMAW) are common.
*   **Titanium Alloys:**
    *   **Characteristics:** High strength-to-weight ratio, excellent corrosion resistance. Highly reactive at elevated temperatures.
    *   **Weldability:** Excellent, but requires extremely careful protection from atmospheric contamination.
    *   **Contamination:** Titanium readily absorbs oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen at welding temperatures, leading to embrittlement of the weld and HAZ. Full shielding with inert gases (Argon or Helium) is essential, including backup shielding on the root side of the joint.
    *   **Oxide Films:** Forms a protective oxide film, but high-temperature reactions with air are the primary concern.
    *   **Welding Processes:** TIG (GTAW) is the preferred process due to its precision and shielding capabilities.

### **3.3. Methods to Improve Weldability of Non-Ferrous Metals:**

*   **Thorough Cleaning:** Removal of oxides, oils, and surface contaminants.
*   **Effective Shielding:** Inert gas shielding (TIG, MIG) is critical to prevent atmospheric contamination and oxidation.
*   **Specialized Filler Metals:** Using filler metals with adjusted compositions to counteract cracking tendencies or match base metal properties.
*   **Controlled Heat Input:** Managing heat input to prevent over-melting and control microstructure.
*   **Preheating (where applicable):** Can sometimes be used for thicker sections to reduce thermal shock or improve fusion, but must be carefully controlled.
*   **Post-Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT):** Used for some alloys (e.g., high-strength aluminum alloys) for stress relief or to achieve specific precipitate hardening.

## 4. Residual Stresses and Distortion

### **4.1. Origin of Residual Stresses:**

Residual stresses are stresses that remain in a material after the external forces that caused them have been removed. In welding, they arise from:

*   **Uneven Heating and Cooling:** Different parts of the workpiece heat up and cool down at different rates.
    *   **Heating:** Material expands.
    *   **Cooling:** Material contracts.
*   **Phase Transformations:** In steels, the transformation from austenite to martensite involves a volume expansion, which also contributes to stresses.
*   **Volume Changes:** Solidification of the weld metal involves shrinkage.

**Mechanism Example (Simple Butt Weld):**
1.  The weld area heats up, expands, and is constrained by the surrounding cooler base metal.
2.  As the weld metal cools and solidifies, it tries to contract.
3.  However, the surrounding cooler base metal is already at a lower temperature and resists this contraction.
4.  This resistance causes tensile residual stresses in the weld metal and compressive stresses in the adjacent base metal.

### **4.2. Types of Residual Stresses:**

*   **First-Order Stresses:** Macro-stresses that are uniform over large areas of the workpiece, caused by non-uniform heating and cooling. These are the dominant stresses in welded structures.
*   **Second-Order Stresses:** Micro-stresses that vary across individual grains, related to crystallographic orientation and phase transformations.
*   **Third-Order Stresses:** Stresses within the atomic lattice.

### **4.3. Distortion:**

Distortion is the change in shape of a welded component. It is a direct consequence of residual stresses. Common types of distortion include:

*   **Angular Distortion (Bending):** Occurs when the weld bead shrinks preferentially, causing the plates to bend. Common in butt welds.
*   **Longitudinal Shrinkage:** Contraction along the length of the weld.
*   **Transverse Shrinkage:** Contraction across the width of the weld.
*   **Buckling:** Out-of-plane deformation, especially in thin sheets.

### **4.4. Factors Affecting Residual Stresses and Distortion:**

*   **Joint Design:** The type of joint (butt, lap, fillet) and its preparation.
*   **Welding Sequence:** The order in which welds are made.
*   **Welding Parameters:** Heat input, welding speed, electrode size.
*   **Material Properties:** Thermal expansion coefficient, modulus of elasticity, yield strength, phase transformation behavior.
*   **Thickness and Geometry of the Workpiece:** Thicker sections and complex geometries can lead to more severe stresses and distortion.
*   **Constraint:** The degree to which the workpiece is restrained during welding. High constraint leads to higher residual stresses.

### **4.5. Methods for Controlling and Mitigating Residual Stresses and Distortion:**

*   **Joint Design:**
    *   **Balanced Welding:** Welding on both sides of a joint simultaneously or alternately to distribute stresses.
    *   **Stringer Beads vs. Weave Beads:** Stringer beads generally produce less distortion than wide weave beads for the same heat input.
    *   **Backstep Welding:** Welding in short segments in the opposite direction of the overall progression.
    *   **Caulking/Stitching:** Intermittent welding.
*   **Preheating:** Reduces the temperature gradient and thus the magnitude of thermal stresses.
*   **Post-Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT):**
    *   **Stress Relieving:** Heating the component to a moderate temperature (e.g., 600-650°C for steels) and holding it for a period, followed by slow cooling, to reduce residual stresses.
*   **Mechanical Aids:**
    *   **Clamps and Fixtures:** Restraining the workpiece to control the direction of distortion.
    *   **Peening:** Hammering the weld bead to introduce compressive stresses, counteracting tensile stresses. This is effective for certain applications but can introduce its own problems if not done correctly.
*   **Welding Sequence Optimization:** Planning the welding sequence to minimize distortion buildup.
*   **Using Lower Heat Input:** Less heat input generally means less contraction and thus less distortion.
*   **Back-Gouging and Back-Welding:** For butt joints, welding from both sides helps equalize shrinkage.

**Referenced from:** *Manufacturing Science by Amitabha Ghosh Asok Kumar Mallik* (Likely discusses these phenomena in detail). *Manufacturing Engineering and Technology by Serope Kalpakjian Steven R. Schmid* (Will cover manufacturing implications and control strategies).

## 5. Defects in Welding

### **5.1. Definition:**

Welding defects are discontinuities or imperfections in the weld that can reduce its strength, integrity, or performance. They are often caused by incorrect welding procedures, poor technique, improper material handling, or contamination.

### **5.2. Classification of Welding Defects:**

Defects can be broadly classified based on their nature and location:

#### **5.2.1. Internal Defects (Non-Visible):**

These are typically detected by non-destructive testing (NDT) methods.

*   **Porosity:** Cavities or voids within the weld metal or at the fusion line.
    *   **Causes:** Entrapped gases (hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen) from the atmosphere, moisture, contaminants, or incorrect filler metal.
    *   **Effects:** Reduces weld strength and ductility.
    *   **Prevention:** Proper cleaning, dry consumables, adequate shielding gas flow, use of low-hydrogen consumables.
*   **Inclusions:** Non-metallic particles trapped in the weld metal.
    *   **Slag Inclusions:** Unmelted flux or slag from the welding process.
        *   **Causes:** Insufficient cleaning between passes (in multi-pass welding), improper welding technique (e.g., too low current), improper flux composition.
        *   **Effects:** Reduces weld strength, can act as stress concentrators.
        *   **Prevention:** Thorough cleaning between passes, correct welding parameters.
    *   **Tungsten Inclusions:** Particles of the tungsten electrode (in TIG welding) that break off.
        *   **Causes:** Electrode contamination, improper electrode angle, incorrect current for electrode size.
        *   **Effects:** Can be detrimental to mechanical properties.
        *   **Prevention:** Careful electrode handling, correct electrode selection and angle.
*   **Lack of Fusion (LOF):** The weld metal has not fused with the base metal or the previous weld bead.
    *   **Causes:** Insufficient heat input, improper joint preparation, incorrect welding angle, surface contamination.
    *   **Effects:** Weakens the joint significantly, prone to cracking.
    *   **Prevention:** Correct welding parameters, thorough joint cleaning, proper torch angle.
*   **Lack of Penetration (LOP):** The weld metal does not extend to the full depth of the joint.
    *   **Causes:** Insufficient heat input, too high welding speed, incorrect joint design (e.g., root gap too large).
    *   **Effects:** Reduces joint strength, creates a notch effect.
    *   **Prevention:** Correct welding parameters, proper joint fit-up.
*   **Undercut:** A groove or indentation along the edge of the weld bead, on the base metal.
    *   **Causes:** Excessive heat input, excessive welding speed, incorrect torch angle.
    *   **Effects:** Reduces the cross-sectional area of the base metal, acting as a stress riser.
    *   **Prevention:** Adjust welding parameters and torch angle.
*   **Cracks:** Discontinuities that form due to tensile stresses exceeding the material's fracture strength. Cracks are often the most severe defects.
    *   **Hot Cracks (Solidification Cracks):** Occur during solidification due to the presence of low-melting-point constituents or due to tensile strains during cooling.
        *   **Causes:** High sulfur and phosphorus content, unfavorable alloy compositions, high restraint.
        *   **Prevention:** Controlled chemistry of base and filler metals, reduced restraint, correct welding sequence.
    *   **Cold Cracks (Hydrogen Cracks, Delayed Cracks):** Occur after welding (hours to days) in steels, especially in the HAZ.
        *   **Causes:** Presence of hydrogen in the weld metal/HAZ, susceptible microstructure (e.g., martensite), tensile residual stresses.
        *   **Prevention:** Preheating, low-hydrogen consumables, PWHT, controlled cooling.
    *   **Lamellar Tearing:** Step-like cracks that occur in the through-thickness direction of rolled steel plates, usually in the base metal near the weld.
        *   **Causes:** Through-thickness tensile strains during welding and/or forming, combined with the anisotropic properties of the steel (preferential sulfur segregation into stringers).
        *   **Prevention:** Using vacuum-degassed steels with low sulfur content, designing joints to reduce through-thickness stresses, using weld metal with good ductility.

#### **5.2.2. External Defects (Visible):**

These are usually visible on the surface of the weld.

*   **Undercut:** (Already mentioned, but can be external too)
*   **Overlap (Cold Lap):** The weld metal fails to fuse with the base metal at the toe of the weld, creating an un-fused surface.
    *   **Causes:** Similar to Lack of Fusion, often due to low heat input or incorrect torch angle.
    *   **Effects:** Reduces effective weld throat, can initiate cracks.
    *   **Prevention:** Correct welding parameters and technique.
*   **Spatter:** Small droplets of molten metal expelled from the welding arc that adhere to the base metal adjacent to the weld.
    *   **Causes:** Incorrect welding parameters (e.g., too high voltage/current in MIG), poor shielding gas coverage.
    *   **Effects:** Aesthetic, but can be a nuisance and potentially damage surfaces if not removed.
    *   **Prevention:** Correct welding parameters, proper shielding gas.
*   **Irregular Weld Bead Profile:** Uneven bead width, height, or convexity/concavity.
    *   **Causes:** Inconsistent welding speed, torch angle, or arc length.
    *   **Effects:** Can affect structural integrity and appearance.
    *   **Prevention:** Consistent welding technique.
*   **Surface Cracks:** Cracks visible on the weld surface.
    *   **Causes:** Similar to internal cracks (hot cracking, cold cracking), but manifest on the surface.
    *   **Prevention:** Same as for internal cracks.

### **5.3. Detection and Inspection:**

*   **Visual Inspection:** For external defects like undercut, overlap, spatter, and surface cracks.
*   **Non-Destructive Testing (NDT):**
    *   **Dye Penetrant Testing (PT):** Detects surface-breaking defects like cracks and porosity.
    *   **Magnetic Particle Testing (MT):** Detects surface and near-surface defects in ferromagnetic materials.
    *   **Ultrasonic Testing (UT):** Detects internal defects like cracks, porosity, and inclusions.
    *   **Radiography Testing (RT) / X-ray:** Detects internal defects like porosity, inclusions, and lack of fusion/penetration.
    *   **Eddy Current Testing (ECT):** Detects surface and near-surface defects.
*   **Destructive Testing:** Mechanical testing of weld samples (tensile tests, bend tests, impact tests) to assess mechanical properties and identify internal defects.

## Practice Questions & Answers

**Q1. Define the Heat Affected Zone (HAZ) and list three factors that influence its size.**
*   **Answer:** The HAZ is the region of base metal adjacent to the weld that has not melted but has undergone microstructural or property changes due to welding heat. Factors influencing HAZ size include: Heat input, material thermal conductivity, welding speed.

**Q2. Explain why medium carbon steels are more prone to cracking during welding than low carbon steels.**
*   **Answer:** Medium carbon steels have higher hardenability. During welding, rapid cooling rates can lead to the formation of brittle martensite in the HAZ, which can cause hydrogen-induced cracking (cold cracking) under the influence of residual stresses.

**Q3. What is the primary challenge when welding aluminum alloys, and how is it typically overcome?**
*   **Answer:** The primary challenge is the tenacious, high-melting-point aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) film. It is overcome by mechanical cleaning prior to welding and using AC welding (like AC TIG) for its cathodic cleaning effect, coupled with inert gas shielding.

**Q4. Describe the fundamental cause of residual stresses in welding.**
*   **Answer:** Residual stresses arise from the non-uniform heating and cooling cycles experienced by the base metal and weld metal. Differential contraction and expansion of these regions, constrained by the surrounding material, lead to internal stresses.

**Q5. Identify two common internal welding defects and two common external welding defects.**
*   **Answer:**
    *   Internal: Porosity, Lack of Fusion.
    *   External: Undercut, Overlap.

**Q6. How can distortion be minimized during welding of a long steel plate seam? (Mention at least two methods).**
*   **Answer:**
    1.  **Backstep Welding:** Welding in segments in the opposite direction of the overall progression reduces the accumulation of shrinkage stresses.
    2.  **Preheating:** Reducing the temperature gradient across the workpiece minimizes the differential contraction.
    3.  **Clamping:** Restraining the plate to control the direction of bending.

**Q7. Why is preheating often necessary for welding martensitic stainless steels?**
*   **Answer:** Martensitic stainless steels have high carbon content and hardenability. Preheating reduces the cooling rate after welding, preventing the formation of brittle martensite in the HAZ and reducing the risk of cracking. Post-weld heat treatment is also typically required.

**Q8. What is the difference between Lack of Fusion (LOF) and Lack of Penetration (LOP)?**
*   **Answer:**
    *   **LOF:** The weld metal has not properly fused with the base metal or the previous weld bead. It's a lack of metallurgical bonding *at* the interface.
    *   **LOP:** The weld metal does not extend to the full intended depth of the joint. It's a lack of *depth* of fusion.

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   The HAZ is a critical region for weld performance. Its size and properties depend heavily on welding parameters and material.
*   Carbon content is paramount for steel weldability.
*   Non-ferrous metals often present challenges due to oxide films, high thermal conductivity, or reactivity.
*   Residual stresses and distortion are inherent to welding and must be managed through proper design, procedures, and post-weld treatments.
*   Understanding welding defects, their causes, and prevention methods is vital for ensuring weld quality and structural integrity.
*   Always refer to welding procedure specifications (WPS) and material safety data sheets (MSDS) for specific welding applications.
