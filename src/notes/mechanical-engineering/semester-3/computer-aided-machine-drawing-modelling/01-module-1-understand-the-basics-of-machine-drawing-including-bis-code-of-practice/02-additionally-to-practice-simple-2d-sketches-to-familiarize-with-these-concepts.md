# Computer-Aided Machine Drawing & Modelling - Module 1: Basics of Machine Drawing

## Module Overview

This module introduces the fundamental principles of machine drawing, which form the bedrock of computer-aided design and manufacturing. We will explore the essential elements of technical drawings, including their standardization, representation techniques, and the critical role of tolerancing in ensuring functional and interchangeable parts. The ultimate goal is to equip you with the ability to interpret and create accurate 2D engineering drawings, a prerequisite for advanced 3D modeling and assembly.

## Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this module, you will be able to:

*   Understand the importance of Indian Standards (BIS) in machine drawing.
*   Differentiate and correctly apply various types of lines in technical drawings.
*   Apply standard dimensioning practices for clear and unambiguous representation of dimensions.
*   Understand and utilize different scales of drawing for effective representation.
*   Interpret and create sectional views to reveal internal features of objects.
*   Comprehend the concepts of geometric tolerances and their significance in manufacturing.
*   Recognize the fundamental importance of Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing (GD&T) in achieving functional requirements.
*   Develop the ability to create simple 2D sketches to reinforce understanding of these concepts.

## 1. BIS Code of Practice for Engineering Drawings

The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) provides a set of guidelines and codes of practice for engineering drawings to ensure uniformity, clarity, and consistency across industries. Adherence to these standards is crucial for effective communication between designers, manufacturers, and quality control personnel.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Standardization:** BIS codes establish standardized methods for drawing representation, line types, dimensioning, lettering, and other aspects of technical drawings.
*   **Clarity and Unambiguity:** Following BIS codes minimizes misinterpretations and ensures that drawings convey precise information.
*   **Interchangeability:** Standardized drawings facilitate the interchangeability of parts, a cornerstone of modern manufacturing.

**Relevant BIS Codes (Illustrative Examples - refer to actual BIS for detailed information):**

*   **IS 101:1990 (Reaffirmed 2005) - Engineering Drawing Practice:** This is a foundational standard covering various aspects of engineering drawing.
*   **IS 1364 (Part 1 to 5):1978 (Reaffirmed 2006) - Dimensions and Tolerances:** This series of standards deals with linear and angular tolerances.
*   **IS 696:1972 (Reaffirmed 2006) - Recommendations for Drawing and Lettering:** Covers aspects of lettering and line conventions.

**Importance:**

*   Ensures that drawings are universally understood within India.
*   Facilitates seamless collaboration between different departments and organizations.
*   Crucial for quality control and inspection processes.

**Important Point to Remember:** Always refer to the latest versions of relevant BIS codes for precise specifications.

## 2. Types of Lines in Engineering Drawings

Lines are the fundamental building blocks of any engineering drawing. Each type of line has a specific purpose and thickness, conveying distinct information about the object being represented. BIS codes dictate the use of various line types.

| Line Type             | Standard Appearance     | Purpose                                                                                                                                                                                                  | BIS Recommendation (General)                               | Example                                       |
| :-------------------- | :---------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- |
| **Visible/Outline**   | Thick, continuous       | Represents visible edges and outlines of an object.                                                                                                                                                      | Thick continuous line (0.5mm - 0.8mm)                      | The outer boundary of a rectangular block.    |
| **Hidden/Dashed**     | Thin, dashed            | Represents edges and outlines that are not visible from the current view.                                                                                                                                | Thin dashed line (0.25mm - 0.35mm)                         | The hole in a block that is not visible.      |
| **Center Line**       | Thin, alternating long & short dashes | Indicates the center of symmetrical features like circles, cylinders, holes, and axes of rotation.                                                                                             | Thin chain line, long dash-short dash (0.25mm - 0.35mm)    | Center of a circular hole or a shaft.         |
| **Dimension Line**    | Thin, continuous        | Used to show the dimensions of an object. Ends with arrowheads or stop marks.                                                                                                                            | Thin continuous line (0.25mm - 0.35mm)                     | Dimensioning the length or diameter of a part. |
| **Extension Line**    | Thin, continuous        | Extends from the feature to which the dimension applies. Dimension lines are placed between extension lines.                                                                                            | Thin continuous line (0.25mm - 0.35mm)                     | Used to show the extent of a measurement.     |
| **Construction/Guide**| Thin, very light        | Used for constructing the drawing, not intended to be visible in the final drawing.                                                                                                                      | Thin continuous line (0.25mm - 0.35mm)                     | Guidelines for lettering or drawing circles.  |
| **Cutting Plane Line**| Thick, alternating long & short dashes with thick ends | Indicates the location and direction of the cutting plane for sectional views.                                                                                                               | Thick chain line, long dash-short dash with thick ends (0.5mm - 0.8mm) | Shows where a section is taken through a part. |
| **Section Line/Hatching** | Thin, continuous, at an angle | Represents the material that has been cut by the cutting plane in a sectional view. Usually drawn at 45° to the main outlines.                                                                           | Thin continuous line (0.25mm - 0.35mm)                     | Shading within a sectional view.              |
| **Leader Line**       | Thin, continuous, with an arrowhead | Connects a note, dimension, or symbol to a feature on the drawing.                                                                                                                             | Thin continuous line (0.25mm - 0.35mm)                     | Pointing to a specific feature for clarification. |
| **Break Line (Short)** | Thin, freehand zig-zag  | Used to show a break in a long object to shorten it and fit it on the drawing sheet.                                                                                                                     | Thin continuous line, freehand zig-zag (0.25mm - 0.35mm)   | Representing a long shaft that is shortened.  |
| **Break Line (Long)**  | Thin, straight with zig-zag ends | Used to show a break in long, straight objects, like beams or shafts, to indicate a discontinuity.                                                                                                   | Thin continuous line, straight with zig-zag ends (0.25mm - 0.35mm) | Breaking a long bar to show its length.       |

**Important Point to Remember:** Line thickness is crucial for distinguishing between different line types. Visible lines are always the thickest.

**Practice Sketch:** Draw a simple object (e.g., a block with a hole and a chamfer) and represent all the relevant line types mentioned above.

## 3. Dimensioning in Engineering Drawings

Dimensioning provides the essential size and location information for manufacturing a part. Accurate and clear dimensioning is paramount for producing parts that conform to design requirements. BIS codes provide guidelines for dimensioning.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Clarity and Readability:** Dimensions should be placed to avoid confusion and be easily readable.
*   **Completeness:** All necessary dimensions must be provided to define the part's geometry.
*   **Unambiguity:** Dimensions should not be open to multiple interpretations.
*   **Avoid Redundancy:** Avoid repeating dimensions unless absolutely necessary (e.g., for reference).
*   **Placement of Dimensions:** Dimensions should be placed outside the object's outline whenever possible.
*   **Arrowheads/Stop Marks:** Used to terminate dimension lines and indicate the extent of the dimension. BIS recommends specific shapes and sizes.

**Types of Dimensioning:**

*   **Linear Dimensioning:** Specifies lengths, widths, heights, and diameters.
    *   **Example:** Dimensioning the length of a block as 50mm.
*   **Angular Dimensioning:** Specifies angles.
    *   **Example:** Dimensioning a chamfer as 45° x 5mm.
*   **Radial Dimensioning:** Specifies radii of arcs and fillets.
    *   **Example:** Dimensioning the radius of a fillet as R10mm.
*   **Diameter Dimensioning:** Specifies diameters of circles and cylindrical features. Usually preceded by the diameter symbol (Ø).
    *   **Example:** Ø20mm.
*   **Coordinate Dimensioning (Baseline Dimensioning):** Dimensions are referenced from a common datum or baseline. This is particularly useful for prismatic parts and CNC machining.
    *   **Example:** X-coordinates and Y-coordinates of various features from a datum corner.
*   **Chain Dimensioning:** Dimensions are placed in a series along a straight line, one after another. This can lead to accumulation of tolerances.
    *   **Example:** 10mm, 20mm, 30mm along a line.

**Dimensioning Elements:**

*   **Dimension Line:** Thin continuous line indicating the extent of the dimension.
*   **Extension Line:** Thin continuous line extending from the feature to the dimension line.
*   **Dimension Value:** The numerical value of the dimension.
*   **Leader Line:** Used to connect a note or dimension to a feature.
*   **Arrowheads/Stop Marks:** Terminate dimension lines.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Place dimensions clearly and avoid overlapping.**
*   **Use the correct line types for dimensioning.**
*   **Always include the necessary symbols (e.g., Ø for diameter, R for radius).**
*   **For features with holes, dimension the diameter and depth/through dimension.**

**Practice Sketch:** Draw a simple block with several holes and chamfers. Dimension it completely using linear, diameter, and angular dimensioning techniques.

## 4. Scales of Drawing

Scales are used to represent the actual size of an object on the drawing sheet when it's either too large or too small to be drawn at its actual size (full scale). This ensures that all features are proportionally represented.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Scale Ratio:** The ratio between a dimension on the drawing and the corresponding dimension on the actual object.
*   **Full Scale:** The object is drawn at its actual size. Represented as 1:1.
*   **Enlarged Scale:** The object is drawn larger than its actual size. Represented as 2:1, 5:1, etc. Used for small features or intricate details.
*   **Reduced Scale:** The object is drawn smaller than its actual size. Represented as 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, etc. Used for large objects like buildings, aircraft, or machinery.

**Representation of Scales:**

*   The scale used for a drawing is typically indicated in the title block or near the drawing itself.
*   For example, "Scale 1:2" means that 1 unit on the drawing represents 2 units on the actual object.
*   "Scale 2:1" means that 2 units on the drawing represent 1 unit on the actual object.

**Choosing a Scale:**

*   **Clarity:** The chosen scale should allow all details to be clearly visible and distinguishable.
*   **Sheet Size:** The scale should enable the drawing to fit comfortably on the drawing sheet without being too small or too large.
*   **Proportionality:** All features must be drawn to scale to maintain correct proportions.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Always indicate the scale of the drawing.**
*   **Even when using a reduced or enlarged scale, dimensions should be given in actual units of the object, not the drawing units.** The drawing is a graphical representation, and the dimensions provide the definitive size information.
*   **Consistency:** If multiple views are used, and a specific scale is applied to one view, it's generally understood that other views representing the same object are also drawn to that scale, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

**Practice Sketch:** Imagine drawing a small screw and a large machine frame. On a hypothetical drawing sheet, indicate how you would represent these using a reduced scale (e.g., 1:5 for the screw) and an enlarged scale (e.g., 2:1 for a specific detail of the frame).

## 5. Sectional Views

Sectional views are used to reveal internal features of an object that are not visible in the external views. This is achieved by imagining the object being cut by a cutting plane.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Cutting Plane:** An imaginary plane used to cut through the object.
*   **Cutting Plane Line:** A thick line with arrowheads indicating the location and direction of the cutting plane.
*   **Section:** The part of the object that remains after the cutting plane has passed through it.
*   **Hatching/Section Lining:** Lines drawn on the cut surface to indicate that it is a section.

**Types of Sectional Views:**

*   **Full Section:** The cutting plane cuts completely through the object.
    *   **Example:** Cutting a cylinder through its diameter to show a circular cross-section.
*   **Half Section:** Used for symmetrical objects, where one half of the object is shown in section and the other half in external view. This conserves space and shows both internal and external features.
    *   **Example:** Cutting a symmetrical flange in half to show both the external profile and the internal bolt holes.
*   **Revolved Section:** A section is revolved 90 degrees and placed within the outline of the object. Used to show the cross-sectional shape of thin parts like ribs or spokes.
    *   **Example:** Showing the cross-section of a spoke in a wheel.
*   **Offset Section:** The cutting plane is offset (bent) to pass through features that are not in line.
    *   **Example:** Cutting through a bolt hole and then an internal passage.
*   **Broken-out Section:** A portion of the object is removed to reveal internal features.
    *   **Example:** Removing a small segment of a larger block to show a hidden cavity.

**Rules for Sectioning:**

*   **Hidden lines behind the cutting plane are usually omitted in sectional views.**
*   **Hatching lines are drawn at an angle (typically 45°) to the main outline or the axis of the object.**
*   **For adjacent sections, the angle of hatching is reversed to distinguish them.**
*   **The cutting plane line with arrowheads should be shown on the view from which the section is taken.**

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Sectional views are crucial for understanding the internal construction of machine parts.**
*   **Always clearly indicate the cutting plane line and the direction of view.**
*   **Hatching indicates the solid material that has been cut.**

**Practice Sketch:** Draw a block with a through hole and a blind hole. Create a full sectional view showing the cut surface of both holes. Draw a symmetrical object (e.g., a pulley) and create a half sectional view.

## 6. Geometric Tolerances and the Importance of GD&T

Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing (GD&T) is a symbolic language used on engineering drawings to specify the allowable variations (tolerances) for geometric features of a part. It goes beyond simple linear and angular dimensions to control form, orientation, location, and profile.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Tolerance:** The allowable variation in a dimension or geometric characteristic.
*   **Geometric Tolerances:** Specify the allowable deviation from perfect geometric form, orientation, location, and profile.
*   **GD&T:** A system that uses a feature control frame to communicate these tolerancing requirements.

**Why GD&T is Important:**

*   **Functional Requirements:** GD&T ensures that parts will assemble and function correctly, even with manufacturing variations. It defines how features relate to each other based on their function.
*   **Reduced Inspection Costs:** By defining functional relationships, GD&T can reduce the need for complex and time-consuming inspection methods.
*   **Improved Communication:** GD&T provides a clear and unambiguous way to communicate design intent, reducing misinterpretations between design, manufacturing, and inspection.
*   **Interchangeability:** GD&T is essential for achieving interchangeability of parts from different manufacturing sources.
*   **Cost Reduction:** Proper GD&T can prevent over-tolerancing (making parts too precise and expensive) and under-tolerancing (leading to functional failures).

**Fundamental GD&T Concepts (Introduction):**

*   **Feature Control Frame:** A rectangular box containing the GD&T symbols, their associated tolerances, and potentially datum references.
    *   **Example:**
        ```
        | Ø 0.05 | A | B |
        ```
        This indicates a positional tolerance of 0.05mm relative to datum A and datum B.
*   **Symbols:** A standardized set of symbols represent various geometric characteristics (e.g., straightness, flatness, circularity, position, profile).
*   **Datums:** Reference points, lines, or planes established on a part used to define the location and orientation of other features. Datums are essential for controlling geometric tolerances.
*   **Tolerances of Form:** Control the straightness, flatness, circularity, and cylindricity of individual features.
*   **Tolerances of Orientation:** Control the angular relationship (perpendicularity, parallelism, angularity) of features.
*   **Tolerances of Location:** Control the position of features relative to datums.
*   **Tolerances of Profile:** Control the overall form and size of irregular surfaces or entire features.

**Textbook References for GD&T:**

*   **Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing by James D. Meadows:** This is a highly regarded textbook for understanding GD&T principles and applications.
*   **Fundamentals of Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing by Alex Krulikowski:** Another excellent resource that provides a clear and practical approach to GD&T.

**Course Outcome Alignment:**

*   **CO3: Practice GD & T in models as well as drawings (Knowledge Level: K2).** This section introduces the concepts, setting the stage for practical application later in the course.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   **GD&T is not just about making parts more precise; it's about making them *functionally* correct.**
*   **Understanding datum features and datum references is crucial for applying GD&T correctly.**
*   **The feature control frame is the key to communicating GD&T requirements.**

**Practice Exercise:**

For a simple block with a hole, consider the following:

1.  **Without GD&T:** How would you dimension the position of the hole using only basic dimensions? What potential issues could arise regarding the hole's alignment?
2.  **With GD&T:** How could you use a positional tolerance (e.g., Ø0.1) to control the location of the hole relative to the edges of the block?

**Answer:**

1.  **Without GD&T:** You would typically dimension the distance from the center of the hole to two adjacent edges of the block. However, this only controls the location of the center point. The hole itself could still be out of square or have variations in its orientation, potentially leading to assembly issues.
2.  **With GD&T:** You would define the two edges of the block as datums (e.g., Datum A and Datum B). Then, you would apply a positional tolerance to the center of the hole, referencing these datums. For example, a feature control frame like `| Ø 0.1 | A | B |` would specify that the center of the hole must lie within a 0.1mm diameter circle, whose center is located by the intersection of the datum planes. This ensures the hole is positioned accurately and oriented correctly relative to the datums.

## 7. Practice: Simple 2D Sketches to Familiarize with Concepts

This section emphasizes the practical application of the learned concepts through creating simple 2D sketches. This hands-on practice is vital for solidifying understanding and developing proficiency.

**Objective:** To reinforce the understanding of line types, dimensioning, and basic views by creating simple 2D representations of mechanical components.

**Suggested Practice Sketches:**

1.  **A Simple Rectangular Block with a Through Hole:**
    *   Draw the front view, top view, and side view.
    *   Use appropriate line types (visible, hidden, center).
    *   Dimension the length, width, height, and the diameter and location of the hole.
    *   Consider applying a scale if the object is too large or small for the paper.

2.  **A Cylindrical Pin:**
    *   Draw the front view and top view.
    *   Use visible, hidden, and center lines.
    *   Dimension the diameter and length.
    *   Add a chamfer at one or both ends and dimension it.

3.  **A Flanged Bushing:**
    *   Draw the front view and top view.
    *   This object will have both cylindrical and flange features.
    *   Use appropriate line types.
    *   Dimension the outer diameter, inner diameter, flange diameter, overall length, and flange thickness.
    *   Add center lines for any through holes.

4.  **A Simple Bracket:**
    *   Draw the front view and top view.
    *   This might involve angled surfaces.
    *   Dimension lengths, widths, heights, and any relevant angles.
    *   Consider adding a sectional view if internal features are important.

**Tips for Sketching:**

*   **Start with a light, freehand sketch to lay out the main features.**
*   **Use a ruler and compass for accuracy if drawing manually.** If using CAD software, utilize its drawing tools.
*   **Pay close attention to line weights and types.**
*   **Ensure dimensions are clear, readable, and complete.**
*   **Review your sketches to identify any areas for improvement.**

*   **Learning Outcomes:** Directly addresses the practice of simple 2D sketches to familiarize with line types, dimensioning, and basic views.
*   **CO1: Apply the knowledge of engineering drawings to interpret 2D drawings and model them using software (Knowledge Level: K3).** These sketches are a crucial step in interpreting and then modeling 2D drawings.
