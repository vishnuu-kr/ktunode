### **Topic: Introduction: Evolution of FMS**

This topic will introduce the fundamental concepts of Flexible Manufacturing Systems (FMS), tracing their historical development from traditional manufacturing paradigms to the sophisticated, automated systems of today. We will explore the driving forces behind this evolution and the key technological advancements that have enabled the creation of FMS.

*   **LO4:** Differentiate FMS from traditional manufacturing systems and other automated systems.

### **Key Concepts and Definitions:**

*   **Manufacturing System:** A collection of interconnected machines, equipment, and personnel that perform manufacturing operations on parts and products.
*   **Mass Production:** Manufacturing a large quantity of identical products with a high degree of specialization and dedicated machinery.
*   **Job Shop Manufacturing:** Manufacturing a small quantity of diverse products, requiring highly skilled labor and versatile machinery.
*   **Batch Manufacturing:** Producing a moderate quantity of products in groups or batches, often involving setup changes between batches.
*   **Automation:** The use of technology to perform tasks with minimal human intervention.
*   **Flexibility:** The ability of a manufacturing system to adapt to changes in product mix, production volume, and processing requirements.
*   **Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS):** A highly automated manufacturing system characterized by its ability to produce a variety of parts or products with minimal downtime and changeover times, often through the use of numerically controlled (NC) machines, robotics, automated material handling systems, and computer control.

### **1. Evolution of Manufacturing Systems:**

The journey to FMS is a story of technological advancement and changing market demands.

#### **1.1. Traditional Manufacturing Paradigms:**

*   **Craft Production:**
    *   **Description:** Highly skilled artisans produce unique or custom-made products. Work is often customized, and the focus is on craftsmanship.
    *   **Characteristics:** Low volume, high variety, highly skilled labor, manual operations.
    *   **Example:** A tailor making a custom suit, a blacksmith creating decorative ironwork.
    *   **Limitations:** Slow production, high cost per unit, difficult to scale.

*   **Mass Production (Fordism):**
    *   **Description:** Pioneered by Henry Ford with the assembly line for the Model T. Focus on standardization, specialization, and high-volume output of identical products.
    *   **Characteristics:** High volume, low variety, specialized machinery, dedicated tooling, assembly lines, division of labor.
    *   **Example:** Early automobile manufacturing, mass production of consumer goods.
    *   **Impact:** Significantly reduced production costs, made products accessible to a wider market.
    *   **Limitations:** Inflexible to product changes, high setup costs for new products, can lead to monotonous work.
    *   **Reference:** Groover, M. P. (2008). *Automation, Production Systems and Computer Integrated Manufacturing.* (Chapter 2: Manufacturing Systems) highlights the principles of mass production.

*   **Batch Manufacturing:**
    *   **Description:** A compromise between craft and mass production. Products are manufactured in discrete batches. Machinery and processes are often adaptable to different product types within a batch.
    *   **Characteristics:** Medium volume, medium variety, semi-specialized machinery, requires setup changes between batches.
    *   **Example:** Manufacturing a batch of shirts of a particular size and color, producing a specific model of engine component.
    *   **Limitations:** Significant downtime due to setup changes, inefficient for high-mix low-volume production.

#### **1.2. The Driving Forces for Flexibility:**

Several factors fueled the need for more flexible manufacturing systems:

*   **Changing Market Demands:**
    *   **Shorter Product Lifecycles:** Products become obsolete faster, requiring quicker adaptation to new designs.
    *   **Increased Product Variety:** Consumers demand more options and customized products.
    *   **Fluctuating Demand:** Market demand can be unpredictable, requiring systems that can ramp up or down production easily.
*   **Technological Advancements:**
    *   **Numerical Control (NC) and Computer Numerical Control (CNC):** Enabled machines to perform complex operations with programmable instructions, reducing the need for dedicated tooling and specialized operators for each task. (Luggen, W. W. (1991). *Flexible Manufacturing Cells and System.* discusses the foundational role of NC/CNC.)
    *   **Robotics:** Allowed for automated material handling, machine loading/unloading, and assembly tasks, increasing automation and reducing reliance on manual labor.
    *   **Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM):** Facilitated faster design iterations and translation of designs into machine instructions, crucial for quick product changeovers.
    *   **Advances in Control Systems and Software:** Enabled integrated control of multiple machines and material handling, the backbone of FMS.
*   **Economic Considerations:**
    *   **Reduced Inventory Costs:** Flexible systems can produce closer to demand, minimizing the need for large inventories.
    *   **Lower Setup Costs:** Minimizing changeover times makes it economically viable to produce smaller batches.
    *   **Improved Machine Utilization:** Reduced idle time for machines.

#### **1.3. The Emergence of Flexible Manufacturing Cells (FMCs) and Systems (FMS):**

*   **Flexible Manufacturing Cell (FMC):**
    *   **Definition:** A group of automated machines and a material handling system that are integrated to produce a family of parts. Typically involves fewer machines than an FMS.
    *   **Characteristics:** A subset of FMS, focused on a specific set of operations or a family of parts. Often centered around a single CNC machine with robotic loading/unloading.
    *   **Example:** A CNC lathe, a robot arm for loading/unloading, and a pallet changer.
    *   **Reference:** Luggen, W. W. (1991). *Flexible Manufacturing Cells and System.* (Chapter 2: Flexible Manufacturing Cells) provides in-depth coverage of FMCs.

*   **Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS):**
    *   **Definition:** A more comprehensive system that extends the concept of an FMC to include multiple machines, automated material handling systems (e.g., Automated Guided Vehicles - AGVs), and a central computer control system. FMS can produce a wider range of parts and handle more complex production sequences.
    *   **Key Components (to be covered in detail in later modules):**
        *   CNC machines (machining centers, lathes, etc.)
        *   Automated Material Handling Systems (AMHS) – AGVs, conveyors, robots.
        *   Tool management systems.
        *   Workpiece handling and fixtures.
        *   Central computer control system for overall system management and part routing.
        *   Inspection and measurement systems.
    *   **Example:** A system with several machining centers, a CNC turning center, an AGV system for transporting parts between machines, and a central supervisory computer that schedules operations and controls material flow.
    *   **Reference:** Shivanand H.K., Benal MM, Koti V (2006). *Flexible Manufacturing System.* (Chapter 1: Introduction to FMS) provides a foundational overview. Maleki, R. A. (1991). *Flexible Manufacturing System.* (Chapter 1: Introduction to Flexible Manufacturing Systems) also offers a good starting point.

#### **1.4. FMS vs. Other Manufacturing Systems:**

| Feature             | Mass Production          | Job Shop                | Batch Manufacturing     | Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS) |
| :------------------ | :----------------------- | :----------------------- | :---------------------- | :---------------------------------- |
| **Volume**          | Very High                | Very Low                 | Medium                  | High to Medium                      |
| **Variety**         | Very Low                 | Very High                | Medium                  | High                                |
| **Flexibility**     | Very Low                 | Very High                | Medium                  | High                                |
| **Automation**      | High (dedicated)         | Low (manual)             | Medium                  | Very High (integrated)              |
| **Setup Time**      | Very Low (for specific part) | Low (general purpose)    | High                    | Low                                 |
| **Tooling**         | Dedicated                | General Purpose          | Semi-specialized        | Adaptable (tool magazines, quick change) |
| **Workforce Skills**| Specialized              | Highly Skilled           | Skilled                 | Skilled (supervision, maintenance)  |
| **Cost per Unit**   | Very Low                 | Very High                | Medium                  | Low to Medium                       |
| **Complexity**      | Low                      | Medium                   | Medium                  | High                                |

### **2. Goals and Benefits of FMS:**

The implementation of FMS is driven by several strategic objectives.

#### **2.1. Primary Goals:**

*   **Increase Productivity:** By reducing setup times, minimizing idle time, and enabling unattended operation.
*   **Improve Machine Utilization:** Keeping machines running for longer periods.
*   **Reduce Lead Times:** Faster production cycles from order to delivery.
*   **Enhance Product Quality:** Consistent, automated processes lead to fewer defects.
*   **Reduce Manufacturing Costs:** Through higher efficiency, reduced labor, and lower inventory.
*   **Respond to Market Changes:** Quickly adapt to new product introductions and variations in demand.

#### **2.2. Key Benefits:**

*   **Reduced Work-in-Process (WIP) Inventory:** Parts move efficiently through the system.
*   **Lower Labor Costs:** Automation reduces the need for manual machine operators.
*   **Improved Flexibility to Produce Different Parts:** Can handle a mix of parts without extensive retooling.
*   **Reduced Manufacturing Cycle Time:** Faster throughput.
*   **Increased Throughput:** More parts produced in a given time.
*   **Better Space Utilization:** Optimized layout and material handling.
*   **Improved Working Conditions:** Reduces hazardous or repetitive tasks for human operators.
*   **Scalability:** Systems can often be expanded with additional machines or cells.

### **3. Key Historical Milestones and Technological Advancements:**

*   **1950s-1960s: Rise of NC/CNC:** The development of numerically controlled machines by companies like Kearney & Trecker and Cincinnati Milacron was a crucial precursor. This allowed for programmable automation of individual machines. (Kalpakjin, S. (1995). *Manufacturing Engineering and Technology.* Chapter 16: Computer Control of Manufacturing Operations) provides context on early automation.
*   **1970s: Early Automation and Robotics:** Integration of robots for material handling and early attempts to link multiple NC machines.
*   **1970s-1980s: The First FMS:** The development and implementation of the first true FMS, often in the aerospace and defense industries, where high-value, low-to-medium volume parts were common. The term "Flexible Manufacturing System" was coined.
*   **1980s Onwards: Maturation and Widespread Adoption:** FMS technology became more sophisticated with advances in computer control, software, networking, and material handling. Adoption spread to other industries.
*   **Integration of CAD/CAM:** The seamless flow of design data to manufacturing processes became increasingly important. (Lenz, J. E., & Eppinger, D. E. (1989). *Flexible Manufacturing.* likely discusses these integration aspects.)
*   **Unattended Machining:** The goal of operating machines for extended periods (e.g., overnight) without human intervention became a primary driver for FMS. This requires robust systems for tool changes, part handling, and process monitoring. (CO2 is relevant here).
*   **Toyota Production System (TPS) Influence:** While not directly an FMS technology, TPS principles like "Just-in-Time" (JIT), "Jidoka" (automation with a human touch), and waste reduction (Muda) have influenced the goals and operational philosophies of modern flexible manufacturing. Efficient flow, reduced setup times, and quality at the source are shared objectives. (CO2 highlights TPS).

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   **FMS is a system, not just a collection of machines.** The integration and control are key.
*   **Flexibility is the defining characteristic.** It's the ability to adapt to changing production needs.
*   **The evolution was driven by both market demands and technological capabilities.**
*   **FMS aims to achieve the efficiency of mass production with the adaptability of job shops.**
*   **NC/CNC technology is the bedrock upon which FMS is built.**

### **Practice Questions:**

1.  **Define Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS) in your own words, highlighting its key differentiating features.**
    *   **Answer:** An FMS is a manufacturing system that uses computer control, automated material handling, and a variety of automated machines (like CNC machines) to produce a range of parts or products with minimal manual intervention and quick changeover times. Its key differentiating feature is its high adaptability to product variety and production volume changes, unlike traditional mass production systems.

2.  **List and briefly explain three driving forces that led to the development of FMS.**
    *   **Answer:**
        1.  **Changing Market Demands:** Shorter product lifecycles and increasing consumer demand for product variety necessitate manufacturing systems that can adapt quickly.
        2.  **Technological Advancements:** Innovations in CNC machines, robotics, and computer control systems made flexible automation feasible.
        3.  **Economic Considerations:** The need to reduce costs through improved efficiency, lower inventory, and reduced setup times pushed for more flexible solutions.

3.  **Compare and contrast Batch Manufacturing with Flexible Manufacturing Systems (FMS) based on at least two key features.**
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Flexibility:** Batch manufacturing has medium flexibility, requiring significant setup time between batches. FMS offers high flexibility, with significantly reduced setup times and the ability to handle a wider variety of parts and sequences.
        *   **Automation:** Batch manufacturing typically uses semi-specialized machinery with manual intervention for setups. FMS employs high levels of integrated automation, including automated material handling and computer control, for machine operations and part flow.

4.  **Which technological advancement is considered a fundamental precursor to FMS? Briefly explain why.**
    *   **Answer:** Numerical Control (NC) and Computer Numerical Control (CNC) technology. This is because CNC machines allow for programmable control of machining operations, reducing the need for specialized tooling for each part and enabling quick changeovers by simply loading a new program. This programmability is essential for the flexibility that defines FMS.

5.  **How might the principles of the Toyota Production System (TPS) relate to the goals of FMS? (Referencing CO2)**
    *   **Answer:** TPS emphasizes efficiency, waste reduction (Muda), and quality at the source. FMS aligns with these principles by aiming to increase productivity, reduce lead times (which minimizes inventory waste), improve quality through automation, and reduce operational inefficiencies by minimizing machine idle time and setup times. The goal of minimizing waste and maximizing throughput is common to both.
