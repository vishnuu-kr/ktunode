# Module 3: 3D or Finite Aero Foils — Effect of Releasing the Wingtips

## Topic: Replacement of Finite Wing by Horseshoe Vortex System & Lifting Line Theory

**Learning Outcomes Covered:**

*   Relate the theoretical concepts to practical aerodynamic phenomena.

### 1. Introduction to Finite Wings and the Need for 3D Aerodynamics

**1.1 Limitations of 2D Aerofoil Theory:**

*   2D aerofoil theory, as typically studied, assumes an infinitely long wing (span = infinity).
*   This assumption leads to:
    *   Uniform flow over the entire span.
    *   No spanwise variation in lift.
    *   No wingtip effects.
*   However, real wings have finite span, leading to phenomena not accounted for in 2D analysis.

**1.2 The Finite Wing Problem:**

*   When a wing has a finite span, the high-pressure air from the lower surface tends to flow around the wingtip to the lower-pressure upper surface.
*   This flow creates a swirling motion at the wingtips, known as **wingtip vortices**.
*   These vortices significantly influence the airflow over the wing and create additional drag.

**Key Concept:** The fundamental difference between 2D and 3D aerodynamics lies in the presence and effect of wingtip vortices due to finite wing span.

---

### 2. The Horseshoe Vortex System: Replacing the Finite Wing

**2.1 Concept:**

*   To analytically model the flow around a finite wing, we can replace the wing with a simplified vortex system.
*   The most common and fundamental model is the **horseshoe vortex**.
*   The horseshoe vortex consists of:
    *   A **bound vortex** that runs along the span of the wing, representing the circulation (lift) generated by the wing.
    *   Two **trailing vortices** that originate from the wingtips and extend infinitely downstream.

**2.2 Structure of the Horseshoe Vortex:**

*   **Bound Vortex:**
    *   Represents the lift-generating mechanism of the wing.
    *   Circulation ($\Gamma$) is constant along its length (initially, in simplified theory).
    *   The magnitude of circulation is directly related to the lift produced by the wing section.
*   **Trailing Vortices:**
    *   Formed due to the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces at the wingtips.
    *   They carry the circulation from the wingtips downstream.
    *   They are responsible for the downwash induced over the wing.

**Visual Representation (Imagine):**

```
        -------> (Trailing Vortex 1 - right tip)
       /
      /
     / (Bound Vortex along wing span)
    /
   /
  -------> (Trailing Vortex 2 - left tip)
```

**Key Concept:** The horseshoe vortex system is a theoretical construct that simplifies the complex 3D flow around a finite wing into manageable vortex components.

### 3. Lifting Line Theory

**3.1 Principle:**

*   Lifting Line Theory (LLT) is a powerful analytical tool for understanding the aerodynamics of finite wings.
*   It makes the simplifying assumption that the entire lift of the wing can be represented by a **single, infinitely thin vortex filament (the lifting line)** along the wing's span.
*   This lifting line is a simplification of the bound vortex in the horseshoe vortex system.

**3.2 Key Assumptions of Lifting Line Theory:**

*   **Thin Wing:** The wing's chord is small compared to its span.
*   **Slender Wing:** The wing's span is large compared to its chord.
*   **Smooth Spanwise Lift Distribution:** The lift distribution is assumed to be continuous and differentiable along the span.
*   **Negligible Viscous Effects:** Focus is primarily on inviscid flow, although the theory can be extended.
*   **No Airfoil Shape Effects (Initially):** The theory primarily focuses on the effect of spanwise lift distribution and ignores the specific aerofoil profile. The aerofoil shape is indirectly represented by the circulation ($\Gamma$).

**3.3 Mathematical Formulation (Conceptual):**

*   The circulation $\Gamma$ is assumed to vary along the wingspan, represented by a function $\Gamma(y)$, where $y$ is the spanwise coordinate.
*   The lift generated by a small section of the wing (wing element) of length $dy$ is given by:
    $dL = \rho V \Gamma(y) dy$
    Where:
    *   $\rho$ is the air density.
    *   $V$ is the freestream velocity.
    *   $\Gamma(y)$ is the circulation at spanwise position $y$.

*   **Downwash ($\mathbf{w}$):** The trailing vortices induce a downward velocity (downwash) on the lifting line. This downwash is crucial as it affects the local angle of attack experienced by the wing.
    *   The downwash velocity at a point on the lifting line due to the trailing vortices is given by:
        $w(y) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{-b/2}^{b/2} \frac{d\Gamma}{d\eta} \frac{1}{y-\eta} d\eta$
        Where:
        *   $b$ is the wingspan.
        *   $\eta$ is the integration variable for spanwise position.
        *   $d\Gamma/d\eta$ is the rate of change of circulation along the span.

*   **Induced Angle of Attack ($\mathbf{\alpha_i}$):** The downwash causes a reduction in the effective angle of attack.
    $\alpha_i(y) = \frac{w(y)}{V}$

*   **Effective Angle of Attack ($\mathbf{\alpha_{eff}}$):**
    $\alpha_{eff}(y) = \alpha_{geom}(y) - \alpha_i(y)$
    Where $\alpha_{geom}(y)$ is the geometric angle of attack of the wing section.

*   **Lift Coefficient Variation:** The local lift coefficient $C_l(y)$ for a wing section is related to the circulation by:
    $C_l(y) = \frac{2 \Gamma(y)}{Vc}$
    Where $c$ is the local chord length.

**Key Concept:** Lifting Line Theory simplifies the wing into a line of varying circulation and quantifies the effect of trailing vortices through induced downwash, leading to an induced angle of attack and consequently, induced drag.

### 4. Wingtip Vortices and Induced Drag

**4.1 Formation of Wingtip Vortices:**

*   At the wingtips, there is no bound vortex to contain the circulation.
*   The high-pressure air from the lower surface spills over to the low-pressure upper surface.
*   This flow creates a powerful rotating vortex that trails from each wingtip.

**4.2 Effect on Downwash:**

*   The trailing vortices create a downwash velocity ($w$) over the wing.
*   This downwash is strongest near the wingtips and diminishes towards the wing root.

**4.3 Induced Angle of Attack:**

*   The downwash effectively tilts the local airflow downwards.
*   This tilt results in an **induced angle of attack** ($\alpha_i$) which is always negative for a lifting wing.

**4.4 Induced Drag ($\mathbf{D_i}$):**

*   Due to the induced angle of attack, the local lift vector is tilted backward relative to the freestream velocity.
*   This backward-tilted component of the lift force is experienced as **induced drag**.
*   Induced drag is a consequence of lift in a finite wing and is therefore a characteristic of 3D aerodynamics.

**4.5 Quantifying Induced Drag:**

*   The induced drag coefficient ($C_{Di}$) is given by:
    $C_{Di} = \frac{C_L^3}{\pi e AR^2}$
    Where:
    *   $C_L$ is the overall wing lift coefficient (integrated lift over the wing area).
    *   $e$ is the **Oswald efficiency factor** (or span efficiency factor). This factor accounts for the deviation of the actual lift distribution from an ideal elliptical distribution. For an elliptical lift distribution, $e=1$. Real wings have $e < 1$.
    *   $AR$ is the **Aspect Ratio** of the wing ($AR = b^2/S$, where $S$ is the wing area).

*   The induced drag force ($D_i$) is:
    $D_i = q S C_{Di}$
    Where $q$ is the dynamic pressure ($q = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2$).

**Importance of Aspect Ratio:**
*   Higher aspect ratio wings (long and slender) generally have lower induced drag for a given lift. This is why gliders and high-altitude aircraft often have high aspect ratio wings.
*   Lower aspect ratio wings (short and stubby) have higher induced drag.

**Importance of Oswald Efficiency Factor:**
*   An elliptical lift distribution (constant $C_l \times c$) along the span results in the most efficient lift production and lowest induced drag ($e=1$).
*   Deviations from the elliptical distribution (e.g., rectangular wings, wings with twist or taper) reduce the efficiency ($e < 1$).

**Key Concepts:** Wingtip vortices cause downwash, which leads to an induced angle of attack and consequently induced drag. Induced drag is inversely proportional to the aspect ratio and the Oswald efficiency factor.

### 5. Effect of Wing Shape (Planform) on Lift and Drag

**5.1 General:**

*   The planform (shape of the wing when viewed from above) significantly influences the lift distribution along the span and, therefore, induced drag.
*   LLT can be used to analyze different planforms.

**5.2 Elliptical Wing:**

*   **Lift Distribution:** Produces an elliptical lift distribution ($\Gamma(y) \propto \sqrt{1 - (2y/b)^2}$).
*   **Oswald Efficiency:** $e = 1$ (theoretically).
*   **Induced Drag:** Minimum for a given lift and aspect ratio.
*   **Pros:** Aerodynamically efficient.
*   **Cons:** Structurally complex and difficult to manufacture.

**5.3 Rectangular Wing:**

*   **Lift Distribution:** Approximately uniform circulation along the span.
*   **Oswald Efficiency:** $e < 1$ (typically around 0.7-0.8).
*   **Induced Drag:** Higher than an elliptical wing of the same aspect ratio and lift.
*   **Pros:** Simple to manufacture, stall characteristics are often gentle.
*   **Cons:** Aerodynamically less efficient in terms of induced drag.

**5.4 Tapered Wing:**

*   **Lift Distribution:** Varies based on the taper ratio. Can be designed to approach elliptical.
*   **Oswald Efficiency:** Generally better than rectangular, but less than elliptical. Can be close to 1 if designed carefully (e.g., with wash-out).
*   **Pros:** Good compromise between aerodynamic efficiency and manufacturability.
*   **Cons:** More complex than rectangular.

**5.5 Swept Wings:**

*   **Aerodynamic Effects:** Introduce significant complexities, including spanwise flow and changes in effective angle of attack along the span due to compressibility effects (though LLT in its basic form doesn't directly handle compressibility).
*   **LLT Application:** LLT can be adapted for swept wings by considering the spanwise component of velocity and effective angle of attack.

**5.6 Wing Twist (Wash-out/Wash-in):**

*   **Wash-out:** Geometric angle of attack is reduced towards the wingtip. This is a common design feature to achieve a lift distribution closer to elliptical and improve stall characteristics (tip stalls last).
*   **Wash-in:** Geometric angle of attack is increased towards the wingtip (less common).

**Key Concept:** The planform and geometric twist of a wing are critical design parameters that dictate the spanwise lift distribution, influencing induced drag and stall behavior.

### 6. Applications and Importance

**6.1 Understanding Induced Drag:**

*   Crucial for aircraft performance calculations, especially at lower speeds and higher angles of attack where induced drag is a significant component of total drag.
*   Informs aircraft design choices for wings (aspect ratio, taper, twist).

**6.2 Aircraft Design:**

*   **Gliders/High-Altitude Aircraft:** Employ high aspect ratio wings to minimize induced drag.
*   **Fighter Jets:** Often have lower aspect ratio wings for maneuverability, accepting higher induced drag at certain flight conditions.
*   **Transport Aircraft:** Strike a balance, often using tapered wings with moderate aspect ratios.

**6.3 Performance Analysis:**

*   LLT provides the foundation for calculating induced drag, which is essential for determining:
    *   Stall characteristics.
    *   Climb performance.
    *   Range and endurance.
    *   Takeoff and landing distances.

**6.4 Connection to CO2:**

*   This topic directly addresses the evaluation of the effects of wingtip vortices on 3D aerofoils and the calculation of induced drag using lifting line theory. It also highlights the impact of finite wing plan shapes on aerodynamic performance.

### 7. Textbook and Reference Content Integration

*   **Kermode's Mechanics of Flight:** Likely covers the fundamental principles of lift, drag, and the concepts of wingtip vortices and induced drag in its early chapters on aerodynamics. It may provide a more intuitive explanation of the physical phenomena.
*   **Anderson's Fundamentals of Aerodynamics:** This is a core reference. It will delve deeply into the mathematical formulation of lifting line theory, including the downwash integral and the derivation of induced drag. Expect detailed explanations of aspect ratio and Oswald efficiency.
*   **Anderson's Introduction to Flight:** Provides a broader overview of flight principles, including a section on the effects of finite wings, introducing the concept of wingtip vortices and their implications without necessarily going into the full mathematical rigor of LLT as in Fundamentals.
*   **Houghton et al.'s Aerodynamics for Engineering Students:** A comprehensive reference that will likely offer a detailed mathematical treatment of lifting line theory, including various methods for approximating spanload distributions and calculating induced drag for different planforms. It might also cover extensions to LLT.

**Important Point to Remember:** Lifting line theory is a *linearized* theory. It works best for moderate angles of attack where the relationship between lift and angle of attack is linear. At very high angles of attack, stall occurs, and the assumptions break down.

### 8. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

Explain the phenomenon of wingtip vortex formation and its direct consequence on the aerodynamic forces acting on a finite wing.

**Answer 1:**

Wingtip vortex formation is caused by the overflow of high-pressure air from the lower surface of the wing to the low-pressure upper surface at the wingtips. This high-pressure-to-low-pressure flow spills around the wingtip, creating a swirling vortex. The direct consequence is the induction of a downwash velocity over the wing. This downwash causes an "induced angle of attack," which effectively tilts the local lift vector backward, resulting in a component of drag known as induced drag.

---

**Question 2:**

The Lifting Line Theory simplifies a finite wing into what fundamental vortex structure? What is the primary role of this simplification?

**Answer 2:**

Lifting Line Theory simplifies a finite wing into a **lifting line**, which is essentially a bound vortex of varying circulation along the wingspan. The primary role of this simplification is to make the analysis of the complex 3D flow around a finite wing tractable, allowing for the calculation of spanwise lift distribution and induced drag.

---

**Question 3:**

Define Aspect Ratio (AR) and Oswald efficiency factor ($e$). How do changes in AR and $e$ affect induced drag?

**Answer 3:**

*   **Aspect Ratio (AR):** Defined as the square of the wingspan ($b$) divided by the wing area ($S$), i.e., $AR = b^2/S$. It is a measure of how long and slender the wing is.
*   **Oswald efficiency factor ($e$):** A dimensionless factor representing the aerodynamic efficiency of a wing's spanload distribution. An elliptical distribution has $e=1$, while real wings have $e < 1$.

**Effect on Induced Drag:** Induced drag ($C_{Di} \propto \frac{C_L^3}{\pi e AR^2}$) is inversely proportional to the aspect ratio and the Oswald efficiency factor. Therefore:
*   Increasing the aspect ratio (making the wing more slender) *decreases* induced drag.
*   Increasing the Oswald efficiency factor (making the lift distribution closer to elliptical) *decreases* induced drag.

**Question 4:**

A glider has a wingspan of 20 meters and a wing area of 30 m². It flies at a speed where its lift coefficient is 0.6. If the Oswald efficiency factor is estimated to be 0.9, calculate the induced drag coefficient. (Assume standard atmospheric conditions if density is needed for force, but only coefficient is asked).

**Given:**
*   $b = 20$ m
*   $S = 30$ m²
*   $C_L = 0.6$
*   $e = 0.9$

**Solution:**

First, calculate the Aspect Ratio (AR):
$AR = \frac{b^2}{S} = \frac{(20 \text{ m})^2}{30 \text{ m}^2} = \frac{400 \text{ m}^2}{30 \text{ m}^2} = 13.33$

Now, use the formula for induced drag coefficient:
$C_{Di} = \frac{C_L^3}{\pi e AR^2}$
$C_{Di} = \frac{(0.6)^3}{\pi \times 0.9 \times (13.33)^2}$
$C_{Di} = \frac{0.216}{\pi \times 0.9 \times 177.69}$
$C_{Di} = \frac{0.216}{502.36}$
$C_{Di} \approx 0.00043$

**Answer:** The induced drag coefficient is approximately 0.00043.

### 9. Important Points to Remember

*   **Finite Wing vs. Infinite Wing:** The key difference is the presence of wingtip vortices in finite wings.
*   **Horseshoe Vortex:** A fundamental model to represent a finite wing's circulation and trailing vortices.
*   **Lifting Line Theory:** Simplifies the wing to a line of varying circulation to analyze 3D effects.
*   **Downwash & Induced Angle of Attack:** Trailing vortices induce downwash, reducing the effective angle of attack and tilting lift backwards.
*   **Induced Drag:** The drag component caused by the downwash and induced angle of attack. It is a characteristic of 3D lift.
*   **Aspect Ratio (AR):** Higher AR means lower induced drag.
*   **Oswald Efficiency ($e$):** Closer to 1 means more efficient lift distribution and lower induced drag. Elliptical wings have $e=1$.
*   **Planform:** The shape of the wing (rectangular, tapered, elliptical) affects the spanwise lift distribution and thus induced drag.
*   **Wash-out:** Reducing the geometric angle of attack towards the tip improves stall characteristics and can approach elliptical lift distribution.
