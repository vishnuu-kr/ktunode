# Quality Engineering and Management: Tools and Techniques in TQM - Scatter Diagram

This module focuses on understanding and applying the Scatter Diagram, a powerful tool within Total Quality Management (TQM). By examining the relationship between two variables, we can gain insights into potential causes and effects, crucial for problem-solving and process improvement.

---

## 1. Introduction to Scatter Diagrams

A Scatter Diagram is a graphical tool used to investigate the relationship between two variables. It helps to determine if there is a correlation between them and the strength and direction of that correlation. This tool is fundamental to understanding cause-and-effect relationships, which is a cornerstone of TQM.

### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Variable:** A quantity that can change or vary. In a scatter diagram, we examine two variables.
*   **Independent Variable (Cause):** The variable that is manipulated or changed. It is plotted on the horizontal (X) axis.
*   **Dependent Variable (Effect):** The variable that is measured and is expected to change in response to the independent variable. It is plotted on the vertical (Y) axis.
*   **Correlation:** A statistical measure that describes the extent to which two variables change together.
    *   **Positive Correlation:** As one variable increases, the other tends to increase.
    *   **Negative Correlation:** As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.
    *   **No Correlation:** There is no discernible relationship between the two variables.
*   **Strength of Correlation:** How closely the data points cluster around a line of best fit. Stronger correlations have points closer to the line.

### Textual Support

*   **Besterfield et al. (2018)** describes the scatter diagram as a plot of paired data points where one variable is plotted on the horizontal axis and the other on the vertical axis. It's used to identify the nature of the relationship between two variables.
*   **Montgomery (Third Edition)** highlights the scatter diagram as one of the "Seven Basic Tools of Quality" and emphasizes its role in visually detecting potential relationships between two variables.

## 2. Purpose and Application of Scatter Diagrams

The primary purpose of a scatter diagram is to visualize and analyze the relationship between two variables to identify potential cause-and-effect relationships, which is directly aligned with **CO3: Implement different tools and techniques in TQM**.

### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Cause-and-Effect Identification:** Helps to hypothesize whether one variable influences another, enabling targeted improvements.
*   **Process Understanding:** Provides insights into how different factors within a process might be interrelated.
*   **Data Validation:** Can help validate or refute theories about relationships between variables.

### Examples

**Example 1: Manufacturing Process**

*   **Independent Variable (X):** Temperature of the curing oven (°C)
*   **Dependent Variable (Y):** Strength of the product (psi)

By plotting pairs of temperature and strength data, a scatter diagram might reveal a positive correlation, indicating that higher curing temperatures lead to stronger products (up to an optimal point).

**Example 2: Customer Service**

*   **Independent Variable (X):** Number of training hours for customer service representatives.
*   **Dependent Variable (Y):** Customer satisfaction score.

A scatter diagram could show a positive correlation, suggesting that increased training leads to higher customer satisfaction.

**Example 3: Software Development**

*   **Independent Variable (X):** Number of bugs found per day during testing.
*   **Dependent Variable (Y):** Time taken to fix bugs (hours).

This might reveal a negative correlation, implying that a higher rate of bugs found leads to longer overall bug-fixing time due to an overwhelmed development team.

## 3. Constructing a Scatter Diagram

Creating a scatter diagram involves a systematic process of data collection and plotting.

### Steps for Construction

1.  **Identify the Variables:** Clearly define the two variables you want to investigate. Determine which is the potential cause (independent) and which is the potential effect (dependent).
2.  **Collect Paired Data:** Gather a sufficient amount of data where both variables are measured for the same instance or subject.
3.  **Label Axes:** Label the horizontal (X) axis with the independent variable and the vertical (Y) axis with the dependent variable. Include units of measurement.
4.  **Determine Scale:** Choose appropriate scales for each axis to ensure all data points can be plotted clearly and the relationship is visible.
5.  **Plot Data Points:** For each pair of data, plot a point at the intersection of the corresponding X and Y values.
6.  **Analyze the Pattern:** Examine the distribution of the plotted points to identify any patterns, trends, or correlations.
7.  **Draw a Line of Best Fit (Optional but Recommended):** If a relationship appears to exist, draw a line that best represents the trend of the data points. This can be done visually or statistically.

### Textual Support

*   **Mitra (Second Edition)** provides step-by-step guidance on constructing scatter diagrams, emphasizing the importance of collecting paired data and plotting them accurately.
*   **Juran and Gryna (Third Edition)** discuss the visual interpretation of scatter diagrams, including how to recognize different types of relationships.

## 4. Interpreting Scatter Diagrams

The interpretation of a scatter diagram is crucial for drawing meaningful conclusions and informing quality improvement efforts. This aligns with **CO3: Implement different tools and techniques in TQM** and **CO4: Implement different statistical quality control techniques**.

### Types of Relationships (Patterns)

*   **Positive Linear Correlation:** Points cluster around an upward-sloping line.
    *   *Example:* As the amount of fertilizer increases, crop yield increases.
*   **Negative Linear Correlation:** Points cluster around a downward-sloping line.
    *   *Example:* As the price of a product increases, the demand decreases.
*   **Non-linear Correlation:** The relationship is not a straight line, but a curve.
    *   *Example:* The relationship between the speed of a machine and its defect rate might increase rapidly after a certain speed.
*   **No Correlation:** Points are scattered randomly with no discernible pattern.
    *   *Example:* The height of a customer and the color of the product they purchase.
*   **Clustering:** Data points may form distinct groups, suggesting different underlying causes or conditions.
*   **Outliers:** Points that lie far away from the general pattern, indicating unusual circumstances or errors.

### Strength of Correlation

*   **Strong Correlation:** Data points are tightly clustered around the line of best fit.
*   **Moderate Correlation:** Data points are somewhat spread out but still show a clear trend.
*   **Weak Correlation:** Data points are widely scattered, but a general trend can still be perceived.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Correlation does not imply causation:** While a scatter diagram can suggest a relationship, it doesn't prove that one variable causes the other. There might be a third, unobserved variable influencing both.
*   **Sample Size:** A larger sample size generally provides more reliable results.
*   **Context is Key:** Interpretation should always be done within the context of the process or system being studied.

### Textual Support

*   **SubburajRamasamy (First Edition)** discusses how to interpret the "slope" and "spread" of points on a scatter diagram to understand the nature and strength of the relationship.
*   **Crosby (1979)**, while not directly focused on scatter diagrams, emphasizes the importance of data-driven decision-making in quality improvement, which scatter diagrams facilitate.

## 5. Advantages and Limitations of Scatter Diagrams

Like any tool, scatter diagrams have their strengths and weaknesses.

### Advantages

*   **Simple to construct and interpret:** Easy to understand visually.
*   **Identifies potential relationships:** Helps to generate hypotheses for further investigation.
*   **Highlights outliers:** Points that deviate from the trend can be easily spotted.
*   **Provides visual evidence:** Makes abstract relationships concrete.
*   **Supports statistical analysis:** Can be a precursor to more sophisticated statistical tests.

### Limitations

*   **Subjective interpretation:** Visual interpretation of the "line of best fit" can be subjective.
*   **Correlation vs. Causation:** Does not prove causation.
*   **Requires paired data:** Not all data sets will have naturally paired observations.
*   **Can be misleading with small sample sizes:** Patterns may appear by chance.
*   **Doesn't quantify the strength of correlation:** While visual strength can be assessed, a specific statistical measure (like correlation coefficient) is needed for precise quantification.

## 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
You are investigating the relationship between the amount of a chemical additive (in grams) and the drying time of a paint (in hours). You collect the following data:

| Additive (g) | Drying Time (hrs) |
| :----------- | :---------------- |
| 10           | 5                 |
| 15           | 4                 |
| 20           | 3.5               |
| 25           | 3                 |
| 30           | 3.2               |
| 35           | 3.1               |
| 40           | 3.3               |

a) Which variable is the independent variable and which is the dependent variable?
b) Sketch a scatter diagram for this data.
c) Describe the relationship you observe between the additive amount and drying time.

**Question 2:**
A company is analyzing the relationship between the number of customer complaints received per day and the number of days the production line experienced downtime. They plot the data and observe points scattered randomly across the graph. What conclusion can you draw from this observation?

**Question 3:**
List three advantages of using a scatter diagram in a quality improvement project.

## 8. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
a) Independent Variable: Additive (g) (plotted on X-axis)
   Dependent Variable: Drying Time (hrs) (plotted on Y-axis)

b) **Sketch:** (You would draw a graph with X-axis labeled "Additive (g)" and Y-axis labeled "Drying Time (hrs)". Plot the points as given.)
    *   (10, 5)
    *   (15, 4)
    *   (20, 3.5)
    *   (25, 3)
    *   (30, 3.2)
    *   (35, 3.1)
    *   (40, 3.3)

c) **Description:** The scatter diagram shows a negative correlation between the amount of additive and drying time. As the amount of additive increases, the drying time tends to decrease, up to approximately 25g. Beyond that point, the drying time appears to stabilize or slightly increase, suggesting a possible non-linear relationship or an optimal range for the additive.

**Answer 2:**
The random scattering of points suggests that there is **no discernible correlation** between the number of customer complaints per day and the number of days the production line experienced downtime. One does not appear to influence the other based on this data.

**Answer 3:**
Three advantages of using a scatter diagram:
1.  **Identifies potential relationships:** It visually reveals if there's a relationship between two variables, helping to form hypotheses.
2.  **Simple to understand:** The visual nature makes it easy for anyone to grasp the potential connection (or lack thereof).
3.  **Highlights outliers:** Unusual data points that deviate from the general trend are easily spotted, prompting further investigation.

---

## 9. Key Points to Remember

*   Scatter diagrams visually represent the relationship between **two variables**.
*   Always plot the **independent (cause) variable on the X-axis** and the **dependent (effect) variable on the Y-axis**.
*   Look for patterns like **positive, negative, non-linear, or no correlation**.
*   **Correlation does not mean causation**. Further investigation is required to establish cause-and-effect.
*   Scatter diagrams are a fundamental tool for **data-driven decision-making** in TQM.
