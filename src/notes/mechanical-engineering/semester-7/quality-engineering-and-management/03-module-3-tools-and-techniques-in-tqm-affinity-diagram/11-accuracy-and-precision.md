# Module 3: Tools and Techniques in TQM - Affinity Diagram

## Topic: Accuracy and Precision

### 1. Introduction to Accuracy and Precision in Quality Management

In Quality Engineering and Management, understanding the difference between accuracy and precision is fundamental to assessing the reliability of measurements, processes, and data. While often used interchangeably in everyday language, in a quality context, they represent distinct aspects of measurement quality. This distinction is crucial when employing various TQM tools and techniques, including the Affinity Diagram, for problem-solving and process improvement.

**Connection to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO3 (Implement different tools and techniques in TQM):** Understanding accuracy and precision is essential for correctly interpreting data gathered and analyzed using tools like the Affinity Diagram.
*   **CO4 (Implement different statistical quality control techniques):** Accuracy and precision are core concepts in statistical process control (SPC) and measurement system analysis (MSA).

### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

#### 2.1 Accuracy

*   **Definition:** Accuracy refers to the closeness of a measurement or a series of measurements to the true or accepted value. It answers the question: "How close are we to the correct value?"
*   **In TQM Context:** An accurate process or measurement consistently produces results that are close to the desired or target outcome.
*   **Synonyms/Related Terms:** Correctness, closeness to true value, validity.

#### 2.2 Precision

*   **Definition:** Precision refers to the closeness of agreement between a series of independent measurements obtained under stipulated conditions. It answers the question: "How close are the measurements to each other?"
*   **In TQM Context:** A precise process or measurement consistently produces similar results, even if those results are not close to the true value.
*   **Synonyms/Related Terms:** Repeatability, reproducibility, consistency, closeness of measurements.

#### 2.3 Relationship between Accuracy and Precision

*   **Accuracy and Precision are Independent:** A process or measurement can be:
    *   **Accurate and Precise:** Measurements are close to the true value and close to each other. This is the ideal state.
    *   **Precise but Not Accurate:** Measurements are close to each other but far from the true value. This indicates a consistent bias or systematic error.
    *   **Accurate but Not Precise:** Measurements are scattered but, on average, are close to the true value. This indicates random error.
    *   **Neither Accurate nor Precise:** Measurements are scattered and far from the true value. This is the worst-case scenario.

### 3. Visualizing Accuracy and Precision

The concepts of accuracy and precision are often best understood through visual aids, particularly when dealing with data points or measurement results.

#### 3.1 Target Analogy

Imagine shooting arrows at a target:

*   **Accurate and Precise:** All arrows land very close to the bullseye and very close to each other.
*   **Precise but Not Accurate:** All arrows land close to each other but are clustered far from the bullseye (e.g., all in the top-left corner).
*   **Accurate but Not Precise:** Arrows are scattered widely but are, on average, around the bullseye.
*   **Neither Accurate nor Precise:** Arrows are scattered widely and are also far from the bullseye.

#### 3.2 Data Distribution Analogy

*   **Accurate and Precise:** Data points cluster tightly around the true value.
*   **Precise but Not Accurate:** Data points cluster tightly, but the cluster is centered away from the true value.
*   **Accurate but Not Precise:** Data points are widely spread, but the mean of the data is close to the true value.
*   **Neither Accurate nor Precise:** Data points are widely spread, and their mean is far from the true value.

### 4. Types of Errors Affecting Accuracy and Precision

Understanding the types of errors helps in diagnosing why a process might be inaccurate or imprecise.

#### 4.1 Systematic Error (Bias)

*   **Definition:** A systematic error is a consistent error that causes measurements to be consistently too high or too low compared to the true value. It affects **accuracy**.
*   **Causes:** Faulty equipment, incorrect calibration, flawed measurement procedures, inherent biases in the process.
*   **Impact:** Shifts the entire distribution of measurements away from the true value.
*   **Example:** A weighing scale that consistently reads 1 kg heavier than the actual weight.

#### 4.2 Random Error (Variance)

*   **Definition:** A random error is an error that varies unpredictably from one measurement to the next. It affects **precision**.
*   **Causes:** Fluctuations in environmental conditions, variations in operator technique, inherent variability in the materials or process.
*   **Impact:** Causes measurements to scatter around their average value.
*   **Example:** Slight variations in temperature or humidity affecting the measurement of a physical dimension, or slight differences in how an operator holds a measuring tool.

### 5. Accuracy and Precision in the Context of the Affinity Diagram

The Affinity Diagram is a tool used to organize and group a large number of ideas or data points into natural clusters based on their relationships. While the Affinity Diagram itself doesn't directly measure accuracy or precision, understanding these concepts is vital for the *quality* of the data being organized.

*   **Data Input:** When using an Affinity Diagram to analyze customer feedback, process issues, or potential causes of defects, the quality (accuracy and precision) of the original data collection is paramount.
*   **Identifying Root Causes:** If the data used in the Affinity Diagram is inaccurate or imprecise, the resulting clusters might not reflect the true relationships or root causes of a problem. This can lead to ineffective solutions.
*   **Process Improvement:** When using the Affinity Diagram to brainstorm solutions, the proposed solutions themselves should aim for accuracy (achieving the desired outcome) and precision (consistency in implementation and results).

**Example Scenario:**

Imagine a manufacturing company uses an Affinity Diagram to analyze customer complaints.

*   **Complaint Data Source:** If the data collected from customer calls (e.g., reasons for complaints) is based on imprecise recordings or inaccurate summaries by call center agents, the initial data points fed into the Affinity Diagram will be flawed.
*   **Affinity Diagram Output:** The diagram might group complaints about "product defects" and "product malfunction" together. However, if the original data was imprecise, a complaint initially coded as a "minor cosmetic flaw" might have been inaccurately categorized as a "major functional defect." This miscategorization can lead the team to focus on the wrong root causes.
*   **Impact on Solutions:** If the team, based on flawed data, concludes that "material quality" is the primary issue, their proposed solutions will be inaccurate for addressing the actual problem.

**Key Takeaway for Affinity Diagram:** **Garbage In, Garbage Out.** The effectiveness of an Affinity Diagram, or any TQM tool, relies heavily on the accuracy and precision of the input data.

### 6. Measurement System Analysis (MSA) and Accuracy/Precision

Measurement systems are critical in any quality process. MSA aims to ensure that the measurement system itself is capable and does not introduce significant errors. Accuracy and precision are central to MSA.

*   **Accuracy in MSA:** Often assessed through **Calibration**. Calibration involves comparing a measurement device against a known standard to ensure it reads correctly.
*   **Precision in MSA:** Often assessed through **Repeatability and Reproducibility (R&R)** studies.
    *   **Repeatability:** The variation observed when the same operator measures the same part multiple times using the same equipment.
    *   **Reproducibility:** The variation observed when different operators measure the same part using the same equipment, or when the same operator measures the same part using different equipment.

**Reference from Textbooks:**

*   **Montgomery's "Introduction to Statistical Quality Control":** Discusses the importance of measurement systems and introduces concepts like bias (accuracy) and variance (precision) in the context of statistical process control and capability analysis.
*   **Besterfield's "Total Quality Management":** Likely covers measurement systems and their role in data collection for quality improvement initiatives, implicitly touching upon accuracy and precision as desirable attributes.
*   **Mitra's "Fundamentals of Quality Control and Improvement":** Provides detailed explanations of measurement error, calibration, and statistical methods for assessing the performance of measurement systems.

### 7. Achieving Accuracy and Precision in Quality Processes

*   **Accurate Processes:**
    *   Clearly define and document standard operating procedures (SOPs).
    *   Use calibrated and well-maintained equipment.
    *   Train personnel thoroughly on correct methods.
    *   Implement control charts to monitor for systematic shifts.
    *   Validate processes against target values.
*   **Precise Processes:**
    *   Minimize variability in raw materials and inputs.
    *   Standardize operating procedures to reduce human error.
    *   Control environmental factors.
    *   Use measurement systems with adequate resolution and sensitivity.
    *   Implement statistical process control (SPC) to detect and correct deviations.

### 9. Practice Questions

**Question 1:** A quality inspector is measuring the diameter of manufactured parts. They measure the same part five times and get readings of 10.05 mm, 10.06 mm, 10.04 mm, 10.05 mm, and 10.06 mm. The true diameter of the part is 10.00 mm. Describe the accuracy and precision of these measurements.

**Question 2:** In a chemical titration, a lab technician consistently obtains a result that is 5% lower than the known concentration of the solution. However, repeated trials yield very similar results. Is the measurement accurate, precise, both, or neither? Explain.

**Question 3:** How does the quality of data (accuracy and precision) impact the effectiveness of using an Affinity Diagram for root cause analysis?

**Question 4:** A measuring instrument has been calibrated and found to be correct. However, when multiple operators use it, they get slightly different readings for the same object. What aspect of measurement quality is likely being compromised?

### 10. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
The measurements (10.05, 10.06, 10.04, 10.05, 10.06 mm) are **precise** because they are very close to each other. However, they are **not accurate** because they are consistently higher than the true diameter of 10.00 mm. There is a systematic error (bias) of approximately +0.05 mm.

**Answer 2:**
The measurement is **precise** because repeated trials yield very similar results, indicating low variability. It is **not accurate** because the results are consistently 5% lower than the known concentration, indicating a systematic error or bias.

**Answer 3:**
The quality of data directly impacts the effectiveness of an Affinity Diagram.
*   **Accurate data** ensures that the ideas, problems, or causes being organized reflect reality and the true nature of the situation. If data is inaccurate, the groupings and subsequent analysis will be based on false premises, leading to incorrect conclusions and ineffective solutions.
*   **Precise data** ensures consistency in the data points themselves. Imprecise data can lead to miscategorization or difficulty in establishing clear relationships between items, making the grouping process subjective and less reliable. In essence, "garbage in, garbage out" applies; inaccurate or imprecise data will lead to an inaccurate or imprecise understanding of the problem, rendering the Affinity Diagram's output unreliable.

**Answer 4:**
The aspect of measurement quality that is likely being compromised is **precision** (specifically, reproducibility). While the instrument is calibrated (accurate), the variation in readings between operators suggests inconsistency in how the instrument is used, or perhaps inherent limitations in the instrument's design that are revealed by different user techniques.

### 11. Important Points to Remember

*   **Accuracy:** Closeness to the true value. Affected by **systematic error (bias)**.
*   **Precision:** Closeness of multiple measurements to each other. Affected by **random error (variance)**.
*   **The ideal state is both accurate and precise.**
*   **You can be precise without being accurate** (consistent error).
*   **You can be accurate on average without being precise** (scattered around the true value).
*   **The quality of data is paramount** for all TQM tools, including the Affinity Diagram.
*   **Measurement System Analysis (MSA)**, including calibration and R&R studies, is crucial for ensuring accuracy and precision.
*   **Systematic errors** are often correctable (e.g., through calibration), while **random errors** are often reduced through process stabilization and control.
