# QUALITY ENGINEERING AND MANAGEMENT: Module 3 - Tools and Techniques in TQM: Affinity Diagram

## Topic: Cause and Effect Analysis (Fishbone Diagram/Ishikawa Diagram)

This module delves into the practical application of tools and techniques used in Total Quality Management (TQM). Specifically, this topic focuses on **Cause and Effect Analysis**, a powerful problem-solving tool often visualized as a **Fishbone Diagram** or **Ishikawa Diagram**. Understanding and utilizing this technique is crucial for identifying the root causes of problems and implementing effective solutions, thereby contributing to the overall quality improvement process.

---

### 1. Introduction to Cause and Effect Analysis

*   **Definition:** Cause and Effect Analysis is a systematic method used to identify, explore, and display the possible causes of a specific problem or effect. It helps in breaking down a complex problem into smaller, manageable components and understanding the relationships between different factors that contribute to an outcome.
*   **Synonyms:** Fishbone Diagram, Ishikawa Diagram, Cause-and-Cause Diagram.
*   **Purpose:**
    *   To identify the root causes of a problem, rather than just the symptoms.
    *   To organize and categorize potential causes.
    *   To encourage brainstorming and group participation in problem-solving.
    *   To facilitate a deeper understanding of the problem's complexities.
    *   To provide a visual representation of the problem's underlying causes.
*   **Relevance to TQM:** A core principle of TQM is continuous improvement, which necessitates understanding and addressing the root causes of deviations from desired quality standards. Cause and Effect Analysis is instrumental in achieving this. (Besterfield et al., 2018, Ch. 7)

---

### 2. Structure of a Cause and Effect Diagram

The diagram is structured to resemble the skeleton of a fish, with the "head" representing the effect (the problem) and the "bones" representing the major categories of potential causes.

*   **The "Head": The Effect (Problem)**
    *   This is the specific problem or outcome that the team is trying to understand and solve.
    *   It should be clearly and concisely stated.
    *   **Example:** "High Customer Complaint Rate for Product X" or "Late Delivery of Finished Goods."
*   **The "Spine": The Main Cause Line**
    *   A horizontal line pointing to the "head."
*   **The "Ribs" (Major Categories): Primary Causes**
    *   These are the main branches that extend from the spine. They represent broad categories of potential causes. The most common categories are the **"6Ms"** (in manufacturing) or **"4Ps"** and **"4Ss"** (in service industries).
*   **The "Bones" (Secondary Causes): Specific Causes**
    *   These are smaller lines branching off the major categories, representing specific, detailed causes within each category.
    *   Further sub-branches can be added to drill down to even more granular causes.

---

### 3. Common Categories for Cause and Effect Diagrams

The selection of categories depends on the nature of the problem and the industry.

#### 3.1. The 6Ms (Manufacturing Focus)

These are widely used in manufacturing environments to categorize potential causes.

*   **Man/People:** Factors related to human involvement, skills, training, motivation, or behavior.
    *   *Examples:* Lack of training, poor communication, operator fatigue, inexperience, wrong attitude.
*   **Machine/Equipment:** Issues related to machinery, tools, or technology used in the process.
    *   *Examples:* Equipment malfunction, worn-out tools, incorrect settings, lack of maintenance, outdated technology.
*   **Material:** Factors related to the raw materials, components, or supplies used.
    *   *Examples:* Substandard material quality, incorrect material specifications, incorrect material handling, supplier issues.
*   **Method/Process:** Issues related to the procedures, work instructions, or operating methods.
    *   *Examples:* Inadequate procedures, unclear work instructions, inefficient process steps, incorrect sequence of operations.
*   **Measurement/Inspection:** Factors related to measurement systems, data collection, or inspection processes.
    *   *Examples:* Inaccurate measuring instruments, incorrect calibration, subjective inspection criteria, poor data recording.
*   **Environment/Mother Nature:** Factors related to the surrounding conditions in which the work is performed.
    *   *Examples:* Temperature fluctuations, humidity, poor lighting, noise, safety hazards, external disturbances.

#### 3.2. The 4Ps (Service/Product Development Focus)

Often used in service industries or for product development.

*   **Policies:** Rules, guidelines, or strategic decisions that impact the process.
    *   *Examples:* Rigid company policies, unclear service level agreements, outdated procedures.
*   **Procedures:** Specific steps or methods followed to perform a task.
    *   *Examples:* Complex approval processes, inefficient workflows, lack of standardized procedures.
*   **People:** Similar to "Man" in the 6Ms, focusing on individuals and their roles.
    *   *Examples:* Inadequate customer service training, poor communication with clients, staff shortages.
*   **Plant/Technology:** The physical facilities, equipment, and technology used.
    *   *Examples:* Outdated IT systems, poorly designed workspace, malfunctioning equipment.

#### 3.3. The 4Ss (Service Focus)

Another set of categories commonly used in service environments.

*   **Surroundings:** The physical and social environment.
    *   *Examples:* Unpleasant waiting areas, noisy call centers, unsupportive team culture.
*   **Suppliers:** External entities providing goods or services.
    *   *Examples:* Unreliable delivery from vendors, poor quality components from suppliers.
*   **Systems:** The processes, procedures, and information flow.
    *   *Examples:* Complicated booking systems, inefficient complaint resolution processes.
*   **Skills:** The capabilities and competencies of the workforce.
    *   *Examples:* Lack of product knowledge among sales staff, insufficient technical skills for support.

**Important Note:** Teams can also define their own relevant categories if the standard ones don't adequately cover the scope of the problem. (Subburaj Ramasamy, 2017, Ch. 4)

### 4. How to Construct a Cause and Effect Diagram (Step-by-Step)

1.  **Identify and Define the Effect (Problem):** Clearly state the problem or effect that needs to be investigated. Write it in the "head" of the fishbone.
    *   *Example:* "Productivity Loss in Assembly Line B."
2.  **Identify Major Cause Categories:** Choose appropriate categories (e.g., 6Ms, 4Ps, 4Ss) and draw them as the main "ribs" branching from the spine.
    *   *Example:* For "Productivity Loss in Assembly Line B," categories might be: Machine, Man, Material, Method, Measurement.
3.  **Brainstorm Potential Causes:** Gather a team and brainstorm all possible causes for the identified effect within each category. Encourage open discussion and avoid premature judgment.
    *   **Techniques for Brainstorming:**
        *   **Round Robin:** Each person contributes one idea at a time.
        *   **Free Association:** Anyone can voice any idea that comes to mind.
        *   **Brainwriting:** Participants write down ideas silently before sharing.
4.  **Drill Down to Root Causes:** For each identified cause, ask "Why?" multiple times (often 5 times, a concept known as the "5 Whys" related to root cause analysis) to uncover the underlying reasons. Add these as secondary or tertiary branches to the diagram.
    *   *Example:*
        *   *Effect:* Productivity Loss in Assembly Line B.
        *   *Category:* Machine.
        *   *Cause 1:* Machine X is frequently breaking down.
        *   *Why?* The bearings are wearing out prematurely.
        *   *Why?* They are not being lubricated correctly.
        *   *Why?* The lubrication schedule is not being followed.
        *   *Why?* Operators are not trained on the new lubrication procedures.
        *   *Why?* Training materials are outdated. (This could be a potential root cause).
5.  **Review and Refine:** Once the diagram is complete, review it to ensure clarity, logical flow, and completeness. Identify the most probable causes to focus on for further investigation or action.
6.  **Action Planning:** Based on the identified root causes, develop an action plan to address them and improve the process.

### 5. Examples of Cause and Effect Diagrams in Practice

#### Example 1: Service Industry - "Customers Complaining About Long Wait Times at a Restaurant"

*   **Effect:** Long Wait Times for Customers.
*   **Major Categories:** People, Process, Plant/Equipment, Policies.

    *   **People:**
        *   Insufficient staff during peak hours
        *   Slow order taking by waitstaff
        *   Inexperienced kitchen staff
        *   Poor communication between front-of-house and kitchen
    *   **Process:**
        *   Inefficient table allocation system
        *   Long time to prepare certain dishes
        *   Slow payment processing
        *   Backlog in food preparation
    *   **Plant/Equipment:**
        *   Limited number of cooking stations
        *   Slow cash registers/POS system
        *   Inefficient kitchen layout
    *   **Policies:**
        *   Strict policy on table turnover time
        *   No reservation system
        *   Limited operating hours for kitchen

#### Example 2: Manufacturing Industry - "High Defect Rate in Welded Components"

*   **Effect:** High Defect Rate in Welded Components.
*   **Major Categories:** Machine, Man, Material, Method, Measurement.

    *   **Machine:**
        *   Welding machine calibration drift
        *   Worn welding tips
        *   Inconsistent power output from the machine
        *   Lack of preventative maintenance
    *   **Man:**
        *   Inexperienced welders
        *   Fatigue of welders on long shifts
        *   Improper welding technique
        *   Lack of standardized work instructions for welders
    *   **Material:**
        *   Inconsistent quality of welding rods
        *   Contamination on base material surface
        *   Incorrect filler material used
        *   Variations in base metal thickness
    *   **Method:**
        *   Incorrect welding speed
        *   Improper joint preparation
        *   Unsuitable welding parameters (voltage, amperage)
        *   Inconsistent torch angle
    *   **Measurement:**
        *   Inaccurate gauges for checking weld penetration
        *   Subjective visual inspection criteria
        *   Calibration issues with testing equipment

### 6. Benefits of Using Cause and Effect Analysis

*   **Systematic Problem Solving:** Provides a structured approach to identifying all potential causes.
*   **Teamwork and Collaboration:** Encourages active participation and diverse perspectives from the team.
*   **Holistic View:** Helps in understanding the interconnectedness of various factors influencing a problem.
*   **Root Cause Identification:** Focuses efforts on addressing fundamental issues, leading to more sustainable solutions.
*   **Improved Communication:** Visual nature of the diagram facilitates clear communication of the problem and its causes.
*   **Foundation for Other Tools:** The identified causes can then be prioritized and addressed using other TQM tools (e.g., Pareto charts, control charts). (Mitra, 1998, Ch. 5)

---

### 7. Relationship with Other Quality Tools

*   **Brainstorming:** Essential for generating the ideas that populate the diagram.
*   **5 Whys:** A crucial technique used within Cause and Effect Analysis to drill down to root causes.
*   **Pareto Chart:** Can be used *after* the Cause and Effect analysis to prioritize the most significant causes identified.
*   **Process Mapping:** Helps in understanding the steps within a process that might be contributing to the effect.
*   **Check Sheets:** Can be used to gather data on the frequency of specific causes identified in the diagram.

---

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Focus on the Effect:** Ensure the problem statement is precise and clear.
*   **Categorization is Key:** Choose categories that are relevant and comprehensive for the specific problem.
*   **Brainstorm Freely:** Encourage participation and avoid criticism during the brainstorming phase.
*   **Ask "Why?" Repeatedly:** The power of the tool lies in digging for root causes.
*   **Validate Causes:** Not all brainstormed causes are necessarily true; they need to be investigated and validated.
*   **Visual Clarity:** A well-structured and legible diagram is crucial for effective communication.
*   **Team Effort:** It is most effective when done by a cross-functional team familiar with the process. (Juran & Gryna, 2004, Ch. 11)

### 10. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:** Define Cause and Effect Analysis and list its common synonyms.
*   **Answer:** Cause and Effect Analysis is a systematic method to identify and explore the potential causes of a specific problem or effect. Its common synonyms include Fishbone Diagram, Ishikawa Diagram, and Cause-and-Cause Diagram.

**Question 2:** Imagine you are a quality engineer at a software company experiencing a high number of customer support tickets related to software bugs.
    a) State the "Effect" for your Cause and Effect Diagram.
    b) List four major categories you might use for this problem, considering it's a service/software context.
    c) For one of the categories, brainstorm three potential causes and then for one of those causes, ask "Why?" twice to find a more specific reason.
*   **Answer:**
    *   a) **Effect:** High Number of Customer Support Tickets Due to Software Bugs.
    *   b) Possible Categories: People (Developers, Testers, Support Staff), Process (Development, Testing, Release, Support), Product (Software Design, Code Quality), Technology (Development Tools, Testing Tools, Infrastructure).
    *   c) Let's take the category **"Process"**:
        *   **Cause 1:** Insufficient code review.
        *   **Why?** Developers are under pressure to meet deadlines.
        *   **Why?** Project timelines are too aggressive.
        *(This leads to the more specific cause: Aggressive Project Timelines)*

**Question 3:** Which of the following is NOT a common category in a Cause and Effect Diagram?
    a) Machine
    b) Market
    c) Method
    d) Man
*   **Answer:** b) Market. The common categories are typically related to the process itself (Man, Machine, Material, Method, Measurement, Environment, or variations like 4Ps/4Ss).

**Question 4:** What is the primary benefit of using the "5 Whys" technique in conjunction with a Cause and Effect Diagram?
*   **Answer:** The primary benefit of using the "5 Whys" technique is to drill down from superficial symptoms to the underlying root causes of a problem, ensuring that solutions address the fundamental issues rather than just the immediate manifestations.

**Question 5 (Applied Scenario):** A hospital is experiencing increased patient wait times in the emergency department. Using the 4Ss framework, create a partial Cause and Effect Diagram showing at least two potential causes under "Systems" and one under "Suppliers" that could contribute to this problem.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Effect:** Increased Patient Wait Times in Emergency Department
    *   **Categories:** Surroundings, Suppliers, Systems, Skills
    *   **Systems:**
        *   Inefficient patient registration process
        *   Poor patient flow management within the department
    *   **Suppliers:**
        *   Delays in receiving lab results from external diagnostic providers
