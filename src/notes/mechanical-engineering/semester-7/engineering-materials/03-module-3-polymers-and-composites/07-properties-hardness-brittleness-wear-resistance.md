# Engineering Materials: Module 3: Polymers and Composites - Properties: Hardness, Brittleness, Wear Resistance

This module focuses on the mechanical properties of polymers and composites, specifically hardness, brittleness, and wear resistance. Understanding these properties is crucial for selecting appropriate materials for various engineering applications.

## 1. Hardness

**1.1. Definition:**
Hardness is a material's resistance to localized plastic deformation, such as scratching, indentation, or abrasion. It is a surface property and is often related to other mechanical properties like tensile strength and elastic modulus.

**1.2. Significance:**
*   **Wear Resistance:** Harder materials generally exhibit better resistance to wear.
*   **Machinability:** While harder materials are more resistant to wear, they can be more difficult to machine.
*   **Surface Integrity:** Hardness influences how a material surface can withstand contact forces without deformation.

**1.3. Measurement of Hardness:**
Various standardized tests are used to measure hardness:

*   **Rockwell Hardness Test:** (Budinski & Budinski, 9th ed., Ch. 5; Callister & Rethwisch, 10th ed., Ch. 6)
    *   **Principle:** Measures the depth of penetration of an indenter (ball or cone) under a specific load. A minor load is applied first, followed by a major load. The hardness value is inversely proportional to the depth of penetration.
    *   **Scales:** Different Rockwell scales (e.g., Rockwell B, Rockwell C) use different indenters and loads, making them suitable for a wide range of materials, from soft polymers to hard steels.
    *   **Advantages:** Fast, reliable, and easy to perform.
    *   **Polymers:** Rockwell hardness is commonly used for polymers. Different scales (e.g., HRR, HRH) are employed depending on the polymer's expected hardness. Softer polymers may be tested using the HRR scale with a 1/8 inch ball indenter and a specific load.
*   **Brinell Hardness Test:** (Callister & Rethwisch, 10th ed., Ch. 6; Askeland, Ch. 8)
    *   **Principle:** A hardened steel or carbide ball is pressed into the material surface under a specific load for a set time. The diameter of the resulting indentation is measured.
    *   **Formula:** BHN = (2F) / (πD(D - √(D² - d²))), where F is the applied load, D is the diameter of the ball, and d is the diameter of the indentation.
    *   **Use:** Primarily used for softer materials, including some softer polymers and composites, as the large indentation provides an average hardness value.
*   **Shore Hardness Test (Durometer):** (Budinski & Budinski, 9th ed., Ch. 5; Shackelford, 9th ed., Ch. 6)
    *   **Principle:** Measures the indentation resistance of a spring-loaded indenter. This test is specifically designed for soft materials like polymers and rubbers.
    *   **Scales:** Common scales are Shore A and Shore D.
        *   **Shore A:** Used for softer elastomers and flexible plastics (e.g., rubber shoe soles, flexible PVC). Features a blunt conical indenter.
        *   **Shore D:** Used for harder plastics and semi-rigid materials (e.g., hard plastics, rigid PVC). Features a sharper conical indenter.
    *   **Advantages:** Portable and useful for in-situ testing.
    *   **Polymers:** Shore hardness is the most common method for measuring the hardness of polymers.
*   **Vickers Hardness Test:** (Callister & Rethwisch, 10th ed., Ch. 6; Raghavan, 6th ed., Ch. 5)
    *   **Principle:** Uses a diamond pyramid indenter. The hardness number is based on the diagonal length of the indentation.
    *   **Use:** Can be used for a wide range of materials, including harder polymers and composites, especially when microscopic hardness is important.

**1.4. Hardness in Polymers:**
*   **Factors Affecting Hardness:**
    *   **Molecular Weight:** Higher molecular weight generally leads to increased hardness.
    *   **Crosslinking:** Crosslinked polymers are generally harder than their linear counterparts due to restricted chain mobility.
    *   **Filler Content:** Incorporation of fillers (e.g., carbon black, glass fibers) significantly increases the hardness of polymers.
    *   **Crystallinity:** Crystalline regions in polymers are harder than amorphous regions.
    *   **Temperature:** Hardness decreases with increasing temperature as polymer chains gain mobility.
    *   **Type of Polymer:** Thermoplastics generally have lower hardness than thermosets.

**1.5. Hardness in Composites:**
*   **Reinforcement:** The type, volume fraction, and orientation of reinforcing fibers (e.g., glass fibers, carbon fibers) greatly influence the composite's hardness.
    *   **Fiber-Reinforced Polymers (FRPs):** Composites with hard, stiff fibers embedded in a polymer matrix tend to be harder than the matrix material alone.
*   **Matrix Properties:** The inherent hardness of the polymer matrix also plays a significant role.

**1.6. Important Points to Remember:**
*   Hardness is a surface property.
*   Different tests are suitable for different material types and hardness ranges.
*   For polymers, Shore hardness (A and D) is most commonly used.
*   Hardness in polymers is influenced by molecular structure, crosslinking, fillers, and temperature.
*   Composites' hardness is a combination of matrix and reinforcement properties.

## 2. Brittleness

**2.1. Definition:**
Brittleness is a material's tendency to fracture with little or no plastic deformation when subjected to stress. Brittle materials absorb very little energy before fracturing.

**2.2. Significance:**
*   **Failure Mode:** Brittle materials fail suddenly and catastrophically, which can be undesirable in many engineering applications where warning signs of failure are preferred.
*   **Impact Resistance:** Brittle materials generally have poor impact resistance.
*   **Machining:** Brittle materials can be prone to chipping or cracking during machining.

**2.3. Brittleness in Polymers:**
*   **Mechanism:** Brittleness in polymers often arises from strong intermolecular forces, high crosslink density, or the presence of rigid segments in the polymer chain, which restrict chain movement and deformation.
*   **Factors Affecting Brittleness:**
    *   **Crosslinking:** High degrees of crosslinking (e.g., in thermosets like brittle epoxies or vulcanized rubber) can lead to increased brittleness.
    *   **Glass Transition Temperature (Tg):** Polymers tend to be brittle below their Tg and more ductile above it. Amorphous polymers will be brittle at room temperature if their Tg is above room temperature.
    *   **Crystallinity:** Highly crystalline polymers can exhibit brittle behavior due to the rigidity of the crystalline structure.
    *   **Presence of Defects:** Voids, cracks, or inclusions can act as stress concentrators, initiating fracture and promoting brittle failure.
    *   **Temperature:** As temperature decreases, polymers generally become more brittle.
    *   **Plasticizers:** The absence or low concentration of plasticizers (which increase chain mobility) can lead to brittleness.
    *   **Molecular Structure:** Rigid polymer backbones or bulky side groups can hinder chain rotation and contribute to brittleness.

*   **Examples:**
    *   **Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) / Acrylic:** Known for its transparency and rigidity, but it is also quite brittle, especially at room temperature. (Shackelford, 9th ed., Ch. 12)
    *   **Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) (rigid):** Unplasticized PVC is a rigid material that can be brittle.
    *   **Epoxy resins (unfilled, highly crosslinked):** Often used as matrices in composites, they can be brittle if not modified.

**2.4. Brittleness in Composites:**
*   **Matrix Domination:** In many polymer matrix composites, the brittleness of the polymer matrix can dominate the overall behavior, especially if the fibers are not optimally arranged or if there are interfacial issues.
*   **Fiber Properties:** While fibers are typically strong and stiff, their own brittleness (e.g., ceramics like glass or carbon fibers) can also contribute to the composite's failure mode. If the fibers fail in a brittle manner, it can lead to rapid crack propagation.
*   **Interphase:** The region between the fiber and matrix, known as the interphase, plays a critical role. A weak interphase can lead to debonding, while a brittle interphase can itself be a source of failure.
*   **Failure Mechanisms:**
    *   **Fiber Fracture:** Brittle failure of the reinforcing fibers.
    *   **Matrix Cracking:** Brittle cracking of the polymer matrix.
    *   **Debonding:** Separation of fibers from the matrix.
    *   **Interlaminar Failure:** Cracking between layers in laminated composites.

*   **Examples:**
    *   **Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP):** While CFRPs are known for their high strength and stiffness, they can exhibit brittle failure if the matrix is brittle or if loading is not aligned with the fibers. (Raghavan, 6th ed., Ch. 14)
    *   **Glass Fiber Reinforced Polymer (GFRP):** Similar to CFRPs, GFRPs can also be brittle depending on the matrix and fiber loading.

**2.5. Quantifying Brittleness:**
Brittleness is often inferred from:
*   **Low Elongation at Break:** A low percentage of elongation before fracture indicates brittle behavior.
*   **Low Impact Strength:** Materials that fracture easily under impact loading are considered brittle.
*   **Fracture Toughness (KIC):** A lower fracture toughness value generally correlates with higher brittleness.

**2.6. Important Points to Remember:**
*   Brittleness is the absence of significant plastic deformation before fracture.
*   In polymers, brittleness is linked to factors like crosslinking, temperature (below Tg), and molecular rigidity.
*   In composites, the matrix and fiber properties, as well as the interphase, contribute to overall brittleness.
*   Brittle materials fail suddenly, offering little warning.

## 3. Wear Resistance

**3.1. Definition:**
Wear resistance is a material's ability to withstand surface damage or material loss due to mechanical action, such as rubbing, scraping, or erosion. It is a complex property influenced by several factors.

**3.2. Significance:**
*   **Durability:** High wear resistance is crucial for components subjected to friction and contact, extending their service life.
*   **Performance:** Wear can alter the dimensions and surface finish of parts, leading to loss of performance and efficiency.
*   **Maintenance:** Components with poor wear resistance may require frequent replacement or maintenance.

**3.3. Types of Wear:**
*   **Abrasive Wear:** Occurs when hard particles or asperities slide against a surface, causing cutting or plowing. (Budinski & Budinski, 9th ed., Ch. 17; Callister & Rethwisch, 10th ed., Ch. 21)
    *   **Example:** Sandpaper rubbing against a polymer surface.
*   **Adhesive Wear:** Occurs when two surfaces in relative motion "weld" together at microscopic contact points (junctions), and these junctions break upon further motion, transferring material. (Askeland, Ch. 16)
    *   **Example:** Two unlubricated metal surfaces sliding against each other.
*   **Erosive Wear:** Caused by the impact of solid particles or impinging fluid jets on a surface. (Raghavan, 6th ed., Ch. 18)
    *   **Example:** Slurry flowing through pipes, sandblasting.
*   **Fatigue Wear (Surface Fatigue):** Results from repeated cyclic stresses at the surface, leading to the formation and propagation of subsurface cracks and eventual material removal.
    *   **Example:** Rolling contact in bearings.

**3.4. Wear Resistance in Polymers:**
Polymers generally have lower wear resistance compared to metals and ceramics due to their lower hardness and mechanical strength. However, their properties can be significantly modified.

*   **Factors Affecting Wear Resistance:**
    *   **Hardness:** Higher hardness generally leads to better wear resistance, especially against abrasion.
    *   **Tensile Strength and Modulus:** Stronger and stiffer polymers tend to resist deformation and wear better.
    *   **Friction Coefficient:** Polymers with low coefficients of friction reduce the energy dissipation and heat generation during sliding, thereby improving wear resistance.
    *   **Molecular Weight and Chain Mobility:** Higher molecular weight and reduced chain mobility (e.g., through crosslinking) can improve wear resistance.
    *   **Crystallinity:** Crystalline polymers generally exhibit better wear resistance than amorphous ones.
    *   **Glass Transition Temperature (Tg):** Polymers are generally more susceptible to wear at temperatures above their Tg.
    *   **Presence of Fillers and Reinforcements:**
        *   **Hard Fillers:** Adding hard particles like silica, alumina, or carbon black can significantly improve abrasive wear resistance. (Budinski & Budinski, 9th ed., Ch. 17)
        *   **Reinforcing Fibers:** Incorporating fibers like glass or carbon fibers can improve the mechanical strength and wear resistance of the polymer matrix.
        *   **Lubricating Fillers:** Fillers like PTFE (Teflon), graphite, or molybdenum disulfide can lower the coefficient of friction and improve wear resistance by providing self-lubrication. (Shackelford, 9th ed., Ch. 14)
    *   **Surface Treatment:** Techniques like plasma treatment or coating can improve the surface hardness and wear resistance of polymers.

*   **Examples:**
    *   **Ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE):** Exhibits excellent wear resistance, especially in sliding applications like joint replacements and conveyor liners, due to its high molecular weight and crystalline structure. (Callister & Rethwisch, 10th ed., Ch. 21)
    *   **Nylon (Polyamide):** Good wear resistance, especially when filled with lubricants or glass fibers. Used in gears and bearings.
    *   **Acetal (POM):** Low friction and good wear resistance, used in self-lubricating bearings and gears.
    *   **PTFE (Teflon):** Extremely low coefficient of friction and good chemical resistance, making it suitable for non-stick coatings and seals, but its mechanical strength can limit its wear resistance in abrasive environments.

**3.5. Wear Resistance in Composites:**
Composites can offer excellent wear resistance, often superior to their constituent polymers.

*   **Factors Influencing Wear Resistance:**
    *   **Matrix Properties:** The wear resistance of the polymer matrix is a primary factor.
    *   **Reinforcement Type and Volume Fraction:**
        *   **Hard, Abrasive-Resistant Fibers:** Fibers like ceramic (e.g., silicon carbide) or hard carbon fibers can significantly enhance abrasive wear resistance.
        *   **Soft, Lubricating Fibers:** Fibers like PTFE can improve tribological properties.
    *   **Fiber Orientation:** Fiber orientation can affect the wear mechanisms. Fibers oriented parallel to the wear surface might offer less protection than those oriented perpendicular to it.
    *   **Interphase Properties:** A strong, well-bonded interphase is crucial for efficient load transfer and to prevent premature delamination or fiber pull-out.
    *   **Filler/Matrix Adhesion:** Good adhesion between fillers and the matrix is essential for effective wear performance.

*   **Examples:**
    *   **Friction Materials:** Composites used in brake pads and clutches often incorporate various fillers and reinforcements (e.g., ceramic fibers, metal particles, graphite) to achieve the desired balance of wear resistance, friction coefficient, and thermal stability.
    *   **Self-Lubricating Composites:** Composites containing solid lubricants (e.g., graphite, PTFE) within a polymer matrix provide excellent wear resistance in demanding bearing applications.
    *   **Abrasion-Resistant Linings:** Composites with hard ceramic particles or fibers are used as wear-resistant linings in chutes, hoppers, and pipes handling abrasive materials.

**3.6. Important Points to Remember:**
*   Wear resistance is a material's ability to withstand surface damage from friction.
*   Factors like hardness, strength, friction coefficient, fillers, and reinforcements significantly influence wear resistance.
*   Polymers can be modified with fillers and fibers to enhance their wear properties.
*   Composites often offer superior wear resistance compared to monolithic polymers.
*   Understanding the specific wear mechanisms relevant to an application is crucial for material selection.

## 4. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
Which of the following tests is most commonly used to measure the hardness of a soft polymer like rubber?
a) Brinell Hardness Test
b) Rockwell Hardness Test
c) Shore Hardness Test
d) Vickers Hardness Test

**Question 2:**
A polymer that fractures with very little deformation upon impact is considered:
a) Ductile
b) Tough
c) Brittle
d) Malleable

**Question 3:**
Which of the following factors will generally *decrease* the wear resistance of a polymer?
a) Increase in hardness
b) Addition of PTFE fillers
c) Operation at temperatures above its Glass Transition Temperature (Tg)
d) Presence of a reinforcing glass fiber network

**Question 4:**
Explain why adding hard fillers like silica to a polymer matrix typically improves its resistance to abrasive wear. (Relates to CO3, CO4)

**Question 5:**
Discuss the trade-off between hardness and machinability in engineering materials, particularly in the context of polymers. (Relates to CO4)

---

## 5. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
c) Shore Hardness Test

**Answer 2:**
c) Brittle

**Answer 3:**
c) Operation at temperatures above its Glass Transition Temperature (Tg)

**Answer 4:**
Hard fillers like silica are significantly harder than the polymer matrix. When subjected to abrasive wear, these hard particles are exposed at the surface. They resist being plowed or cut by the abrasive particles, effectively preventing the softer polymer matrix from being easily removed. The hard fillers act as sacrificial elements, bearing the brunt of the abrasive action and thus protecting the bulk of the polymer.

**Answer 5:**
There is often an inverse relationship between hardness and machinability. Generally, as a material becomes harder, it becomes more difficult to cut or shape using machining processes like turning, milling, or drilling. This is because harder materials require more force to deform and remove material, leading to increased tool wear and potential for tool breakage. For polymers, extremely hard polymers might require specialized machining techniques or generate significant heat during machining, which can lead to melting or degradation. Therefore, a balance must be struck when selecting a polymer for an application where both wear resistance (often correlated with hardness) and ease of manufacturing are important.

## 8. Important Points to Remember (Summary)

*   **Hardness:** Resistance to surface indentation/scratching. Shore hardness is key for polymers. Influenced by molecular weight, crosslinking, and fillers.
*   **Brittleness:** Fracture with little plastic deformation. Polymers become brittle below Tg. Composites' brittleness depends on matrix and fiber properties.
*   **Wear Resistance:** Ability to withstand surface damage from friction. Influenced by hardness, strength, friction coefficient, and fillers/reinforcements. UHMWPE and composites are often excellent in wear applications.
*   **Material Selection:** Understanding these properties is vital for choosing polymers and composites for applications where durability, surface integrity, and failure modes are critical.
