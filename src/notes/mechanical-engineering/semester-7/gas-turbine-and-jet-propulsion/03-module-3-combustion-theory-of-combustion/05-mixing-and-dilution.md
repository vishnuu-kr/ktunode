# GAS TURBINE AND JET PROPULSION: Module 3: Combustion – Theory of Combustion

## Topic: Mixing and Dilution

This module delves into the critical processes of mixing and dilution within the combustion chamber of gas turbines. These processes are fundamental to achieving efficient, stable, and low-emission combustion, directly impacting overall engine performance and longevity.

---

*   **Understand the fundamental principles of mixing fuel and air in a combustion chamber.**
*   **Explain the concept and purpose of dilution air in gas turbine combustion.**
*   **Analyze the effects of mixing and dilution on combustion temperature, flame stability, and emissions.**
*   **Identify different methods and techniques used for achieving effective mixing and dilution.**
*   **Relate mixing and dilution strategies to the design and performance of gas turbine combustion chambers.**

### 1. Introduction to Mixing and Dilution

The combustion chamber is where the chemical energy of the fuel is converted into thermal energy. For this to happen efficiently and safely, the fuel must be thoroughly mixed with the combustion air, and the resulting high-temperature gases must be diluted to meet the temperature limits of downstream turbine components.

*   **Primary Purpose:**
    *   **Mixing:** To bring fuel and air into intimate contact for complete and efficient combustion.
    *   **Dilution:** To reduce the peak combustion temperature to protect the turbine blades from excessive thermal stress and creep.

*   **Relationship to Combustion:**
    *   **Incomplete Mixing:** Leads to localized fuel-rich or fuel-lean zones, resulting in incomplete combustion, lower efficiency, and increased emissions (e.g., CO, unburnt hydrocarbons).
    *   **Insufficient Dilution:** Results in excessively high gas temperatures entering the turbine, potentially causing material failure and reduced engine life.

*   **Textbook References:**
    *   **Ganesan (2017):** Chapter on Combustion Chambers, discussing fuel injection, atomization, and air swirl for mixing.
    *   **Yahya (2011):** Chapter on Gas Turbine Combustion, covering the role of primary, secondary, and dilution air.
    *   **Mathur & Mathur (2010):** Section on combustion chamber design, highlighting mixing and dilution techniques.
    *   **Rolls Royce (2015):** Discusses the importance of controlled combustion and temperature profiles.
    *   **Cohen (2019):** Details the stages of combustion and the need for temperature control.

### 2. The Role of Air in Combustion Chambers

The air supplied to the combustion chamber is typically divided into three categories, each with specific functions related to mixing and dilution.

#### 2.1 Primary Air (for Combustion)

*   **Function:** The portion of air that directly participates in the combustion process, mixed with the fuel. It's responsible for initiating and sustaining the flame.
*   **Mixing Requirements:** Needs to be thoroughly mixed with the fuel for complete combustion.
*   **Stoichiometry:** Ideally, this air is close to stoichiometric or slightly lean to ensure complete burning.
*   **Percentage:** Typically 15-30% of the total air.
*   **Ganesan (2017):** Emphasizes the need for rapid and uniform mixing of primary air and fuel.

#### 2.2 Secondary Air (for Dilution and Burning)

*   **Function:** Introduced downstream of the primary zone to:
    *   **Complete Combustion:** Ensure any unburnt fuel or intermediates from the primary zone are fully combusted.
    *   **Dilution:** Reduce the high temperatures generated in the primary zone.
    *   **Stabilization:** Help stabilize the flame.
*   **Mixing Requirements:** Mixes with the primary combustion products.
*   **Yahya (2011):** Describes secondary air as a crucial component for controlling the axial temperature profile.
*   **Percentage:** Typically 20-30% of the total air.

#### 2.3 Dilution Air (for Temperature Reduction)

*   **Function:** Introduced furthest downstream to further reduce the overall gas temperature to levels acceptable for the turbine inlet. This is critical for turbine blade life.
*   **Mixing Requirements:** Needs to mix effectively to achieve a uniform, lower temperature.
*   **Effect on Combustion:** Does not directly participate in combustion but dilutes the combustion products.
*   **Mathur & Mathur (2010):** Explains how dilution air is strategically introduced to create a specific radial temperature profile.
*   **Percentage:** Typically 40-60% of the total air.

### 3. Mixing and its Impact on Combustion

Effective mixing is paramount for efficient and clean combustion. Poor mixing leads to a host of undesirable outcomes.

#### 3.1 Fuel Atomization and Vaporization

*   **Importance:** For liquid fuels, atomization (breaking the fuel into fine droplets) and vaporization (converting liquid to vapor) are crucial preliminary steps for effective mixing with air.
*   **Methods:**
    *   **Pressure Atomizers:** Fuel is forced through a small orifice at high pressure.
    *   **Airblast Atomizers:** A high-velocity air stream is used to shear the fuel.
    *   **Swirl Atomizers:** Incorporate swirl to improve atomization.
*   **Ganesan (2017):** Discusses various atomizer designs and their impact on droplet size distribution.
*   **Yahya (2011):** Mentions the critical role of droplet size in achieving rapid vaporization and combustion.

#### 3.2 Air Swirl and Turbulence

*   **Air Swirl:** Introducing a swirling motion to the incoming air (primary and secondary) promotes better mixing with the fuel.
    *   **Mechanism:** Swirl creates centrifugal forces that help distribute fuel and air radially and axially, enhancing turbulence.
    *   **Flame Stabilization:** The central recirculation zone created by swirl helps re-ignite partially burnt gases, stabilizing the flame.
*   **Turbulence:** Natural turbulence within the airflow and induced turbulence from swirl accelerates the mixing process.
    *   **Impact:** Higher turbulence leads to faster molecular diffusion and reaction rates.
*   **Cohen (2019):** Explains how swirl generators (stators) are used in combustion chambers to enhance mixing and flame stability.
*   **Rolls Royce (2015):** Highlights how advanced aerodynamic designs promote controlled mixing and turbulence.

#### 3.3 Effects of Poor Mixing

*   **Incomplete Combustion:**
    *   **CO Formation:** Local fuel-rich zones where oxygen is scarce.
    *   **Unburnt Hydrocarbons (UHC):** Fuel pockets that don't come into contact with sufficient oxygen.
*   **Reduced Combustion Efficiency:** Less of the fuel's chemical energy is converted to heat.
*   **Increased Emissions:** Higher levels of CO, UHC, and soot.
*   **Combustion Instability:** Fluctuations in flame holding due to uneven fuel-air distribution.
*   **CO2 Formation:** Local fuel-lean zones where excess oxygen is present, but combustion may not be complete due to poor mixing.

### 4. Dilution and its Impact on Temperature Control

Dilution air is essential for managing the extremely high temperatures generated by combustion to protect the turbine.

#### 4.1 Purpose of Dilution

*   **Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT) Control:** The primary goal is to reduce the peak combustion temperature to a level that the turbine blades can withstand without premature failure.
*   **Temperature Profile:** Dilution air is injected to create a desired temperature profile at the turbine inlet, typically a "banana" profile (lower temperature at the casing, higher in the center) or a "flat" profile, depending on design considerations.
*   **Material Limitations:** Turbine blades are made of high-temperature alloys, but there are still limits to the temperatures they can endure.

#### 4.2 Dilution Techniques

*   **Air Admission Holes/Sleeves:** Dilution air is introduced through carefully positioned holes or slots along the combustion chamber liner.
*   **Airflow Patterns:** The placement and size of these holes are designed to create specific mixing patterns.
    *   **Axial Injection:** Air injected parallel to the main flow.
    *   **Radial Injection:** Air injected towards the center.
*   **Ganesan (2017):** Discusses the design of dilution holes and their impact on the axial temperature distribution.
*   **Yahya (2011):** Explains how the dilution zone is engineered to ensure uniform temperature reduction.

#### 4.3 Effects of Dilution

*   **Reduced Peak Temperature:** Directly addresses the problem of overheating turbine components.
*   **Lower Flame Temperature:** While necessary, a very low flame temperature can lead to incomplete combustion. A balance is required.
*   **Reduced Combustion Efficiency (if excessive):** If too much air is used for dilution, the overall oxygen-air mixture can become too lean for efficient combustion, leading to higher CO and UHC emissions.
*   **Impact on Specific Impulse (Jet Engines):** Lowering the turbine inlet temperature reduces the exhaust gas temperature and velocity, which can affect thrust.

### 5. Mixing and Dilution in Different Combustion Chamber Types

The specific strategies for mixing and dilution vary depending on the type of combustion chamber.

#### 5.1 Can Type Combustors

*   **Description:** Independent cylindrical combustion chambers, each with its own fuel injector and liner.
*   **Mixing:** Fuel is injected into a swirling primary air stream within each can.
*   **Dilution:** Dilution air is introduced through holes in the liner, downstream of the primary zone, and often through a transition duct connecting the can to the turbine.
*   **Ganesan (2017):** Provides examples of can-type combustor designs.

#### 5.2 Annular Type Combustors

*   **Description:** A single annular combustion chamber formed between the compressor exit casing and a central duct.
*   **Mixing:** Fuel is injected from multiple injectors along the annulus, and air is swirled and introduced through the liner.
*   **Dilution:** Dilution air is introduced through many holes around the circumference of the liner, allowing for more uniform mixing and temperature control.
*   **Yahya (2011):** Discusses the advantages of annular combustors for better temperature uniformity.
*   **Mathur & Mathur (2010):** Highlights the space-saving and efficiency benefits of annular designs.

#### 5.3 Tubular-Annular (Can-Annular) Type Combustors

*   **Description:** A compromise between can and annular types, featuring multiple can-type combustors arranged within an outer casing.
*   **Mixing:** Similar to can-type combustors within each can.
*   **Dilution:** Dilution air is introduced through the liners of the individual cans and into the transition ducts.

### 6. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Stoichiometric Air-Fuel Ratio (SAR):** The ideal air-fuel ratio where all fuel and oxygen are consumed in the combustion process.
*   **Equivalence Ratio ($\phi$):** The ratio of the actual air-fuel ratio to the stoichiometric air-fuel ratio.
    *   $\phi < 1$: Lean mixture (excess air)
    *   $\phi = 1$: Stoichiometric mixture
    *   $\phi > 1$: Rich mixture (excess fuel)
*   **Flame Stabilization:** The process of maintaining a continuous and stable flame within the combustion chamber, often achieved by creating a recirculation zone.
*   **Temperature Profile:** The distribution of gas temperature at the turbine inlet, typically across the diameter of the combustor exit.
*   **Coking:** The deposition of carbon particles (soot) due to incomplete combustion, often caused by poor mixing.
*   **Soot:** Carbon particles formed in rich fuel-air mixtures or during incomplete combustion.

### 7. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**

Explain the distinct roles of primary, secondary, and dilution air in a gas turbine combustion chamber. How does the proportion of each air stream affect combustion efficiency and turbine inlet temperature?

**Answer:**

*   **Primary Air:** Mixed with fuel for initial combustion. Controls flame stability and initial burning. A sufficient amount ensures complete combustion in the primary zone. Too little leads to incomplete combustion and soot.
*   **Secondary Air:** Aids complete combustion of intermediates and begins the dilution process. Helps control the axial temperature profile.
*   **Dilution Air:** Primarily responsible for lowering the overall gas temperature to protect the turbine. Excessive dilution can lead to lower combustion efficiency and increased CO/UHC emissions.

The proportions are crucial:

*   Increasing primary air beyond stoichiometric levels (leaner primary zone) can reduce peak temperatures but may lead to incomplete combustion if mixing is poor.
*   Increasing dilution air reduces turbine inlet temperature but can decrease combustion efficiency if the overall mixture becomes too lean for sustained reaction.

**Question 2:**

Discuss the importance of air swirl in gas turbine combustion. What are the primary benefits of inducing swirl, and how is it achieved in practice?

**Answer:**

Air swirl is crucial for:

1.  **Enhanced Mixing:** Creates centrifugal forces that distribute fuel and air radially and axially, promoting rapid and uniform mixing.
2.  **Flame Stabilization:** Generates a central recirculation zone that traps and re-ignites partially burnt gases, ensuring a stable flame that doesn't lift off the burner.
3.  **Improved Atomization/Vaporization:** In airblast atomizers, swirl contributes to the shearing action of fuel droplets.

Achieved through:

*   **Swirler Vanes:** Stator vanes are designed to impart a swirling motion to the incoming air.
*   **Combustor Liner Geometry:** Specific profiling of the liner can also influence airflow patterns.

**Question 3:**

A gas turbine experiences high levels of soot formation. What are the likely causes related to mixing and dilution, and what modifications could be considered to mitigate this issue?

**Answer:**

High soot formation typically indicates incomplete combustion, often due to:

*   **Poor Fuel-Air Mixing:**
    *   Ineffective atomization of liquid fuel.
    *   Insufficient primary air or poor distribution of primary air to fuel.
    *   Low turbulence in the primary zone.
*   **Improper Dilution:**
    *   If dilution air is introduced too early or too aggressively, it can quench the combustion process before it's complete.
    *   However, soot is primarily a result of *rich* zones where oxygen is deficient, so poor mixing in the *primary* zone is the more likely culprit for soot formation.

**Mitigation Strategies:**

*   **Improve Atomization:** Enhance fuel injector design or operating pressure.
*   **Optimize Primary Air Flow:** Ensure adequate primary air and improve its distribution.
*   **Increase Swirl/Turbulence:** Redesign swirlers or use vortex breakdown techniques to improve mixing in the primary zone.
*   **Adjust Air Admission:** Ensure dilution air is introduced downstream and doesn't prematurely quench combustion. Re-evaluate the timing and distribution of secondary and dilution air.

**(CO1: K3, CO3: K4)**

**Question 4:**

Compare and contrast the mixing and dilution strategies employed in can-type and annular combustion chambers.

**Answer:**

*   **Can-Type:**
    *   **Mixing:** Concentrated within individual cans. Fuel injected into a swirling primary air stream in each can.
    *   **Dilution:** Introduced through discrete holes in the liner of each can and transition duct. Can lead to less uniform temperature profiles across the annulus.
*   **Annular:**
    *   **Mixing:** Spread across a larger annular area with multiple fuel injectors. Air swirl and entry through a continuous liner.
    *   **Dilution:** Introduced through a large number of smaller holes distributed around the entire annulus. This allows for more precise control and generally leads to more uniform turbine inlet temperature profiles.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Mixing is Key for Efficiency and Emissions:** Thorough mixing of fuel and air ensures complete combustion, minimizing CO, UHC, and soot.
*   **Dilution is Key for Durability:** Dilution air reduces the turbine inlet temperature to prevent thermal damage to turbine blades.
*   **Trade-offs Exist:** Over-dilution can reduce combustion efficiency and increase emissions. Insufficient mixing leads to poor combustion and high emissions.
*   **Combustor Design is a Balancing Act:** Engineers must carefully balance the needs for efficient mixing, flame stability, complete combustion, and controlled dilution to achieve optimal performance and longevity.
*   **Airflow Management:** The precise distribution of primary, secondary, and dilution air through strategically placed holes and diffusers is critical to achieving the desired outcome.
*   **Atomization and Vaporization:** These are critical pre-mixing steps for liquid fuels.
