# Module 4: Jet Propulsion – Cycles and Analysis – Gas Turbine Engines

## Topic: Propulsive Efficiency

This module delves into the fundamental principles of jet propulsion, focusing on the analysis of gas turbine engines and their efficiency in generating thrust. We will specifically examine the concept of propulsive efficiency and its critical role in determining the overall performance of jet engines.

---

### 1. Introduction to Jet Propulsion and Thrust Generation

*   **Definition:** Jet propulsion is a method of propulsion where a high-speed jet of fluid (typically air or exhaust gases) is expelled from the rear of a vehicle to generate a forward thrust. This thrust propels the vehicle forward according to Newton's third law of motion (for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction).
*   **How Thrust is Generated:** Gas turbine engines operate on a thermodynamic cycle (Brayton cycle). They ingest air, compress it, mix it with fuel and combust it, and then expand the hot gases through a turbine and nozzle. The expulsion of these high-velocity gases creates a reactive force, i.e., thrust.
*   **Key Components of a Jet Engine:**
    *   **Inlet:** Captures and directs air into the compressor.
    *   **Compressor:** Increases the pressure of the incoming air. (Refer to Yahya, Chapter 3 for detailed compressor analysis).
    *   **Combustor:** Mixes fuel with compressed air and ignites it, increasing the temperature and volume of the gases.
    *   **Turbine:** Extracts energy from the hot gases to drive the compressor and accessories. (Refer to Yahya, Chapter 4 for detailed turbine analysis).
    *   **Nozzle:** Accelerates the exhaust gases to a high velocity, generating thrust.
*   **Basic Thrust Equation:** The net thrust ($F_{net}$) generated by a jet engine is given by:
    $F_{net} = (\dot{m}_e v_e - \dot{m}_a v_a) + (p_e - p_a) A_e$
    Where:
    *   $\dot{m}_e$ = mass flow rate of exhaust gases
    *   $v_e$ = velocity of exhaust gases
    *   $\dot{m}_a$ = mass flow rate of air ingested
    *   $v_a$ = velocity of ambient air (vehicle speed)
    *   $p_e$ = pressure of exhaust gases at the nozzle exit
    *   $p_a$ = ambient pressure
    *   $A_e$ = area of the nozzle exit

    For a simple turbojet engine, the mass flow rates are approximately equal ($\dot{m}_e \approx \dot{m}_a = \dot{m}$), and if the exit pressure is equal to ambient pressure ($p_e = p_a$), the thrust equation simplifies to:
    $F_{net} \approx \dot{m} (v_e - v_a)$

### 2. Defining Propulsive Efficiency

*   **Concept:** Propulsive efficiency measures how effectively the kinetic energy of the jet is converted into useful propulsive power for the vehicle. It compares the useful work done by the jet to the kinetic energy imparted to the jet.
*   **Propulsive Power ($P_p$):** The useful power delivered by the jet to propel the vehicle.
    $P_p = F_{net} \times v_a$
    $P_p = \dot{m} (v_e - v_a) \times v_a$ (assuming $p_e = p_a$ and $\dot{m}_e = \dot{m}_a$)
*   **Kinetic Energy of the Jet:** The total kinetic energy per unit time of the exhaust jet.
    $KE_j = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m}_e v_e^2$ (assuming $\dot{m}_e = \dot{m}_a = \dot{m}$)
*   **Propulsive Efficiency ($\eta_p$):** The ratio of propulsive power to the rate of kinetic energy imparted to the jet.
    $\eta_p = \frac{P_p}{KE_j} = \frac{F_{net} \times v_a}{\frac{1}{2} \dot{m}_e v_e^2}$
    Substituting $F_{net} \approx \dot{m} (v_e - v_a)$:
    $\eta_p = \frac{\dot{m} (v_e - v_a) v_a}{\frac{1}{2} \dot{m} v_e^2}$
    $\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - v_a) v_a}{v_e^2}$

---

### 3. Factors Affecting Propulsive Efficiency

*   **Relative Velocity ($v_e - v_a$):** This is the "slip" between the jet and the aircraft. A higher relative velocity means more momentum change per unit mass, which is good for thrust.
*   **Aircraft Speed ($v_a$):** This is the speed at which the useful work is done.
*   **Jet Exhaust Velocity ($v_e$):** This is the speed at which the mass is expelled.

**Analysis of the Propulsive Efficiency Equation:** $\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - v_a) v_a}{v_e^2}$

*   **Case 1: $v_a = 0$ (Aircraft at rest)**
    $\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - 0) \times 0}{v_e^2} = 0$
    *   **Implication:** If the aircraft is not moving, there is no propulsive power ($P_p = F_{net} \times 0 = 0$), hence zero propulsive efficiency, even though thrust is generated. This is intuitively correct as no work is being done.

*   **Case 2: $v_a = v_e$ (Jet velocity equals aircraft speed)**
    $\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - v_e) v_e}{v_e^2} = 0$
    *   **Implication:** If the exhaust velocity is equal to the aircraft speed, the net thrust is zero ($F_{net} = \dot{m} (v_e - v_a) = 0$), and therefore the propulsive power is zero, leading to zero propulsive efficiency.

*   **Case 3: $v_a \to \infty$ (Hypothetical)**
    As $v_a$ becomes very large compared to $v_e$, the term $(v_e - v_a)$ becomes negative, meaning thrust is negative. Propulsive efficiency would also tend towards zero.

*   **Maximum Propulsive Efficiency:** To find the condition for maximum propulsive efficiency, we can differentiate $\eta_p$ with respect to the ratio $v_a/v_e$ and set it to zero. Let $x = v_a/v_e$. Then $v_a = x v_e$.
    $\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - x v_e) x v_e}{v_e^2} = 2 (1-x) x = 2x - 2x^2$
    To find the maximum, $\frac{d\eta_p}{dx} = 2 - 4x = 0$.
    This gives $x = \frac{1}{2}$.
    Therefore, maximum propulsive efficiency occurs when $v_a = \frac{1}{2} v_e$.
    The maximum propulsive efficiency is:
    $\eta_{p,max} = \frac{2 (\frac{1}{2}v_e - v_e) v_e}{v_e^2} = \frac{2 (-\frac{1}{2}v_e) v_e}{v_e^2} = -1$ (Oops, something is wrong here. Let's re-evaluate.)

    Let's re-evaluate the substitution:
    $\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - v_a) v_a}{v_e^2}$
    Let the ratio of aircraft speed to jet speed be $\lambda = \frac{v_a}{v_e}$.
    $\eta_p = \frac{2 v_a}{v_e} \frac{(v_e - v_a)}{v_e} = 2 \lambda (1 - \lambda)$
    Now, to find the maximum propulsive efficiency, we differentiate $\eta_p$ with respect to $\lambda$:
    $\frac{d\eta_p}{d\lambda} = \frac{d}{d\lambda} (2\lambda - 2\lambda^2) = 2 - 4\lambda$
    Setting the derivative to zero for maximum:
    $2 - 4\lambda = 0 \implies \lambda = \frac{1}{2}$
    So, maximum propulsive efficiency occurs when $v_a = \frac{1}{2} v_e$.
    The maximum propulsive efficiency is:
    $\eta_{p,max} = 2 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) = 2 \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$ or 50%.

    **Important Point:** The maximum theoretical propulsive efficiency for a simple jet is 50%. This occurs when the aircraft speed is half the jet exhaust velocity.

### 4. Propulsive Efficiency in Different Jet Engine Types

The definition of propulsive efficiency remains the same, but the analysis of $v_e$ and the addition of bypass air in turbofan engines significantly alter the practical efficiency.

#### 4.1 Turbojet Engine

*   **Operation:** All the air passes through the core engine (compressor, combustor, turbine). The exhaust consists entirely of hot, high-velocity gases.
*   **Jet Velocity ($v_e$):** Relatively high.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency:** Tends to be lower at lower flight speeds and higher at higher flight speeds. This is because to achieve high propulsive efficiency ($\lambda \approx 0.5$), the jet velocity needs to be roughly twice the flight speed. For high-speed flight, $v_a$ is high, requiring a high $v_e$, which is characteristic of turbojets. However, at low speeds, the high $v_e$ leads to low propulsive efficiency.
*   **Example:** A Concorde SST (Supersonic Transport) had turbojet engines optimized for high-speed supersonic flight where turbojets are more suitable.

#### 4.2 Turbofan Engine

*   **Operation:** A significant portion of air bypasses the core engine and is accelerated by a fan. This bypass air is expelled at a lower velocity than the core exhaust.
*   **Effective Jet Velocity:** The concept of a single "jet velocity" is replaced by considering the mass flow and velocity of both the bypass air and the core exhaust. For analysis, an equivalent jet velocity ($v_{e,eq}$) or a specific thrust term is often used.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency:** Turbofans have significantly higher propulsive efficiency than turbojets, especially at subsonic speeds. This is because the fan increases the mass flow rate of air being accelerated and lowers the average exit velocity of the expelled air, bringing the effective exhaust velocity closer to the aircraft speed.
    *   The bypass ratio (BPR) is the ratio of the mass flow rate of bypass air to the mass flow rate of air passing through the core engine.
    *   High BPR turbofans (like those on modern airliners) are more propulsive efficient.
*   **Thrust Equation for Turbofan:**
    $F_{net} = \dot{m}_{bypass} (v_{e,bypass} - v_a) + \dot{m}_{core} (v_{e,core} - v_a) + (p_{e,bypass} - p_a)A_{e,bypass} + (p_{e,core} - p_a)A_{e,core}$
    Where subscripts 'bypass' and 'core' denote the respective streams.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency for Turbofan:** The general formula still applies, but $v_e$ is replaced by an effective exit velocity that accounts for the mixed flow of bypass and core streams. A simplified approach often assumes $p_e = p_a$ for both streams, and focuses on the mass-averaged exit velocity.
    Let $\dot{m}_t = \dot{m}_{bypass} + \dot{m}_{core}$ be the total mass flow.
    $F_{net} = \dot{m}_t v_{e,avg} - \dot{m}_t v_a$ (if we define $v_{e,avg}$ such that the momentum change is captured).
    A more direct way is to consider the weighted average velocity of the expelled air:
    $v_{e,eff} = \frac{\dot{m}_{bypass} v_{e,bypass} + \dot{m}_{core} v_{e,core}}{\dot{m}_{bypass} + \dot{m}_{core}}$
    Then, the propulsive efficiency can be approximated as:
    $\eta_p \approx \frac{2 (\frac{\dot{m}_{bypass} v_{e,bypass} + \dot{m}_{core} v_{e,core}}{\dot{m}_{bypass} + \dot{m}_{core}} - v_a) v_a}{\frac{\dot{m}_{bypass} v_{e,bypass}^2 + \dot{m}_{core} v_{e,core}^2}{\dot{m}_{bypass} + \dot{m}_{core}}}$
    This shows that with a high bypass ratio, $\dot{m}_{bypass}$ is large, and $v_{e,bypass}$ is generally lower than $v_{e,core}$. This tends to bring the effective exit velocity closer to $v_a$, increasing $\eta_p$.
*   **Example:** Boeing 747, Airbus A380 use high-bypass turbofan engines, making them very fuel-efficient for subsonic commercial flight.

#### 4.3 Prop-Fan / Ultra-High Bypass Ratio (UHBR) Engines

*   **Operation:** Similar to turbofans but with very large diameter fans, often exposed or partially ducted, operating at much lower speeds.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency:** These engines aim for even higher propulsive efficiencies by further reducing the effective exhaust velocity, getting closer to the ideal condition ($v_a \approx v_e/2$) for subsonic flight.
*   **Trade-offs:** Lower propulsive efficiency at very high speeds due to increased drag from larger fan diameter and lower jet velocity not being optimal for high speed.

### 5. Overall Efficiency and Specific Concepts

While propulsive efficiency is crucial, it's part of a larger picture of engine performance.

*   **Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$):** Measures how effectively the heat energy released from fuel combustion is converted into kinetic energy of the exhaust gases. This is related to the Brayton cycle efficiency. (Refer to Ganesan, Chapter 3 on "Gas Turbine Cycle").
    $\eta_{th} = \frac{\text{Useful energy transferred to working fluid}}{\text{Heat supplied}} = \frac{W_{net, turbine} - W_{net, compressor}}{Q_{in}}$
*   **Mechanical Efficiency ($\eta_{mech}$):** Accounts for power losses due to friction and other mechanical inefficiencies in driving the compressor and accessories.
*   **Jet Power ($P_j$):** The rate at which kinetic energy is added to the jet.
    $P_j = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m}_e v_e^2$
*   **Shaft Power ($P_{shaft}$):** The power delivered by the turbine shaft.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency ($\eta_p$):** As defined earlier.
*   **Overall Efficiency ($\eta_{overall}$):** The product of thermal efficiency, mechanical efficiency, and propulsive efficiency. It represents the ratio of useful propulsive power to the heat energy supplied by the fuel.
    $\eta_{overall} = \eta_{th} \times \eta_{mech} \times \eta_p$

    *   **Why overall efficiency matters:** A high thermal efficiency doesn't guarantee good overall performance if propulsive efficiency is low, and vice versa. Modern engines aim to optimize all these efficiencies for a given application.

### 6. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** A turbojet engine produces a net thrust of 50,000 N at a flight speed of 250 m/s. If the exhaust jet velocity is 500 m/s, calculate the propulsive efficiency.
**(Knowledge Level: K3 - Applying principles)**

**Answer 1:**
Given:
$F_{net} = 50,000 \, N$
$v_a = 250 \, m/s$
$v_e = 500 \, m/s$

Propulsive Power, $P_p = F_{net} \times v_a = 50,000 \, N \times 250 \, m/s = 12,500,000 \, W = 12.5 \, MW$.

To calculate propulsive efficiency using the formula $\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - v_a) v_a}{v_e^2}$, we need the mass flow rate. However, if we assume the thrust equation $F_{net} \approx \dot{m}(v_e - v_a)$, we can find $\dot{m}$:
$50,000 \, N = \dot{m} (500 \, m/s - 250 \, m/s)$
$50,000 \, N = \dot{m} (250 \, m/s)$
$\dot{m} = \frac{50,000 \, N}{250 \, m/s} = 200 \, kg/s$.

Now, Kinetic Energy of the jet per unit time, $KE_j = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m} v_e^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 200 \, kg/s \times (500 \, m/s)^2 = 100 \, kg/s \times 250,000 \, m^2/s^2 = 25,000,000 \, W = 25 \, MW$.

Propulsive Efficiency, $\eta_p = \frac{P_p}{KE_j} = \frac{12,500,000 \, W}{25,000,000 \, W} = 0.5$.

Alternatively, using the direct formula:
$\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - v_a) v_a}{v_e^2} = \frac{2 (500 - 250) \times 250}{500^2} = \frac{2 (250) \times 250}{250,000} = \frac{125,000}{250,000} = 0.5$.

So, the propulsive efficiency is 0.5 or 50%.

**Question 2:** For a jet engine, at what condition is the propulsive efficiency maximized? What is the value of this maximum efficiency?
**(Knowledge Level: K2 - Understanding principles)**

**Answer 2:**
The propulsive efficiency ($\eta_p$) is maximized when the aircraft speed ($v_a$) is half the jet exhaust velocity ($v_e$).
$\eta_p = \frac{2 (v_e - v_a) v_a}{v_e^2}$
The maximum propulsive efficiency occurs when $v_a = \frac{1}{2} v_e$.
The value of this maximum efficiency is:
$\eta_{p,max} = \frac{2 (v_e - \frac{1}{2}v_e) (\frac{1}{2}v_e)}{v_e^2} = \frac{2 (\frac{1}{2}v_e) (\frac{1}{2}v_e)}{v_e^2} = \frac{2 \times \frac{1}{4} v_e^2}{v_e^2} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$ or 50%.

**Question 3:** Explain why turbofan engines generally have higher propulsive efficiencies than turbojets at subsonic flight speeds.
**(Knowledge Level: K4 - Analyzing performance)**

**Answer 3:**
Turbofan engines have higher propulsive efficiencies at subsonic speeds because they accelerate a larger mass of air to a lower average velocity compared to turbojets.
*   **Turbojets:** Expel all air through the core at high velocity. To achieve good propulsive efficiency ($\lambda \approx 0.5$), the jet velocity must be high. This high jet velocity leads to a large difference between jet velocity and aircraft speed at low flight speeds, resulting in low propulsive efficiency.
*   **Turbofans:** The fan bypasses a large portion of the air around the core. This bypass air is accelerated to a lower velocity than the core exhaust. By mixing these streams, the turbofan effectively accelerates a much larger mass of air (total mass flow) to a lower average velocity. This lower average exit velocity is closer to the aircraft's flight speed, reducing the term $(v_e - v_a)$ and bringing the ratio $v_a/v_e$ closer to the optimal 0.5, thereby increasing propulsive efficiency. High bypass ratio engines are particularly effective at this.

**Question 4:** A turbofan engine has a bypass ratio of 5:1. The bypass air is expelled at 200 m/s, and the core exhaust is at 500 m/s. The flight speed is 200 m/s. Assuming the mass flow rate through the core is $\dot{m}_c$, the bypass mass flow rate is $\dot{m}_b = 5\dot{m}_c$. Assume $p_e = p_a$ for both streams. Calculate the approximate propulsive efficiency.
**(Knowledge Level: K3 - Applying principles)**

**Answer 4:**
Given:
$\dot{m}_b = 5\dot{m}_c$
$v_{e,bypass} = 200 \, m/s$
$v_{e,core} = 500 \, m/s$
$v_a = 200 \, m/s$

Total mass flow rate, $\dot{m}_t = \dot{m}_b + \dot{m}_c = 5\dot{m}_c + \dot{m}_c = 6\dot{m}_c$.

Thrust from bypass air: $F_{bypass} = \dot{m}_b (v_{e,bypass} - v_a) = 5\dot{m}_c (200 - 200) = 0 \, N$.
Thrust from core air: $F_{core} = \dot{m}_c (v_{e,core} - v_a) = \dot{m}_c (500 - 200) = 300\dot{m}_c \, N$.

Net Thrust, $F_{net} = F_{bypass} + F_{core} = 0 + 300\dot{m}_c = 300\dot{m}_c \, N$.

Propulsive Power, $P_p = F_{net} \times v_a = (300\dot{m}_c) \times 200 = 60,000\dot{m}_c \, W$.

Kinetic Energy of bypass jet per unit time: $KE_{j,bypass} = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m}_b v_{e,bypass}^2 = \frac{1}{2} (5\dot{m}_c) (200)^2 = \frac{1}{2} (5\dot{m}_c) (40,000) = 100,000\dot{m}_c \, W$.
Kinetic Energy of core jet per unit time: $KE_{j,core} = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m}_c v_{e,core}^2 = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m}_c (500)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \dot{m}_c (250,000) = 125,000\dot{m}_c \, W$.

Total Kinetic Energy imparted to jets, $KE_{total} = KE_{j,bypass} + KE_{j,core} = 100,000\dot{m}_c + 125,000\dot{m}_c = 225,000\dot{m}_c \, W$.

Propulsive Efficiency, $\eta_p = \frac{P_p}{KE_{total}} = \frac{60,000\dot{m}_c}{225,000\dot{m}_c} = \frac{60}{225} = \frac{12}{45} = \frac{4}{15}$.

$\eta_p \approx 0.2667$ or 26.67%.

*Note: This calculation shows that despite the bypass air contributing no thrust in this specific scenario (because $v_{e,bypass} = v_a$), it significantly lowers the propulsive efficiency because it adds to the total kinetic energy imparted to the exhaust streams without contributing to useful propulsive power.* This highlights the importance of matching $v_a$ to the effective exit velocity.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Propulsive efficiency ($\eta_p$) is the ratio of propulsive power ($F_{net} \times v_a$) to the kinetic energy imparted to the jet ($\frac{1}{2}\dot{m}v_e^2$).**
*   **Maximum theoretical propulsive efficiency for a simple jet is 50%, occurring when $v_a = \frac{1}{2} v_e$.**
*   **At $v_a = 0$ or $v_a = v_e$, propulsive efficiency is zero.**
*   **Turbofan engines, especially high-bypass ratio ones, have higher propulsive efficiencies at subsonic speeds due to accelerating a larger mass of air to a lower average velocity.**
*   **Propulsive efficiency is a critical factor in overall fuel economy for aircraft.**
*   **Overall efficiency ($\eta_{overall}$) considers thermal, mechanical, and propulsive efficiencies.**
