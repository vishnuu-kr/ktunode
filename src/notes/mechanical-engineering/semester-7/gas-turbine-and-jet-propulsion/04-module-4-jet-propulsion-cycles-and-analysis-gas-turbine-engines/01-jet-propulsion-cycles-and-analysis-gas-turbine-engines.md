# GAS TURBINE AND JET PROPULSION

## Module 4: Jet Propulsion – Cycles and Analysis – Gas Turbine Engines

This module delves into the fundamental thermodynamic cycles that power jet engines and the analytical methods used to assess their performance. We will explore the operation of gas turbine engines and their application in propulsion systems.

---

### 1. Introduction to Jet Propulsion

Jet propulsion is a method of propulsion where a working fluid is expelled at high velocity from a nozzle, creating a reactive thrust. This thrust propels the vehicle forward. The core of most modern jet engines is a gas turbine.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Thrust:** The force generated by the expulsion of a high-velocity fluid.
*   **Newton's Third Law:** For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. This is the fundamental principle behind jet propulsion.
*   **Working Fluid:** Typically air or combustion products.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency:** The ratio of useful thrust power to the kinetic energy imparted to the working fluid.

**Understanding (CO4 - K2):** Jet propulsion relies on expelling mass rearward to move forward. Think of releasing a balloon – the escaping air pushes the balloon forward.

---

### 2. The Brayton Cycle: The Foundation of Gas Turbine Engines

The Brayton cycle is the thermodynamic cycle that describes the operation of a gas turbine engine. It consists of four ideal processes:

1.  **Isentropic Compression:** Air is compressed in a compressor, increasing its pressure and temperature.
2.  **Isobaric Heat Addition:** Fuel is burned in the combustor, adding heat to the compressed air at constant pressure.
3.  **Isentropic Expansion:** The hot, high-pressure gas expands through a turbine, producing work.
4.  **Isobaric Heat Rejection:** The exhaust gases are cooled at constant pressure, returning to the initial state (in an open cycle, this heat is rejected to the atmosphere).

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Compressor:** Increases the pressure of the working fluid (air).
*   **Combustor (Combustion Chamber):** Where fuel is injected and burned, adding heat at constant pressure.
*   **Turbine:** Extracts work from the high-pressure, high-temperature gas to drive the compressor and often an output shaft.
*   **Nozzle:** Accelerates the hot exhaust gases to produce thrust.
*   **Work Ratio:** The ratio of net work output (turbine work minus compressor work) to turbine work. A high work ratio is desirable for engine efficiency.

**Thermodynamic Analysis of the Ideal Brayton Cycle (CO1 - K3):**

Let's analyze the ideal Brayton cycle using specific heat ratio ($\gamma$) and the pressure ratio ($r_p = P_2/P_1$).

*   **State Points:**
    *   1: Inlet (Ambient conditions)
    *   2: Compressor Outlet / Combustor Inlet
    *   3: Combustor Outlet / Turbine Inlet
    *   4: Turbine Outlet / Nozzle Inlet

*   **Temperatures:**
    *   $T_1$: Ambient temperature
    *   $T_2 = T_1 \cdot r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$ (Compressor outlet temperature)
    *   $T_3$: Turbine inlet temperature (TIT) – a critical design parameter.
    *   $T_4 = T_3 / r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$ (Turbine outlet temperature)

*   **Work Done:**
    *   **Compressor Work Input ($W_c$):** $c_p (T_2 - T_1)$
    *   **Turbine Work Output ($W_t$):** $c_p (T_3 - T_4)$
    *   **Net Work Output ($W_{net}$):** $W_t - W_c = c_p (T_3 - T_4 - T_2 + T_1)$

*   **Heat Added ($Q_{in}$):** $c_p (T_3 - T_2)$

*   **Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$):**
    $\eta_{th} = \frac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}} = \frac{c_p (T_3 - T_4 - T_2 + T_1)}{c_p (T_3 - T_2)}$
    Substituting $T_2$ and $T_4$ in terms of $T_1$ and $r_p$:
    $\eta_{th} = \frac{1 - (T_4/T_3)}{1 - (T_2/T_1)} = \frac{1 - (1/r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma})}{1 - (1/r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma})} \times \frac{T_1}{T_1} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}$

    **Observation:** The thermal efficiency of the ideal Brayton cycle increases with increasing pressure ratio ($r_p$) and decreasing ambient temperature ($T_1$).

**Example:**
Consider an ideal Brayton cycle with inlet conditions $T_1 = 288 K$, $P_1 = 100 kPa$. The pressure ratio $r_p = 8$. Assume $\gamma = 1.4$ and $c_p = 1.005 kJ/kg \cdot K$.
Calculate $T_2$, $T_4$, $\eta_{th}$.

*   $T_2 = 288 \cdot 8^{(1.4-1)/1.4} = 288 \cdot 8^{0.4/1.4} = 288 \cdot 8^{0.2857} = 288 \cdot 1.746 \approx 502.8 K$
*   $T_4 = T_3 / r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$. We need $T_3$. Let's assume $T_3 = 1200 K$ for now.
    $T_4 = 1200 / 8^{0.2857} = 1200 / 1.746 \approx 687.3 K$
*   $\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{8^{0.2857}} = 1 - \frac{1}{1.746} \approx 1 - 0.5727 \approx 0.4273$ or 42.73%

**Reference:** Ganesan, V. "Gas Turbines." Chapter 3 discusses the Brayton cycle.

### 3. Real Brayton Cycle and Component Efficiencies

In reality, the Brayton cycle processes are not ideal due to irreversibilities.

**Key Concepts & Deviations from Ideal Cycle:**

*   **Compressor Inefficiency:** Compression is not isentropic. Actual compressor work input is higher than ideal. Represented by **isentropic or adiabatic efficiency ($\eta_c$)**.
    $\eta_c = \frac{\text{Ideal Compressor Work}}{\text{Actual Compressor Work}} = \frac{c_p (T_{2s} - T_1)}{c_p (T_2 - T_1)}$
    where $T_{2s}$ is the isentropic outlet temperature.
*   **Turbine Inefficiency:** Expansion through the turbine is not isentropic. Actual turbine work output is lower than ideal. Represented by **isentropic or adiabatic efficiency ($\eta_t$)**.
    $\eta_t = \frac{\text{Actual Turbine Work}}{\text{Ideal Turbine Work}} = \frac{c_p (T_3 - T_4)}{c_p (T_3 - T_{4s})}$
    where $T_{4s}$ is the isentropic outlet temperature.
*   **Pressure Drops:** There are pressure losses in the combustor and inlet/exhaust ducts due to friction and flow disturbances.
*   **Combustor Efficiency ($\eta_{cb}$):** Not all fuel injected burns completely. $\eta_{cb}$ is the ratio of actual heat released to the theoretical heat available from the fuel. Typically very high (0.95-0.99).

**Analysis of Real Brayton Cycle (CO1 - K3, CO3 - K4):**

The thermal efficiency of a real Brayton cycle is lower than the ideal cycle due to component inefficiencies.

*   **Actual Net Work:** $W_{net, real} = W_{t, real} - W_{c, actual}$
*   **Actual Heat Input:** $Q_{in, actual} = \frac{c_p (T_3 - T_2')}{\eta_{cb}}$, where $T_2'$ is the actual compressor outlet temperature and $T_3$ is the turbine inlet temperature.
*   **Real Thermal Efficiency:** $\eta_{th, real} = \frac{W_{net, real}}{Q_{in, actual}}$

**Impact of Pressure Losses:**
Pressure drops in the combustor ($P_2 > P_3$) reduce the effective pressure ratio across the turbine, leading to lower turbine work and overall efficiency.

**Example:**
Continuing the previous example, let $\eta_c = 0.85$ and $\eta_t = 0.88$. Let the combustor pressure drop be 4% of $P_2$. $T_1 = 288 K$, $P_1 = 100 kPa$, $r_p = 8$. $T_3 = 1200 K$.

*   **Isentropic Compressor Outlet Temp ($T_{2s}$):** 502.8 K (from previous example)
*   **Actual Compressor Outlet Temp ($T_2$):** $T_2 = T_1 + \frac{T_{2s} - T_1}{\eta_c} = 288 + \frac{502.8 - 288}{0.85} = 288 + \frac{214.8}{0.85} \approx 288 + 252.7 \approx 540.7 K$
*   **Actual Compressor Work ($W_{c, actual}$):** $1.005 \cdot (540.7 - 288) \approx 254.0 kJ/kg$

*   **Pressure at Combustor Inlet ($P_2$):** $P_2 = P_1 \cdot r_p = 100 \cdot 8 = 800 kPa$.
*   **Pressure at Combustor Outlet ($P_3$):** $P_3 = P_2 \cdot (1 - 0.04) = 800 \cdot 0.96 = 768 kPa$.
*   **Effective Pressure Ratio for Turbine ($r_{p,t}$):** $P_2/P_3 = 800/768 \approx 1.042$ (This is incorrect. It should be $P_3/P_4$). The pressure ratio across the turbine is $P_3/P_4$. Since $P_4 = P_1$, $r_{p,t} = P_3/P_1 = 768/100 = 7.68$.
*   **Isentropic Turbine Outlet Temp ($T_{4s}$):** $T_{4s} = T_3 / r_{p,t}^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 1200 / (7.68)^{0.2857} = 1200 / 1.73 \approx 693.6 K$
*   **Actual Turbine Outlet Temp ($T_4$):** $T_4 = T_3 - \eta_t \cdot (T_3 - T_{4s}) = 1200 - 0.88 \cdot (1200 - 693.6) = 1200 - 0.88 \cdot 506.4 \approx 1200 - 445.6 \approx 754.4 K$
*   **Actual Turbine Work ($W_{t, actual}$):** $1.005 \cdot (1200 - 754.4) \approx 448.0 kJ/kg$

*   **Net Work ($W_{net, real}$):** $448.0 - 254.0 = 194.0 kJ/kg$
*   **Heat Input ($Q_{in, actual}$):** Assuming $\eta_{cb} \approx 1$, $Q_{in, actual} = 1.005 \cdot (1200 - 540.7) \approx 663.0 kJ/kg$
*   **Real Thermal Efficiency:** $\eta_{th, real} = \frac{194.0}{663.0} \approx 0.2926$ or 29.26%.

**Important Point to Remember:** Component inefficiencies significantly reduce the overall thermal efficiency compared to the ideal Brayton cycle.

**Reference:** Yahya, S. M. "Turbines, Compressors and Fans." Chapters 5 and 7 cover compressor and turbine efficiencies. Mathur, M. L. "Gas Turbine & Jet Rocket Propulsion." Chapter 4 details performance analysis.

### 4. Gas Turbine Engine Configurations for Propulsion

The basic Brayton cycle is adapted for different propulsion applications.

#### 4.1 Turbojet Engine

This is the simplest form of jet engine. All the air passing through the compressor is compressed, heated in the combustor, expanded through the turbine (driving the compressor), and then expelled through a nozzle at high velocity to produce thrust.

**Diagram:** Intake -> Compressor -> Combustor -> Turbine -> Nozzle

**Thrust Generation:** Primarily from the high-velocity exhaust gases.

**Characteristics:**
*   High exhaust velocity.
*   Efficient at high speeds.
*   Noisy.
*   Relatively poor fuel economy at low speeds.

**Analysis (CO4 - K2):** The thrust is generated by accelerating a mass of air and fuel through the engine.

**Reference:** Mathur, M. L. "Gas Turbine & Jet Rocket Propulsion." Chapter 7 provides a detailed breakdown of turbojet engines. Rolls Royce, "The Jet Engine," offers an excellent practical perspective.

#### 4.2 Turbofan Engine

A significant advancement over the turbojet. A large fan is added at the front, driven by a separate turbine stage. The fan bypasses a substantial portion of the air around the core engine (compressor, combustor, turbine).

**Diagram:** Intake -> Fan -> Bypass Duct (most air) AND Core Engine (Compressor -> Combustor -> Turbine) -> Exhaust Nozzles

**Thrust Generation:**
*   **Core Thrust:** From the hot exhaust gases of the core engine.
*   **Bypass Thrust:** From the cooler, high-volume airflow accelerated by the fan.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Bypass Ratio (BPR):** The ratio of the mass flow rate of air bypassing the core to the mass flow rate of air through the core.
    *   Low-bypass turbofan (e.g., older fighter jets): BPR ~ 0.5 - 2
    *   High-bypass turbofan (e.g., commercial airliners): BPR ~ 5 - 10+

**Advantages of Turbofans (CO1 - K3, CO3 - K4):**

*   **Improved Fuel Efficiency:** The fan accelerates a larger mass of air, but at a lower velocity than a turbojet. This leads to higher propulsive efficiency, especially at lower speeds.
*   **Reduced Noise:** The bypass air mixes with the hot exhaust, diluting the noise.
*   **Higher Thrust:** Particularly at lower speeds, due to the larger mass flow rate.

**Analysis:** The overall thrust of a turbofan is the sum of the core thrust and the bypass thrust. The analysis involves considering the momentum change of both the core flow and the bypass flow.

**Example:** A high-bypass turbofan engine for an airliner might have a bypass ratio of 6:1. This means for every 1 kg of air going through the core, 6 kg of air is accelerated by the fan.

**Reference:** Ganesan, V. "Gas Turbines." Chapter 8 discusses turbofan engines. Yahya, S. M. "Turbines, Compressors and Fans." Chapter 9 covers fans and their integration.

#### 4.3 Turboprop Engine

In a turboprop engine, the turbine power is used to drive a propeller, which is the primary source of thrust. The gas turbine core acts as a power source. A gearbox is used to reduce the high rotational speed of the turbine to a suitable speed for the propeller.

**Diagram:** Intake -> Compressor -> Combustor -> Turbine -> Gearbox -> Propeller

**Thrust Generation:** Primarily by the propeller, accelerating a large mass of air at a relatively low velocity. A small amount of thrust is also produced by the core engine exhaust.

**Characteristics:**
*   Excellent fuel efficiency at lower speeds (typically below Mach 0.6).
*   Very effective for takeoff and climb.
*   Propeller efficiency decreases significantly at higher speeds.

**Analysis (CO4 - K2):** The engine's purpose is to produce shaft power to drive the propeller. The overall efficiency is a combination of the gas turbine efficiency and the propeller efficiency.

**Reference:** Mathur, M. L. "Gas Turbine & Jet Rocket Propulsion." Chapter 8 discusses turboprop engines.

#### 4.4 Turboshaft Engine

Similar to a turboprop, but the turbine power is used to drive an output shaft, typically to power rotors (helicopters) or other machinery (e.g., power generation). The propeller or rotor is not exposed to the main airstream in the same way as a turboprop.

**Diagram:** Intake -> Compressor -> Combustor -> Turbine -> Gearbox -> Output Shaft

**Thrust Generation:** Minimal direct thrust from exhaust. Primary function is to deliver mechanical power.

**Applications:** Helicopters, tanks, boats, power generation.

**Reference:** Ganesan, V. "Gas Turbines." Chapter 8 also covers turboshaft engines.

### 5. Thrust Equation and Specific Thrust

**Thrust Equation (CO4 - K2):**

The net thrust ($F$) developed by a jet engine is the rate of change of momentum of the working fluid plus any pressure difference at the nozzle exit.

For a simple turbojet, neglecting ambient pressure variations and exit pressure differences:
$F = \dot{m}_a (V_j - V_a)$
where:
*   $\dot{m}_a$ is the mass flow rate of air through the engine.
*   $V_j$ is the jet velocity (exhaust velocity).
*   $V_a$ is the aircraft velocity (flight speed).

For a turbofan engine with bypass, the thrust is:
$F = \dot{m}_{core}(V_j - V_a) + \dot{m}_{bypass}(V_{j,bypass} - V_a)$
where:
*   $\dot{m}_{core}$ and $\dot{m}_{bypass}$ are the mass flow rates in the core and bypass streams, respectively.
*   $V_j$ and $V_{j,bypass}$ are the jet velocities of the core and bypass streams.

**Specific Thrust ($F_s$):**

This is the thrust per unit mass flow rate of air entering the engine.
$F_s = \frac{F}{\dot{m}_a}$

For a turbojet: $F_s = V_j - V_a$
For a turbofan: $F_s = \frac{\dot{m}_{core}}{\dot{m}_a}(V_j - V_a) + \frac{\dot{m}_{bypass}}{\dot{m}_a}(V_{j,bypass} - V_a)$

**Important Point to Remember:** For high subsonic flight, high-bypass turbofans produce more thrust per unit of air mass flow than turbojets because they accelerate a larger mass of air to a lower average velocity.

**Reference:** Cohen, H. "Gas Turbine Theory." Chapter 10 discusses thrust and propulsion.

### 6. Cycle Variations and Improvements

The basic Brayton cycle can be modified to improve its performance.

#### 6.1 Reheat (Afterburning)

This involves injecting fuel into the exhaust stream of the turbine, before the nozzle. This increases the exhaust gas temperature and velocity, thereby significantly increasing thrust. It is primarily used in military aircraft for short periods (e.g., takeoff, combat maneuvering) due to very high fuel consumption.

**Impact:** Increases thrust significantly but drastically reduces thermal and propulsive efficiency.

**Reference:** Mathur, M. L. "Gas Turbine & Jet Rocket Propulsion." Chapter 7 covers afterburning.

#### 6.2 Intercooling

Cooling the air between stages of a multi-stage compressor reduces the work required for compression. This improves the net work output of the cycle and increases thermal efficiency. However, intercoolers add weight and complexity.

**Impact:** Reduces compressor work, increases net work, and improves thermal efficiency. However, intercooling is rarely used in jet engines due to weight and drag penalties, but can be beneficial in industrial gas turbines.

#### 6.3 Regeneration (Recuperation)

A heat exchanger is used to transfer heat from the hot turbine exhaust gases to the compressed air entering the combustor. This reduces the amount of fuel needed for a given turbine inlet temperature, thus increasing thermal efficiency.

**Impact:** Increases thermal efficiency, especially at lower turbine inlet temperatures. However, regenerators add weight, pressure drop, and cost. More common in industrial gas turbines and some specialized aircraft engines.

**Reference:** Ganesan, V. "Gas Turbines." Chapter 3 discusses cycle modifications like regeneration.

### 7. Aerodynamic Considerations in Components

The performance of gas turbine engines heavily relies on the aerodynamic design of their components.

#### 7.1 Compressor Aerodynamics (CO2 - K4)

*   **Types:** Axial flow and centrifugal. Axial flow compressors are dominant in jet engines due to their high flow rates and efficiency at high speeds.
*   **Blade Aerofoils:** Designed to efficiently compress the air, converting kinetic energy into pressure energy.
*   **Stall and Surge:**
    *   **Stall:** Local flow separation on compressor blades.
    *   **Surge:** A condition of unstable operation where flow in the compressor oscillates or reverses.
*   **Work Input:** Related to changes in velocity and tangential force on the blades.
*   **Stage Characteristics:** Each stage (rotor + stator) contributes to the overall pressure rise and efficiency.

**Reference:** Yahya, S. M. "Turbines, Compressors and Fans." Chapters 5 and 6 are dedicated to compressor aerodynamics.

#### 7.2 Turbine Aerodynamics (CO2 - K4)

*   **Types:** Impulse and reaction turbines. Jet engine turbines are typically reaction turbines.
*   **Blade Design:** Designed to extract maximum work from the expanding gases.
*   **Work Output:** Achieved by the force exerted by the gas on the rotating blades.
*   **Stage Efficiency:** Influenced by factors like incidence losses, profile losses, leakage losses, and tip clearance.

**Reference:** Yahya, S. M. "Turbines, Compressors and Fans." Chapters 7 and 8 cover turbine aerodynamics.

#### 7.3 Nozzle Aerodynamics (CO4 - K2)

*   **Convergent Nozzle:** Used for subsonic exhaust velocities.
*   **Convergent-Divergent (C-D) Nozzle:** Used when the exhaust velocity is expected to be supersonic. The diverging section accelerates the flow further.
*   **Mass Flow Rate:** Determined by the throat area and the conditions at the throat (choked flow).

**Reference:** Cohen, H. "Gas Turbine Theory." Chapter 10 discusses nozzle performance.

### 8. Gas Turbine Engine Performance Metrics

*   **Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$):** Ratio of net work output to heat input. Measures how effectively heat energy is converted into useful work.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency ($\eta_{p}$):** Ratio of useful thrust power to the kinetic energy imparted to the working fluid.
    $\eta_{p} = \frac{F \cdot V_a}{\frac{1}{2}\dot{m}_a(V_j^2 - V_a^2)} = \frac{2 V_a}{V_j + V_a}$
    (For a turbojet)
*   **Overall Efficiency ($\eta_o$):** Product of thermal efficiency and propulsive efficiency. $\eta_o = \eta_{th} \cdot \eta_{p}$
*   **Specific Fuel Consumption (SFC):** The amount of fuel consumed per unit of thrust produced. Lower SFC indicates better fuel economy.
    $SFC = \frac{\dot{m}_f}{F}$ (where $\dot{m}_f$ is fuel flow rate)

**Important Point to Remember:** For jet propulsion, high propulsive efficiency is crucial. High-bypass turbofans achieve better propulsive efficiency than turbojets by moving a larger mass of air at a lower velocity.

### Practice Questions & Answers

**Question 1:** An ideal Brayton cycle has a pressure ratio of 10:1. The turbine inlet temperature is 1400 K and the ambient temperature is 290 K. Calculate the thermal efficiency of the cycle.
*(Assume $\gamma = 1.4$)*

**Answer 1:**
The thermal efficiency of an ideal Brayton cycle is given by:
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}$
Given: $r_p = 10$, $\gamma = 1.4$.
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{10^{(1.4-1)/1.4}} = 1 - \frac{1}{10^{0.4/1.4}} = 1 - \frac{1}{10^{0.2857}}$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{1.9307} = 1 - 0.5179 = 0.4821$
So, the thermal efficiency is 48.21%.

**Question 2:** A turbojet engine operates with the following conditions:
Inlet air temperature ($T_1$) = 288 K
Inlet air pressure ($P_1$) = 100 kPa
Compressor pressure ratio ($r_p$) = 12
Turbine inlet temperature ($T_3$) = 1500 K
Compressor isentropic efficiency ($\eta_c$) = 88%
Turbine isentropic efficiency ($\eta_t$) = 90%
Mass flow rate of air ($\dot{m}_a$) = 50 kg/s
Assume $c_p = 1.005$ kJ/kg·K and $\gamma = 1.4$. Neglect pressure losses in the combustor.
Calculate:
(a) Net work output per kg of air.
(b) Thermal efficiency.
(c) If the exhaust velocity ($V_j$) is 800 m/s, what is the thrust?

**Answer 2:**

(a) **Net work output per kg of air:**
*   **Isentropic Compressor Outlet Temperature ($T_{2s}$):**
    $T_{2s} = T_1 \cdot r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 288 \cdot 12^{(1.4-1)/1.4} = 288 \cdot 12^{0.2857} = 288 \cdot 1.970 \approx 567.4 K$
*   **Actual Compressor Outlet Temperature ($T_2$):**
    $T_2 = T_1 + \frac{T_{2s} - T_1}{\eta_c} = 288 + \frac{567.4 - 288}{0.88} = 288 + \frac{279.4}{0.88} \approx 288 + 317.5 = 605.5 K$
*   **Actual Compressor Work Input ($W_{c, actual}$):**
    $W_{c, actual} = c_p (T_2 - T_1) = 1.005 \cdot (605.5 - 288) = 1.005 \cdot 317.5 \approx 319.1 kJ/kg$

*   **Pressure Ratio across Turbine ($r_{p,t}$):** $P_3/P_4 = P_2/P_1 = r_p = 12$ (assuming no pressure loss)
*   **Isentropic Turbine Outlet Temperature ($T_{4s}$):**
    $T_{4s} = T_3 / r_{p,t}^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 1500 / 12^{0.2857} = 1500 / 1.970 \approx 761.4 K$
*   **Actual Turbine Outlet Temperature ($T_4$):**
    $T_4 = T_3 - \eta_t \cdot (T_3 - T_{4s}) = 1500 - 0.90 \cdot (1500 - 761.4) = 1500 - 0.90 \cdot 738.6 = 1500 - 664.7 \approx 835.3 K$
*   **Actual Turbine Work Output ($W_{t, actual}$):**
    $W_{t, actual} = c_p (T_3 - T_4) = 1.005 \cdot (1500 - 835.3) = 1.005 \cdot 664.7 \approx 667.9 kJ/kg$

*   **Net Work Output ($W_{net}$):**
    $W_{net} = W_{t, actual} - W_{c, actual} = 667.9 - 319.1 = 348.8 kJ/kg$

(b) **Thermal efficiency:**
*   **Heat Input ($Q_{in}$):** Assume combustor efficiency $\eta_{cb} \approx 1$.
    $Q_{in} = c_p (T_3 - T_2) = 1.005 \cdot (1500 - 605.5) = 1.005 \cdot 894.5 \approx 899.0 kJ/kg$
*   **Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$):**
    $\eta_{th} = \frac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}} = \frac{348.8}{899.0} \approx 0.3880$
    So, the thermal efficiency is 38.80%.

(c) **Thrust:**
Assuming aircraft velocity ($V_a$) = 0 (for simplicity, often it's considered in problems). If $V_a$ is not given, we assume it's zero to calculate the static thrust.
$F = \dot{m}_a (V_j - V_a)$
$F = 50 \, \text{kg/s} \cdot (800 \, \text{m/s} - 0 \, \text{m/s})$
$F = 40000 \, \text{N}$ or $40 \, \text{kN}$

**Question 3:** Explain the primary advantage of a high-bypass turbofan engine over a turbojet engine for commercial aviation.

**Answer 3:** The primary advantage of a high-bypass turbofan engine over a turbojet for commercial aviation is **improved fuel efficiency and reduced noise levels**. The fan accelerates a large mass of bypass air, which contributes significantly to the overall thrust. This bypass air is accelerated to a lower velocity compared to the high-velocity exhaust of a turbojet. Moving a larger mass of air at a lower velocity is more propulsively efficient at the subsonic speeds typical of commercial aircraft. This higher propulsive efficiency translates directly to lower fuel consumption. Additionally, the bypass air mixes with and dilutes the hot, fast core exhaust, significantly reducing the engine's noise footprint.

### 9. Key Points to Remember

*   **Brayton Cycle:** The fundamental thermodynamic cycle for gas turbines.
*   **Efficiency:** Thermal efficiency increases with pressure ratio, but decreases with increasing turbine inlet temperature (for ideal cycles, but real cycles benefit from higher TIT). Component efficiencies (compressor, turbine) are crucial.
*   **Propulsion:** Thrust is generated by accelerating a mass of fluid.
*   **Engine Types:** Turbojets, turbofans, turboprops, and turboshafts are variations of the gas turbine engine, each suited for different applications.
*   **Turbofans:** High-bypass turbofans offer superior fuel efficiency and lower noise for commercial aviation due to better propulsive efficiency.
*   **Thrust Equation:** $F = \dot{m}(V_{exit} - V_{inlet})$ is central to propulsion analysis.
*   **Propulsive Efficiency:** Key to understanding why high-bypass engines are preferred for many applications.
