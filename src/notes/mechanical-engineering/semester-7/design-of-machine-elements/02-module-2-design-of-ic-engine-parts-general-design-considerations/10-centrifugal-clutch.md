# DESIGN OF MACHINE ELEMENTS

## Module 2: Design of IC Engine Parts: General Design Considerations

### Topic: Centrifugal Clutch

---

### **1. Introduction to Centrifugal Clutches**

**Definition:** A centrifugal clutch is a type of automatic clutch that engages and disengages based on the rotational speed of the driving shaft. It utilizes centrifugal force generated by weights to transmit torque.

**Purpose:**
*   To automatically connect and disconnect a prime mover (like an IC engine) from a driven load without manual intervention.
*   To allow the engine to start and idle without stalling under load.
*   To protect the engine and driven machinery from sudden shocks and overloads.

**Applications in IC Engines:**
*   Go-karts
*   Motorcycles (especially scooters)
*   Lawnmowers
*   Small industrial machinery powered by IC engines
*   Generators

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Engagement Speed ($N_e$):** The rotational speed at which the clutch starts to engage.
*   **Disengagement Speed ($N_d$):** The rotational speed at which the clutch fully disengages. Typically, $N_d > N_e$.
*   **Slip:** The difference in rotational speed between the driving and driven members. Slip occurs during engagement and when the load torque exceeds the clutch's capacity.
*   **Centrifugal Force:** The outward force experienced by a rotating mass, proportional to its mass, rotational speed, and radius.

---

### **2. Working Principle**

The centrifugal clutch operates based on the principle of centrifugal force acting on masses (shoes or weights).

*   **At Low Speeds (Idle):** The centrifugal force acting on the shoes is insufficient to overcome the spring force holding them inwards. The shoes are away from the outer drum, and no torque is transmitted.
*   **As Speed Increases:** When the engine speed reaches the engagement speed ($N_e$), the centrifugal force acting on the shoes becomes equal to the spring force. The shoes start to move outwards.
*   **Engagement:** As the speed further increases, the shoes press against the inner surface of the clutch drum, creating friction. This friction transmits torque from the driving shaft (connected to shoes) to the driven shaft (connected to the drum).
*   **Full Engagement:** At higher speeds, the centrifugal force is sufficiently large to press the shoes firmly against the drum, transmitting maximum torque.
*   **Disengagement:** When the engine speed drops below the disengagement speed ($N_d$), the centrifugal force decreases, allowing the springs to pull the shoes back, thus disconnecting the load.

---

### **3. Components of a Centrifugal Clutch**

1.  **Driving Member (Spider/Hub):**
    *   Connected to the prime mover's crankshaft.
    *   Houses the rotating masses (shoes).
    *   Often includes keys or splines for secure connection to the engine shaft.

2.  **Shoes (Weights):**
    *   These are the primary engaging elements.
    *   Typically made of friction material or have a friction lining attached.
    *   They are pivotally mounted on the driving member.
    *   As speed increases, they swing outwards due to centrifugal force.
    *   *Design Consideration:* Material of shoes and lining is critical for friction and heat dissipation. Common materials include friction pads (e.g., asbestos-based, organic, ceramic) bonded or riveted to metal shoes.

3.  **Springs:**
    *   Provide the force to keep the shoes retracted at low speeds and to control engagement.
    *   Connect the shoes to the driving member.
    *   The stiffness and pre-tension of the springs determine the engagement speed.
    *   *Design Consideration:* Spring material, wire diameter, coil diameter, and number of coils are important. Springs can be coil springs or sometimes diaphragm springs.

4.  **Clutch Drum (Driven Member):**
    *   The outer housing that rotates with the driven shaft.
    *   The inner surface of the drum is where the shoes make contact.
    *   Torque is transmitted from the shoes to the drum via friction.
    *   *Design Consideration:* Material of the drum should have good wear resistance and thermal properties. Cast iron or steel are common. The inner surface finish is important for consistent friction.

5.  **Hub/Shaft Connection:**
    *   The part that connects to the engine's output shaft.

---

### **4. Design Considerations and Calculations**

The design of a centrifugal clutch involves balancing centrifugal forces, spring forces, and frictional forces to achieve the desired engagement speed and torque capacity.

#### **4.1. Engagement Speed Calculation**

The engagement speed is determined by the balance of forces acting on a shoe. Consider a shoe of mass $m$ at a radius $r$ from the center of rotation.

*   **Centrifugal Force ($F_c$):**
    $F_c = m \omega^2 r$
    where $\omega$ is the angular velocity in rad/s.
    If speed is given in RPM ($N$), then $\omega = \frac{2 \pi N}{60}$.
    $F_c = m \left(\frac{2 \pi N}{60}\right)^2 r$

*   **Spring Force ($F_s$):**
    At engagement, the centrifugal force must overcome the spring force and any initial offset.
    $F_s = F_{spring\_initial} + F_{centrifugal\_force\_at\_disengagement}$ (or a force related to spring stiffness and deflection)
    For a simplified analysis, we can consider the spring force at engagement to be a certain value $F_{s,e}$ that the centrifugal force must overcome.

*   **Force on the Friction Lining ($F_n$):**
    The shoe is typically pivoted. The centrifugal force can be resolved into components. For a shoe pivoted at one end, the centrifugal force acts radially outwards. The spring force acts inwards. The net radial force pushes the shoe against the drum.
    A common simplification is to consider the force that presses the shoe against the drum to be related to the centrifugal force and spring force.
    If the shoe pivots at a point and its mass center is at radius $r_{cm}$, then $F_c$ acts at $r_{cm}$. The spring is often attached to the shoe's outer edge or its mass center.

    Let's consider a simplified model where the shoe pivots at point P. Let the mass of the shoe be $m$, and its center of mass be at radius $r_{cm}$. The spring is attached to the shoe and the spider.
    At engagement speed $N_e$ (corresponding to $\omega_e$), the centrifugal force is $F_c = m \omega_e^2 r_{cm}$.
    This centrifugal force must overcome the spring force $F_s$.
    The force pressing the shoe against the drum ($F_p$) is a component of the net radial force.

    **Simplified Approach:**
    At engagement speed $N_e$, the centrifugal force developed by the shoe's mass ($m$) at its effective radius ($r_e$) equals the spring force ($F_{s,e}$) required to initiate contact.
    $F_c = F_s$
    $m \omega_e^2 r_e = F_{s,e}$
    $\omega_e^2 = \frac{F_{s,e}}{m r_e}$
    $N_e = \frac{60}{2 \pi} \sqrt{\frac{F_{s,e}}{m r_e}}$

    *   $m$: Mass of the shoe.
    *   $r_e$: Effective radius at which centrifugal force acts for engagement.
    *   $F_{s,e}$: Spring force at the engagement radius.

    **Norton (5th Ed.)** and **Bhandari (5th Ed.)** discuss the analysis of forces on shoes. The actual calculation can be complex due to the shoe geometry and pivot points.

    **Example:** A shoe of mass 0.5 kg is to engage at 1000 RPM. The effective radius of the shoe's center of mass is 50 mm (0.05 m). If the spring force at this radius is 200 N, what is the engagement speed?
    Here, we assume the spring force $F_{s,e} = 200$ N is the force that the centrifugal force must overcome.
    $F_c = m \omega_e^2 r_e$
    $200 = 0.5 \times \omega_e^2 \times 0.05$
    $\omega_e^2 = \frac{200}{0.5 \times 0.05} = \frac{200}{0.025} = 8000$
    $\omega_e = \sqrt{8000} \approx 89.44$ rad/s
    $N_e = \frac{60 \times 89.44}{2 \pi} \approx 854$ RPM.

#### **4.2. Torque Transmission Capacity**

The torque transmitted depends on the normal force exerted by the shoes on the drum, the coefficient of friction, and the radius of contact.

*   **Normal Force ($F_n$):**
    The normal force is the force with which the shoe presses against the drum. It's a result of the centrifugal force minus the spring force component and other resisting forces.
    $F_n = \frac{F_c \cos \alpha - F_s \cos \beta}{\cos(\alpha + \beta)}$ where $\alpha$ is the angle between $F_c$ and the normal to the contact surface, and $\beta$ is the angle between $F_s$ and the normal.
    A simpler approach often used in textbooks is to consider the net radial force pushing the shoe against the drum.

    **Simplified Normal Force:**
    Let $F_c$ be the centrifugal force acting at radius $r$. Let $F_s$ be the spring force acting at some point. The net force pressing the shoe against the drum depends on the geometry.
    If we assume the shoe is pivoted such that the centrifugal force directly contributes to the normal force, then the normal force $F_n$ can be approximated.

    **Norton (5th Ed.)** provides a detailed analysis of shoe forces. For a simple case, if $F_c$ is the only radial force and $F_s$ is the spring force opposing it, the net radial force is $F_c - F_s$. This force, if it acts radially, presses the shoe against the drum.

    **Common Simplification:** For a shoe of mass $m$ operating at speed $\omega$ at radius $r$, the centrifugal force is $F_c = m \omega^2 r$. If the spring force is $F_s$, the force available to press the shoe against the drum can be related to $F_c$ and $F_s$.
    The *normal force* ($F_n$) exerted by the shoe on the drum's inner surface is crucial.
    A common model equates the effective centrifugal force component (after subtracting spring force and considering pivot geometry) to the normal force.

    **Let's use a common textbook approach:** Assume the effective centrifugal force $F_c'$ at speed $\omega$ which overcomes the spring force $F_s$ directly contributes to the normal force $F_n$ on the drum.
    $F_n \approx F_c' - F_s'$
    Where $F_c'$ is the component of centrifugal force perpendicular to the spring force direction, and $F_s'$ is the spring force component in the same direction.

    **Bhandari (5th Ed.)** suggests the force with which the shoe presses the drum is $F_p = m r \omega^2 \cos \phi - F_s \cos \psi$, where $\phi$ and $\psi$ are angles related to the shoe geometry.

    **Simpler approximation for torque calculation:**
    The tangential friction force developed by one shoe is $F_f = \mu F_n$, where $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction.
    The torque transmitted by one shoe is $T_{shoe} = F_f \times r = \mu F_n \times r$.
    The total torque transmitted by the clutch is $T = Z \times T_{shoe} = Z \mu F_n r$, where $Z$ is the number of shoes.

    **Power Transmitted ($P$):**
    $P = T \omega$
    $P = Z \mu F_n r \omega$

    **Relating Normal Force to Mass and Speed:**
    A widely used formula for the normal force exerted by a shoe is derived by considering the equilibrium of forces on the shoe. For a shoe pivoted at one end, the centrifugal force $m \omega^2 r$ acts at the center of mass. Let the spring force $F_s$ be applied at a distance $l_s$ from the pivot, and the shoe mass center be at $r_{cm}$. The normal force $F_n$ is related to the radial forces.

    **From Norton (5th Ed.) and other sources:**
    For a shoe of mass $m$ with center of mass at radius $r$, let the spring force be $F_s$. The effective centrifugal force component causing the shoe to press against the drum is often approximated as $(m \omega^2 r - F_s)$, assuming the spring acts directly opposite to the centrifugal force effect.
    So, $F_n \approx m \omega^2 r - F_s$. (This is a simplification, actual geometry matters).
    Torque per shoe, $T_{shoe} = \mu F_n r = \mu (m \omega^2 r - F_s) r$.
    Total Torque, $T = Z \mu (m \omega^2 r - F_s) r$.

    **Engagement Speed Calculation Revisited:** Engagement starts when $F_c \approx F_s$.
    $m \omega_e^2 r \approx F_s$.
    This implies that for torque transmission ($T > 0$), $\omega > \omega_e$.
    $m \omega^2 r > F_s$.

    The maximum torque transmitted occurs at maximum speed, where $F_s$ is overcome by a large margin.

    **Formula for Engagement Speed ($N_e$):**
    From $m \omega_e^2 r = F_s$,
    $N_e = \frac{60}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{F_s}{mr}}$

    **Formula for Torque Capacity ($T$):**
    Let $r_{eff}$ be the effective radius of friction.
    Normal force per shoe, $F_n = m \omega^2 r_{eff} - F_s$ (where $F_s$ is the spring force at $r_{eff}$).
    Torque per shoe, $T_{shoe} = \mu F_n r_{eff} = \mu (m \omega^2 r_{eff} - F_s) r_{eff}$.
    Total Torque, $T = Z \mu (m \omega^2 r_{eff} - F_s) r_{eff}$.

    Where:
    *   $Z$: Number of shoes.
    *   $m$: Mass of each shoe.
    *   $\omega$: Angular velocity of the engine shaft (rad/s).
    *   $r_{eff}$: Effective radius of friction contact on the drum.
    *   $F_s$: Spring force at radius $r_{eff}$.
    *   $\mu$: Coefficient of friction between shoes and drum.

    **Important:** The spring force $F_s$ is not constant; it depends on the deflection. For a coil spring, $F_s = k \Delta x$, where $k$ is the spring stiffness and $\Delta x$ is the deflection. The deflection is related to the radius change.

#### **4.3. Design Steps (General Procedure)**

1.  **Determine Power and Speed Requirements:**
    *   Engine power output ($P$).
    *   Engine speed range (idle, engagement speed $N_e$, maximum speed $N_{max}$).
    *   Torque requirements of the driven load.

2.  **Select Number of Shoes ($Z$):**
    *   Typically 3 or 4 shoes for good balance and uniform torque.

3.  **Select Material for Shoes and Drum:**
    *   Shoes: Friction material (e.g., organic compounds, Kevlar) for lining, metal backing.
    *   Drum: Cast iron or hardened steel for wear resistance.

4.  **Determine Engagement Speed ($N_e$):**
    *   This is a critical parameter. It should be above engine idle speed but below the speed where the load needs to be fully engaged.

5.  **Calculate Shoe Mass ($m$):**
    *   Based on the desired engagement speed and spring force.
    *   $m = \frac{F_s}{r_e \omega_e^2}$, where $\omega_e = \frac{2 \pi N_e}{60}$.

6.  **Determine Spring Force ($F_s$):**
    *   Choose a spring whose characteristics ($k$, initial compression) provide the required $F_s$ at the engagement radius $r_e$.
    *   Springs are often designed to provide a force that increases with deflection, allowing for a smoother engagement.
    *   *Data Books (Bhandari, PSG)* can be used to select standard springs or calculate spring parameters.

7.  **Calculate Effective Friction Radius ($r_{eff}$):**
    *   This is the radius at which the shoes make contact with the drum. It depends on the shoe dimensions and mounting.

8.  **Determine Coefficient of Friction ($\mu$):**
    *   Refer to friction data for the selected shoe lining material and drum material. Typical values range from 0.2 to 0.5.

9.  **Calculate Maximum Torque Transmission Capacity:**
    *   Using the derived formula: $T_{max} = Z \mu (m \omega_{max}^2 r_{eff} - F_s) r_{eff}$.
    *   Ensure this capacity is sufficient for the driven load at the required operating speeds.

10. **Check for Heat Dissipation:**
    *   During slip, kinetic energy is converted into heat. The clutch must be able to dissipate this heat to prevent overheating and wear.
    *   The rate of heat generation is proportional to the slip speed and the torque transmitted.
    *   *Heat Generation Rate ($Q$)*: $Q = T_{slip} (\omega_{engine} - \omega_{drum})$.
    *   This is a more advanced design consideration and often involves empirical methods or specialized software. For basic design, ensuring sufficient surface area for the drum and appropriate materials is important.

11. **Design of Shoe and Drum:**
    *   **Shoes:** Shape, material, thickness of friction lining. Consider stress distribution under load.
    *   **Drum:** Diameter, thickness, material for strength and heat dissipation.

12. **Lubrication:**
    *   Some clutches may require specific lubrication for smooth operation and wear reduction.

### **5. Design Example (Illustrative)**

**Problem:** Design a centrifugal clutch for a 5 HP, 3000 RPM IC engine to drive a pump. The clutch should engage at 1500 RPM. Assume the clutch has 3 shoes. The engine shaft speed can vary from idle (1000 RPM) to maximum (3000 RPM).

**Given:**
*   Power, $P = 5$ HP $= 5 \times 746 = 3730$ W.
*   Maximum engine speed, $N_{max} = 3000$ RPM.
*   Engagement speed, $N_e = 1500$ RPM.
*   Number of shoes, $Z = 3$.
*   Engine idle speed = 1000 RPM.

**Assumptions & Selections:**
*   Coefficient of friction, $\mu = 0.3$.
*   Let's select the effective friction radius $r_{eff} = 60$ mm $= 0.06$ m.
*   Assume the shoe mass center is at $r_{eff}$.

**Calculations:**

1.  **Calculate Torque at Maximum Power:**
    Maximum torque $T_{max\_power} = \frac{P}{\omega_{max}} = \frac{3730 \text{ W}}{\frac{2 \pi \times 3000}{60} \text{ rad/s}} = \frac{3730}{314.16} \approx 11.9$ Nm.
    This is the torque the clutch must transmit at 3000 RPM.

2.  **Calculate Angular Velocity at Engagement:**
    $\omega_e = \frac{2 \pi N_e}{60} = \frac{2 \pi \times 1500}{60} = 50 \pi \approx 157.08$ rad/s.

3.  **Determine Required Shoe Mass ($m$):**
    At engagement, the centrifugal force should overcome the spring force. A common design approach is to set the centrifugal force at engagement equal to the spring force.
    $F_c = m \omega_e^2 r_{eff} = F_s$.
    Let's assume a spring force $F_s = 100$ N for initial calculation.
    $m = \frac{F_s}{r_{eff} \omega_e^2} = \frac{100}{0.06 \times (157.08)^2} = \frac{100}{0.06 \times 24674} \approx \frac{100}{1480.44} \approx 0.0675$ kg per shoe.
    Let's verify if this mass is reasonable. A shoe might weigh around 0.1-0.5 kg depending on size. This seems plausible.

4.  **Calculate Torque Capacity at Maximum Speed:**
    At $N_{max} = 3000$ RPM, $\omega_{max} = \frac{2 \pi \times 3000}{60} = 100 \pi \approx 314.16$ rad/s.
    The torque transmitted by the clutch is $T = Z \mu (m \omega^2 r_{eff} - F_s) r_{eff}$.
    $T_{3000} = 3 \times 0.3 \times (0.0675 \times (314.16)^2 \times 0.06 - 100) \times 0.06$
    $T_{3000} = 0.9 \times (0.0675 \times 98696 \times 0.06 - 100) \times 0.06$
    $T_{3000} = 0.9 \times (400.6 - 100) \times 0.06$
    $T_{3000} = 0.9 \times 300.6 \times 0.06 \approx 16.23$ Nm.

    This torque capacity (16.23 Nm) is greater than the required torque at max power (11.9 Nm), which is good.

5.  **Refine Spring Force if needed:**
    If the calculated torque was insufficient, we would need to increase shoe mass, spring force, or friction radius. For instance, if we wanted to transmit 15 Nm at 3000 RPM:
    $15 = 3 \times 0.3 \times (m \times (314.16)^2 \times 0.06 - F_s) \times 0.06$
    $15 = 0.9 \times (m \times 98696 \times 0.06 - F_s) \times 0.06$
    $15 = 0.9 \times (5921.76 m - F_s) \times 0.06$
    $15 = 0.32 \times (5921.76 m - F_s)$
    $46.875 = 5921.76 m - F_s$.

    We also have the engagement condition: $F_s = m \times 0.06 \times (157.08)^2 = 592.17 m$.
    Substitute $F_s$:
    $46.875 = 5921.76 m - 592.17 m = 5329.59 m$
    $m = \frac{46.875}{5329.59} \approx 0.0088$ kg. This is too small, indicating our initial $F_s$ choice might have been too low or the radius too small for the given parameters.

    Let's re-evaluate using a slightly larger radius $r_{eff} = 80$ mm = 0.08 m.
    Engagement condition: $F_s = m \omega_e^2 r_{eff} = m (157.08)^2 \times 0.08 = m \times 24674 \times 0.08 = 1974 m$.
    Torque at 3000 RPM: $T = Z \mu (m \omega_{max}^2 r_{eff} - F_s) r_{eff}$
    $11.9 \text{ Nm} = 3 \times 0.3 \times (m (314.16)^2 \times 0.08 - F_s) \times 0.08$
    $11.9 = 0.9 \times (m \times 98696 \times 0.08 - F_s) \times 0.08$
    $11.9 = 0.9 \times (7895.7 m - F_s) \times 0.08$
    $11.9 = 0.57 \times (7895.7 m - F_s)$
    $20.88 = 7895.7 m - F_s$.

    Substitute $F_s = 1974 m$:
    $20.88 = 7895.7 m - 1974 m = 5921.7 m$
    $m = \frac{20.88}{5921.7} \approx 0.0035$ kg. Still too small.

    This highlights the iterative nature of design and the sensitivity to parameters. The initial choice of $F_s = 100$ N with $r_{eff} = 60$ mm led to a reasonable shoe mass. Let's stick with that.

    *Revised Calculations with $r_{eff} = 60$ mm and $F_s = 100$ N:*
    *   $m = 0.0675$ kg (per shoe).
    *   $T_{max\_capacity} = 16.23$ Nm at 3000 RPM. This is sufficient.

6.  **Select Springs:**
    *   We need springs that provide a force of $F_s = 100$ N at the engagement radius $r_e = 60$ mm. The force will increase as the shoe moves outwards.
    *   Assume the spring is compressed by a certain amount at the disengaged position and stretches/compresses to provide $100$ N at $r_e$. The spring stiffness ($k$) and initial length will be determined based on the desired engagement speed and spring travel.

7.  **Drum Design:**
    *   Drum diameter $\approx 2 \times r_{eff} = 120$ mm.
    *   Material: Cast iron.
    *   Calculate required thickness based on centrifugal stress and wear considerations.

8.  **Shoe Design:**
    *   Shape of the shoe to ensure good contact and friction.
    *   Thickness of friction lining.

### **6. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** What is the primary function of a centrifugal clutch in an IC engine driven system?

**Answer:** The primary function is to automatically engage and disengage the driven load based on the engine's rotational speed, allowing the engine to start and idle without stalling and protecting the machinery from shocks.

**Question 2:** If a centrifugal clutch has 4 shoes, each weighing 0.2 kg, and the effective friction radius is 75 mm, calculate the engagement speed if the spring force at engagement is 250 N per shoe.

**Answer:**
Given:
$m = 0.2$ kg
$r_{eff} = 75$ mm $= 0.075$ m
$F_s = 250$ N
At engagement speed ($N_e$), $F_c = F_s$.
$F_c = m \omega_e^2 r_{eff}$
$250 = 0.2 \times \omega_e^2 \times 0.075$
$250 = 0.015 \omega_e^2$
$\omega_e^2 = \frac{250}{0.015} = 16666.67$
$\omega_e = \sqrt{16666.67} \approx 129.1$ rad/s
$N_e = \frac{60 \times \omega_e}{2 \pi} = \frac{60 \times 129.1}{2 \pi} \approx 1233$ RPM.

**Question 3:** A centrifugal clutch with 3 shoes is transmitting 10 Nm of torque at 2000 RPM. If the coefficient of friction is 0.35 and the effective friction radius is 50 mm, what is the normal force exerted by each shoe on the drum?

**Answer:**
Given:
$T = 10$ Nm
$N = 2000$ RPM $\implies \omega = \frac{2 \pi \times 2000}{60} \approx 209.44$ rad/s
$Z = 3$
$\mu = 0.35$
$r_{eff} = 50$ mm $= 0.05$ m

Total torque $T = Z \times (\mu F_n \times r_{eff})$
$10 = 3 \times (0.35 \times F_n \times 0.05)$
$10 = 3 \times 0.0175 \times F_n$
$10 = 0.0525 F_n$
$F_n = \frac{10}{0.0525} \approx 190.48$ N.
This is the normal force *per shoe*.

**Question 4:** What factors influence the engagement speed of a centrifugal clutch?

**Answer:** The engagement speed is primarily influenced by:
1.  **Mass of the shoes ($m$):** Heavier shoes require less speed to generate sufficient centrifugal force.
2.  **Spring stiffness and pre-tension ($F_s$):** Stiffer or higher pre-tensioned springs require more centrifugal force (higher speed) to overcome.
3.  **Effective radius ($r_{eff}$):** A larger radius means a larger centrifugal force for the same mass and speed.
4.  **Shoe geometry and pivot arrangement:** These affect how the centrifugal force is translated into a force pressing against the drum.

### **7. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Automatic Operation:** Centrifugal clutches are automatic, eliminating the need for manual engagement/disengagement.
*   **Engagement Speed is Crucial:** It determines when the clutch starts transmitting torque and must be set above engine idle speed.
*   **Torque Capacity:** Dependent on shoe mass, friction coefficient, normal force (driven by speed and spring force), and friction radius.
*   **Heat Dissipation:** Significant heat is generated during slip. The clutch design must account for this to prevent overheating.
*   **Spring Design:** The spring is a critical component for controlling engagement speed and is often a significant design challenge.
*   **Data Books are Essential:** For selecting materials, friction coefficients, standard spring properties, and component dimensions, refer to Bhandari, PSG, or other relevant data books.
*   **Simplifications in Formulas:** Many formulas used are simplified. Actual design might require more detailed analysis considering shoe dynamics and friction characteristics.
