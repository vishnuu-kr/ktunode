# DESIGN OF MACHINE ELEMENTS - Module 2: Design of IC Engine Parts: General Design Considerations

## Topic: Band Brake

---

### 1. Introduction to Band Brakes

Band brakes are a type of friction brake used to slow down or stop rotating machinery. They are particularly common in applications where a strong braking force is required, such as in hoists, winches, cranes, and automotive parking brakes. A band brake consists of a flexible metal band lined with friction material, which encircles a rotating drum or pulley. When the band is tightened, the friction between the lining and the drum generates a braking torque.

*   **Key Concept:** Friction is the fundamental principle behind the operation of a band brake. The braking torque is proportional to the coefficient of friction, the normal force applied by the band, and the radius of the drum.

---

### 2. Types of Band Brakes

Band brakes can be broadly classified into two main types based on how the band is applied:

*   **Simple Band Brake:** In a simple band brake, one end of the band is anchored to a fixed point, and the other end is connected to the actuating lever. When the lever is pulled, the band tightens around the drum.
*   **Differential Band Brake:** This type is more efficient and self-energizing. It uses two anchor points for the band, and the lever mechanism applies force to both ends of the band, creating a more uniform pressure distribution and a potentially higher braking torque for a given input force.

---

### 3. Working Principle and Force Analysis

The operation of a band brake relies on the concept of friction to convert kinetic energy into heat.

#### 3.1 Simple Band Brake

**Diagram:** (Imagine a pulley with a band wrapped around it. One end of the band is anchored to a fixed pivot, and the other end is connected to a lever.)

**Force Analysis:**

Let:
*   $T_1$: Tension in the tight side of the band
*   $T_2$: Tension in the slack side of the band
*   $\mu$: Coefficient of friction between the band lining and the drum
*   $\theta$: Angle subtended by the band on the drum (in radians)
*   $P$: Pressure exerted by the band on the drum
*   $W$: Width of the band
*   $D$: Diameter of the drum
*   $R$: Radius of the drum
*   $F$: Actuating force

The relationship between the tensions in the tight and slack sides of the band is given by the **belt friction equation**:

$T_1 / T_2 = e^{\mu\theta}$

Where:
*   $T_1$ is the tension in the tighter side (where the pulling force is applied or where the rotation is tending to pull the band tighter).
*   $T_2$ is the tension in the looser side.

The braking torque ($T_b$) is the sum of the torques due to the tensions on the drum:

$T_b = (T_1 - T_2) R$

The actuating force ($F$) is applied to a lever, and the position of the pivot relative to the point of force application determines the mechanical advantage. For example, if the force $F$ is applied at a distance 'a' from the pivot and the band is connected at a distance 'b' from the pivot, the torque applied by the lever is $F \times a$. This torque must overcome the torque required to create the tension difference $T_1 - T_2$.

**Self-Energizing (or Braking) Effect:**

In a simple band brake, if the force is applied to the end of the band that is wrapping around the drum in the direction of rotation, the friction force will further tighten the band, increasing the braking torque for the same input force. This is known as the self-energizing effect.

#### 3.2 Differential Band Brake

**Diagram:** (Imagine a pulley with a band wrapped around it. Both ends of the band are attached to levers, which are pivoted at different points and actuated by a single force.)

**Working Principle:**

In a differential band brake, the band is anchored at two points. The actuating lever is connected to both ends of the band, but at different distances from the pivot of the lever. This arrangement ensures that the tension in the band is applied more effectively, and the self-energizing effect can be controlled.

**Force Analysis:**

Let the band be wrapped around the drum over an angle $\theta$.
Let the pivot points for the two ends of the band be $O_1$ and $O_2$.
Let the actuating lever be pivoted at $O$.
The band is attached to the lever at points $A$ and $B$.

The relationship between tensions remains $T_1 / T_2 = e^{\mu\theta}$.
The braking torque is still $T_b = (T_1 - T_2) R$.

The advantage of the differential band brake lies in the lever mechanism, which can amplify the braking force. The forces applied at points $A$ and $B$ to the band are generated by the lever. By strategically placing the pivot and attachment points, a small actuating force can generate a large braking torque.

*   **Important Point:** The differential band brake is generally more efficient and requires less effort to operate compared to a simple band brake due to its ability to utilize the self-energizing effect more effectively.

### 4. Design Considerations for Band Brakes

When designing a band brake, several factors need to be considered to ensure safe and effective operation:

#### 4.1 Material Selection

*   **Band Material:** Typically made of steel (e.g., mild steel or spring steel) for flexibility and strength.
*   **Lining Material:** This is crucial for friction performance. Common lining materials include:
    *   **Asbestos:** Historically used, but due to health concerns, it's being replaced.
    *   **Organic Materials:** Composites of synthetic fibers, resins, and fillers offering good friction and wear characteristics.
    *   **Ceramic Materials:** Used in high-performance applications for excellent heat resistance.
    *   **Sintered Materials:** Metal powder composites offering superior performance at high temperatures.
*   **Drum Material:** Usually cast iron or steel, capable of withstanding friction and heat without excessive wear.

#### 4.2 Friction and Heat Dissipation

*   **Coefficient of Friction ($\mu$):** This is a critical parameter. It depends on the lining material, drum material, temperature, and presence of contaminants. Values typically range from 0.3 to 0.5.
*   **Heat Generation:** Friction converts kinetic energy into heat. The braking torque produces heat at the interface between the band lining and the drum.
*   **Heat Dissipation:** The design must ensure adequate heat dissipation to prevent overheating, which can lead to:
    *   **Fade:** Loss of braking effectiveness due to high temperatures.
    *   **Material Degradation:** Damage to the lining and drum.
    *   **Increased Wear:** Accelerated deterioration of components.
*   **Cooling:** For applications with frequent or heavy braking, provisions for cooling, such as fins on the drum or forced air cooling, might be necessary.

#### 4.3 Wear and Durability

*   **Lining Thickness:** The lining needs to be thick enough to withstand wear over the expected lifespan of the brake.
*   **Drum Surface:** The drum surface should be smooth and wear-resistant.
*   **Wear Rate:** The wear rate of the lining is influenced by pressure, temperature, and the nature of the friction surfaces.

#### 4.4 Pressure Distribution

*   **Uniformity:** Ideally, the pressure distribution between the band lining and the drum should be uniform to maximize braking torque and minimize localized wear. However, in practice, it is often non-uniform.
*   **Maximum Permissible Pressure:** The lining material has a maximum allowable pressure it can withstand. This needs to be calculated based on the applied forces and the contact area.

#### 4.5 Mechanical Strength and Rigidity

*   **Band Strength:** The band must be strong enough to withstand the tensions $T_1$ and $T_2$ without yielding or fracturing. Stress analysis should be performed.
*   **Lever and Pivot Strength:** The actuating lever and pivot points must be designed to handle the applied forces and torques.
*   **Mounting Structure:** The frame or structure to which the brake is mounted must be rigid enough to support the braking forces without significant deflection.

#### 4.6 Actuating Mechanism

*   **Force Required:** The design of the actuating lever and the point of force application determines the effort needed to operate the brake.
*   **Return Mechanism:** A spring or other mechanism is usually required to retract the band and release the brake when the actuating force is removed.

### 5. Design Procedure for Band Brakes

The design process for a band brake typically involves the following steps:

1.  **Determine Operating Requirements:**
    *   Braking torque required ($T_b$).
    *   Maximum permissible peripheral speed of the drum ($v$).
    *   Operating environment (temperature, dust, moisture).
    *   Duty cycle (frequency and duration of braking).

2.  **Select Band and Drum Materials and Lining:** Based on operating requirements and available resources. Consult material property tables and friction data.

3.  **Determine Coefficient of Friction ($\mu$) and Allowable Pressure ($P_{max}$):** Obtain these values from design handbooks or manufacturer data for the selected lining and drum materials.

4.  **Calculate Angle of Contact ($\theta$):** This is a design choice. A larger angle provides more braking torque for a given tension but requires a longer band. Typically, it ranges from $180^\circ$ to $270^\circ$.

5.  **Calculate Tensions ($T_1$ and $T_2$):**
    *   Using the braking torque formula: $T_b = (T_1 - T_2) R$.
    *   Using the belt friction equation: $T_1 / T_2 = e^{\mu\theta}$.
    *   Solve these two equations simultaneously for $T_1$ and $T_2$.

6.  **Determine Actuating Force and Lever Design:**
    *   Calculate the torque required from the actuating mechanism.
    *   Design the lever and determine the point of force application to achieve the desired mechanical advantage.

7.  **Check for Self-Energizing Effect (if applicable):**
    *   For a simple band brake, check if $e^{\mu\theta} > 1$. If so, the brake is self-energizing.
    *   Ensure that the self-energizing effect does not lead to uncontrolled braking (e.g., by limiting the angle of wrap or using a differential mechanism).

8.  **Determine Band Width ($W$):**
    *   Calculate the total braking force exerted by the band on the drum: $F_{total} = T_1 + T_2$.
    *   Alternatively, the normal force per unit width can be approximated by integrating pressure distribution. A simplified approach is to consider the average normal force.
    *   Calculate the required width based on the maximum permissible pressure: $W = F_{normal\_average} / P_{max}$. Ensure sufficient contact area.

9.  **Stress Analysis of the Band:**
    *   Calculate the maximum tensile stress in the band, which occurs in the tight side ($T_1$).
    *   Consider the bending stress if the band is significantly curved.
    *   Select an appropriate factor of safety and determine the required band thickness.

10. **Check Heat Dissipation Capacity:**
    *   Calculate the heat generated per unit time: $H = T_b \times \omega$, where $\omega$ is the angular velocity of the drum.
    *   Estimate the heat dissipated by radiation and convection. Compare the generated heat with the dissipation capacity. If inadequate, consider modifications like larger drum diameter, fins, or forced cooling.

11. **Design of Pivot and Support:**
    *   Design the pivot for the actuating lever and the anchor points of the band.
    *   Ensure these components can withstand the applied forces and provide smooth operation.

12. **Select Factor of Safety:** A factor of safety (typically 3-5 for static loads, higher for dynamic or critical applications) should be applied to account for uncertainties in material properties, loads, and operating conditions.

### 6. Examples and Applications

*   **Automotive Parking Brakes:** Many older vehicles used band brakes for parking, where continuous application is not required.
*   **Industrial Hoists and Cranes:** Used to hold heavy loads when the hoist motor is off.
*   **Winches:** For controlling the unwinding or winding of cable.
*   **Conveyor Systems:** To stop or hold conveyor belts.
*   **Bicycle Coaster Brakes:** A common example of a simple band brake integrated into the rear hub of a bicycle.

### 7. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
A band brake is used to stop a rotating drum of diameter 200 mm. The band subtends an angle of $200^\circ$ on the drum. The coefficient of friction is 0.3. The brake is actuated by a lever. Calculate the ratio of the tight side tension to the slack side tension. If the maximum tension in the band is limited to 1000 N, determine the braking torque.

**Solution 1:**
*   Diameter $D = 200$ mm, Radius $R = 100$ mm $= 0.1$ m
*   Angle of contact $\theta = 200^\circ = 200 \times (\pi/180)$ radians $\approx 3.49$ radians
*   Coefficient of friction $\mu = 0.3$
*   Maximum tension $T_1 = 1000$ N

Ratio of tensions:
$T_1 / T_2 = e^{\mu\theta} = e^{0.3 \times 3.49} = e^{1.047} \approx 2.85$

Braking Torque $T_b = (T_1 - T_2) R$
First, find $T_2$: $T_2 = T_1 / 2.85 = 1000 / 2.85 \approx 350.88$ N
$T_b = (1000 - 350.88) \times 0.1 = 649.12 \times 0.1 = 64.912$ Nm

**Question 2:**
A simple band brake is used to stop a fly wheel rotating at 120 rpm. The drum diameter is 300 mm, and the band subtends an angle of $225^\circ$. The coefficient of friction is 0.35. The maximum tension in the band is limited to 1200 N. The actuating lever has a total length of 400 mm, and the pivot is located such that the band is attached at 100 mm from the pivot on one side and the force is applied at the other end of the lever. The brake is to be self-energizing. Determine:
a) The braking torque.
b) The actuating force required.

**Solution 2:**
*   Drum diameter $D = 300$ mm, Radius $R = 150$ mm $= 0.15$ m
*   Angle of contact $\theta = 225^\circ = 225 \times (\pi/180)$ radians $\approx 3.927$ radians
*   Coefficient of friction $\mu = 0.35$
*   Maximum tension $T_1 = 1200$ N

a) Braking Torque:
Ratio of tensions: $T_1 / T_2 = e^{\mu\theta} = e^{0.35 \times 3.927} = e^{1.374} \approx 3.95$
$T_2 = T_1 / 3.95 = 1200 / 3.95 \approx 303.8$ N
Braking Torque $T_b = (T_1 - T_2) R = (1200 - 303.8) \times 0.15 = 896.2 \times 0.15 = 134.43$ Nm

b) Actuating Force:
For a simple band brake, the force $F$ is applied to the lever. Let's assume the lever mechanism is designed such that the force $F$ is applied at a distance 'a' from the pivot and the band is attached at a distance 'b' from the pivot.
The problem states the lever has a total length of 400 mm. The band is attached at 100 mm from the pivot on one side. To be self-energizing, the force should be applied on the side that tightens the band.
Let's assume the band is attached at point B, 100 mm from the pivot O. $OB = 100$ mm.
The force $F$ is applied at the other end of the lever, so let's say at point A, where $OA = 400$ mm.
The torque generated by the actuating force is $T_{actuating} = F \times OA$.
This torque must overcome the torque to create the tension difference. The torque needed to apply $T_1$ and $T_2$ to the band depends on how the lever connects to the band.

In a simple band brake, the lever applies a force to one end of the band. Let's assume the force $F$ is applied to the lever at a distance 'a' from the pivot and the lever pulls the end of the band at a distance 'b' from the pivot.
If the band is attached at 100 mm and the force is applied at 400 mm from the pivot, and the brake is self-energizing, the force must be applied in a way that increases $T_1$.
Let's assume the lever connects to the slack side of the band. The tension $T_2$ needs to be applied. Then the brake tightens.
Consider the case where the force $F$ is applied to the lever at distance $a$ from the pivot, and the band is pulled at distance $b$ from the pivot. The torque generated is $F \times a$. This torque results in the tensions.
If the band is attached at 100 mm from the pivot and the force is applied at 400 mm from the pivot, and the brake is self-energizing, the force F should be applied at the end that increases the tension on the tight side.
The torque applied by the lever to create the tensions is $(T_1 \times b) - (T_2 \times \text{lever arm for } T_2)$.
However, a more direct approach for a simple band brake often assumes the lever directly applies the tension difference. If the lever pulls the band at a distance $b$ from the pivot, the torque applied by the band tension is $(T_1 - T_2) \times b$. If the actuating force $F$ is applied at distance $a$, then $F \times a = (T_1 - T_2) \times b$.
Let's re-evaluate the setup: "the band is attached at 100 mm from the pivot on one side and the force is applied at the other end of the lever." This means the lever has the band attached at one point and the force applied at another.
Let the pivot be O. Band attached at B, OB = 100 mm. Force F applied at A, OA = 400 mm.
The torque to overcome is the one generated by the tensions. If the lever is pushing the band, it's $(T_1 \times b) + (T_2 \times \text{lever arm for } T_2)$. If it's pulling, it's $(T_1 - T_2) \times \text{effective lever arm}$.

A common configuration for a simple band brake actuated by a lever is where the lever pulls on the tight side of the band. The torque applied by the lever to create $T_1$ and $T_2$ needs to be determined.
Let's assume the actuating force $F$ is applied at distance $a$ from the pivot, and the band is pulled at distance $b$ from the pivot. The torque applied by the lever is $F \times a$. This torque is used to create the tension difference $T_1 - T_2$.
If $F$ is applied at 400 mm and the band is attached at 100 mm from the pivot.
The torque provided by the lever is $F \times 400$. This torque causes the tension $T_1$ and $T_2$.
If the lever pulls the tight side of the band at a distance $b$ from the pivot, then the torque exerted by the band is $T_1 \times b$. To hold the system, the lever must provide at least this torque against the pivot reaction.
$F \times 400 = T_1 \times 100$
$F = T_1 \times (100/400) = 1200 \times (1/4) = 300$ N.
However, this is for holding the band. We need to consider the input force to initiate the braking.

Let's assume the question implies that the lever applies a torque to create the tension difference. If the lever pulls the band at distance $b$ from the pivot, the torque is $T_1 \times b$. The actuating force $F$ is applied at distance $a$ from the pivot. The torque applied is $F \times a$.
So, $F \times a = T_1 \times b$.
Given $a = 400$ mm and $b = 100$ mm (assuming the band is attached at this distance and this is the effective lever arm for $T_1$).
$F \times 400 = 1200 \times 100$
$F = (1200 \times 100) / 400 = 300$ N.
For self-energizing, the force is usually applied in a direction that increases $T_1$.

**Revised Approach for Actuating Force:**
The lever mechanism is designed to apply a force to the band. The torque generated by this force is then transmitted to the drum.
In a simple band brake, the lever is usually pivoted such that when it pulls the band, it increases the tension $T_1$.
Let the pivot be at O. Band attached at B, OB = 100 mm. Force F applied at A, OA = 400 mm.
The force applied by the lever to the band at B creates a tension. This tension, combined with friction, creates $T_1$ and $T_2$.
If the lever pulls the band at B, the torque that needs to be overcome by the lever at point A is the torque produced by the tensions.
The torque produced by the band is $(T_1-T_2)R$.
The lever mechanism converts the input force $F$ into the required tensions.
Let's assume the lever acts on the band at distance $b$ from the pivot, and the force $F$ is applied at distance $a$ from the pivot. The net torque applied by the lever to the band should produce the tension difference $T_1 - T_2$.
The torque provided by the lever is $F \times a$. This torque needs to create the tension $T_1$. So, $F \times a = T_1 \times b$.
$F = T_1 \times (b/a) = 1200 \times (100/400) = 300$ N.
This is the force required to create tension $T_1$ if the lever is directly pulling.

For self-energizing, the force $F$ should be applied on the side that wraps in the direction of rotation.
Consider the equilibrium of the lever. The torque applied by $F$ at $a$ must overcome the torque exerted by the band at $b$.
If the force is applied at 400mm and the band is attached at 100mm, and the brake is self-energizing.
The torque generated by the force $F$ is $F \times 400$. This torque must be sufficient to create the tension $T_1$.
The torque applied to the drum is $(T_1 - T_2)R$.
Let's assume the lever applies the force to the tight side of the band. The torque exerted by the band on the lever is $T_1 \times b$, where $b$ is the effective lever arm of the band.
So, $F \times a = T_1 \times b$.
$F = T_1 \times (b/a) = 1200 \times (100/400) = 300$ N.
This calculation assumes the lever is directly responsible for creating $T_1$.

For a true self-energizing setup, the force applied by the lever at one end of the band can increase the tension at the other end due to friction.

Let's re-examine the self-energizing condition and force application. For self-energizing, the force is applied on the "trailing" side of the band.
Assume the drum rotates counter-clockwise. The band is wrapped such that $T_1$ is on the bottom and $T_2$ is on the top. The force is applied to the top end.

### 8. Key Points to Remember

*   Band brakes rely on friction to provide braking torque.
*   The belt friction equation $T_1/T_2 = e^{\mu\theta}$ is fundamental.
*   Braking torque $T_b = (T_1 - T_2)R$.
*   Self-energizing effect occurs when friction helps tighten the band, increasing braking torque.
*   Heat dissipation is crucial to prevent brake fade and material damage.
*   Lining material selection is critical for friction, wear, and temperature resistance.
*   Uniform pressure distribution and sufficient contact area are important for performance and durability.
*   Stress analysis of the band and lever mechanism is necessary for structural integrity.
*   Differential band brakes offer better efficiency and control.
