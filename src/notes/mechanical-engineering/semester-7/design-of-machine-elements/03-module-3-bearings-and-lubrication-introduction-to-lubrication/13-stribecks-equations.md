# Design of Machine Elements: Module 3 - Bearings and Lubrication: Introduction to Lubrication

## Topic: Stribeck's Equations

### 1. Introduction to Lubrication and its Importance

Lubrication is the process of reducing friction between surfaces by introducing a lubricant between them. In machine elements, proper lubrication is crucial for:

*   **Reducing Friction:** Minimizes energy loss due to sliding or rolling.
*   **Reducing Wear:** Prevents direct contact between mating surfaces, thus minimizing abrasive, adhesive, and fatigue wear.
*   **Cooling:** The lubricant acts as a heat transfer medium, dissipating heat generated by friction.
*   **Sealing:** Prevents the ingress of contaminants like dust and moisture and the egress of lubricant.
*   **Corrosion Prevention:** Protects metal surfaces from oxidation and chemical attack.
*   **Shock Absorption:** Can cushion impacts between surfaces.

**Key Concept:** The primary goal of lubrication is to maintain a thin film of lubricant between moving surfaces to prevent direct metal-to-metal contact.

### 2. Stribeck's Contribution to Lubrication Theory

Richard Stribeck, a German engineer, made significant contributions to the understanding of friction in lubricated bearings in the early 20th century. His experimental work laid the foundation for the concept of **hydrodynamic lubrication** and led to the development of **Stribeck's equation** (often referred to as the Stribeck curve when plotted).

**Context:** Before Stribeck's work, the understanding of friction in lubricated bearings was largely empirical. Stribeck's experiments aimed to systematically investigate the relationship between friction and operating conditions in journal bearings.

### 3. The Stribeck Curve

The Stribeck curve graphically represents the relationship between the **coefficient of friction ($\mu$)** and a **bearing characteristic number**, often referred to as the **Sommerfeld number (S)** or a similar dimensionless group representing operating conditions.

**Key Features of the Stribeck Curve:**

*   **High Speed / Low Load (Region A - Boundary Lubrication):** At very low speeds or very high loads, the lubricant film is thin, and there is significant metal-to-metal contact. Friction is high and is primarily due to the shear of the lubricant boundary layer and direct surface interactions.
*   **Transition Region (Region B - Mixed Lubrication):** As speed increases or load decreases, the lubricant film starts to thicken. Some direct surface contact still occurs, but it's intermittent. The coefficient of friction decreases rapidly in this region.
*   **Hydrodynamic Lubrication Region (Region C - Full Film Lubrication):** At a specific point (the minimum friction point), a stable, continuous film of lubricant completely separates the moving surfaces. Friction is at its minimum and is primarily due to the viscous shear of the lubricant itself.
*   **Viscous Friction Dominates (Region D - Thick Film/Viscous Lubrication):** At very high speeds or very low loads, the lubricant film becomes very thick. The friction is dominated by the viscous shear of the lubricant, and the coefficient of friction starts to increase again, though at a much slower rate than in Region A.

**Visual Representation (Conceptual):**

```
Coefficient of Friction (μ)
      ^
      |      /------\
      |     /        \
      |    /          \
      |   /            \
      |  /              \
      | /                \
      +---------------------> Bearing Characteristic Number (e.g., Sommerfeld Number)
      A       B       C       D
```

**Important Note:** The exact shape and the boundaries between regions can vary depending on the specific bearing design, lubricant properties, and surface roughness.

### 4. Stribeck's Equation and the Bearing Characteristic Number

Stribeck's work led to the development of an empirical equation that relates the coefficient of friction to various operating parameters. While the exact form of "Stribeck's equation" can vary in literature, it generally incorporates the following parameters:

*   **Pressure (p):** Applied load per unit projected area.
*   **Viscosity ($\eta$ or Z):** Viscosity of the lubricant at operating temperature.
*   **Speed (N or U):** Relative speed between the journal and bearing.

The bearing characteristic number, often represented by the **Sommerfeld Number (S)**, is a dimensionless group that combines these parameters and indicates the severity of the operating conditions. A common form of the Sommerfeld number for a plain journal bearing is:

$S = \frac{(D/2)^2 \omega \eta}{W/A}$

Where:
*   $D$ is the diameter of the journal.
*   $\omega$ is the angular velocity of the journal (radians/sec).
*   $\eta$ is the dynamic viscosity of the lubricant.
*   $W$ is the total radial load on the bearing.
*   $A$ is the projected area of the bearing ($A = L \times D$, where $L$ is the length of the bearing).

**Relationship to Stribeck's Equation:** Stribeck found that the coefficient of friction ($\mu$) is a function of this bearing characteristic number:

$\mu = f(S)$

Specifically, his experiments showed that friction is high for small values of S (boundary/mixed lubrication), reaches a minimum at an optimal value of S, and then increases again for larger values of S (viscous lubrication).

**Alternative Bearing Characteristic Numbers:** Other dimensionless groups similar to the Sommerfeld number are also used, such as the **Raimondi-Boyd parameter**, which is often derived from Stribeck's principles and used in bearing design charts.

### 5. Practical Implications and Design Considerations

Stribeck's work is fundamental to the design of fluid-film bearings (hydrodynamic and hydrostatic bearings). Understanding the Stribeck curve helps engineers to:

*   **Select Lubricants:** Choose a lubricant with appropriate viscosity for the operating temperature and load to ensure proper film formation.
*   **Determine Bearing Proportions:** Design bearings with suitable dimensions (diameter, length) and clearances to achieve the desired Sommerfeld number and operate in the most efficient region of the Stribeck curve.
*   **Predict Friction and Wear:** Estimate the friction and wear characteristics of a bearing under different operating conditions.
*   **Identify Critical Operating Points:** Recognize the conditions under which the bearing might transition from full film lubrication to mixed or boundary lubrication, potentially leading to increased wear.

**Linking to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO3: Develop of Sliding Contact Bearing for Industrial Applications:** Stribeck's equations and the Stribeck curve are directly applicable to understanding and designing sliding contact bearings like journal bearings and thrust bearings. They inform decisions about lubricant selection, bearing geometry, and operating conditions to ensure longevity and efficiency.
*   **Introduction to Lubrication:** The entire topic of Stribeck's equations is a cornerstone of introducing lubrication concepts, specifically hydrodynamic lubrication and the regimes of lubrication.

### 6. Key Concepts and Definitions to Remember

*   **Lubrication:** The process of reducing friction between moving surfaces using a lubricant.
*   **Friction:** The force resisting relative motion between surfaces in contact.
*   **Hydrodynamic Lubrication:** A regime where a full film of lubricant completely separates moving surfaces, generated by the relative motion of the surfaces.
*   **Boundary Lubrication:** A regime where the lubricant film is very thin, and direct metal-to-metal contact or interaction of adsorbed lubricant layers occurs. Friction is high.
*   **Mixed Lubrication:** A regime between boundary and hydrodynamic lubrication where some direct surface contact occurs, but it's intermittent.
*   **Stribeck Curve:** A plot of the coefficient of friction ($\mu$) versus a bearing characteristic number (e.g., Sommerfeld number).
*   **Sommerfeld Number (S):** A dimensionless parameter representing bearing operating conditions, typically calculated as $S = \frac{(D/2)^2 \omega \eta}{W/A}$.
*   **Viscosity ($\eta$):** The resistance of a fluid to flow.
*   **Coefficient of Friction ($\mu$):** The ratio of the friction force to the normal force.

### 7. Important Points to Remember

*   Stribeck's work was crucial in establishing the principles of hydrodynamic lubrication.
*   The Stribeck curve illustrates the transition of lubrication regimes based on operating conditions.
*   The minimum point on the Stribeck curve represents the optimal operating condition for lowest friction and wear, corresponding to full film hydrodynamic lubrication.
*   The Sommerfeld number is a key parameter used to characterize the operating conditions of a journal bearing and predict its lubrication behavior.
*   Understanding the Stribeck curve is essential for designing efficient and durable sliding contact bearings.

### 8. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:** Describe the different lubrication regimes as depicted by the Stribeck curve. What are the primary mechanisms of friction in each regime?

**Answer:**
*   **Boundary Lubrication (Region A):** High friction, significant metal-to-metal contact, friction dominated by shear of adsorbed layers and asperities.
*   **Mixed Lubrication (Region B):** Transition phase, intermittent metal-to-metal contact, friction decreases as lubricant film starts to form.
*   **Hydrodynamic Lubrication (Region C):** Full lubricant film separates surfaces, low friction dominated by viscous shear of the lubricant.
*   **Viscous Lubrication (Region D):** Thick lubricant film, friction increases due to viscous shear, but still low compared to boundary lubrication.

**Question 2:** Define the Sommerfeld number for a journal bearing and explain the significance of its value in relation to the Stribeck curve.

**Answer:** The Sommerfeld number for a journal bearing is defined as $S = \frac{(D/2)^2 \omega \eta}{W/A}$. It is a dimensionless parameter that characterizes the operating conditions. A small Sommerfeld number typically indicates boundary or mixed lubrication (high friction), while larger values indicate hydrodynamic lubrication. The minimum friction point on the Stribeck curve occurs at an optimal Sommerfeld number.

**Question 3:** A plain journal bearing has a diameter of 50 mm and a length of 50 mm. It operates at 1200 rpm with a radial load of 4 kN. The lubricant has a viscosity of 0.02 Pa.s at the operating temperature. Calculate the Sommerfeld number for this bearing.

**Solution:**
Given:
$D = 50$ mm $= 0.05$ m
$L = 50$ mm $= 0.05$ m
$N = 1200$ rpm
$W = 4$ kN $= 4000$ N
$\eta = 0.02$ Pa.s

First, convert rotational speed to angular velocity:
$\omega = \frac{2 \pi N}{60} = \frac{2 \pi \times 1200}{60} = 40\pi$ rad/s $\approx 125.66$ rad/s

Projected Area, $A = L \times D = 0.05 \times 0.05 = 0.0025$ m$^2$

Now, calculate the Sommerfeld number:
$S = \frac{(D/2)^2 \omega \eta}{W/A}$
$S = \frac{(0.05/2)^2 \times 125.66 \times 0.02}{4000 / 0.0025}$
$S = \frac{(0.025)^2 \times 125.66 \times 0.02}{1600000}$
$S = \frac{0.000625 \times 125.66 \times 0.02}{1600000}$
$S = \frac{0.00157075}{1600000}$
$S \approx 0.98 \times 10^{-9}$

**Answer:** The Sommerfeld number is approximately $0.98 \times 10^{-9}$.
*(Note: This is a very small Sommerfeld number, suggesting operation in the boundary or mixed lubrication regime, which would imply high friction and wear if this is the intended operating point.)*

**Question 4:** Which regime of lubrication, as described by the Stribeck curve, offers the lowest friction and wear? Explain why.

**Answer:** The **hydrodynamic lubrication regime (Region C)** offers the lowest friction and wear. This is because, in this regime, a continuous, full film of lubricant completely separates the mating surfaces. This prevents direct metal-to-metal contact, thereby minimizing both adhesive and abrasive wear. The friction in this regime is primarily due to the viscous shear of the lubricant film itself, which is generally much lower than friction caused by asperity interactions or boundary layer shear.
