# Mobile Robotics: Module 2 - Kinematic Model
## Topic: Dynamics of Wheeled Mobile Robots (WMR) with Slip

---

### **Introduction to Dynamics of WMR with Slip**

This topic delves into the dynamic modeling of Wheeled Mobile Robots (WMRs), specifically considering the crucial aspect of wheel slip. While kinematic models describe the motion of a robot without regard to forces and torques, dynamic models incorporate these elements to understand how forces influence motion. Slip, a deviation from pure rolling, significantly impacts a WMR's real-world behavior and is essential to consider for accurate modeling and control.

### **Key Concepts and Definitions:**

*   **Dynamics:** The study of motion considering the forces and torques that cause it.
*   **Lagrangian Dynamics:** A powerful method for deriving dynamic equations by considering the kinetic and potential energy of a system.
*   **Newton-Euler Dynamics:** Another approach to deriving dynamic equations by directly applying Newton's laws of motion and Euler's equations for rotation.
*   **Generalized Coordinates:** A minimal set of variables that completely describe the configuration of a robot (e.g., position and orientation of the robot base, and wheel angles).
*   **Generalized Velocities:** The time derivatives of the generalized coordinates.
*   **Inertia Matrix (M):** A symmetric, positive-definite matrix relating the generalized accelerations to the generalized forces/torques.
*   **Centrifugal and Coriolis Forces/Torques (C):** Terms arising from the non-linear coupling between generalized velocities.
*   **Gravitational Forces/Torques (G):** Terms due to gravity, usually relevant for robots with significant vertical motion or non-symmetric mass distribution.
*   **Generalized Forces/Torques (τ):** External forces and torques applied to the robot, including motor torques.
*   **Actuator Torques:** Torques generated by the robot's motors to drive the wheels.
*   **Wheel Slip:** Deviation from pure rolling between the wheel and the ground. This can be:
    *   **Longitudinal Slip:** Slip in the direction of wheel motion.
    *   **Lateral Slip:** Slip perpendicular to the direction of wheel motion.
*   **Pure Rolling:** The condition where the point of contact between the wheel and the ground has zero velocity relative to the ground.
*   **Contact Model:** Mathematical representation of the interaction between the wheel and the ground, crucial for modeling slip.
*   **Tire Models (e.g., Pacejka's Magic Formula):** Empirical or analytical models that describe the relationship between slip, forces, and torques at the tire-ground contact patch.
*   **Friction Forces:** Forces generated at the contact patch due to friction between the wheel and the ground. These forces are what resist slip.
*   **Stiffness and Damping:** Properties of the tire and ground that influence the slip response.

### **Deriving the Dynamic Model of a WMR with Slip**

We will primarily use the **Lagrangian formulation** for deriving the dynamic equations, as it's systematic and handles constraints well. For a WMR, we need to consider the generalized coordinates for the robot's base and the individual wheels.

**1. Generalized Coordinates:**

For a typical differential drive robot with two independently driven wheels, a minimal set of generalized coordinates could be:

*   $q_1 = x$: The robot's position in the x-direction (world frame).
*   $q_2 = y$: The robot's position in the y-direction (world frame).
*   $q_3 = \theta$: The robot's orientation (heading).
*   $q_{4,L} = \phi_L$: The angular position of the left wheel.
*   $q_{4,R} = \phi_R$: The angular position of the right wheel.

So, the generalized coordinate vector is $q = [x, y, \theta, \phi_L, \phi_R]^T$.

**2. Kinematic Constraints and Virtual Velocities:**

The ideal kinematic constraints for a WMR (without slip) relate the wheel velocities to the robot's base velocities:

*   $v_x = \dot{x} \cos(\theta) + \dot{y} \sin(\theta)$ (Forward velocity of the robot's center of motion)
*   $v_y = -\dot{x} \sin(\theta) + \dot{y} \cos(\theta)$ (Lateral velocity of the robot's center of motion)
*   $\omega = \dot{\theta}$ (Angular velocity of the robot's heading)

For a differential drive robot, the velocities of the wheel contact points are related to the robot's base velocity and wheel rotation:

*   Left wheel forward velocity: $\dot{x}_L = v_x - \omega \frac{B}{2}$ (where B is the wheel base)
*   Right wheel forward velocity: $\dot{x}_R = v_x + \omega \frac{B}{2}$

If the wheels are rolling without slip, their forward velocities are directly related to their angular velocities ($\dot{\phi}$) and radius ($r$):

*   $\dot{x}_L = r \dot{\phi}_L$
*   $\dot{x}_R = r \dot{\phi}_R$

**3. Incorporating Slip:**

Slip introduces a deviation from the pure rolling condition. We can model the wheel velocities at the contact patch as:

*   $\dot{x}_{wheel, L} = r \dot{\phi}_L - s_L$ (Longitudinal slip for left wheel)
*   $\dot{x}_{wheel, R} = r \dot{\phi}_R - s_R$ (Longitudinal slip for right wheel)

where $s_L$ and $s_R$ are the longitudinal slip velocities. Similarly, we can define lateral slip velocities. However, for wheeled mobile robots, lateral slip is often a significant factor, especially during turning or on slippery surfaces.

A more comprehensive way to model slip is to define the velocity of the wheel's center of mass relative to the ground at the point of contact. Let the wheel's forward velocity (tangential to its rotation) be $v_t$ and its lateral velocity be $v_l$. The actual velocity of the wheel center relative to the ground at the contact point is:

*   $v_{wheel\_contact} = [v_t, v_l]^T$ (in the wheel's frame)

In the robot's frame (e.g., forward and lateral to the robot's heading), these velocities are related to the robot's body velocities ($\dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{\theta}$) and wheel velocities.

For a differential drive robot, the velocities of the wheel centers in the robot's forward-lateral frame are:

*   $v_{fL} = \dot{x} - \omega \frac{B}{2}$
*   $v_{lL} = \dot{y}$
*   $v_{fR} = \dot{x} + \omega \frac{B}{2}$
*   $v_{lR} = \dot{y}$

The actual velocities of the wheel contact points with respect to the ground, in the robot's frame, are given by:

*   $\dot{x}_{contact, L} = v_{fL} - r \dot{\phi}_L$ (Longitudinal slip velocity for left wheel)
*   $\dot{y}_{contact, L} = v_{lL}$ (Lateral slip velocity for left wheel)
*   $\dot{x}_{contact, R} = v_{fR} - r \dot{\phi}_R$ (Longitudinal slip velocity for right wheel)
*   $\dot{y}_{contact, R} = v_{lR}$ (Lateral slip velocity for right wheel)

**The key insight is that for dynamics with slip, the kinematic constraints that relate wheel rotations to base motion are *modified* by slip velocities.**

**4. Kinetic Energy (T):**

The kinetic energy of the system includes the translational and rotational kinetic energy of the robot's body and the rotational kinetic energy of the wheels.

$T = \frac{1}{2} m (\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2) + \frac{1}{2} I_{body} \dot{\theta}^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_{wheel, L} \dot{\phi}_L^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_{wheel, R} \dot{\phi}_R^2$

where:
*   $m$ is the total mass of the robot.
*   $I_{body}$ is the moment of inertia of the robot's body about its vertical axis.
*   $I_{wheel, L}$ and $I_{wheel, R}$ are the moments of inertia of the left and right wheels, respectively.

**5. Potential Energy (V):**

Assuming a flat ground, the potential energy is constant and can be ignored for deriving equations of motion ($V = 0$).

**6. Lagrangian (L):**

$L = T - V = T$

**7. Euler-Lagrange Equations:**

The equations of motion are derived using the Euler-Lagrange equations:

$\frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = Q_i$

where $Q_i$ are the generalized non-conservative forces and torques. For our robot, these will include motor torques and forces due to slip.

**Let's apply this to a simplified case for understanding:**

Consider the case where the robot is moving only in its forward direction ($\dot{y}=0$, $\dot{\theta}=0$). The generalized coordinates are $x, \phi_L, \phi_R$.

*   $q = [x, \phi_L, \phi_R]^T$
*   $T = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_{wheel, L} \dot{\phi}_L^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_{wheel, R} \dot{\phi}_R^2$

Equations of motion:
*   For $x$: $\frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot{x}} \right) - \frac{\partial T}{\partial x} = Q_x$
    $\frac{d}{dt} (m \dot{x}) - 0 = Q_x$
    $m \ddot{x} = Q_x$

*   For $\phi_L$: $\frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot{\phi}_L} \right) - \frac{\partial T}{\partial \phi_L} = Q_{\phi_L}$
    $\frac{d}{dt} (I_{wheel, L} \dot{\phi}_L) - 0 = Q_{\phi_L}$
    $I_{wheel, L} \ddot{\phi}_L = Q_{\phi_L}$

Now, the crucial part: **$Q_x$ and $Q_{\phi_L}$ are not just motor torques.** They must account for the forces and torques arising from slip.

The force $Q_x$ is the sum of the driving forces exerted by the wheels on the robot's body. If there's slip, the relationship between wheel angular acceleration and robot linear acceleration is not straightforward.

The generalized forces are related to physical forces and torques through virtual work. The virtual work done by external forces and torques is $\delta W = \sum_i Q_i \delta q_i$.

For the driving wheels, the torques applied by the motors are $\tau_L$ and $\tau_R$. The virtual work done by these torques is $\delta W_{motor} = \tau_L \delta \phi_L + \tau_R \delta \phi_R$.

Now consider slip. The force at the wheel-ground contact is related to the slip velocity through a friction model. This friction force translates into forces and torques at the wheel's axle.

**Let's reconsider the wheel-ground interaction:**

*   **Pure Rolling:** Force $F_{drive}$ is transmitted, related to motor torque by $F_{drive} = \tau/r$.
*   **Slip:** The force at the contact patch is limited by friction. The actual force transmitted is a function of slip. A common model is a friction circle or a more sophisticated tire model like Pacejka's Magic Formula.

For a differential drive robot with slip, we can express the dynamics in terms of the robot's base velocities ($\dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{\theta}$) and wheel velocities ($\dot{\phi}_L, \dot{\phi}_R$).

The velocity of the robot's center is $[v_x, v_y, \omega]^T$ in its own frame.
The velocities of the wheel contact points relative to the ground, in the robot's frame, are:

$\begin{pmatrix} v_{contact,L} \\ v_{slip,L} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v_x - \omega \frac{B}{2} - r \dot{\phi}_L \\ v_y \end{pmatrix}$ (Left wheel)

$\begin{pmatrix} v_{contact,R} \\ v_{slip,R} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v_x + \omega \frac{B}{2} - r \dot{\phi}_R \\ v_y \end{pmatrix}$ (Right wheel)

where $v_{contact}$ is the velocity component in the direction of wheel rotation (rolling velocity) and $v_{slip}$ is the slip velocity component (perpendicular to rolling or relative velocity at contact).

The forces and torques generated at the contact patch are functions of these slip velocities.
Let $F_{L,f}$ and $F_{L,s}$ be the longitudinal and lateral friction forces for the left wheel, and $F_{R,f}$ and $F_{R,s}$ for the right wheel.

These forces generate net forces and torques on the robot's body.

Using Newton-Euler equations, we can directly formulate the dynamics:

$\mathbf{M} \ddot{q} + \mathbf{C}(\dot{q}) \dot{q} + \mathbf{G}(q) = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{ext}$

For a WMR, the state vector might be $q = [x, y, \theta, \phi_L, \phi_R]^T$.

**A common approach for WMR dynamics with slip is to directly model the forces and torques acting on the robot's body and wheels.**

Let the robot's body state be $(x, y, \theta)$ and its velocities be $(\dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{\theta})$.
Let the wheel states be $\phi_L, \phi_R$ and their velocities $\dot{\phi}_L, \dot{\phi}_R$.

The relationship between robot motion and wheel rotation is:

$\begin{pmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{y} \\ \dot{\theta} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v \\ \omega \end{pmatrix}$

where $v$ is the robot's forward velocity.

The velocities of the wheel contact points, in the robot's frame:
Left wheel: $[v_x - \omega \frac{B}{2}, v_y]^T$
Right wheel: $[v_x + \omega \frac{B}{2}, v_y]^T$

The velocities of the wheel contact points relative to the ground, considering slip:
Let the wheel contact velocities be denoted by $v_{c,L}$ and $v_{c,R}$ (tangential to wheel rotation).
The actual slip velocities are $s_L = v_{c,L} - r \dot{\phi}_L$ and $s_R = v_{c,R} - r \dot{\phi}_R$.

The forces and torques generated at the contact patch are functions of these slip velocities.
Let $F_{sL}$ and $F_{sR}$ be the tangential forces at the contact patch for the left and right wheels, respectively. These forces depend on the slip: $F_{sL} = F_L(s_L)$ and $F_{sR} = F_R(s_R)$.
The lateral slip forces ($F_{yL}, F_{yR}$) also play a role.

The dynamics of the robot's base can be written as:
$m \ddot{x} - m \dot{y} \dot{\theta} = F_{sL} \cos\theta - F_{sR} \sin\theta + F_{yL} \sin\theta + F_{yR} \cos\theta$
$m \ddot{y} + m \dot{x} \dot{\theta} = F_{sL} \sin\theta + F_{sR} \cos\theta - F_{yL} \cos\theta + F_{yR} \sin\theta$
$I_{body} \ddot{\theta} = \tau_{body} + (\text{moment due to } F_{sL}, F_{sR}, F_{yL}, F_{yR})$

This becomes very complex quickly. A more structured approach is to use the full generalized coordinates and Lagrangian.

**From Corke's "Robotics, Vision and Control" (Chapter 8 - Dynamics):**

Corke often presents robot dynamics in the form $M(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + C(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}} + G(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{F}$.
For wheeled robots, the relationship between generalized forces $\mathbf{F}$ and motor torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is given by the transpose of the Jacobian that maps generalized velocities to wheel velocities: $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{J}^T \boldsymbol{\tau}$.

**To handle slip, we need to modify the definition of the Jacobian and the generalized forces.**

A common simplification is to consider only **longitudinal slip** and assume **no lateral slip** (which is valid for differential drive robots if they track their intended path). In this case, the kinematic constraint between wheel velocity and robot forward velocity is modified:

Instead of $v_x = r \dot{\phi}_{avg}$, we have:
$v_x - \omega \frac{B}{2} = r \dot{\phi}_L - s_L$ (Left wheel)
$v_x + \omega \frac{B}{2} = r \dot{\phi}_R - s_R$ (Right wheel)

The slip velocities $s_L$ and $s_R$ are related to the friction forces at the contact patch. These friction forces oppose the slip.
The torque generated by the motor is $\tau$. The torque at the contact patch is related to the motor torque and the friction: $\tau_{contact} = \tau - I_{wheel} \ddot{\phi}_L$.
The friction force $F_{sL}$ is related to the contact torque by $F_{sL} = \tau_{contact} / r$.

So, $F_{sL} = (\tau_L - I_{wheel} \ddot{\phi}_L) / r$
And $s_L = (v_x - \omega \frac{B}{2}) - r \dot{\phi}_L$

The relationship between $F_{sL}$ and $s_L$ is often modeled using a tire model. For example, a simple linear model: $F_{sL} = k_s s_L$, where $k_s$ is a slip stiffness. However, tire friction is non-linear.

**Using the Lagrangian formulation with slip:**

The kinetic energy terms for wheels now depend on the slip velocity, which implicitly depends on $\dot{\phi}$ and base velocities. This makes the direct application of Lagrangian more challenging because the wheel velocities are no longer simple functions of $\dot{\phi}$ alone.

**A more practical approach often involves:**

1.  **Deriving the dynamics assuming pure rolling.** This gives a baseline model.
2.  **Modeling the slip-induced forces and torques separately.** These are added to the nominal dynamics.

**Let's consider the dynamics of the robot's base and the wheels independently and then couple them.**

**Robot Base Dynamics:**
$m \ddot{x} - m \dot{y} \dot{\theta} = \sum F_{xi}$
$m \ddot{y} + m \dot{x} \dot{\theta} = \sum F_{yi}$
$I_{body} \ddot{\theta} = \sum \tau_i$

The forces and torques on the base are the result of the forces applied by the wheels.
For the left wheel:
*   Longitudinal force: $F_{sL}$ (acts in robot's forward direction)
*   Lateral force: $F_{yL}$ (acts in robot's lateral direction)
*   Torque about the wheel's axle: $\tau_{contact,L}$ (transmits driving torque to ground)

In the robot's frame:
$F_{x,total} = (F_{sL} + F_{sR}) \cos\theta + (F_{yL} + F_{yR}) \sin\theta$
$F_{y,total} = -(F_{sL} + F_{sR}) \sin\theta + (F_{yL} + F_{yR}) \cos\theta$
$\tau_{body} = -\frac{B}{2} F_{sL} + \frac{B}{2} F_{sR} - \frac{B}{2} F_{yL} + \frac{B}{2} F_{yR}$ (This is a simplification; lateral forces also create moments.)

**Wheel Dynamics:**
For each wheel, the motor torque $\tau$ is related to its angular acceleration and the forces at the contact patch:

$I_{wheel} \ddot{\phi}_L = \tau_L - r F_{sL} - \text{other friction/damping}$

The slip velocities are defined as:
$s_L = (v_x - \omega \frac{B}{2}) - r \dot{\phi}_L$ (assuming $v_y=0$)
$s_R = (v_x + \omega \frac{B}{2}) - r \dot{\phi}_R$ (assuming $v_y=0$)

The forces $F_{sL}, F_{sR}$ are non-linear functions of $s_L, s_R$ (and $F_{yL}, F_{yR}$ would be functions of lateral slip and steering angle).

**The dynamic model can be written in matrix form:**

$\mathbf{M}_{body} \begin{pmatrix} \ddot{x} \\ \ddot{y} \\ \ddot{\theta} \end{pmatrix} + \mathbf{C}_{body}(\dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{\theta}) \begin{pmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{y} \\ \dot{\theta} \end{pmatrix} + \mathbf{G}_{body}(x, y, \theta) = \mathbf{F}_{wheel\_on\_body}$

And for the wheels:

$\mathbf{M}_{wheel} \begin{pmatrix} \ddot{\phi}_L \\ \ddot{\phi}_R \end{pmatrix} + \mathbf{C}_{wheel} \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\phi}_L \\ \dot{\phi}_R \end{pmatrix} + \mathbf{G}_{wheel} = \mathbf{F}_{motor}$

The coupling between these parts comes from:
1.  How $\mathbf{F}_{wheel\_on\_body}$ depends on wheel forces ($F_{sL}, F_{sR}, F_{yL}, F_{yR}$).
2.  How wheel forces depend on slip velocities ($s_L, s_R$).
3.  How slip velocities depend on base velocities and wheel velocities.

$\mathbf{F}_{wheel\_on\_body}$ are forces like $F_{sL}\cos\theta$, etc.
$\mathbf{F}_{motor}$ are the motor torques $\tau_L, \tau_R$.

**Example: Simplified planar dynamics with longitudinal slip only.**

Assume the robot moves on a flat plane, $\dot{y}=0$. The state is $(x, \theta, \phi_L, \phi_R)$.
$T = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_{body} \dot{\theta}^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_{wheel} (\dot{\phi}_L^2 + \dot{\phi}_R^2)$

Kinematic relationships with slip:
$v_{x,L} = \dot{x} - \dot{\theta}\frac{B}{2} = r \dot{\phi}_L - s_L$
$v_{x,R} = \dot{x} + \dot{\theta}\frac{B}{2} = r \dot{\phi}_R - s_R$

Let's express slip velocities $s_L, s_R$ in terms of accelerations. This requires knowing the forces.
Using Newton-Euler is often more direct for robots with wheels.

Consider the robot's center of mass (COM).
Translational dynamics: $m\ddot{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{F}_{total}$
Rotational dynamics: $I_{body}\ddot{\theta} = \tau_{total}$

The forces and torques applied by the wheels are:
$\mathbf{F}_{total} = \mathbf{R}(\theta) \begin{pmatrix} F_{sL} + F_{sR} \\ F_{yL} + F_{yR} \end{pmatrix}$ where $\mathbf{R}(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ is the rotation matrix.
$\tau_{total} = \frac{B}{2} (F_{sR} - F_{sL}) + \text{moments from lateral forces}$

The wheel dynamics are:
$I_{wheel} \ddot{\phi}_L = \tau_L - r F_{sL}$ (ignoring axle friction for simplicity)
$I_{wheel} \ddot{\phi}_R = \tau_R - r F_{sR}$

And the slip definitions:
$s_L = (\dot{x} - \dot{\theta}\frac{B}{2}) - r \dot{\phi}_L$
$s_R = (\dot{x} + \dot{\theta}\frac{B}{2}) - r \dot{\phi}_R$

The forces $F_{sL}, F_{sR}$ are functions of $s_L, s_R$.
e.g., using Pacejka's Magic Formula, which is complex.

**Simplified Tire Model (Bilinear):**
For small slip, $F_s = k_s s$. For large slip, $F_s$ saturates at $\mu N$, where $\mu$ is the friction coefficient and $N$ is the normal force.

A common approach in control literature is to work with the robot's forward and angular velocities ($v, \omega$) as control inputs, and relate them to wheel torques through a dynamic model that includes slip effects.

$\begin{pmatrix} \dot{v} \\ \dot{\omega} \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{A} \begin{pmatrix} v \\ \omega \end{pmatrix} + \mathbf{B} \begin{pmatrix} \tau_{eff,L} \\ \tau_{eff,R} \end{pmatrix} + \mathbf{D}_{slip} (\text{slip related terms})$

Where $\tau_{eff}$ might be effective torques after considering friction losses.

**The full dynamic model of a WMR with slip is complex and depends heavily on the chosen tire model and the robot's kinematic configuration.**

**Key Takeaway for Derivation:**
The core idea is that the standard kinematic relationships between wheel angular velocities and robot base velocities are broken by slip. The forces and torques generated at the wheel-ground contact are functions of these slip velocities and the ground's friction properties. These forces then act on the robot's body to influence its translation and rotation.

### **Tire Models and Friction:**

The relationship between slip and the forces/torques at the wheel-ground contact is crucial.

*   **Longitudinal Slip ($s$):** $s = \frac{r\dot{\phi} - v_{contact}}{v_{contact}}$ or $s = \frac{r\dot{\phi} - v_{contact}}{r\dot{\phi}}$ (definitions vary). The essential idea is the relative tangential velocity at the contact patch.
*   **Lateral Slip Angle ($\alpha$):** The angle between the direction of wheel travel and the direction the wheel is pointing. $\alpha = \arctan\left(\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right)$ in the wheel's frame.

**Common Tire Models:**

1.  **Linear Slip Model:** $F_s = k_s s$ and $F_y = k_\alpha \alpha$. Simple but only valid for very small slips.
2.  **Bilinear/Piecewise Linear Model:** Combines linear behavior for small slips with a constant maximum friction force for larger slips.
3.  **Pacejka's Magic Formula:** An empirical model that accurately captures the non-linear behavior of tires across a wide range of slip. It is expressed as:
    $F_y = D \sin(C \arctan(B s - B s \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} (1-s) \text{ if } s \ge 0)))$
    (This is a simplified representation, the actual formula is more involved and considers parameters like peak friction, stiffness, etc.)

**How slip affects dynamics:**

*   **Reduced Traction:** When slip occurs, the available friction force is often less than the maximum static friction, leading to reduced acceleration.
*   **Degraded Control:** If the robot's controller assumes pure rolling, slip can cause it to deviate from the desired path.
*   **Instability:** High slip can lead to loss of control, especially lateral slip, which can cause the robot to skid.
*   **Odometry Errors:** Wheel encoders measure wheel rotations. If there is slip, these rotations do not directly correspond to the robot's displacement, leading to odometry drift.

---

### **Methods for Dealing with Slip:**

1.  **Accurate Dynamic Modeling:** Using advanced tire models in the derivation of the robot's dynamic equations. This is computationally intensive.
2.  **Feedback Control with Slip Compensation:**
    *   **State Estimation:** Using sensors like IMUs, vision, or slip sensors to estimate the actual slip velocities.
    *   **Model-Based Control:** Incorporating the estimated slip into the control law. For example, by adjusting motor torques to counteract the effect of slip.
3.  **Friction-Aware Path Planning:** Planning paths that are less prone to causing excessive slip, considering the expected surface conditions.
4.  **Sensor Fusion:** Combining data from various sensors (encoders, IMU, GPS, vision) to get a more robust estimate of the robot's state, which can implicitly account for slip effects.
5.  **Robust Control:** Designing controllers that are inherently less sensitive to model uncertainties, including those arising from slip.

---

### **Relationship to Sensors (CO4):**

*   **Wheel Encoders:** Measure wheel angular velocity. Essential for calculating slip ($r\dot{\phi}$). However, they are affected by slip themselves (measure rotation, not necessarily displacement).
*   **Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs):** Provide acceleration and angular velocity. Can be used with kinematic models to estimate position and orientation, but drift over time.
*   **Visual Odometry/SLAM:** Estimate robot pose by tracking features in the environment. Less directly affected by wheel slip as they measure motion relative to the environment.
*   **Slip Sensors:** Specialized sensors (e.g., strain gauges on axles, optical sensors) directly measuring slip. Rare in general-purpose robots.
*   **GPS:** Provides absolute position but with limited accuracy and update rates, and is not affected by wheel slip.

**Sensor fusion is key.** For instance, fusing encoder data with IMU data allows for a more accurate estimation of velocity and orientation, and the difference between estimated and expected wheel speeds can indicate slip.

---

### **Relationship to Control (CO6):**

*   **Path Following:** Controllers that aim to follow a desired trajectory need to account for slip. A controller that only uses wheel velocities to achieve desired linear and angular velocities will perform poorly if slip is present.
*   **Torque Control vs. Velocity Control:** Direct torque control of motors can be more robust to slip than velocity control, as torque is directly related to the force generated at the contact patch.
*   **Feedback Linearization:** Techniques that linearize the robot's dynamics can be more effective if the dynamic model accurately captures slip.
*   **Adaptive Control:** Controllers that can adapt their parameters based on estimated slip or friction conditions can improve performance.

---

### **Examples:**

*   **Differential Drive Robot Turning:** When a differential drive robot turns, the outer wheel travels a longer path than the inner wheel. If the wheels are not controlled precisely, or if the surface is slippery, the wheels may slip, leading to deviations from the intended arc.
*   **Robot on a Slippery Surface:** Accelerating or braking on ice or wet surfaces will cause significant slip, dramatically reducing the forces that can be transmitted and making control difficult.
*   **Omnidirectional Robots:** Robots with mecanum or omni-wheels are particularly susceptible to slip due to their complex contact mechanics and the need for precise control of multiple wheels to achieve desired motion.

---

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Dynamics with slip is a more realistic model than pure rolling.**
*   **Slip breaks the direct kinematic link between wheel rotation and robot motion.**
*   **Tire models are essential for quantifying the relationship between slip and contact forces.**
*   **Slip leads to errors in odometry and reduced control performance.**
*   **Sensor fusion and advanced control techniques are needed to mitigate the effects of slip.**
*   **The complexity of the dynamic model increases significantly when slip is included.**

### **Practice Questions and Exercises:**

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
Explain why a dynamic model that ignores wheel slip might be insufficient for controlling a mobile robot on a slippery surface. (CO3, K4)

**Answer:**
Ignoring slip means assuming that the tangential velocity of the wheel's contact point is always equal to the robot's forward velocity component at that point ($v_{contact} = r\dot{\phi}$). On a slippery surface, the maximum friction force is reduced, and when the required driving force exceeds this limit, slip occurs. In such cases, the wheel might rotate faster than expected relative to the ground (positive slip) or slower (negative slip). This leads to:
1.  **Odometry Errors:** Wheel encoders measure rotation, not displacement. If there's slip, the robot's actual displacement will be different from what the encoders indicate.
2.  **Control Inaccuracy:** A controller that relies on precise kinematic relationships will fail to achieve the desired robot velocities. For example, trying to turn sharply might result in the robot skidding sideways instead of following the planned arc.
3.  **Reduced Traction:** The robot's ability to accelerate, brake, or steer is limited by the available friction, which is highly dependent on slip.

**Question 2 (Derivation - Simplified):**
Consider a simplified one-wheeled robot (like a unicycle) moving on a horizontal plane. Assume it has a single driven wheel of radius $r$ and moment of inertia $I_w$. The robot's body has mass $m$ and moment of inertia $I_b$ about the wheel's contact point. The wheel is driven by a motor applying torque $\tau$.

a) Write down the kinetic energy of the system.
b) If there is longitudinal slip $s$, express the relationship between the wheel's angular velocity $\dot{\phi}$ and the robot's forward velocity $v$.
c) Using the concept of generalized forces and virtual work, and assuming a longitudinal slip force $F_s$ at the contact patch, what is the equation of motion for the robot's forward velocity and the wheel's angular velocity? (Assume no other forces except those from the wheel). (CO3, K4)

**Answer:**

a) **Kinetic Energy (T):**
The system has translational kinetic energy of the robot's body and rotational kinetic energy of the wheel.
$T = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_w \dot{\phi}^2$

b) **Relationship with Slip:**
The velocity of the wheel's contact point relative to the ground is $v_{contact} = r\dot{\phi}$.
The robot's forward velocity is $v$.
Longitudinal slip $s$ can be defined as $s = \frac{r\dot{\phi} - v}{v}$ (or similar definitions). A more useful form for dynamics is to express the velocity difference:
$v_{contact} - v = r\dot{\phi} - v$
If we define slip velocity $s_v = r\dot{\phi} - v$, then the slip force $F_s$ is a function of $s_v$.
So, $v = r\dot{\phi} - s_v$.

c) **Equations of Motion (using virtual work):**
The virtual work done by the motor torque $\tau$ is $\delta W_\tau = \tau \delta \phi$.
The virtual work done by the slip force $F_s$ (acting in the direction of motion) is $\delta W_{Fs} = F_s \delta x$.
The relationship between $\delta x$ and $\delta \phi$ is $\delta x = r \delta \phi$ in the absence of slip.
However, with slip, the forces are coupled.

Let's use the generalized coordinates $x$ (robot's position) and $\phi$ (wheel angle).
Lagrangian: $L = T = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} I_w \dot{\phi}^2$

The generalized forces are:
$Q_x$: Force acting on the robot's translation. This is the slip force $F_s$.
$Q_\phi$: Torque acting on the wheel's rotation. This is the motor torque $\tau$ minus the torque due to the slip force at the contact patch ($r F_s$).
$Q_\phi = \tau - r F_s$

Equations of motion from Euler-Lagrange:
For $x$: $\frac{d}{dt} \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = Q_x$
$\frac{d}{dt}(m \dot{x}) - 0 = F_s$
$m \ddot{x} = F_s$  (Equation 1: Robot's translational dynamics)

For $\phi$: $\frac{d}{dt} \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\phi}}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \phi} = Q_\phi$
$\frac{d}{dt}(I_w \dot{\phi}) - 0 = \tau - r F_s$
$I_w \ddot{\phi} = \tau - r F_s$  (Equation 2: Wheel's rotational dynamics)

Now, we need a model for $F_s$ as a function of slip.
Slip velocity $s_v = r\dot{\phi} - \dot{x}$.
Assume a linear slip model: $F_s = k_s s_v = k_s (r\dot{\phi} - \dot{x})$.

Substitute $F_s$ into Equation 1:
$m \ddot{x} = k_s (r\dot{\phi} - \dot{x})$
$m \ddot{x} + k_s \dot{x} - k_s r \dot{\phi} = 0$

Substitute $F_s$ into Equation 2:
$I_w \ddot{\phi} = \tau - r [k_s (r\dot{\phi} - \dot{x})]$
$I_w \ddot{\phi} + k_s r^2 \dot{\phi} - k_s r \dot{x} = \tau$

These are the coupled differential equations describing the dynamics with slip. Note how the slip force couples the two equations.

**Question 3 (Application):**
A differential drive robot is commanded to move forward at 0.1 m/s. Its wheels have encoders that read a rotational speed corresponding to 0.12 m/s. What is the longitudinal slip velocity (in m/s) if the wheel radius is 0.05 m? (CO6, K3)

**Answer:**
Wheel tangential velocity due to rotation: $v_{wheel\_tangential} = r \dot{\phi} = 0.12 \, \text{m/s}$
Robot's forward velocity: $v_{robot} = 0.10 \, \text{m/s}$
Longitudinal slip velocity $s_v = v_{wheel\_tangential} - v_{robot}$
$s_v = 0.12 \, \text{m/s} - 0.10 \, \text{m/s} = 0.02 \, \text{m/s}$

This indicates that the wheel is slipping forward relative to the ground, meaning it's rotating faster than required for pure rolling at the commanded robot speed. This would result in odometry error and potential loss of traction.
