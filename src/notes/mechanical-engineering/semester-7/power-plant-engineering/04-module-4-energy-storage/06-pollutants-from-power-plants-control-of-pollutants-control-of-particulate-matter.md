# POWER PLANT ENGINEERING: Module 4 - Energy Storage

## Topic: Pollutants from Power Plants and Their Control

**Module Focus:** This module explores the critical aspect of environmental impact from power generation, focusing on the pollutants emitted by various power plants and the technologies used to control them. Understanding these aspects is crucial for sustainable and responsible energy production.

**Course Outcomes Addressed:**
*   **CO5:** Discuss the economics of power generation and pollution from power plants and their effect on the environment (Knowledge Level: K2)

---

### 1. Pollutants from Power Plants

Power plants, especially those relying on fossil fuels, are significant sources of air and water pollution. Identifying and understanding these pollutants is the first step towards effective control.

#### 1.1 Air Pollutants from Fossil Fuel Power Plants (Primarily Coal and Oil)

*   **Particulate Matter (PM):**
    *   **Definition:** Fine solid or liquid particles suspended in the air, ranging in size from sub-micron to several hundred microns.
    *   **Sources:** Incomplete combustion of fuel, ash from coal combustion (fly ash and bottom ash), and soot.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Fly Ash:** Fine particles carried out of the furnace with flue gases.
        *   **Bottom Ash:** Larger particles that fall to the bottom of the furnace.
    *   **Health Impacts:** Respiratory problems (asthma, bronchitis), cardiovascular diseases, and can carry toxic substances like heavy metals.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** Reduces visibility, can settle on vegetation, and contribute to acid rain (indirectly).
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Combustion and Ash Handling. Nag (2017) Chapter on Boiler Performance and Emissions.

*   **Sulfur Dioxide (SO2):**
    *   **Definition:** A colorless gas with a pungent odor.
    *   **Sources:** Combustion of sulfur-containing fuels, primarily coal and residual fuel oil. The sulfur content in coal varies significantly based on its origin.
    *   **Health Impacts:** Respiratory irritant, can worsen asthma and bronchitis.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** Major contributor to acid rain, which damages forests, lakes, and buildings. Also contributes to the formation of fine particulate matter.
    *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on Boiler Emissions and Control. El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Air Pollution Control.

*   **Nitrogen Oxides (NOx):**
    *   **Definition:** A group of gases, primarily nitric oxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2).
    *   **Sources:** High-temperature combustion processes where atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen react. Occurs in boilers, gas turbines, and internal combustion engines.
    *   **Health Impacts:** Respiratory irritant, can contribute to respiratory diseases.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** Precursor to smog formation (ground-level ozone) and acid rain. Contributes to eutrophication of water bodies.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Air Pollution Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on Boiler Emissions and Control.

*   **Carbon Monoxide (CO):**
    *   **Definition:** A colorless, odorless, and poisonous gas.
    *   **Sources:** Incomplete combustion of carbonaceous fuels.
    *   **Health Impacts:** Reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood, leading to suffocation.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** While not a direct greenhouse gas, it can influence atmospheric chemistry.
    *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on Combustion.

*   **Carbon Dioxide (CO2):**
    *   **Definition:** A colorless, odorless gas.
    *   **Sources:** Complete combustion of carbonaceous fuels.
    *   **Health Impacts:** Not directly toxic at atmospheric concentrations, but contributes to greenhouse effect.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** The primary greenhouse gas responsible for global warming and climate change.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Fossil Fuels and Combustion. Nag (2017) Chapter on Boiler Performance and Emissions.

*   **Trace Elements and Heavy Metals:**
    *   **Examples:** Mercury (Hg), Lead (Pb), Cadmium (Cd), Arsenic (As).
    *   **Sources:** Present as impurities in coal and fuel oil. Vaporize during combustion and can be released into the atmosphere, often attached to particulate matter.
    *   **Health Impacts:** Highly toxic, can cause neurological damage, developmental problems, and cancer.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** Bioaccumulation in food chains, contamination of soil and water.
    *   **Reference:** Nagpal & Sharma (2012) Chapter on Environmental Aspects of Power Generation.

#### 1.2 Wastewater from Steam Power Plants

*   **Sources:**
    *   **Boiler Blowdown:** Continuous or intermittent removal of concentrated dissolved solids from the boiler water to maintain water quality.
    *   **Cooling Tower Blowdown:** Removal of concentrated dissolved solids from the circulating cooling water to prevent scaling and fouling.
    *   **Condenser Cooling Water:** Can lead to thermal pollution if discharged directly without cooling.
    *   **Ash Handling Water:** Water used for sluicing ash from furnaces.
    *   **Chemical Cleaning Wastes:** Effluents from cleaning boilers and other equipment.
*   **Pollutants:**
    *   **Total Dissolved Solids (TDS):** Salts, minerals, and other dissolved substances.
    *   **Suspended Solids:** Fine particles of ash, silt, or other matter.
    *   **Heavy Metals:** Dissolved or suspended metals leached from ash or equipment.
    *   **Acids/Alkalis:** From chemical cleaning processes.
    *   **Thermal Pollution:** Elevated water temperature from cooling water discharge.
*   **Environmental Impacts:**
    *   **TDS/Suspended Solids:** Can increase turbidity in receiving waters, affecting aquatic life.
    *   **Heavy Metals:** Toxic to aquatic organisms and can enter the food chain.
    *   **Thermal Pollution:** Reduces dissolved oxygen in water, stressing aquatic ecosystems and potentially causing fish kills.
*   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Water Treatment and Pollution Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on Cooling Systems and Water Management.

#### 1.3 Pollution from Nuclear Power Plants

*   **Radioactive Waste:**
    *   **Definition:** Materials that contain radioactive elements in quantities exceeding established limits.
    *   **Sources:**
        *   **Low-Level Waste (LLW):** Contaminated protective clothing, tools, rags, filters, etc.
        *   **Intermediate-Level Waste (ILW):** Resins, chemical sludges, metal fuel cladding.
        *   **High-Level Waste (HLW):** Spent nuclear fuel, which is highly radioactive and generates significant heat.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** If not managed properly, can cause long-term contamination of soil, water, and air, posing severe health risks to living organisms.
    *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on Nuclear Power Plants. El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Nuclear Power Generation.

*   **Thermal Pollution:**
    *   **Sources:** Similar to fossil fuel plants, heat is rejected to cooling water during the process of steam condensation. Nuclear plants often require larger cooling water volumes due to lower thermal efficiency compared to some conventional plants.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** Affects aquatic ecosystems as discussed previously.
    *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on Cooling Systems and Water Management.

*   **Routine Radioactive Effluents (Airborne & Liquid):**
    *   **Sources:** Small, controlled releases of radioactive materials from plant systems (e.g., ventilation systems, liquid waste processing).
    *   **Control:** These releases are strictly monitored and kept well below regulatory limits through sophisticated filtration and containment systems.
    *   **Environmental Impacts:** Generally negligible due to stringent controls, but continuous monitoring is essential.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Nuclear Power Generation.

### 2. Control of Pollutants from Power Plants

Effective control of pollutants is vital for minimizing the environmental footprint of power generation. Various technologies are employed for this purpose.

#### 2.1 Control of Particulate Matter (PM)

The primary goal is to remove PM from flue gases before they are released into the atmosphere.

*   **Mechanical Collectors (Gravitational Settlers):**
    *   **Principle:** Uses gravity to settle larger particles.
    *   **Devices:** Dust chambers.
    *   **Efficiency:** Low for fine particles (typically < 20 microns).
    *   **Application:** Often used as a pre-cleaner before more efficient devices.
    *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on Air Pollution Control Equipment.

*   **Cyclone Separators:**
    *   **Principle:** Utilizes centrifugal force to separate particles from the gas stream. Flue gas enters tangentially, creating a vortex that throws particles to the outer wall, where they settle down.
    *   **Efficiency:** Good for particles > 10-15 microns.
    *   **Advantages:** Simple design, low cost, low maintenance.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Limited efficiency for very fine particles.
    *   **Reference:** Nagpal & Sharma (2012) Chapter on Air Pollution Control.

*   **Fabric Filters (Baghouses):**
    *   **Principle:** Flue gas passes through porous fabric bags, which physically trap the dust particles. The bags are periodically cleaned by shaking, reverse air flow, or pulse jet cleaning.
    *   **Efficiency:** Very high, can achieve > 99.9% removal efficiency, even for sub-micron particles.
    *   **Advantages:** Excellent for fine particle removal.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Higher capital and operating costs, require regular maintenance, can be damaged by high temperatures or corrosive gases.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Particulate Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on Air Pollution Control Equipment.

*   **Electrostatic Precipitators (ESPs):**
    *   **Principle:** Uses an electrostatic charge to remove particles. Particles in the flue gas are given an electrical charge, and then these charged particles are attracted to and collected on oppositely charged electrodes.
    *   **Efficiency:** High, typically 95-99% removal efficiency for a wide range of particle sizes.
    *   **Advantages:** Can handle large gas volumes, high temperatures, and high humidity; relatively low pressure drop; can achieve high efficiencies.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Sensitive to gas resistivity (which is affected by fuel composition and temperature), higher capital cost, can be affected by re-entrainment of collected dust.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Particulate Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on Air Pollution Control Equipment. Nagpal & Sharma (2012) Chapter on Air Pollution Control.

**Important Point:** The choice of PM control technology depends on factors like the particle size distribution, gas flow rate, temperature, and desired removal efficiency.

#### 2.2 Control of Sulfur Dioxide (SO2)

SO2 is primarily controlled using flue gas desulfurization (FGD) systems.

*   **Flue Gas Desulfurization (FGD):**
    *   **Definition:** A process that removes sulfur dioxide from the flue gas of fossil-fuel power plants.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Wet Scrubbing:**
            *   **Principle:** Flue gas is brought into contact with a slurry or solution of an alkaline sorbent (e.g., limestone, lime). SO2 reacts with the sorbent to form a solid byproduct (e.g., gypsum).
            *   **Common Process:** Limestone-gypsum process.
                *   **Steps:** Absorption of SO2 by limestone slurry -> Oxidation of absorbed SO2 to sulfate -> Formation of gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O).
            *   **Byproduct:** Gypsum, which can be used in construction materials.
            *   **Advantages:** High SO2 removal efficiency (up to 98-99%), mature technology, gypsum is a valuable byproduct.
            *   **Disadvantages:** Requires significant water usage, potential for wastewater discharge, operates at lower temperatures which can cause plume re-entrainment.
            *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on SO2 Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on FGD Systems.
        *   **Dry Scrubbing:**
            *   **Principle:** A dry sorbent (e.g., hydrated lime) is injected into the flue gas, reacting with SO2 to form a dry powder, which is then collected by a particulate control device (e.g., ESP or baghouse).
            *   **Advantages:** Less water required, simpler process, no wet byproducts.
            *   **Disadvantages:** Lower SO2 removal efficiency compared to wet scrubbing, requires careful sorbent injection.
            *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on FGD Systems.
        *   **Semi-Dry Scrubbing:**
            *   **Principle:** A slurry of sorbent is sprayed into the flue gas, but the water evaporates, leaving a dry powder.

*   **Low-NOx Burners and Overfire Air:**
    *   **Principle:** These techniques are primarily for NOx control but can indirectly affect SO2 emissions by altering combustion conditions. They do not remove SO2 directly.

*   **Fuel Switching/Coal Pre-treatment:**
    *   **Principle:** Using low-sulfur coal or oil. Washing coal can remove some sulfur compounds before combustion.
    *   **Effectiveness:** Reduces the amount of SO2 produced, but doesn't eliminate it.

**Important Point:** FGD is the most common and effective method for controlling SO2 emissions from large power plants.

#### 2.3 Control of Nitrogen Oxides (NOx)

NOx formation is primarily controlled by modifying combustion conditions and by post-combustion treatment.

*   **Combustion Modification:**
    *   **Low-NOx Burners (LNBs):**
        *   **Principle:** Design burners to create staged combustion zones with reduced oxygen availability in the primary combustion zone and lower peak temperatures. This minimizes the formation of thermal NOx.
        *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on NOx Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on Combustion Control.
    *   **Overfire Air (OFA):**
        *   **Principle:** Injecting secondary air above the main combustion zone to complete combustion at lower temperatures and with less excess oxygen, reducing NOx formation.
        *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on Combustion Control.
    *   **Flue Gas Recirculation (FGR):**
        *   **Principle:** Recycling a portion of the flue gas back into the combustion chamber. This dilutes the oxygen and reduces the peak combustion temperature, thereby reducing NOx formation.
        *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on NOx Control.

*   **Post-Combustion Control:**
    *   **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR):**
        *   **Principle:** Flue gas is mixed with ammonia (NH3) and passed over a catalyst bed at elevated temperatures (300-400°C). Ammonia reacts with NOx to form nitrogen (N2) and water (H2O).
        *   **Efficiency:** Very high, can remove > 90% of NOx.
        *   **Advantages:** High removal efficiency.
        *   **Disadvantages:** Requires a catalyst, ammonia storage and handling, higher capital and operating costs.
        *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on NOx Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on SCR Systems.
    *   **Selective Non-Catalytic Reduction (SNCR):**
        *   **Principle:** Ammonia or urea is injected directly into the furnace at a specific temperature window (870-1150°C). The reagents react with NOx without a catalyst.
        *   **Efficiency:** Lower than SCR (typically 30-60%).
        *   **Advantages:** Lower capital cost than SCR as no catalyst is needed.
        *   **Disadvantages:** Narrower operating temperature window, lower efficiency, potential for ammonia slip.
        *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on NOx Control.

**Important Point:** Combustion modification techniques are generally more cost-effective for initial NOx reduction, while SCR and SNCR are used for achieving very low NOx emission levels.

#### 2.4 Control of Wastewater from Steam Power Plants

The objective is to treat wastewater to remove pollutants before discharge or reuse.

*   **Boiler Blowdown Treatment:**
    *   **Methods:**
        *   **Flash Tanks:** Utilize the pressure difference to vaporize a portion of the blowdown, separating steam from the dissolved solids.
        *   **Evaporators/Crystallizers:** Further concentration of dissolved solids.
        *   **Reverse Osmosis (RO) / Ion Exchange:** Used for high-purity water requirements or for recovery of water.
    *   **Goal:** Reduce the TDS and suspended solids concentration in the discharge.

*   **Cooling Tower Blowdown Treatment:**
    *   **Methods:** Similar to boiler blowdown, but often less stringent requirements.
    *   **Chemical Treatment:** Addition of scale and corrosion inhibitors.
    *   **Blowdown Optimization:** Balancing water conservation with maintaining water quality in the cooling tower.

*   **Ash Handling Wastewater:**
    *   **Methods:**
        *   **Settling Ponds/Ash Ponds:** Allow suspended solids (ash) to settle out by gravity.
        *   **Filtration:** Mechanical filtration to remove remaining suspended solids.
        *   **Dewatering:** Thickening and dewatering of ash slurry to reduce water content.
    *   **Goal:** Remove suspended ash particles.

*   **Thermal Pollution Control:**
    *   **Methods:**
        *   **Cooling Towers:** Re-circulate cooling water and dissipate heat to the atmosphere through evaporation.
        *   **Cooling Ponds/Lakes:** Use large bodies of water for heat dissipation.
        *   **Spray Ponds:** Spraying warm water into the air to cool it.
    *   **Goal:** Reduce the temperature of discharged water.

*   **General Wastewater Treatment:**
    *   **Neutralization:** Adjusting pH if acidic or alkaline wastes are present.
    *   **Chemical Precipitation:** Adding chemicals to precipitate dissolved metals.
    *   **Filtration:** Removing precipitated solids.

*   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Water Treatment and Pollution Control. Nag (2017) Chapter on Water Management in Power Plants.

#### 2.5 Pollution from Nuclear Power Plants - Control and Management

*   **Radioactive Waste Management:**
    *   **Key Principle:** Containment, Isolation, and Decay.
    *   **Low-Level Waste (LLW):**
        *   **Treatment:** Compaction, incineration, solidification (e.g., in cement).
        *   **Disposal:** Licensed near-surface disposal facilities.
    *   **Intermediate-Level Waste (ILW):**
        *   **Treatment:** Cementation, vitrification (for some specific ILW).
        *   **Disposal:** Disposal in engineered facilities, often at greater depths than LLW.
    *   **High-Level Waste (HLW) / Spent Nuclear Fuel:**
        *   **Treatment:** Currently, the primary strategy is "store and wait" for decay and heat reduction.
        *   **Interim Storage:** On-site storage in pools of water (for cooling and shielding) or in dry cask storage systems.
        *   **Long-Term Disposal:** The internationally accepted solution is deep geological repositories. This involves encapsulating the waste and burying it deep underground in stable geological formations. This is a complex and long-term undertaking that is still under development in many countries.
    *   **Reference:** Nag (2017) Chapter on Nuclear Waste Management. El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Nuclear Fuel Cycle and Waste.

*   **Control of Routine Radioactive Effluents:**
    *   **Airborne Effluents:** Flue gases from the reactor building and other areas are passed through multiple stages of filtration (e.g., HEPA filters) and sometimes charcoal adsorbers to remove radioactive particles and isotopes like Iodine-131. Releases are monitored continuously.
    *   **Liquid Effluents:** Liquid waste is collected, treated (e.g., evaporation, ion exchange), and monitored. If the radioactivity is below permissible limits, it is discharged. Highly radioactive liquid waste is typically solidified and managed as solid waste.
    *   **Shielding and Containment:** Robust physical barriers (concrete, steel) are used to prevent the release of radioactive materials into the environment.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil (2017) Chapter on Nuclear Power Plant Safety.

*   **Thermal Pollution Control:** Nuclear plants use cooling towers, cooling ponds, or once-through cooling systems similar to fossil fuel plants to manage thermal discharge.

**Important Point:** The management of radioactive waste, especially HLW, is a significant challenge for the nuclear industry, requiring long-term planning and robust safety protocols.

### 3. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** Briefly explain the working principle of an Electrostatic Precipitator (ESP) and list its main advantages.

**Answer:**
An ESP works by imparting an electrical charge to the particulate matter in the flue gas. These charged particles are then attracted to oppositely charged collecting electrodes, where they accumulate. Periodically, the collected dust is removed from the electrodes.
**Advantages:**
*   High removal efficiency for a wide range of particle sizes.
*   Can handle large volumes of flue gas.
*   Operates at high temperatures and humidity.
*   Relatively low pressure drop.

**Question 2:** Differentiate between wet scrubbing and dry scrubbing for SO2 control.

**Answer:**
*   **Wet Scrubbing:** Involves bringing flue gas into contact with a liquid slurry or solution of an alkaline sorbent (e.g., limestone). SO2 reacts with the sorbent to form a wet byproduct, often gypsum. It typically achieves higher SO2 removal efficiency.
*   **Dry Scrubbing:** Involves injecting a dry alkaline sorbent into the flue gas, where it reacts with SO2 to form a dry powder. This powder is then collected along with fly ash by a particulate control device. It uses less water and produces a dry byproduct.

**Question 3:** What is the primary environmental concern associated with nuclear power plants, and how is it managed?

**Answer:**
The primary environmental concern is the generation of radioactive waste, particularly high-level waste (HLW) from spent nuclear fuel. This waste remains radioactive and hazardous for thousands of years.
**Management:**
*   **Low-level waste:** Treated and disposed of in licensed near-surface facilities.
*   **Intermediate-level waste:** Treated and disposed of in engineered facilities.
*   **High-level waste (spent fuel):** Currently managed through on-site storage (in pools or dry casks) awaiting the development and implementation of permanent deep geological repositories for long-term isolation. Stringent containment, shielding, and monitoring are employed at all stages.

**Question 4:** Explain two methods used for controlling NOx emissions at the combustion stage in thermal power plants.

**Answer:**
Two methods for controlling NOx emissions at the combustion stage are:
1.  **Low-NOx Burners (LNBs):** These burners are designed to stage the combustion process, creating zones with reduced oxygen and lower peak temperatures. This inhibits the formation of thermal NOx.
2.  **Overfire Air (OFA):** This involves injecting additional air into the furnace above the main combustion zone. This completes the combustion process at lower temperatures and with less excess oxygen, thereby reducing NOx formation.

**Question 5:** What is thermal pollution from steam power plants, and what are two methods to mitigate it?

**Answer:**
Thermal pollution is the degradation of water quality by any process that changes ambient water temperature. In steam power plants, this occurs when large volumes of cooling water, heated during the condensation of steam, are discharged back into natural water bodies.
**Mitigation methods:**
1.  **Cooling Towers:** These facilities dissipate the heat from the cooling water to the atmosphere through evaporation, thereby reducing the temperature of the water before it is recirculated or discharged.
2.  **Cooling Ponds/Lakes:** Large man-made or natural water bodies are used to store the warm cooling water, allowing it to cool down naturally through heat transfer with the atmosphere before being discharged.

### 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **Pollutant Identification:** Know the main pollutants from fossil fuel plants (PM, SO2, NOx, CO, CO2, heavy metals) and nuclear plants (radioactive waste, thermal pollution).
*   **Health and Environmental Impacts:** Understand the consequences of each pollutant on human health and the environment (acid rain, smog, global warming, toxicity).
*   **Control Technologies:** Be familiar with the specific control devices for each pollutant (ESPs, Baghouses for PM; FGD for SO2; LNBs, SCR, SNCR for NOx; treatment systems for wastewater; waste management protocols for nuclear).
*   **Efficiency and Trade-offs:** Recognize that different control technologies have varying efficiencies, costs, and operational considerations.
*   **Integrated Approach:** Pollution control often requires a combination of methods, including fuel selection, combustion modification, and post-combustion treatment.
*   **Nuclear Safety:** Emphasize the strict safety and containment measures for nuclear power plants, especially concerning radioactive waste.
