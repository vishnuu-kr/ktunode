# POWER PLANT ENGINEERING: Module 3 - Thermal Power Plants

## Introduction to Thermal Power Plants

**Definition:** A thermal power plant is a power generation facility that converts thermal energy (heat) into electrical energy. The primary source of heat can be fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas), nuclear fuels, or renewable sources like biomass. The heat is used to produce steam, which then drives a turbine connected to an electric generator.

**Importance:** Thermal power plants form the backbone of most electricity generation systems worldwide due to their reliability, flexibility, and ability to generate large amounts of power.

## 1. Layout and Components of a Thermal Power Plant (Steam Based)

**CO1: Explain the layout, components and working of steam, gas, and thermal power plants.** (K2)

The typical layout of a thermal power plant (especially a coal-fired one, which is very common) involves several interconnected systems.

### 1.1 Major Components and Their Functions:

**a) Fuel Handling and Preparation System:**
*   **Purpose:** To receive, store, process, and transport fuel to the boiler.
*   **Components:**
    *   **Coal Handling:** Stackers, reclaimers, crushers (primary, secondary), pulverizers (for fine grinding).
    *   **Oil Handling:** Storage tanks, pumps, heaters.
    *   **Natural Gas:** Pipelines, pressure regulators.
*   **Key Concept:** Fuel needs to be in a suitable size and form for efficient combustion. Pulverization of coal is crucial for maximizing surface area and improving burning efficiency. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 3; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 3)

**b) Boiler (Steam Generator):**
*   **Purpose:** To generate high-pressure, high-temperature steam by burning fuel.
*   **Components:**
    *   **Furnace:** Where fuel combustion takes place.
    *   **Boiler Tubes:** Water circulating through these tubes absorbs heat and converts into steam.
    *   **Superheater:** Heats the saturated steam to a higher temperature (superheated steam). This increases the enthalpy and improves turbine efficiency.
    *   **Reheater:** Reheats steam after it has passed through the initial stages of the turbine, improving overall cycle efficiency.
    *   **Economizer:** Preheats the feedwater using flue gases before it enters the boiler, improving thermal efficiency.
    *   **Air Preheater:** Heats the combustion air using flue gases, also improving thermal efficiency.
*   **Types of Boilers:** Fire-tube boilers (less common in large power plants), Water-tube boilers (standard for power plants).
*   **Key Concept:** Combustion efficiency and heat transfer efficiency are paramount. Superheating and reheating are critical for improving the performance of the Rankine cycle. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 4; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 4)

**c) Steam Turbine:**
*   **Purpose:** To extract thermal energy from the high-pressure, high-temperature steam and convert it into mechanical rotational energy.
*   **Components:**
    *   **Blades/Buckets:** Attached to the rotor, they are acted upon by the steam jet, causing rotation.
    *   **Nozzles:** Direct the high-velocity steam onto the turbine blades.
    *   **Rotor:** Shaft on which the blades are mounted.
    *   **Casing:** Encloses the rotor and blades.
*   **Types of Turbines:**
    *   **Impulse Turbine:** Steam velocity changes across the nozzle, and pressure remains constant across the blades.
    *   **Reaction Turbine:** Steam velocity changes across the nozzle, and pressure also drops across the moving blades, causing a reactive force.
    *   **Compound Turbines:** Combinations of impulse and reaction stages (e.g., Curtis, Rateau, Zoelly stages).
*   **Key Concept:** Turbine efficiency is influenced by steam conditions, blade design, and leakage. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 5; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 5)

**d) Generator:**
*   **Purpose:** To convert the mechanical energy from the turbine into electrical energy.
*   **Princ:** Electromagnetic induction. A rotating magnetic field (produced by exciting coils on the rotor) induces voltage in stationary coils (stator windings).
*   **Key Concept:** Generator efficiency is typically very high (over 98%). Synchronous generators are used to produce AC power at the required frequency.

**e) Condenser:**
*   **Purpose:** To condense the low-pressure exhaust steam from the turbine back into water. This creates a vacuum at the turbine exhaust, increasing the pressure drop across the turbine and thereby improving efficiency.
*   **Cooling Medium:** Usually water from a river, lake, or cooling tower.
*   **Types:** Surface condensers (where cooling water and steam do not mix), Jet condensers (less common in large plants).
*   **Key Concept:** Maintaining a low condenser pressure is crucial for turbine performance. The efficiency of the condenser is measured by its "cleanliness" or heat transfer rate. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 6; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 6)

**f) Cooling Tower:**
*   **Purpose:** To dissipate the heat absorbed by the cooling water in the condenser to the atmosphere. This is essential when a large supply of cooling water is not readily available.
*   **Types:** Natural draft, Mechanical draft (induced draft, forced draft).
*   **Key Concept:** Cooling towers are essential for plants located away from large bodies of water. They involve evaporative cooling.

**g) Feedwater System:**
*   **Purpose:** To pump the condensed water (feedwater) back to the boiler at high pressure.
*   **Components:** Feedwater pumps, feedwater heaters (to preheat feedwater using steam extracted from turbine stages, improving cycle efficiency).
*   **Key Concept:** Water purity is critical to prevent scaling and corrosion in the boiler tubes. Deaerators are used to remove dissolved gases (oxygen and carbon dioxide) from the feedwater. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 7; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 7)

**h) Ash Handling System:**
*   **Purpose:** To remove fly ash and bottom ash produced during coal combustion.
*   **Methods:** Mechanical (bucket elevators, screw conveyors), pneumatic (using air pressure), hydraulic (using water).
*   **Key Concept:** Proper disposal of ash is an environmental consideration.

**i) Flue Gas Treatment System:**
*   **Purpose:** To remove pollutants from the flue gases before they are released into the atmosphere.
*   **Components:**
    *   **Electrostatic Precipitators (ESPs):** Remove fly ash particles.
    *   **Scrubbers:** Remove sulfur dioxide (SO2) through chemical reactions (e.g., lime/limestone scrubbers).
    *   **Low-NOx Burners:** Reduce the formation of nitrogen oxides (NOx) during combustion.
    *   **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR) / Selective Non-Catalytic Reduction (SNCR):** Reduce NOx emissions.
*   **Key Concept:** Environmental regulations mandate stringent control of emissions. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 12; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 12)

## 2. Working Principle of a Thermal Power Plant (Steam Based)

**CO1: Explain the layout, components and working of steam, gas, and thermal power plants.** (K2)

The operation of a thermal power plant follows a thermodynamic cycle, primarily the Rankine cycle.

### 2.1 The Rankine Cycle:

**Definition:** The Rankine cycle is a thermodynamic cycle that describes the operation of steam-based power plants. It consists of four main processes.

**Process 1-2: Isentropic Compression (in Pump):**
*   Feedwater is pumped from a low pressure to a high pressure.
*   This process is assumed to be isentropic (reversible adiabatic) for simplicity.
*   Work input is required from the pump ($W_{pump}$).

**Process 2-3: Isobaric Heat Addition (in Boiler):**
*   High-pressure water is heated in the boiler, first to saturation temperature, then evaporated into saturated steam.
*   Further heating occurs in the superheater to produce superheated steam.
*   Heat is added at constant pressure ($Q_{in}$).

**Process 3-4: Isentropic Expansion (in Turbine):**
*   High-pressure, high-temperature steam expands through the turbine, doing work.
*   This process is assumed to be isentropic.
*   Work output is produced by the turbine ($W_{turbine}$).

**Process 4-1: Isobaric Heat Rejection (in Condenser):**
*   Low-pressure steam from the turbine exhaust is condensed back into liquid water by rejecting heat to the cooling medium.
*   This occurs at constant pressure.
*   Heat is rejected to the surroundings ($Q_{out}$).

### 2.2 Cycle Efficiency:

**Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$):** The ratio of the net work output to the heat input.
$$ \eta_{th} = \frac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}} = \frac{W_{turbine} - W_{pump}}{Q_{in}} $$
Also, $Q_{in}$ is the heat added in the boiler, and $Q_{out}$ is the heat rejected in the condenser.
$$ W_{net} = Q_{in} - Q_{out} $$
$$ \eta_{th} = \frac{Q_{in} - Q_{out}}{Q_{in}} = 1 - \frac{Q_{out}}{Q_{in}} $$

**Key Concept:** The Rankine cycle's efficiency can be improved by increasing the average temperature of heat addition (higher boiler pressure and superheat temperature) and decreasing the average temperature of heat rejection (lower condenser pressure, which is limited by cooling water temperature).

## 3. Performance Parameters of Simple and Modified Rankine Cycles

**CO2: Calculate the performance parameters of simple and modified Rankine cycles.** (K3)

### 3.1 Simple Rankine Cycle Parameters:

*   **Net Work Output ($W_{net}$):**
    $$ W_{net} = W_{turbine} - W_{pump} $$
    $$ W_{turbine} = h_3 - h_4 $$
    $$ W_{pump} = h_2 - h_1 $$
    Where $h$ denotes specific enthalpy at different states.
*   **Heat Input ($Q_{in}$):**
    $$ Q_{in} = h_3 - h_2 $$
*   **Heat Rejected ($Q_{out}$):**
    $$ Q_{out} = h_4 - h_1 $$
*   **Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$):**
    $$ \eta_{th} = \frac{h_3 - h_4 - (h_2 - h_1)}{h_3 - h_2} $$
*   **Specific Steam Consumption (SSC):** Mass of steam required per unit of net work output.
    $$ SSC = \frac{1}{W_{net}} \text{ (kJ/kJ or kg/kWh if W is in MJ and mass flow rate is in kg/s)} $$
    Often expressed as kg/kWh, which is $3600 / W_{net}$ (where $W_{net}$ is in kJ/kg).
*   **Back Work Ratio (BWR):** Ratio of pump work to turbine work.
    $$ BWR = \frac{W_{pump}}{W_{turbine}} $$
    For steam power plants, BWR is typically very small (0.01-0.02), meaning pump work is negligible compared to turbine work.

### 3.2 Modifications to the Rankine Cycle for Improved Efficiency:

**a) Reheating:**
*   **Description:** Steam is expanded in the high-pressure (HP) turbine, then sent back to the boiler for reheating, and then expanded in the low-pressure (LP) turbine.
*   **Benefits:**
    *   Increases net work output because the steam expands to a lower pressure in the second turbine stage.
    *   Reduces the moisture content in the exhaust steam, protecting the LP turbine blades from erosion.
    *   Increases the average temperature of heat addition.
*   **Cycle States:**
    *   1-2: Isentropic compression (pump)
    *   2-3: Isobaric heat addition (boiler)
    *   3-4: Isentropic expansion (HP turbine)
    *   4-5: Isobaric reheating (reheater)
    *   5-6: Isentropic expansion (LP turbine)
    *   6-1: Isobaric heat rejection (condenser)
*   **Calculations:**
    $$ W_{turbine} = (h_3 - h_4) + (h_5 - h_6) $$
    $$ W_{pump} = h_2 - h_1 $$
    $$ Q_{in} = (h_3 - h_2) + (h_5 - h_4) $$
    $$ \eta_{th} = \frac{(h_3 - h_4) + (h_5 - h_6) - (h_2 - h_1)}{(h_3 - h_2) + (h_5 - h_4)} $$
*   **Key Concept:** Reheating is a crucial modification for modern high-power thermal plants. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 9; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 9)

**b) Regeneration (Feedwater Heating):**
*   **Description:** Steam is extracted from certain stages of the turbine and used to heat the feedwater in a series of feedwater heaters.
*   **Benefits:**
    *   Increases the average temperature of heat addition by preheating the feedwater.
    *   Reduces the amount of heat to be added in the boiler for the same amount of steam.
    *   Increases thermal efficiency.
*   **Cycle States:**
    *   1-2: Isentropic compression (pump)
    *   2-3: Isobaric heat addition (boiler)
    *   3-4: Isentropic expansion (turbine)
    *   4-x: Extraction of steam at state x
    *   x-y: Expansion to condenser pressure
    *   Feedwater heating: Condensate (state 2) is heated by extracted steam in a series of heaters (e.g., open and closed heaters).
*   **Types of Feedwater Heaters:**
    *   **Closed Feedwater Heaters:** Condensate flows on one side, extracted steam on the other. No direct mixing.
    *   **Open Feedwater Heaters:** Condensate and extracted steam mix directly, preheating the condensate. Requires an additional pump for condensate returning from closed heaters.
*   **Calculations:** Requires balancing mass and energy for each feedwater heater. If 'y' is the fraction of steam extracted at a certain point, the mass flow rate through subsequent turbine stages and the boiler changes.
*   **Key Concept:** Regeneration is an effective method for improving efficiency, especially in large plants with multiple extraction points. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 8; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 8)

**c) Combined Reheating and Regeneration:**
*   Most modern power plants utilize both reheating and regeneration to maximize efficiency.

## 4. Performance Parameters of Steam Turbines and Steam Nozzles

**CO3: Calculate the performance parameters of steam turbines and steam nozzles.** (K3)

### 4.1 Steam Turbine Performance:

**a) Isentropic Efficiency ($\eta_t$):**
*   **Definition:** The ratio of the actual work output of the turbine to the ideal work output (assuming isentropic expansion).
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \eta_t = \frac{\text{Actual Work Output}}{\text{Isentropic Work Output}} = \frac{W_{actual}}{W_{ideal}} = \frac{h_3 - h_{4a}}{h_3 - h_{4s}} $$
    Where:
    *   $h_3$: Enthalpy at turbine inlet.
    *   $h_{4a}$: Actual enthalpy at turbine exhaust (determined from Mollier chart or steam tables using outlet pressure and quality/temperature).
    *   $h_{4s}$: Isentropic enthalpy at turbine exhaust (determined from Mollier chart or steam tables using outlet pressure and assuming isentropic expansion from inlet conditions).
*   **Key Concept:** Turbine efficiency accounts for irreversibilities like friction and leakage.

**b) Stage Efficiency:**
*   Turbines are divided into stages. Each stage has its own efficiency (e.g., isentropic efficiency, adiabatic efficiency).

**c) Volumetric Flow Rate:** The volume of steam passing through the turbine. Increases significantly from HP to LP stages due to decreasing steam density.

**d) Steam Velocity:** Velocity of steam through the turbine stages.

**e) Blade Speed:** Rotational speed of the turbine rotor blades.

**f) Impulse vs. Reaction Turbines:**
*   **Impulse Turbine:** $\eta_{t} = \frac{2 C_s C_b \cos\alpha}{(C_s \cos\alpha)^2 + (C_b)^2}$ (simplified for one stage)
*   **Reaction Turbine:** $\eta_{t} = \frac{2 C_s C_b \cos\alpha}{C_s^2 + C_b^2 (1 + \cos\alpha)}$ (simplified for one stage)
    *   $C_s$: Steam jet velocity.
    *   $C_b$: Blade velocity.
    *   $\alpha$: Blade inlet angle.
*   **Degree of Reaction:** Ratio of enthalpy drop in the moving blades to the total enthalpy drop in the stage.

### 4.2 Steam Nozzle Performance:

**a) Nozzle Function:** To convert the thermal energy (enthalpy) of steam into kinetic energy (velocity) by expanding it.

**b) Isentropic Nozzle Efficiency ($\eta_n$):**
*   **Definition:** The ratio of the actual kinetic energy leaving the nozzle to the ideal kinetic energy (assuming isentropic expansion).
*   **Formula:**
    $$ \eta_n = \frac{\text{Actual Kinetic Energy}}{\text{Isentropic Kinetic Energy}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} V_{a}^2}{\frac{1}{2} V_{s}^2} = \frac{V_{a}^2}{V_{s}^2} $$
    Where:
    *   $V_a$: Actual velocity of steam leaving the nozzle.
    *   $V_s$: Isentropic velocity of steam leaving the nozzle.
    *   Using energy balance: $V_s = \sqrt{2(h_{in} - h_{out,s})}$ and $V_a = \sqrt{2\eta_n(h_{in} - h_{out,s})} = \sqrt{2(h_{in} - h_{out,a})}$
    $$ \eta_n = \frac{h_{in} - h_{out,a}}{h_{in} - h_{out,s}} $$
    Where $h_{in}$ is inlet enthalpy and $h_{out,a}$ and $h_{out,s}$ are actual and isentropic outlet enthalpies, respectively.
*   **Key Concept:** Nozzle efficiency accounts for friction and other losses within the nozzle.

**c) Velocity Coefficient ($C_v$):**
*   **Definition:** The ratio of actual velocity to isentropic velocity.
*   **Formula:** $C_v = V_a / V_s$
*   **Relationship:** $\eta_n = C_v^2$

**d) Critical Pressure Ratio:**
*   For a convergent-divergent nozzle expanding steam, there's a critical pressure ratio (ratio of pressure at the throat to inlet pressure) below which the flow at the throat is sonic ($M=1$). This ratio depends on the specific heat ratio ($\gamma$) of the steam:
    $$ \left(\frac{P^*}{P_0}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} $$
*   **Key Concept:** This ratio determines the nozzle's design and its ability to achieve supersonic velocities. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapter 5; El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 5)

## 5. Energy Storage Systems in Power Plants

**CO4: Explain the working of various energy storage systems.** (K2)

While not a primary component of a thermal power plant itself, energy storage systems (ESS) are increasingly integrated to improve grid stability, manage intermittency of renewables, and enhance the economics of power generation.

### 5.1 Types of Energy Storage Systems:

**a) Pumped Hydro Storage (PHS):**
*   **Working:** Uses two reservoirs at different elevations. During periods of low electricity demand (or surplus renewable generation), water is pumped from the lower reservoir to the upper reservoir. During periods of high demand, water is released from the upper reservoir through turbines to generate electricity.
*   **Advantages:** Mature technology, large capacity, long lifespan.
*   **Disadvantages:** Requires specific geographical conditions (topography).

**b) Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS):**
*   **Working:** Stores electrical energy electrochemically in batteries (e.g., Lithium-ion, flow batteries). Electrical energy is converted to chemical energy during charging and back to electrical energy during discharging.
*   **Advantages:** Fast response, modular, can be deployed at various scales.
*   **Disadvantages:** Limited lifespan (cycle life), cost, resource availability for some battery chemistries.

**c) Compressed Air Energy Storage (CAES):**
*   **Working:** Air is compressed and stored underground (e.g., in caverns, aquifers, or depleted gas fields) or in above-ground vessels during periods of low electricity demand. When electricity is needed, the compressed air is released, heated (often with natural gas or by storing heat from compression), and expanded through a turbine to generate electricity.
*   **Advantages:** Large scale, long duration storage.
*   **Disadvantages:** Requires suitable geological formations, efficiency can be affected by heat losses and supplementary fuel.

**d) Thermal Energy Storage (TES):**
*   **Working:** Stores thermal energy by heating or cooling a storage medium (e.g., water, molten salt, phase change materials). This stored heat can be used later to generate steam for a power plant or for direct heating applications.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Molten Salt Storage:** Used in Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) plants.
    *   **Sensible Heat Storage:** Storing heat by raising the temperature of a substance.
    *   **Latent Heat Storage:** Storing heat through phase change (e.g., solid to liquid).
*   **Relevance to Thermal Power Plants:** Can be integrated with solar thermal power plants or used to store waste heat from industrial processes or even from the thermal power plant itself for later use.

**e) Flywheel Energy Storage:**
*   **Working:** Stores rotational kinetic energy. An electric motor accelerates a rotor to high speeds, storing energy. When power is needed, the motor acts as a generator, decelerating the rotor and producing electricity.
*   **Advantages:** Very fast response, high power capability.
*   **Disadvantages:** Limited energy capacity, high self-discharge rate.

**Key Concept:** Energy storage is crucial for grid modernization, enabling better integration of renewable energy sources and improving overall grid reliability and flexibility. (Ref: El Wakil, 2017, Chapter 14; Nag, 2017, Chapter 14 - potentially discussed in broader context of power systems)

## 6. Economics of Power Generation and Pollution from Thermal Power Plants

**CO5: Discuss the economics of power generation and pollution from power plants and their effect on the environment.** (K2)

### 6.1 Economics of Power Generation:

**a) Cost Components:**
*   **Capital Costs (Fixed Costs):**
    *   **Construction Costs:** Land, buildings, equipment (boilers, turbines, generators, etc.), civil works.
    *   **Engineering & Design Costs:**
    *   **Financing Costs:** Interest during construction.
*   **Operating and Maintenance (O&M) Costs (Variable & Fixed):**
    *   **Fuel Costs:** The most significant variable cost for thermal power plants (coal, gas, oil).
    *   **Labor Costs:** Operators, maintenance staff.
    *   **Maintenance & Repairs:** Routine checks, replacement parts.
    *   **Water & Chemical Costs:** For boiler feed, cooling, and water treatment.
    *   **Ash Disposal Costs:**
    *   **Emission Control Costs:** Operation of scrubbers, ESPs, etc.
    *   **Taxes & Insurance:**

**b) Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE):**
*   **Definition:** The average net present cost of electricity generation for a power plant over its lifetime. It accounts for all costs (capital, O&M, fuel, financing) and energy produced.
*   **Formula (Simplified):**
    $$ \text{LCOE} = \frac{\text{Sum of Costs over Lifetime} (NPV)}{\text{Sum of Electricity Produced over Lifetime} (NPV)} $$
*   **Key Concept:** LCOE is a key metric for comparing the economic viability of different power generation technologies.

**c) Factors Affecting Economics:**
*   **Fuel Price Volatility:** Significant impact on operating costs for fossil fuel plants.
*   **Plant Capacity Factor:** The ratio of actual energy produced to the maximum possible energy that could have been produced if the plant ran at full capacity continuously. Higher capacity factor leads to lower LCOE.
*   **Plant Efficiency:** More efficient plants consume less fuel for the same power output, reducing operating costs.
*   **Interest Rates:** Affect capital costs and financing.
*   **Environmental Regulations:** Costs associated with pollution control can be substantial.
*   **Technology Advancement:** Newer technologies can offer higher efficiencies and lower costs.

### 6.2 Pollution from Thermal Power Plants and Environmental Effects:

**a) Major Pollutants and Their Sources:**

*   **Sulfur Dioxide ($SO_2$):**
    *   **Source:** Combustion of fuels containing sulfur (especially coal and fuel oil).
    *   **Environmental Effects:** Acid rain (damages forests, lakes, buildings), respiratory problems, formation of particulate matter.
    *   **Control:** Flue Gas Desulfurization (FGD) using scrubbers.

*   **Nitrogen Oxides ($NO_x$):**
    *   **Source:** High-temperature combustion in the boiler, where nitrogen and oxygen in the air react.
    *   **Environmental Effects:** Smog formation (ground-level ozone), acid rain, respiratory problems, formation of particulate matter.
    *   **Control:** Low-NOx burners, Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR), Selective Non-Catalytic Reduction (SNCR).

*   **Particulate Matter (PM):**
    *   **Source:** Incomplete combustion, ash particles carried with flue gas.
    *   **Environmental Effects:** Respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, reduced visibility, deposition on surfaces.
    *   **Control:** Electrostatic Precipitators (ESPs), Baghouses (fabric filters).

*   **Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$):**
    *   **Source:** Complete combustion of fossil fuels.
    *   **Environmental Effects:** **Greenhouse gas**, contributing to global warming and climate change.
    *   **Control:** Improving plant efficiency, Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) technologies, transitioning to low-carbon or renewable energy sources.

*   **Carbon Monoxide (CO):**
    *   **Source:** Incomplete combustion.
    *   **Environmental Effects:** Toxic gas, reduces oxygen-carrying capacity of blood.
    *   **Control:** Ensuring complete combustion through proper boiler design and operation.

*   **Mercury (Hg):**
    *   **Source:** Trace amounts in coal.
    *   **Environmental Effects:** Neurotoxin, bioaccumulates in food chains, particularly in fish.
    *   **Control:** Activated carbon injection, specific scrubbers.

**b) Water Pollution:**
*   **Thermal Pollution:** Discharge of heated cooling water into rivers or lakes can decrease dissolved oxygen and harm aquatic life.
*   **Chemical Pollution:** Discharge of treated wastewater containing chemicals used in the plant.

**c) Ash Disposal:**
*   **Source:** Bottom ash and fly ash from coal combustion.
*   **Environmental Effects:** Land use for ash ponds, potential for groundwater contamination if not properly managed.

**d) Mitigation Strategies:**
*   **Advanced Emission Control Technologies:** ESPs, scrubbers, SCR/SNCR.
*   **Efficient Combustion:** Optimizing boiler operation to reduce pollutant formation.
*   **Fuel Switching:** Using lower-sulfur fuels or natural gas.
*   **Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS):** Emerging technology to mitigate $CO_2$ emissions.
*   **Sustainable Ash Management:** Recycling ash for construction materials, secure disposal in lined ash ponds.
*   **Water Conservation:** Using closed-loop cooling systems, efficient water treatment.

**Key Concept:** Balancing the need for reliable and affordable electricity with the imperative to protect the environment is a major challenge for thermal power plants. (Ref: Nag, 2017, Chapters 12 & 13; El Wakil, 2017, Chapters 12 & 13; Nagpal & Sharma, 2012; Gupta, 2012)

a) The enthalpy at each state point.
b) The net work output per kg of steam.
c) The thermal efficiency of the cycle.
d) The specific steam consumption.

**Answer 1:**

*   State 3 (Steam inlet to turbine): Superheated steam at 3 MPa, 400°C. $h_3 \approx 3230.9 \text{ kJ/kg}$

A more accurate value would be around 2100-2200 kJ/kg for wet steam. Let's use $h_{4s} = 2150 \text{ kJ/kg}$ for this calculation.

a) Enthalpies: $h_1 \approx 191.8 \text{ kJ/kg}$, $h_2 \approx 194.8 \text{ kJ/kg}$, $h_3 \approx 3230.9 \text{ kJ/kg}$, $h_{4s} \approx 2150 \text{ kJ/kg}$ (Note: actual $h_4$ would be higher due to wetness, affecting real efficiency).

b) Pump work: $w_p = h_2 - h_1 \approx 194.8 - 191.8 = 3.0 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
   Turbine work (ideal): $w_t = h_3 - h_{4s} \approx 3230.9 - 2150 = 1080.9 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
   Net work: $w_{net} = w_t - w_p \approx 1080.9 - 3.0 = 1077.9 \text{ kJ/kg}$.

c) Heat input: $q_{in} = h_3 - h_2 \approx 3230.9 - 194.8 = 3036.1 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
   Thermal efficiency: $\eta_{th} = \frac{w_{net}}{q_{in}} \approx \frac{1077.9}{3036.1} \times 100\% \approx 35.5\%$.

d) Specific Steam Consumption: $SSC = \frac{3600}{w_{net}} \approx \frac{3600}{1077.9} \approx 3.34 \text{ kg/kWh}$.

**Question 2 (CO2 - Reheating):**
Consider the previous Rankine cycle (Question 1). If the steam is reheated to 400°C after expanding in the HP turbine to 400 kPa, and then expanded in the LP turbine to 10 kPa. Assume ideal reheat cycle.
Calculate:
a) The enthalpy at all state points.
b) The net work output per kg of steam.
c) The thermal efficiency of the reheat cycle.

**Answer 2:**
*(Requires further steam table lookups)*
*   State 1, 2, 3 as before.
*   State 4s (Expansion from 3 MPa, 400°C to 400 kPa): From Mollier chart/tables, assuming $s_3 = s_{4s}$, $h_{4s} \approx 2830 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
*   State 5 (Reheated steam at 400 kPa, 400°C): $h_5 \approx 3345 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
*   State 6s (Expansion from 5 to 10 kPa): From Mollier chart/tables, assuming $s_5 = s_{6s}$, $h_{6s} \approx 2230 \text{ kJ/kg}$.

a) Enthalpies: $h_1 \approx 191.8$, $h_2 \approx 194.8$, $h_3 \approx 3230.9$, $h_{4s} \approx 2830$, $h_5 \approx 3345$, $h_{6s} \approx 2230 \text{ kJ/kg}$.

b) Turbine work: $w_t = (h_3 - h_{4s}) + (h_5 - h_{6s}) \approx (3230.9 - 2830) + (3345 - 2230) = 400.9 + 1115 = 1515.9 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
   Pump work: $w_p \approx 3.0 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
   Net work: $w_{net} = w_t - w_p \approx 1515.9 - 3.0 = 1512.9 \text{ kJ/kg}$.

c) Heat input: $q_{in} = (h_3 - h_2) + (h_5 - h_{4s}) \approx (3230.9 - 194.8) + (3345 - 2830) = 3036.1 + 515 = 3551.1 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
   Thermal efficiency: $\eta_{th} = \frac{w_{net}}{q_{in}} \approx \frac{1512.9}{3551.1} \times 100\% \approx 42.6\%$.

**Question 3 (CO3 - Nozzle Efficiency):**
Steam enters a nozzle at 3 MPa and 400°C with negligible velocity. The steam expands to 10 kPa. If the actual exit velocity is 800 m/s, calculate the nozzle efficiency.

**Answer 3:**
*   Inlet enthalpy ($h_{in}$) = $h_3$ from Question 1 = 3230.9 kJ/kg.
*   Isentropic exit enthalpy ($h_{out,s}$) = $h_{4s}$ from Question 1 = 2150 kJ/kg (for ideal expansion to 10 kPa).
*   Ideal kinetic energy per kg: $\frac{1}{2} V_s^2 = h_{in} - h_{out,s} = 3230.9 - 2150 = 1080.9 \text{ kJ/kg} = 1080900 \text{ J/kg}$.
*   Ideal exit velocity: $V_s = \sqrt{2 \times 1080900} \approx 1470.2 \text{ m/s}$.
*   Actual kinetic energy per kg: $\frac{1}{2} V_a^2 = \frac{1}{2} (800 \text{ m/s})^2 = 320000 \text{ J/kg} = 320 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
*   Nozzle efficiency: $\eta_n = \frac{\text{Actual KE}}{\text{Ideal KE}} = \frac{320 \text{ kJ/kg}}{1080.9 \text{ kJ/kg}} \times 100\% \approx 29.6\%$.
    *(Alternative calculation using actual enthalpy drop)*:
    $\frac{1}{2} V_a^2 = h_{in} - h_{out,a}$
    $h_{out,a} = h_{in} - \frac{1}{2} V_a^2 = 3230.9 - 320 = 2910.9 \text{ kJ/kg}$.
    $\eta_n = \frac{h_{in} - h_{out,a}}{h_{in} - h_{out,s}} = \frac{3230.9 - 2910.9}{3230.9 - 2150} = \frac{320}{1080.9} \times 100\% \approx 29.6\%$.

**Question 4 (CO5 - Pollution Control):**
Name two primary methods used to reduce $SO_2$ emissions from a coal-fired power plant and briefly explain how they work.

**Answer 4:**
1.  **Flue Gas Desulfurization (FGD) / Scrubbers:**
    *   **Explanation:** Flue gases are passed through a "scrubber" where they come into contact with an absorbent material, typically a slurry of lime (calcium oxide) or limestone (calcium carbonate). The sulfur dioxide reacts chemically with the absorbent to form calcium sulfite ($CaSO_3$), which is then often oxidized to calcium sulfate ($CaSO_4$, gypsum). This removes $SO_2$ from the flue gas before it's released to the atmosphere.
2.  **Fuel Switching:**
    *   **Explanation:** Using coal with a lower sulfur content or switching to fuels with no or very low sulfur content, such as natural gas, can significantly reduce $SO_2$ emissions at the source.

## Important Points to Remember:

*   **Rankine Cycle:** The fundamental cycle for steam power plants. Efficiency improvements come from increasing boiler pressure/temperature and decreasing condenser pressure.
*   **Superheating & Reheating:** Crucial for improving turbine efficiency and reducing moisture in LP stages.
*   **Regeneration:** Preheating feedwater using extracted steam improves thermal efficiency by increasing the average temperature of heat addition.
*   **Turbine & Nozzle Efficiency:** Account for irreversible losses and are critical for actual power output. Use Mollier charts or steam tables for calculations.
*   **Condenser Pressure:** Must be kept as low as possible, limited by cooling water temperature and air leakage.
*   **Environmental Controls:** Essential for complying with regulations and minimizing the impact of power plants. $SO_2$, $NO_x$, and $CO_2$ are major concerns.
*   **Economics:** Fuel cost is a significant operating expense. LCOE is the standard metric for comparing plant economics.
