# ACOUSTICS AND NOISE CONTROL - Module 2: Point Source, Spherical Source, Line Source, Monopole, Dipole

## Topic: Near-and Far-Field Spectrum

### 1. Introduction to Sound Fields

Sound generated by a source propagates outwards, creating different acoustic environments around it. These environments are broadly categorized as the **near-field** and the **far-field**. The characteristics of the sound wave, particularly its pressure and particle velocity, vary significantly between these two regions. Understanding the distinction between near-field and far-field is crucial for analyzing sound propagation from various source types (point, spherical, line sources) and for designing effective noise control strategies.

**Key Concept:** The behavior of acoustic waves is governed by their distance from the source.

*   **Ver & Beranek - Noise and Vibration Control Engineering:** Chapters on sound sources and propagation.

### 2. Defining the Near-Field and Far-Field

The transition from near-field to far-field is not a sharp boundary but a gradual change in the characteristics of the acoustic field. It's often defined in terms of the **acoustic wavelength ($\lambda$)** of the sound being emitted.

#### 2.1. Near-Field

*   **Definition:** The region close to the sound source where the acoustic pressure and particle velocity are strongly coupled and vary significantly with distance from the source. In the near-field, both pressure and velocity fluctuations are substantial and can be out of phase.
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Complex Pressure-Velocity Relationship:** The relationship between acoustic pressure ($p$) and particle velocity ($u$) is not simply $p = \rho c u$ (the impedance of free space). Other impedance terms become significant.
    *   **Reactive Component:** The acoustic field contains a significant **reactive** component, characterized by stored energy (potential energy in the pressure field and kinetic energy in the particle velocity field). This reactive component diminishes as you move away from the source.
    *   **Strong Distance Dependence:** Pressure and velocity often decrease much faster than $1/r$ (where $r$ is the distance from the source).
    *   **Phase Differences:** Significant phase differences exist between pressure and velocity.
    *   **Dominant Effect:** Reactive forces are dominant in this region.

#### 2.2. Far-Field (or Radiation Field)

*   **Definition:** The region far from the sound source where the acoustic field is dominated by **propagating waves**. The relationship between pressure and particle velocity approaches the characteristic impedance of the medium ($\rho c$).
*   **Characteristics:**
    *   **Purely Real Impedance:** The acoustic impedance ($Z = p/u$) is primarily real and equal to the characteristic impedance of the medium ($\rho c$).
    *   **Radiated Energy:** The field is dominated by **active** (propagating) energy, which radiates outwards.
    *   **$1/r$ Decay:** For a simple point source (monopole), the acoustic pressure amplitude decays as $1/r$, and the sound intensity decays as $1/r^2$.
    *   **In-Phase Pressure and Velocity:** Pressure and particle velocity are in phase.
    *   **Dominant Effect:** Radiation pressure is dominant in this region.

#### 2.3. Transition Region

*   **Definition:** The region between the near-field and the far-field.
*   **Characteristics:** A gradual transition where the reactive components of the field decrease, and the amplitude decay approaches the $1/r$ characteristic of the far-field.

**Important Point to Remember:** The distinction between near-field and far-field is crucial for understanding how sound energy is transmitted and how it interacts with the environment.

---

### 3. Defining the Boundaries (Rules of Thumb)

There's no single, absolute boundary, but several commonly used criteria to define the transition from near-field to far-field are based on the source dimension ($D$) and the acoustic wavelength ($\lambda$).

*   **Criterion 1 (Distance based on wavelength):**
    *   **Far-field typically starts at:** $r > \lambda$
    *   **Near-field typically extends up to:** $r < \lambda$
    *   *This is a very general rule and often insufficient on its own.*

*   **Criterion 2 (Distance based on source dimension and wavelength):** This is a more robust criterion that considers the size of the source.
    *   **Far-field starts at:** $r > D$ and $r > \lambda$
    *   **Near-field includes:** $r \le D$ and $r \le \lambda$
    *   A commonly used approximation for the onset of the far-field is:
        $r > \frac{D^2}{\lambda}$
        or equivalently,
        $r > \frac{2 \pi D^2}{\lambda}$ (This accounts for the phase variations across the source surface)

    *   **Where:**
        *   $r$: Distance from the center of the source
        *   $D$: Characteristic dimension of the source (e.g., diameter of a spherical source, length of a line source)
        *   $\lambda$: Wavelength of the sound ($\lambda = c/f$, where $c$ is the speed of sound and $f$ is the frequency)

**Example:** Consider a loudspeaker with a diameter of 0.3 meters emitting a sound at 100 Hz in air (speed of sound $c \approx 343$ m/s).
*   $\lambda = c/f = 343 / 100 = 3.43$ meters.
*   $D = 0.3$ meters.
*   The transition to the far-field is approximately at $r > D^2/\lambda = (0.3)^2 / 3.43 \approx 0.09 / 3.43 \approx 0.026$ meters.
    *   This suggests that even at very close distances (less than 3 cm), the field is still complex.
*   If we use $r > \lambda$, the far-field starts at $r > 3.43$ meters. This is a much larger distance, highlighting the importance of considering the source dimension.

**Key Concept:** The ratio of the source dimension to the wavelength ($D/\lambda$) is a crucial parameter in determining the near-field and far-field characteristics.

*   **Low Frequency ($f$ is low, $\lambda$ is large, $D/\lambda$ is small):** The source often behaves as if it's in the far-field even at relatively close distances. The source dimension is much smaller than the wavelength.
*   **High Frequency ($f$ is high, $\lambda$ is small, $D/\lambda$ is large):** The near-field extends to much larger distances relative to the source dimension.

### 4. Near-and Far-Field Spectra of Different Source Types

The spectral characteristics (how the sound's energy is distributed across different frequencies) differ in the near-field and far-field.

#### 4.1. Point Source (Monopole)

A monopole is an idealized source that radiates sound uniformly in all directions, pulsating radially. It represents a volume change.

*   **Ideal Monopole:** Emits sound of a single frequency.
*   **Real-world examples:** A small pulsating sphere, a source of pure volume variation.

**Near-Field Spectrum (Monopole):**
*   **Dominant Components:** Contains both a real (radiation) component and an imaginary (reactive) component.
*   **Pressure:**
    *   $p(r, t) \approx \frac{i \omega Q_0}{4 \pi} \left( \frac{e^{-ikr}}{r} + \frac{e^{-ikr}}{-ikr} \right)$
    *   **Where:**
        *   $Q_0$: Source strength (volume velocity)
        *   $\omega = 2\pi f$: Angular frequency
        *   $k = \omega/c$: Wave number
        *   $r$: Distance from the source
        *   $i$: Imaginary unit
    *   The term $\frac{e^{-ikr}}{r}$ is the **radiation component** (far-field).
    *   The term $\frac{e^{-ikr}}{-ikr}$ is the **reactive component** (near-field), which decays faster with distance ($1/r^2$).
*   **Particle Velocity:**
    *   $u(r, t) \approx \frac{Q_0}{4 \pi r^2} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{ikr} \right) e^{-ikr}$
    *   The term $\frac{Q_0}{4 \pi r^2}$ is the **radiation component**.
    *   The term $\frac{Q_0}{4 \pi r^2} \frac{1}{ikr}$ is the **reactive component**, which decays faster with distance ($1/r^3$).
*   **Acoustic Impedance ($Z = p/u$):**
    *   $Z \approx \rho c \left( \frac{ikr}{1+ikr} \right) = \rho c \left( \frac{1}{1 - i/(kr)} \right)$
    *   At large distances ($kr \gg 1$, i.e., far-field): $Z \approx \rho c$ (purely real).
    *   At short distances ($kr \ll 1$, i.e., near-field): $Z \approx \rho c (ikr) = i \omega \rho \frac{r}{1} = i \omega m$ (purely imaginary, like mass reactance).

**Far-Field Spectrum (Monopole):**
*   **Dominant Component:** Only the radiation component remains significant.
*   **Pressure:** $p(r, t) \approx \frac{i \omega Q_0}{4 \pi r} e^{-ikr}$
*   **Particle Velocity:** $u(r, t) \approx \frac{Q_0}{4 \pi r^2} e^{-ikr}$ (This approximation is usually made for far-field, but technically it's $u \approx \frac{p}{\rho c}$)
*   **Sound Intensity:** The intensity decays as $1/r^2$.
*   **Spectrum:** The spectrum of the sound radiated is directly proportional to the spectrum of the source strength ($Q_0$). For a broadband source, the sound pressure level at a given frequency will follow the spectral distribution of $Q_0$ (adjusted for $1/r$ decay).

**Key Takeaway for Monopole:** The near-field is dominated by a reactive component that decays rapidly with distance ($1/r^2$ for pressure), while the far-field is dominated by the propagating wave that decays as $1/r$.

#### 4.2. Dipole

A dipole is formed by two monopoles of equal strength but opposite phase, separated by a small distance. It represents a force acting on the medium, causing oscillations.

*   **Ideal Dipole:** Two monopoles separated by distance $d$, with strengths $+Q_0$ and $-Q_0$.
*   **Real-world examples:** A vibrating rigid sphere, a reciprocating piston, a loudspeaker cone vibrating back and forth.

**Near-Field Spectrum (Dipole):**
*   **Pressure:**
    *   $p(r, t) \approx \frac{i \omega Q_0 d \cos \theta}{2 \pi c} \left( \frac{1}{r} + \frac{1}{ikr} - \frac{1}{(ikr)^2} \right) e^{-ikr}$
    *   **Where:**
        *   $\theta$: Angle between the dipole axis and the direction of observation.
        *   The terms $\frac{1}{r}$ and $\frac{1}{ikr}$ are from the individual monopoles.
        *   The term $-\frac{1}{(ikr)^2}$ is the **dipole radiation term**, which decays as $1/r^3$.
*   **Particle Velocity:**
    *   $u(r, t) \approx \frac{Q_0 d \cos \theta}{2 \pi} \left( \frac{1}{r^3} + \frac{1}{ikr^2} - \frac{1}{(ikr)^2} \right) e^{-ikr}$
    *   The term $\frac{1}{r^3}$ is the dominant near-field component decaying as $1/r^3$.
*   **Acoustic Impedance ($Z = p/u$):**
    *   At large distances ($kr \gg 1$): $Z \approx \rho c$ (purely real).
    *   At short distances ($kr \ll 1$): $Z \approx \rho c (ikr)^2 = -\omega^2 \rho \frac{r^2}{c}$ (purely imaginary, like stiffness reactance). This is more complex due to the distance dependence.

**Far-Field Spectrum (Dipole):**
*   **Dominant Component:** The term proportional to $1/r$ in the pressure equation becomes dominant.
*   **Pressure:** $p(r, t) \approx \frac{i \omega Q_0 d \cos \theta}{2 \pi c r} e^{-ikr}$
*   **Radiation Pattern:** The sound pressure is proportional to $\cos \theta$. It's maximum along the dipole axis (where $\theta=0$) and zero perpendicular to it (where $\theta=90^\circ$). This is known as a **figure-of-eight** directivity pattern.
*   **Sound Intensity:** The intensity decays as $1/r^2$.
*   **Spectrum:** The spectrum of radiated sound from a dipole source is proportional to the source's velocity multiplied by the frequency ($f$ or $\omega$). This means that at higher frequencies, a dipole source will generally radiate more sound power. For example, if a loudspeaker cone vibrates with a constant velocity, the radiated sound power is proportional to $f^2$.

**Key Takeaway for Dipole:** The near-field has components decaying as $1/r^2$ and $1/r^3$, while the far-field decays as $1/r$ and exhibits a $\cos \theta$ directivity pattern. The radiated spectrum from a dipole typically increases with frequency.

#### 4.3. Line Source

A line source is an extended source where sound is emitted along a line.

*   **Ideal Line Source:** Infinite length, emitting sound uniformly along its length.
*   **Real-world examples:** A long vibrating beam, a row of loudspeakers, airflow noise from a slot.

**Near-Field and Far-Field (Line Source):**
The analysis of line sources is more complex than point sources due to their extended nature. The concept of distance from a single point is replaced by the distance from the axis of the line source.

*   **Cylindrical Waves:** For an infinite line source, the waves are cylindrical.
*   **Pressure:**
    *   **Near-Field:** Contains terms that decay faster than $1/r$ and are more complex than simple monopole or dipole behavior.
    *   **Far-Field:** For distances $r$ much larger than the wavelength and the source length, the sound field approaches that of a point source (for end-fire radiation) or a specific cylindrical wave pattern.
*   **Acoustic Intensity:** The intensity typically decays as $1/r$ for an infinite line source, unlike the $1/r^2$ decay for a point source. This means sound energy spreads over a larger area as it propagates outwards.
*   **Spectrum:** The spectral content depends on how the source strength is distributed along the line and how it varies with frequency. For a uniformly vibrating line, the radiated sound power is generally proportional to frequency for low frequencies and approaches a constant for higher frequencies, but this can be very source-dependent.

**Key Takeaway for Line Source:** The sound field is more complex, with cylindrical wave propagation. The intensity decay is typically $1/r$, and the spectral content depends heavily on the source details.

*   **Ekinsler, Frey, Coppens, Sanders - Fundamentals of Acoustics:** Chapters on acoustic radiation from extended sources.
*   **Ver & Beranek - Noise and Vibration Control Engineering:** Chapters on sound sources and their characteristics.

### 5. Practical Implications and Measurements

Understanding the near-field and far-field is crucial for:

1.  **Noise Source Identification:**
    *   **Near-field measurements:** Can be used to pinpoint the exact locations of noise generation on machinery. However, these measurements are highly sensitive to the measurement position and the specific source characteristics (monopole vs. dipole).
    *   **Far-field measurements:** Provide a more general assessment of the overall sound power radiated by a source, independent of precise location and close-up effects.

2.  **Noise Control Design:**
    *   **Near-field treatments:** Materials placed very close to the source can be effective in absorbing or reflecting the reactive energy in the near-field.
    *   **Far-field treatments:** Barriers, silencers, and enclosures are designed to reduce sound propagation in the far-field.

3.  **Sound Power Determination:** Sound power is typically measured or calculated from far-field sound pressure levels to represent the total sound energy emitted by a source, independent of the environment.

**Example Scenario:**
Imagine measuring the noise from a fan.

*   **Near the fan blades:** You might find strong localized noise sources, perhaps with a dipole-like behavior due to the air movement. The acoustic pressure and velocity might be complex.
*   **Several meters away from the fan:** The sound will be more uniformly distributed (though perhaps with directional lobes depending on the fan design), and the pressure will decay roughly as $1/r$. This is the far-field.

**Relevant Reference Books:**
*   **Crocker - Handbook of Noise and Vibration Control:** Sections on noise measurement techniques and source characterization.
*   **Barron - Industrial Noise Control and Acoustics:** Discusses practical applications of acoustic principles in industry, including source measurement.

### 6. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
Define the near-field and far-field regions of a sound source. What are the key differences in terms of acoustic characteristics?

**Answer 1:**
*   **Near-field:** The region close to the source where acoustic pressure and particle velocity are strongly coupled, exhibiting significant phase differences and containing substantial reactive energy. The amplitude decay is faster than $1/r$.
*   **Far-field:** The region far from the source dominated by propagating waves. Pressure and particle velocity are in phase and related by the characteristic impedance of the medium ($\rho c$). The acoustic pressure amplitude decays as $1/r$.

**Question 2:**
For a monopole (point source), how does the acoustic pressure amplitude typically decay with distance ($r$) in the near-field and far-field?

**Answer 2:**
*   **Near-field:** The pressure contains components that decay as $1/r^2$ (reactive component) and the radiation component ($1/r$). The $1/r^2$ term dominates at very close distances.
*   **Far-field:** The pressure decays predominantly as $1/r$.

**Question 3:**
What is the typical directivity pattern for a dipole source in the far-field? How does the radiated sound power relate to frequency for a dipole source with constant velocity?

**Answer 3:**
*   The far-field directivity pattern for a dipole source is a **figure-of-eight**, meaning sound pressure is maximum along the dipole axis and zero perpendicular to it.
*   For a dipole source vibrating with constant velocity, the radiated sound power is proportional to the **square of the frequency** ($f^2$ or $\omega^2$).

**Question 4:**
A loudspeaker has a diameter of 0.2 meters. What is the approximate distance at which the far-field behavior begins for a sound frequency of 500 Hz in air (c = 343 m/s)?

**Answer 4:**
*   $D = 0.2$ m
*   $f = 500$ Hz
*   $c = 343$ m/s
*   $\lambda = c/f = 343 / 500 = 0.686$ m
*   Approximate far-field onset: $r > D^2/\lambda = (0.2)^2 / 0.686 = 0.04 / 0.686 \approx 0.058$ meters or 5.8 cm.
*   Also, $r > \lambda$ means $r > 0.686$ meters. The more conservative criterion is $r > \max(D, \lambda, D^2/\lambda)$ for a general approximation, or often just $r > D^2/\lambda$ is used. So, approximately 5.8 cm is the rough transition.

**Question 5:**
Explain why measuring noise in the near-field might be useful for identifying specific noise sources on a piece of equipment.

**Answer 5:**
Measuring in the near-field allows for detailed localization of noise generation. The strong dependence of pressure and velocity on position and the presence of reactive components can help distinguish between different types of noise sources (e.g., a small vibrating surface versus a pulsating volume) by examining their specific spatial and spectral characteristics. This detail can be invaluable for targeted noise control interventions.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Near-field vs. Far-field:** Defined by distance relative to the source dimension ($D$) and acoustic wavelength ($\lambda$).
*   **Key Transition:** Often approximated by $r \approx D^2/\lambda$ or $r \approx \lambda$.
*   **Near-field Characteristics:** High pressure/velocity coupling, presence of reactive components, rapid amplitude decay.
*   **Far-field Characteristics:** Propagating waves, pressure/velocity in phase, real impedance ($\rho c$), $1/r$ pressure decay for point sources.
*   **Source Types:**
    *   **Monopole:** Uniform radiation, $1/r$ pressure decay in far-field.
    *   **Dipole:** Directional radiation ($\cos \theta$ in far-field), radiated power $\propto f^2$ for constant velocity.
    *   **Line Source:** Cylindrical waves, $1/r$ intensity decay for infinite sources.
*   **Practical Use:** Crucial for noise source identification, measurement strategies, and the design of noise control treatments.
