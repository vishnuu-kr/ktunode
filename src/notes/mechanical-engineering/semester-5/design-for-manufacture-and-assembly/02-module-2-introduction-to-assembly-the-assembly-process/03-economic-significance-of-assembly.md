# Design for Manufacture and Assembly (DFMA) - Module 2: Introduction to Assembly

## Topic: Economic Significance of Assembly

This module focuses on understanding the fundamental principles of assembly and its crucial role in product development. This particular topic delves into why efficient and well-designed assembly processes are economically vital for any manufacturing enterprise.

---

### Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this topic, you will be able to:

*   Understand the impact of assembly on overall product cost.
*   Identify key factors that contribute to assembly costs.
*   Appreciate how design choices influence assembly efficiency and cost.
*   Recognize the economic benefits of implementing DFMA principles during the assembly stage.
*   Evaluate the trade-offs between manufacturing and assembly costs.

---

### Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Assembly:** The process of joining individual components or sub-assemblies to form a complete product. This can involve various methods like fastening, welding, gluing, or interference fitting.
*   **Assembly Cost:** The total cost associated with bringing together individual parts to create a final product. This includes labor, tooling, equipment, overhead, and quality control.
*   **Manual Assembly:** Assembly performed by human operators.
*   **Automated Assembly:** Assembly performed by machines and robots.
*   **Design for Assembly (DFA):** A set of principles and guidelines that aim to simplify product assembly, reduce the number of parts, and minimize assembly time and cost.
*   **Cost Driver:** Any factor that directly influences the cost of a product or process. In assembly, cost drivers include part complexity, tolerance requirements, assembly method, and labor rates.
*   **Product Lifecycle Cost:** The sum of all costs associated with a product from its conception through design, manufacturing, distribution, use, service, and disposal. Assembly costs are a significant portion of this.

### Economic Significance of Assembly

Assembly is often a significant contributor to the overall cost of a product, sometimes even exceeding the cost of manufacturing the individual components. Efficient assembly design and processes are therefore paramount for achieving cost-effectiveness and market competitiveness.

#### 1. Impact of Assembly on Overall Product Cost

*   **Labor Costs:** Manual assembly is heavily reliant on labor. Complex assembly sequences or the need for highly skilled operators can drastically increase labor expenses.
    *   *Example:* Assembling a complex electronic device with numerous small components and intricate wiring will require more skilled labor and time than assembling a simple mechanical toy.
*   **Time Costs:** The time taken for assembly directly translates to cost. Longer assembly times mean more labor hours, higher equipment utilization costs, and potentially slower production cycles.
*   **Tooling and Equipment Costs:** Specialized tools, jigs, fixtures, and automated assembly equipment are often required. The cost of acquiring, maintaining, and operating this equipment can be substantial.
*   **Quality and Rework Costs:** Poorly designed assemblies can lead to errors, defects, and the need for rework or scrap. These costs can be very high, impacting both profitability and customer satisfaction.
    *   *Example:* Parts that are difficult to orient correctly during assembly can lead to incorrect installations, requiring disassembly and reassembly, or worse, functional defects.
*   **Inventory and Material Handling:** The number of parts to be assembled and the logistics of moving them to the assembly station contribute to costs. Reducing part count and simplifying handling can yield significant savings.

#### 2. Key Factors Contributing to Assembly Costs

*   **Number of Parts:** A fundamental principle of DFA is to minimize the number of parts. Each part adds to the assembly process: it needs to be handled, oriented, and joined.
    *   **Boothroyd, Dewhurst, & Knight (2010):** Emphasize that for manual assembly, the cost of handling and inserting a part can be more significant than the cost of manufacturing the part itself. They propose a metric called the "assembly difficulty" which is inversely related to the ease with which a part can be handled and inserted.
*   **Part Handling:** The ease with which a part can be gripped, fed, and manipulated by an operator or robot. Parts that are small, lightweight, flexible, or have irregular shapes can be difficult and time-consuming to handle.
*   **Part Orientation:** The required orientation of a part for insertion. If a part needs to be inserted in a specific orientation, it adds complexity and time to the assembly process. Parts designed for "nesting" or self-orienting capabilities significantly reduce assembly time.
    *   **Molloy, Tilley, & Warman (1998):** Highlight that parts requiring precise orientation often necessitate more complex fixturing and skilled labor.
*   **Insertion:** The ease with which a part can be inserted into its mating component. Obstructions, tight tolerances, and the need for force application can complicate insertion.
*   **Fastening Methods:** The type of fastener and the method used for joining parts have a significant impact.
    *   **Screws and Nuts:** Can be time-consuming to handle and drive, especially if multiple fasteners are used.
    *   **Snap Fits:** Can significantly reduce assembly time and eliminate the need for separate fasteners.
    *   **Adhesives:** Can provide strong joints but may require curing time and precise application.
*   **Assembly Operations:** The complexity and number of assembly operations required. Minimizing the number of distinct assembly steps is crucial.
*   **Assembly System:** Whether assembly is manual, semi-automated, or fully automated influences the cost structure. While automation has high upfront costs, it can lead to lower per-unit costs for high-volume production.
    *   **Boothroyd (2005):** Discusses the economic justification for automation, highlighting that it becomes more viable as production volume increases.
*   **Quality Control:** The effort and resources required to ensure the product is assembled correctly and meets quality standards.

#### 3. Design Choices and Their Impact on Assembly

*   **Simplification:** Reducing the number of parts by integrating functions into fewer components.
    *   *Example:* Designing a bracket with built-in features for alignment or mounting, rather than a separate bracket and multiple fasteners.
*   **Standardization:** Using standard components and fasteners can reduce variety, simplify inventory, and leverage established handling and assembly techniques.
*   **Modularity:** Designing the product in modules that can be assembled independently and then integrated. This can facilitate parallel assembly and simplify troubleshooting.
*   **Clearances and Tolerances:** Designing with appropriate clearances allows for easier insertion of parts and reduces the likelihood of jamming or interference.
    *   **Chitale & Gupta (2011):** Advise designing for generous clearances where possible to accommodate manufacturing variations and simplify assembly.
*   **Chamfers and Lead-ins:** Adding chamfers to mating features helps guide parts into place, making insertion easier and faster.
    *   *Example:* Chamfering the edge of a shaft that needs to be inserted into a bore.
*   **Symmetry:** Designing parts that are symmetrical about at least one axis can reduce the need for precise orientation during assembly.
*   **Self-Locating Features:** Incorporating features that naturally guide parts into their correct positions during assembly.

#### 4. Economic Benefits of Implementing DFMA in Assembly

*   **Reduced Manufacturing Cost:** By simplifying assembly, the overall cost of production is lowered.
*   **Increased Productivity:** Faster assembly times lead to higher output with the same resources.
*   **Improved Quality:** Simpler, well-designed assemblies are less prone to errors, reducing defects and rework.
*   **Lower Labor Costs:** Reduced assembly time and complexity can lead to lower labor expenses, especially in manual assembly.
*   **Reduced Tooling and Equipment Costs:** Simpler assemblies may require less specialized tooling or simpler automation.
*   **Faster Time-to-Market:** Streamlined assembly processes can contribute to quicker product development cycles.
*   **Enhanced Competitiveness:** Lower production costs and higher quality can provide a significant competitive advantage.

#### 5. Trade-offs Between Manufacturing and Assembly Costs

*   **Complexity vs. Part Count:** Sometimes, integrating functions into a single part (reducing part count) can make that part more complex to manufacture, potentially increasing its manufacturing cost. A careful balance needs to be struck.
    *   **Dieter (2000):** Discusses how material selection and manufacturing processes influence both part cost and assembly considerations. A design that is easy to assemble might require a more expensive manufacturing process for a single component.
*   **Automation vs. Manual Assembly:** Automating assembly has high upfront investment costs but can yield significant savings in high-volume production. For low-volume production, manual assembly might be more cost-effective.
    *   **Bralla (1998):** Provides guidance on evaluating the cost-effectiveness of various manufacturing and assembly techniques based on production volume.

### Examples

*   **Remote Control:** A classic example is the design of a simple remote control. Early designs might have had many small buttons, a complex circuit board, and numerous screws to hold the casing together. Modern designs often integrate more functions into fewer buttons, use snap-fits for assembly, and have fewer components, significantly reducing assembly time and cost.
*   **Automotive Assembly:** The automotive industry heavily relies on DFA principles. Using snap-fits for interior trim panels, designing seats that slide into place with minimal fasteners, and modularizing components all contribute to faster and more cost-effective assembly.
*   **Consumer Electronics:** The design of smartphones and other consumer electronics emphasizes minimal fasteners and integrated components. For instance, many devices are assembled using clips or adhesives rather than screws, allowing for quicker assembly and easier disassembly for repair.

---

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Assembly is a major cost driver.** Do not underestimate its impact on overall product cost.
*   **The "cost of handling and inserting" a part can exceed its manufacturing cost.**
*   **Minimize the number of parts.** This is a foundational principle of DFA.
*   **Design for ease of handling and orientation.**
*   **Prioritize assembly methods that are fast, reliable, and cost-effective.**
*   **Consider the entire product lifecycle cost**, not just manufacturing cost.
*   **DFMA is an iterative process.** Design decisions made during the manufacturing design phase directly impact assembly.

### Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
A product requires 20 screws for assembly. If the average time to handle and drive one screw is 30 seconds, and the labor rate is $20 per hour, calculate the total labor cost for screw assembly for 10,000 units.

**Answer:**
*   Time per unit = 20 screws * 30 seconds/screw = 600 seconds = 10 minutes
*   Total time for 10,000 units = 10,000 units * 10 minutes/unit = 100,000 minutes
*   Total time in hours = 100,000 minutes / 60 minutes/hour = 1666.67 hours
*   Total labor cost = 1666.67 hours * $20/hour = **$33,333.40**

**Question 2:**
Explain how using snap-fits instead of screws for joining two plastic parts can impact assembly costs. Mention at least three specific economic benefits.

**Answer:**
Using snap-fits instead of screws can impact assembly costs by:

1.  **Reducing Labor Time:** Snap-fits are typically much faster to engage than handling and driving screws, directly lowering labor costs per unit.
2.  **Eliminating Fastener Costs:** Snap-fits remove the need to purchase screws, washers, and potentially nuts, saving on material costs.
3.  **Simplifying Tooling and Automation:** Snap-fit assembly often requires less specialized tooling (like torque wrenches) or can be more easily automated with simpler robotic grippers compared to screw insertion, reducing capital expenditure.
4.  **Reducing Rework/Defect Costs:** Properly designed snap-fits can be less prone to over-tightening or cross-threading issues that can occur with screws, potentially reducing rework and scrap.

**Question 3:**
Discuss the trade-off between the number of parts in a product and the complexity of individual parts from an economic assembly perspective.

**Answer:**
There is a fundamental trade-off:

*   **Minimizing Part Count:** Each part added to a product incurs costs related to handling, orientation, and joining. Therefore, reducing the number of parts by integrating functions into fewer components generally leads to lower assembly costs.
*   **Increasing Part Complexity:** However, integrating functions often results in more complex individual parts. These complex parts can be more expensive to manufacture (e.g., requiring more intricate molding, machining, or advanced materials). They might also present new challenges in handling or feeding during assembly if not designed carefully.

The economic optimum lies in finding a balance. A slight increase in the manufacturing cost of a part might be justified if it leads to a significant reduction in assembly time and complexity, especially for high-volume products. Conversely, a simple part that is extremely cheap to manufacture might incur disproportionately high assembly costs if it's difficult to handle or orient. This highlights the importance of a holistic approach to design, considering both manufacturing and assembly economics.
