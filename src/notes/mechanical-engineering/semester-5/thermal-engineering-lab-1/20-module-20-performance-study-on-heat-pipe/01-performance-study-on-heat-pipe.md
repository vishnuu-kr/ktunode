# Thermal Engineering Lab-1: Module 20 - Performance Study on Heat Pipe

## 1. Introduction to Heat Pipes

**What is a Heat Pipe?**

A heat pipe is a passive, high-efficiency heat transfer device that utilizes phase change of a working fluid to transfer thermal energy. It consists of a sealed container, a working fluid, and a wick structure. Heat pipes are known for their exceptionally high thermal conductivity, which can be orders of magnitude higher than solid conductors.

**Working Principle:**

1.  **Evaporation:** Heat is applied to the **evaporator section** of the heat pipe. This heat vaporizes the working fluid within the wick.
2.  **Vapor Transport:** The generated vapor, at a higher pressure, flows rapidly along the hollow core of the heat pipe to the cooler **condenser section**.
3.  **Condensation:** In the condenser section, the vapor releases its latent heat of vaporization to the external cooling medium, condensing back into a liquid.
4.  **Wicking:** The liquid then returns to the evaporator section through the wick structure via capillary action, completing the cycle.

**Key Components:**

*   **Sealed Container (Casing):** Typically a metal tube (e.g., copper, aluminum) that contains the working fluid and wick. It must be compatible with the working fluid and capable of withstanding the operating pressures and temperatures. (Incropera & DeWitt, 2011)
*   **Working Fluid:** The fluid that undergoes phase change (evaporation and condensation). The choice of working fluid depends on the operating temperature range. Examples include:
    *   Ammonia (for low temperatures)
    *   Water (for moderate temperatures)
    *   Sodium, Potassium (for high temperatures)
    *   Refrigerants (for specific applications)
    *   *Considerations for working fluid selection:* Vapor pressure, boiling point, freezing point, surface tension, viscosity, thermal conductivity, latent heat, compatibility with casing and wick. (Cengel, 2012)
*   **Wick Structure:** A porous material lining the inner surface of the casing, responsible for capillary return of the condensed liquid to the evaporator. Common types include:
    *   **Screen Mesh:** Multiple layers of fine metal screens.
    *   **Sintered Wick:** Powdered metal sintered to the inner wall.
    *   **Grooved Wick:** Longitudinal grooves machined into the inner wall.
    *   *Wick Properties:* Porosity, permeability, capillary pressure, pore size distribution. (Sachdeva, 2012)

**Types of Heat Pipes:**

*   **Conventional Heat Pipe:** The most common type with a wick structure for liquid return.
*   **Wickless Heat Pipe (or Thermo-siphon):** Relies on gravity for liquid return. Only operates when gravity assists the liquid flow back to the evaporator.
*   **Diode Heat Pipe:** Allows heat transfer in one direction only.
*   **Variable Conductance Heat Pipe (VCHP):** Controls the thermal resistance and temperature by incorporating a non-condensable gas.

## 2. Performance Parameters of a Heat Pipe

The performance of a heat pipe is evaluated based on its ability to transfer heat efficiently under various operating conditions. Key performance parameters include:

*   **Thermal Resistance ($R_{th}$):** The resistance to heat flow, defined as the temperature difference between the evaporator and condenser per unit of heat transfer rate.
    $$ R_{th} = \frac{T_{evap} - T_{cond}}{Q} $$
    A lower thermal resistance indicates better performance. (Holman, 2009)
*   **Effective Thermal Conductivity ($k_{eff}$):** An equivalent thermal conductivity of the heat pipe that accounts for its ability to transfer heat with a minimal temperature drop. It is calculated based on the heat transfer rate, length, and the temperature difference across the heat pipe.
    $$ k_{eff} = \frac{Q \cdot L}{A_{cross} \cdot (T_{evap} - T_{cond})} $$
    where $L$ is the length of the heat pipe and $A_{cross}$ is the cross-sectional area of the heat pipe.
*   **Heat Transfer Capacity (Q_max):** The maximum amount of heat a heat pipe can transfer before its performance degrades due to various limitations.

## 3. Limitations of Heat Pipe Performance

Several factors can limit the maximum heat transfer rate of a heat pipe. Understanding these limitations is crucial for designing and operating heat pipes effectively.

### 3.1. Capillary Limit ($Q_{cap}$)

This is the most common limit. It occurs when the capillary pressure generated by the wick is insufficient to return the condensed liquid to the evaporator against the opposing forces (gravity, vapor flow pressure drop).

*   **Forces involved:**
    *   **Capillary Pressure ($P_{cap}$):** Driven by surface tension and the curvature of the liquid-vapor interface in the wick pores.
    *   **Gravitational Pressure Drop ($\Delta P_{grav}$):** Due to the weight of the liquid column in the wick. It can be positive (opposing flow) or negative (assisting flow), depending on the orientation.
    *   **Liquid Vapor Pressure Drop ($\Delta P_{vapor}$):** The pressure drop experienced by the vapor flowing from the evaporator to the condenser.
    *   **Liquid Pressure Drop ($\Delta P_{liquid}$):** The pressure drop of the liquid flowing back through the wick from the condenser to the evaporator.

*   **Condition for Capillary Limit:**
    $$ P_{cap} \ge |\Delta P_{vapor}| + |\Delta P_{liquid}| + |\Delta P_{grav}| $$
    The capillary limit is reached when the capillary pressure is no longer sufficient to overcome the combined pressure drops.

### 3.2. Sonic Limit ($Q_{sonic}$)

At high vapor velocities, the vapor flow can become sonic at the exit of the evaporator section. This limits the mass flow rate of the vapor and thus the heat transfer rate.

*   **Governing Principle:** The vapor velocity reaches the speed of sound in the vapor.
*   **Expression (simplified):**
    $$ Q_{sonic} \approx A_{evap} \cdot \rho_v \cdot h_{fg} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R}{T_{v}}} $$
    where $A_{evap}$ is the evaporator cross-sectional area, $\rho_v$ is the vapor density, $h_{fg}$ is the latent heat of vaporization, $\gamma$ is the specific heat ratio, $R$ is the specific gas constant for the vapor, and $T_v$ is the vapor temperature.

### 3.3. Entrainment Limit ($Q_{entrainment}$)

At high vapor velocities, the shear forces exerted by the vapor on the liquid-vapor interface in the wick can entrain liquid droplets and carry them towards the condenser. This can starve the evaporator of liquid.

*   **Governing Principle:** Vapor shear force overcomes surface tension forces holding the liquid in the wick.
*   **Condition:** Occurs when the vapor velocity is high enough to cause droplets to be stripped from the wick.

### 3.4. Boiling Limit ($Q_{boiling}$)

In the evaporator section, if the heat flux is too high, nucleate boiling can occur. This can lead to the formation of vapor bubbles that block the liquid flow through the wick, similar to dry-out.

*   **Governing Principle:** Local heat flux exceeds the critical heat flux (CHF) for nucleate boiling in the wick structure.
*   **Manifestation:** Formation of vapor bubbles that obstruct liquid return.

### 3.5. Melting/Decomposition Limit

For some working fluids and wick materials, at very high temperatures, the working fluid can decompose or the container material can melt, limiting the operating temperature range.

## 4. Experimental Setup for Performance Study

A typical experimental setup for studying the performance of a heat pipe involves:

*   **Heat Pipe Unit:** The heat pipe under investigation, with clearly defined evaporator, adiabatic, and condenser sections.
*   **Heat Source:** To provide heat to the evaporator section (e.g., electric heater, hot water bath).
*   **Cooling System:** To remove heat from the condenser section (e.g., fan, cold water bath, chilled fluid circulation).
*   **Temperature Measurement Devices:** Thermocouples or RTDs strategically placed along the length of the heat pipe (evaporator, adiabatic, condenser), on the heat source, and on the cooling medium.
*   **Power Measurement Device:** To measure the heat input to the evaporator (e.g., voltmeter, ammeter for electric heaters).
*   **Flow Meter:** To measure the flow rate of the cooling medium if a liquid cooling system is used.
*   **Data Acquisition System:** To record temperature and power measurements simultaneously.

**Experimental Procedure:**

1.  **Setup Assembly:** Assemble the heat pipe and connect the heat source, cooling system, and instrumentation.
2.  **Evaporator Heating:** Apply a controlled amount of heat to the evaporator section.
3.  **Condenser Cooling:** Maintain a constant cooling condition in the condenser section.
4.  **Steady State:** Allow the system to reach steady state, where temperatures are no longer changing significantly.
5.  **Data Recording:** Record all relevant parameters:
    *   Heat input ($Q$)
    *   Temperatures at various points along the heat pipe ($T_{evap}$, $T_{cond}$, intermediate temperatures)
    *   Cooling medium inlet and outlet temperatures (if applicable)
    *   Cooling medium flow rate (if applicable)
6.  **Varying Parameters:** Repeat the measurements by:
    *   Varying the heat input to the evaporator.
    *   Varying the cooling conditions in the condenser.
    *   Changing the orientation of the heat pipe (to assess gravitational effects).
    *   Changing the operating temperature range.

## 5. Data Analysis and Performance Evaluation

From the collected experimental data, the following can be calculated:

*   **Heat Transfer Rate ($Q$):**
    *   If using an electric heater: $Q = V \cdot I$ (Voltage x Current)
    *   If using a liquid cooling bath: $Q = \dot{m}_c \cdot c_{p,c} \cdot (T_{c,out} - T_{c,in})$ (Mass flow rate of coolant x Specific heat of coolant x Temperature difference of coolant)
*   **Temperature Difference ($\Delta T$):** $T_{evap} - T_{cond}$
*   **Thermal Resistance ($R_{th}$):** $R_{th} = \Delta T / Q$
*   **Effective Thermal Conductivity ($k_{eff}$):** $k_{eff} = (Q \cdot L) / (A_{cross} \cdot \Delta T)$
*   **Performance Curves:** Plot $R_{th}$ vs. $Q$, $k_{eff}$ vs. $Q$, or $\Delta T$ vs. $Q$ to visualize the heat pipe's performance.

**Analysis of Results:**

*   **Effect of Heat Load:** How does thermal resistance change with increasing heat input? (Ideally, it should remain low until a limit is reached).
*   **Effect of Cooling Conditions:** How do changes in condenser temperature or cooling fluid flow rate affect performance?
*   **Effect of Orientation:** How does gravity influence the thermal resistance and heat transfer capacity?
*   **Identification of Limits:** Observe the point where thermal resistance starts to increase rapidly or the temperature difference becomes unacceptably large, indicating the onset of a performance limitation.

## 7. Key Concepts and Definitions to Remember

*   **Heat Pipe:** A passive, two-phase heat transfer device.
*   **Latent Heat of Vaporization:** The energy absorbed or released during phase change.
*   **Capillary Action:** The ability of a porous material to draw liquid against gravity.
*   **Thermal Resistance:** A measure of how well a material or device conducts heat.
*   **Effective Thermal Conductivity:** An apparent conductivity that represents the heat pipe's overall heat transfer capability.
*   **Evaporator, Adiabatic Section, Condenser:** The distinct regions of a heat pipe.
*   **Performance Limits:** Capillary, Sonic, Entrainment, Boiling limits.
*   **Steady State:** A condition where system parameters (e.g., temperature) remain constant over time.

## 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Working Fluid Selection:** Crucial for the operating temperature range.
*   **Wick Structure:** Dictates capillary pumping capability and pressure drop.
*   **Orientation Matters:** Gravity can significantly impact performance, especially for wickless heat pipes.
*   **Limitations Exist:** Heat pipes are not infinitely capable; understanding their limits is vital for design.
*   **Passive Device:** Heat pipes require no external power for operation once initiated (unlike active cooling systems).
*   **High Thermal Conductivity:** Their primary advantage.

## 9. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:** A heat pipe using water as the working fluid is tested. At a heat input of 50 W, the evaporator temperature is 95°C and the condenser temperature is 90°C. The heat pipe has a length of 0.5 m and a cross-sectional area of $1 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2$. Calculate the thermal resistance and effective thermal conductivity of the heat pipe under these conditions.

**Answer 1:**
*   **Heat Transfer Rate (Q):** 50 W
*   **Temperature Difference ($\Delta T$):** $T_{evap} - T_{cond} = 95^\circ C - 90^\circ C = 5^\circ C$
*   **Thermal Resistance ($R_{th}$):**
    $$ R_{th} = \frac{\Delta T}{Q} = \frac{5^\circ C}{50 \text{ W}} = 0.1 \frac{^\circ C}{W} $$
*   **Effective Thermal Conductivity ($k_{eff}$):**
    $$ k_{eff} = \frac{Q \cdot L}{A_{cross} \cdot \Delta T} = \frac{50 \text{ W} \cdot 0.5 \text{ m}}{(1 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2) \cdot 5^\circ C} $$
    $$ k_{eff} = \frac{25 \text{ W}\cdot\text{m}}{5 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2\cdot^\circ C} = 5 \times 10^4 \frac{\text{W}}{\text{m}\cdot^\circ C} $$

**Question 2:** List and briefly explain the four main limitations that can restrict the heat transfer capacity of a conventional heat pipe.

**Answer 2:**
1.  **Capillary Limit:** The capillary pressure generated by the wick is insufficient to return the condensed liquid to the evaporator against pressure drops (vapor flow, liquid flow, gravity).
2.  **Sonic Limit:** The vapor velocity in the evaporator becomes sonic, limiting the mass flow rate.
3.  **Entrainment Limit:** High vapor velocities cause shear forces to strip liquid from the wick, leading to dry-out.
4.  **Boiling Limit:** Excessive heat flux in the evaporator leads to nucleate boiling and vapor blockage in the wick.

**Question 3:** Explain why a heat pipe has a much higher effective thermal conductivity than a solid copper rod of the same dimensions.

**Answer 3:**
A heat pipe achieves its high effective thermal conductivity through a two-phase heat transfer mechanism. The latent heat of vaporization and condensation, which is significantly larger than the sensible heat transfer in a solid conductor, is transferred by the working fluid. The rapid flow of vapor from the evaporator to the condenser with a very small temperature difference, coupled with the efficient phase change process, results in a much lower overall thermal resistance and thus a higher effective thermal conductivity compared to simple conduction through a solid material. (Cengel, 2012; Sachdeva, 2012)

**Question 4:** What are the key components of a conventional heat pipe?

**Answer 4:** The key components are the sealed container (casing), the working fluid, and the wick structure.
