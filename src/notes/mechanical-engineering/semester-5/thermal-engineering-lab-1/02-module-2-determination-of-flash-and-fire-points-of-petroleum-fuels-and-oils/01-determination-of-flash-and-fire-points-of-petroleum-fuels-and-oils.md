# Thermal Engineering Lab-1: Module 2 - Determination of Flash and Fire Points of Petroleum Fuels and Oils

## 1. Introduction

This module focuses on the experimental determination of two crucial properties of petroleum fuels and oils: the flash point and the fire point. These properties are essential for understanding the flammability characteristics of these substances, which have significant implications for safety, storage, handling, and transportation.

## 3. Key Concepts and Definitions

### 3.1. Flash Point

*   **Definition:** The **flash point** of a liquid is the lowest temperature at which the liquid gives off enough vapor to ignite momentarily when an ignition source is applied in the presence of air.
*   **Mechanism:** As the liquid is heated, its vapor pressure increases. At a certain temperature, the concentration of vapor above the liquid surface becomes sufficient to form a flammable mixture with air. When exposed to a spark or flame, this vapor-air mixture ignites, producing a brief flash of flame.
*   **Significance:**
    *   **Safety Classification:** Flash point is a primary criterion for classifying flammable and combustible liquids according to safety regulations (e.g., NFPA, OSHA).
    *   **Storage and Handling:** Liquids with lower flash points require more stringent safety precautions during storage and handling to prevent ignition.
    *   **Transportation:** Flash point is a critical parameter for determining transportation regulations and hazard classifications.
*   **Types of Flash Point Tests:**
    *   **Open-Cup Method:** The vapor is allowed to mix freely with the air. This generally results in a slightly lower flash point than closed-cup methods due to greater vapor release.
    *   **Closed-Cup Method:** The vapor is contained within a closed vessel. This method provides a more accurate representation of the vapor concentration under typical storage conditions. Common closed-cup testers include Pensky-Martens Closed Cup and Abel Closed Cup.

### 3.2. Fire Point

*   **Definition:** The **fire point** of a liquid is the lowest temperature at which the liquid gives off enough vapor to continue burning in the presence of an ignition source after the initial flash has occurred.
*   **Mechanism:** Once the flash point is reached, the vapor continues to be generated as the liquid is heated. At the fire point, the rate of vapor generation is sufficient to sustain combustion.
*   **Significance:**
    *   **Fire Hazard Assessment:** The fire point indicates a more significant fire hazard than the flash point, as it represents the temperature at which a sustained fire can occur.
    *   **Fire Prevention:** Understanding the fire point helps in designing fire prevention systems and emergency response procedures.
*   **Relationship to Flash Point:** The fire point is always higher than the flash point for a given liquid. The difference between the fire point and the flash point is often referred to as the "flaming range."

### 3.3. Vapor Pressure

*   **Definition:** Vapor pressure is the pressure exerted by the vapor of a liquid in equilibrium with its condensed phase (liquid or solid) at a given temperature.
*   **Relevance:** As temperature increases, the vapor pressure of a liquid increases. This directly influences the concentration of flammable vapors above the liquid surface, which is crucial for both flash and fire point determination. (Refer to Heat Transfer by Holman J.P for detailed discussions on vapor pressure and phase change phenomena).

### 3.4. Flammable vs. Combustible Liquids

*   **Flammable Liquids:** Liquids with a flash point below 100°F (37.8°C).
*   **Combustible Liquids:** Liquids with a flash point at or above 100°F (37.8°C).

## 4. Theoretical Basis and Principles

The determination of flash and fire points is based on the principle of **combustion**, which requires three components:

1.  **Fuel:** In this case, the petroleum fuel or oil.
2.  **Oxidizer:** Oxygen from the air.
3.  **Ignition Source:** A spark or flame.

When a liquid is heated, it starts to vaporize. These vapors mix with the surrounding air to form a flammable mixture. The flash point is the lowest temperature at which this mixture is capable of ignition. The fire point is the temperature at which the rate of vapor generation is high enough to sustain combustion after ignition.

The relationship between temperature, vapor pressure, and flammability is a key concept in thermal engineering. As described by Cengel's "Heat Transfer: A Practical Approach," the rate of heat transfer to the liquid and the subsequent vaporization process are critical. The concentration of fuel vapor in the air-fuel mixture dictates its flammability limits. The flash and fire points are specific temperatures at which these limits are met.

## 5. Apparatus Required

The specific apparatus may vary depending on the standard method used (e.g., ASTM D93 for Pensky-Martens Closed Cup, ASTM D56 for Tag Closed Cup). A typical setup for determining flash and fire points using a Pensky-Martens Closed Cup tester includes:

*   **Heating Bath:** Usually a water or oil bath for controlled heating of the sample.
*   **Test Cup:** A brass cup designed to hold the sample.
*   **Heating Device:** A burner or electric heater to control the bath temperature.
*   **Thermometer:** To measure the temperature of the sample in the cup.
*   **Stirrer:** To ensure uniform temperature distribution within the sample.
*   **Ignition Source:** A flame, typically from a gas burner with a regulated pilot flame.
*   **Shutter:** To open and close the aperture through which the ignition source is applied.
*   **Timer:** To control the interval between temperature increases and ignition attempts.

**(Refer to Sachdeva's "Fundamentals of Engineering, Heat and Mass Transfer" or Incropera & DeWitt's "Heat and Mass Transfer" for discussions on heat transfer mechanisms that influence vaporization rates, which are fundamental to understanding these experiments.)**

## 6. Experimental Procedure (Illustrative - Pensky-Martens Closed Cup Method)

**Note:** Always refer to the specific standard operating procedure (SOP) or the relevant ASTM/IP standard for the exact procedure.

### 6.1. Apparatus Preparation

1.  Ensure the apparatus is clean and dry.
2.  Assemble the apparatus according to the manufacturer's instructions.
3.  Fill the heating bath with water or oil to the recommended level.

### 6.2. Sample Preparation and Filling

1.  Fill the test cup with the petroleum fuel or oil to the indicated fill mark. Avoid introducing air bubbles.
2.  Ensure the sample temperature is below the expected flash point.

### 6.3. Heating and Ignition

1.  Place the filled test cup in the heating bath.
2.  Insert the cover assembly, ensuring the shutter is closed.
3.  Position the thermometer such that the bulb is just below the surface of the liquid.
4.  Start the stirrer at the specified rate.
5.  Begin heating the bath at a specified rate (e.g., 2-3°C per minute).
6.  As the temperature approaches the expected flash point, reduce the heating rate and increase the frequency of ignition source application.
7.  Apply the ignition source for a brief period (e.g., 1 second) by opening the shutter and passing the flame across the aperture.
8.  Observe for a flash. Record the temperature at which the first momentary flash occurs in the vapor above the liquid. This is the **flash point**.

### 6.4. Determining the Fire Point

1.  Immediately after determining the flash point, continue heating the sample at a slightly increased rate (e.g., 1°C per minute).
2.  Continue applying the ignition source at regular intervals (e.g., every 5°C increase in temperature).
3.  Observe for a sustained flame that continues to burn for at least 5 seconds. Record the temperature at which this occurs. This is the **fire point**.

### 6.5. Repeatability and Reporting

1.  Repeat the experiment at least twice for the same sample to ensure consistency and obtain a reliable average value.
2.  Record all temperatures accurately.
3.  Report the flash and fire points clearly, along with the method used.

## 7. Factors Affecting Flash and Fire Points

*   **Chemical Composition:** The presence of volatile components (e.g., lighter hydrocarbons) in the fuel will lead to lower flash and fire points.
*   **Purity:** Impurities or contaminants can alter the vaporization characteristics and thus affect the flash and fire points.
*   **Ambient Pressure:** While typically performed at atmospheric pressure, changes in pressure can slightly influence vapor pressure and thus the flash point.
*   **Vapor Space:** The volume and configuration of the vapor space above the liquid can influence the accumulation of flammable vapors.
*   **Rate of Heating:** A slower rate of heating allows for better equilibrium between the liquid and vapor phases, leading to a more accurate flash point determination.
*   **Ignition Source Strength and Duration:** The intensity and duration of the ignition source are critical for a valid test.

**(Kothandaraman's "Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer" provides insights into how phase change processes are influenced by heat transfer rates, which is relevant to understanding how heating rates impact vaporization and consequently flash points.)**

## 10. Practice Questions and Answers

**Q1. Define flash point and fire point in your own words.**

**Answer:**
*   **Flash Point:** The lowest temperature at which a liquid fuel gives off enough vapor to ignite briefly when exposed to a flame.
*   **Fire Point:** The lowest temperature at which a liquid fuel will continue to burn for at least five seconds after ignition.

**Q2. Why is the fire point always higher than the flash point?**

**Answer:** At the flash point, there is just enough vapor to ignite momentarily. At the fire point, the rate of vapor generation is significantly higher, allowing the combustion to sustain itself after the initial ignition. More heat input (higher temperature) is required to achieve this sustained vaporization.

**Q3. List three safety precautions that must be taken when performing this experiment.**

**Answer:**
1.  Conduct the experiment in a well-ventilated area.
2.  Keep a fire extinguisher readily accessible.
3.  Wear safety goggles and gloves.

**Q4. How does the rate of heating affect the observed flash point?**

**Answer:** A slower rate of heating generally leads to a more accurate flash point. This is because it allows the liquid and its vapors to reach thermal equilibrium, ensuring that the vapor concentration above the liquid accurately reflects the ambient temperature. Rapid heating can lead to premature ignition of a small pocket of vapor, resulting in an artificially low flash point.

**Q5. What are the main differences between the open-cup and closed-cup methods for determining flash points? Which is generally considered more representative of real-world conditions for storage and handling?**

**Answer:**
*   **Open-Cup:** The vapor mixes freely with the surrounding air. This usually results in a slightly lower flash point because more vapor is readily available for ignition.
*   **Closed-Cup:** The vapor is contained within a closed vessel. This method is generally considered more representative of real-world conditions for storage and handling, as liquids are often stored in closed containers, and the vapor concentration within the container is more controlled.

**Q6. If a petroleum product has a flash point of 85°C, is it classified as a flammable or combustible liquid according to the standard definition? Explain.**

**Answer:** It is classified as a **combustible liquid**. Flammable liquids have flash points below 37.8°C (100°F), while combustible liquids have flash points at or above 37.8°C (100°F).
