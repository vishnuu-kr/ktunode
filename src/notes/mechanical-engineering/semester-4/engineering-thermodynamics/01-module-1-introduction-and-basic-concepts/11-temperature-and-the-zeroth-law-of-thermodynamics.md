## Topic: Temperature and the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

### **1. Introduction to Thermodynamics**

Thermodynamics is the science of energy and its transformations. It deals with heat, work, and their relationship to various forms of energy.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **System:** A region of space chosen for study.
*   **Surroundings:** Everything outside the system.
*   **Boundary:** The real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings.
*   **Universe:** The system and its surroundings combined.
*   **Closed System:** A system where energy can cross the boundary, but mass cannot. (Cengel et al., 2011)
*   **Open System (Control Volume):** A system where both energy and mass can cross the boundary. (Cengel et al., 2011)
*   **Isolated System:** A system where neither energy nor mass can cross the boundary.

**Course Outcome Alignment:** CO1 (K1, K2) - Understanding basic thermodynamic concepts.

### **2. Temperature**

Temperature is a fundamental property that indicates the degree of hotness or coldness of a substance.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Sensible Temperature:** The perceived hotness or coldness.
*   **Microscopic Interpretation:** Temperature is directly related to the average kinetic energy of the molecules within a substance. Higher temperature means faster molecular motion. (Nag, 2017)
*   **Thermodynamic Property:** Temperature is an intensive property, meaning it is independent of the mass or size of the system.

**CO1 Alignment:** CO1 (K1, K2) - Understanding the concept of temperature.

### **3. The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics**

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics provides a fundamental basis for temperature measurement.

**Statement of the Zeroth Law:**

"If two thermodynamic systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then they are in thermal equilibrium with each other." (Cengel et al., 2011)

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Thermal Equilibrium:** Two systems are in thermal equilibrium if there is no net transfer of heat between them when they are brought into thermal contact. This implies they are at the same temperature. (Shapiro & Moran, 2006)
*   **Implication:** The Zeroth Law establishes that temperature is a state function and allows for the development of thermometers.

**Example:**

Imagine System A and System B are in thermal contact with System C. If System A and System C are in thermal equilibrium (meaning no heat flows between them), and System B and System C are also in thermal equilibrium, then Systems A and B are also in thermal equilibrium. This means A, B, and C are all at the same temperature.

### **4. Temperature Scales**

To quantify temperature, we use various scales.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Absolute Temperature Scale:** A scale where zero represents the absolute minimum possible temperature.
*   **Celsius Scale (°C):** A scale where 0°C is the freezing point of water and 100°C is the boiling point of water at standard atmospheric pressure.
*   **Fahrenheit Scale (°F):** Another common scale where 32°F is the freezing point of water and 212°F is the boiling point of water.
*   **Kelvin Scale (K):** The absolute thermodynamic temperature scale. 0 K (absolute zero) is the theoretical point where all molecular motion ceases. It is the SI unit for temperature. (Cengel et al., 2011)
*   **Rankine Scale (°R):** The absolute temperature scale corresponding to the Fahrenheit scale.

**Conversion Formulas:**

*   **Celsius to Fahrenheit:** $T_F = \frac{9}{5} T_C + 32$
*   **Fahrenheit to Celsius:** $T_C = \frac{5}{9} (T_F - 32)$
*   **Celsius to Kelvin:** $T_K = T_C + 273.15$
*   **Kelvin to Celsius:** $T_C = T_K - 273.15$
*   **Fahrenheit to Rankine:** $T_R = T_F + 459.67$
*   **Rankine to Fahrenheit:** $T_F = T_R - 459.67$

**Important Note:** For thermodynamic calculations, especially involving the ideal gas law or Carnot efficiency, the absolute temperature scale (Kelvin or Rankine) is crucial.

**CO1 Alignment:** CO1 (K1, K2) - Understanding different temperature scales.

**Example:**

Convert 25°C to Kelvin and Fahrenheit.

*   **Kelvin:** $T_K = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 \, \text{K}$
*   **Fahrenheit:** $T_F = \frac{9}{5}(25) + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77 \, \text{°F}$

### **5. Thermometers and Temperature Measurement**

Thermometers are devices used to measure temperature, relying on the Zeroth Law.

**Key Concepts & Principles:**

*   **Thermometric Property:** A physical property of a substance that changes predictably with temperature (e.g., volume, pressure, electrical resistance).
*   **Working Principle:** A thermometer is a system that is brought into thermal contact with another system (the object whose temperature is to be measured). According to the Zeroth Law, when the thermometer and the object are in thermal equilibrium, they are at the same temperature. The thermometric property of the thermometer is then calibrated to indicate this temperature. (Nag, 2017)

**Types of Thermometers (Examples):**

*   **Constant-Volume Gas Thermometer:** Uses the pressure of a fixed amount of gas at constant volume. This is considered one of the most accurate types for defining temperature scales. (Sonntag et al., 2014)
*   **Liquid-in-Glass Thermometer:** Uses the thermal expansion of a liquid (like mercury or alcohol) in a glass tube.
*   **Resistance Thermometer:** Uses the change in electrical resistance of a material with temperature (e.g., platinum resistance thermometer - PRT).
*   **Thermocouple:** Uses the thermoelectric effect where a voltage is generated at the junction of two dissimilar metals, proportional to the temperature difference.

**CO1 Alignment:** CO1 (K1, K2) - Understanding how temperature is measured.

### **6. Absolute Zero**

Absolute zero is the theoretical lowest possible temperature.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Absolute Zero:** The temperature at which the particles of a substance have minimal kinetic energy (effectively zero, though quantum mechanical zero-point energy still exists). On the Kelvin scale, this is 0 K.
*   **Theoretical Limit:** It is impossible to reach absolute zero through any finite number of steps. (Ansermet & Brechet, 2019)

**CO1 Alignment:** CO1 (K1, K2) - Understanding the concept of absolute zero.

### **7. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:** State the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics in your own words.

**Answer:** The Zeroth Law states that if two systems are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other. This allows us to define a common property (temperature) that is shared by systems in thermal equilibrium.

**Question 2:** Convert 50°C to the Rankine scale.

**Answer:**
First, convert Celsius to Fahrenheit:
$T_F = \frac{9}{5} (50) + 32 = 90 + 32 = 122 \, \text{°F}$
Next, convert Fahrenheit to Rankine:
$T_R = T_F + 459.67 = 122 + 459.67 = 581.67 \, \text{°R}$

**Question 3:** Two systems, A and B, are brought into thermal contact. If no heat flows between them, what can be concluded about their temperatures?

**Answer:** They are in thermal equilibrium, meaning they have the same temperature.

**Question 4:** Why is it important to use absolute temperature scales (Kelvin or Rankine) in thermodynamic calculations?

**Answer:** Absolute temperature scales are used because many thermodynamic relationships, such as the ideal gas law ($PV = nRT$) and the efficiency of heat engines, are directly proportional to or dependent on absolute temperature. Using non-absolute scales can lead to incorrect results, especially when dealing with ratios of temperatures or when absolute zero is a relevant concept.

**Question 5:** If a thermometer shows a reading of 20°C, and this thermometer was calibrated using a constant-volume gas thermometer where the gas pressure was found to be linearly related to temperature, what would be the equivalent reading on the Fahrenheit scale?

**Answer:**
The question is essentially asking for a temperature conversion. The calibration method details are context for why thermometers work but don't change the conversion itself.
$T_F = \frac{9}{5} T_C + 32$
$T_F = \frac{9}{5} (20) + 32 = 36 + 32 = 68 \, \text{°F}$

### **8. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Zeroth Law:** The foundation for temperature measurement.
*   **Thermal Equilibrium:** The state where no net heat transfer occurs between systems in contact.
*   **Temperature:** A measure of the average kinetic energy of molecules and an indicator of hotness/coldness.
*   **Absolute Temperature Scales (K, R):** Essential for many thermodynamic equations.
*   **Absolute Zero (0 K):** The theoretical lowest possible temperature.
*   **Intensive Property:** Temperature does not depend on the mass of the system.
