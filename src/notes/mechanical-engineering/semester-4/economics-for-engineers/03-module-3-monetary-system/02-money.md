# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 3: Monetary System - Topic: Money

## 1. Introduction to Money

### 1.1. What is Money?

Money is anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange for goods and services. It's a crucial element of any modern economy, facilitating transactions and enabling economic activity.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Medium of Exchange:** The primary function of money; it's accepted by everyone for buying and selling.
*   **Store of Value:** Money can be saved and used later; it retains its purchasing power over time (though inflation can erode this).
*   **Unit of Account:** Money provides a common measure of value for goods and services, allowing for easy comparison and accounting.
*   **Standard of Deferred Payment:** Money can be used to settle debts and payments in the future.

*   **Geetika, Ghosh & Chodhury (2015):** Likely defines money and its functions in the context of its role in the economy.
*   **Thuesen & Fabrycky (1966):** May touch upon money's role in financial transactions within engineering projects.
*   **Paneerselvam (2012):** Will likely discuss money as a fundamental concept in economic analysis relevant to engineering decisions.

### 1.2. Evolution of Money

*   **Barter System:** The direct exchange of goods and services without the use of money. This system is inefficient due to the "double coincidence of wants" (both parties must have what the other wants) and the lack of divisibility and storability of many goods.
    *   **Example:** Trading a bag of rice for a pair of shoes.
*   **Commodity Money:** Money that has intrinsic value, derived from the material it's made of.
    *   **Examples:** Gold coins, silver coins, salt, shells.
*   **Representative Money:** Money that represents a claim on a commodity.
    *   **Example:** Gold certificates that could be exchanged for actual gold.
*   **Fiat Money:** Money that has value because the government declares it to be legal tender. It has no intrinsic value.
    *   **Example:** Paper currency and coins in most modern economies.

**Important Point to Remember:** The move from barter to commodity and then fiat money reflects a growing need for efficiency, standardization, and ease of transactions in increasingly complex economies.

### 1.3. Functions of Money

As mentioned in 1.1, money performs several vital functions:

1.  **Medium of Exchange:** Eliminates the barter problem.
2.  **Measure of Value (Unit of Account):** Simplifies price comparisons.
3.  **Store of Value:** Allows for saving and future consumption.
4.  **Standard of Deferred Payment:** Facilitates credit and loans.

**Example:** When you buy a cup of coffee for $3, money acts as a medium of exchange. The $3 is the unit of account, measuring the coffee's value. If you save $3 for later, it's a store of value. If you promise to pay $3 next week for a book, it's a standard of deferred payment.

## 2. Measures of Money Supply

The money supply refers to the total amount of money in circulation within an economy. Economists use different measures to categorize and quantify this.

### 2.1. Monetary Aggregates

Monetary aggregates are statistical measures of the money supply, often categorized by liquidity.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Liquidity:** How easily an asset can be converted into cash without loss of value.

**Common Measures (Examples, specific definitions can vary by country):**

*   **M0 (Monetary Base):** The most liquid form of money. Includes physical currency in circulation and commercial banks' reserves held at the central bank.
    *   **Reference:** Khan M. Y. (2011) will provide detailed definitions of these aggregates in the context of the Indian financial system.
*   **M1:** A narrow measure of money supply. Includes currency in circulation (held by the public), demand deposits (checking accounts), and other checkable deposits.
    *   **Example:** Cash in your wallet, money in your checking account.
*   **M2:** A broader measure than M1. Includes M1 plus savings deposits, small-denomination time deposits (CDs), and retail money market mutual funds.
    *   **Example:** M1 components + money in your savings account.
*   **M3:** An even broader measure. Includes M2 plus large-denomination time deposits, institutional money market funds, and other less liquid assets.

**Important Point to Remember:** Central banks monitor these aggregates to understand the liquidity in the economy and to implement monetary policy. Changes in money supply can influence inflation, interest rates, and economic growth.

### 2.2. Role of Central Banks in Managing Money Supply

Central banks (like the Federal Reserve in the US or the Reserve Bank of India) are responsible for managing the money supply to achieve macroeconomic stability.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Monetary Policy:** Actions undertaken by a central bank to manipulate the money supply and credit conditions to stimulate or restrain economic activity.

**Tools of Monetary Policy:**

*   **Open Market Operations:** The buying and selling of government securities by the central bank.
    *   **Buying securities:** Injects money into the economy, increasing the money supply.
    *   **Selling securities:** Withdraws money from the economy, decreasing the money supply.
*   **Reserve Requirements:** The fraction of deposits that commercial banks are required to hold in reserve, either in their vaults or at the central bank.
    *   **Lowering reserve requirements:** Allows banks to lend more, increasing the money supply.
    *   **Raising reserve requirements:** Forces banks to lend less, decreasing the money supply.
*   **Discount Rate (or Policy Rate):** The interest rate at which commercial banks can borrow money directly from the central bank.
    *   **Lowering the discount rate:** Encourages banks to borrow more, increasing the money supply.
    *   **Raising the discount rate:** Discourages borrowing, decreasing the money supply.

## 3. Demand for Money

The demand for money refers to the desire of individuals and firms to hold their assets in the form of money.

### 3.1. Motives for Holding Money

John Maynard Keynes identified three main motives for holding money:

1.  **Transactions Motive:** People hold money to finance everyday transactions (buying goods and services). This demand is influenced by income levels and the frequency of payments.
    *   **Example:** You keep money in your checking account to pay for groceries, rent, and utilities.
2.  **Precautionary Motive:** People hold money to meet unexpected expenses or emergencies. This demand is influenced by uncertainty and the need for a financial buffer.
    *   **Example:** Keeping some savings aside for a medical emergency or car repair.
3.  **Speculative Motive:** People hold money to take advantage of future investment opportunities, particularly when they expect interest rates to rise (which would lead to a fall in bond prices). When interest rates are high, people prefer to hold bonds; when interest rates are low, they prefer to hold money.
    *   **Example:** Holding cash because you anticipate buying stocks at a lower price in the future if the market falls.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Geetika, Ghosh & Chodhury (2015):** Will likely elaborate on Keynesian motives for money demand.
*   **Paneerselvam (2012):** May connect money demand to investment decisions and economic activity.

### 3.2. Factors Affecting Money Demand

*   **Income Level:** Higher income generally leads to higher demand for money for transactions.
*   **Interest Rates:** Higher interest rates increase the opportunity cost of holding money (as you could earn more by investing), thus reducing the demand for money (especially for speculative purposes).
*   **Price Level:** A higher price level means more money is needed to purchase the same quantity of goods and services, increasing the demand for money.
*   **Financial Innovation:** Development of new payment systems or financial instruments can affect the demand for money.
*   **Uncertainty:** Greater economic or personal uncertainty can increase the precautionary demand for money.

## 4. Supply of Money

The supply of money is determined by the actions of the central bank and the banking system.

### 4.1. Money Multiplier

The money multiplier is the process by which an initial deposit into the banking system leads to a larger increase in the overall money supply.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Excess Reserves:** Reserves held by banks above the legally required minimum.
*   **Money Multiplier Formula:** $1 / \text{Reserve Requirement Ratio}$

**Example:**

Suppose the reserve requirement ratio is 10% (0.10). If a person deposits $1,000 into a bank:

1.  The bank must keep $100 ($1,000 * 0.10$) as reserves.
2.  It can lend out the remaining $900.
3.  This $900 is deposited into another bank, which must keep $90 ($900 * 0.10$) as reserves and can lend out $810.
4.  This process continues, with each subsequent loan and deposit being smaller.

The total increase in the money supply from the initial $1,000 deposit would be:
$1,000 + 900 + 810 + 729 + ... = $1,000 * (1 / 0.10) = $10,000$.

**Important Point to Remember:** The money multiplier is a simplified model. In reality, factors like banks holding excess reserves and people holding more cash can reduce the actual multiplier effect.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Geetika, Ghosh & Chodhury (2015):** Will likely explain the money multiplier in detail as a core concept of monetary creation.
*   **Paneerselvam (2012):** May link the money multiplier to credit creation and its impact on economic growth.

### 4.2. Factors Affecting Money Supply

*   **Central Bank Actions:** As discussed earlier (open market operations, reserve requirements, discount rate).
*   **Banks' Willingness to Lend:** If banks are cautious and hold more excess reserves, the money multiplier is weakened, and the money supply grows slower.
*   **Public's Preference for Holding Cash:** If the public decides to hold more cash and fewer deposits, this reduces the amount of money available for lending by banks, thus reducing the money supply.

### 5.1. The Liquidity Preference Theory

Developed by Keynes, this theory explains how interest rates are determined by the supply of and demand for money.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Liquidity Preference:** The demand for money.
*   **Interest Rate:** The "price" of holding money rather than interest-earning assets. It's the reward for parting with liquidity.

**How it works:**

*   **Increase in Money Supply:** When the central bank increases the money supply (e.g., by buying bonds), there is more money available than people want to hold at the current interest rate. To get rid of this excess money, people buy bonds. Increased demand for bonds pushes their prices up, and as bond prices and interest rates are inversely related, interest rates fall.
*   **Decrease in Money Supply:** When the central bank decreases the money supply, there is less money available. People sell bonds to obtain money, increasing the supply of bonds. Increased supply of bonds pushes their prices down, leading to a rise in interest rates.

**Example:** If the central bank injects a large amount of money into the economy, people have more cash. They might use this cash to buy bonds. This increased demand for bonds raises bond prices and, consequently, lowers interest rates.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Geetika, Ghosh & Chodhury (2015):** Will likely detail Keynes's liquidity preference theory.
*   **Paneerselvam (2012):** May present this theory as a foundational element of understanding how financial markets operate.

### 5.2. Implications for Engineering Economy

Understanding the relationship between money supply and interest rates is vital for engineering projects.

*   **Cost of Capital:** Interest rates directly influence the cost of borrowing money to finance engineering projects. Higher interest rates mean higher borrowing costs, making projects less attractive.
*   **Investment Decisions:** Engineers must consider the prevailing interest rates when evaluating the profitability of investments. The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) of a project is compared against the cost of capital (which is influenced by interest rates).
*   **Inflation:** Changes in money supply can also impact inflation. High inflation erodes the purchasing power of future earnings, which needs to be considered in long-term engineering economic analyses.

## 6. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Explain the four main functions of money and why each is important for a modern economy.

**Answer:**
1.  **Medium of Exchange:** Facilitates transactions, eliminating the inefficiencies of barter.
2.  **Store of Value:** Allows individuals and firms to save wealth for future use.
3.  **Unit of Account:** Provides a common yardstick for measuring the value of goods and services, simplifying economic calculations.
4.  **Standard of Deferred Payment:** Enables borrowing and lending, facilitating credit transactions and future payments.

**Question 2:**
If the reserve requirement ratio is 15%, what is the maximum potential increase in the money supply from an initial deposit of $5,000 into the banking system?

**Answer:**
The money multiplier is calculated as $1 / \text{Reserve Requirement Ratio}$.
Money Multiplier = $1 / 0.15 = 6.67$ (approximately)
Maximum potential increase in money supply = Initial Deposit * Money Multiplier
Maximum potential increase = $5,000 * 6.67 \approx $33,350$

**Question 3:**
Describe the three motives for holding money as outlined by Keynes. How might an increase in economic uncertainty affect these motives?

**Answer:**
The three motives are:
1.  **Transactions Motive:** For day-to-day purchases.
2.  **Precautionary Motive:** To cover unexpected expenses.
3.  **Speculative Motive:** To take advantage of future investment opportunities.

An increase in economic uncertainty would likely increase the **precautionary motive** as people seek to build a larger financial buffer for unforeseen events. It might also increase the **speculative motive** if investors become more risk-averse and prefer holding cash over uncertain investments. The **transactions motive** might remain relatively stable or slightly increase if people anticipate price rises.

**Question 4:**
How does a central bank's decision to sell government bonds affect the money supply and interest rates?

**Answer:**
When a central bank sells government bonds:
*   **Money Supply:** It withdraws money from the economy as people and banks buy these bonds. This reduces the money supply.
*   **Interest Rates:** With a reduced money supply and increased demand for money (as people spend cash to buy bonds), interest rates tend to rise. This is because the "price" of borrowing money increases.

**Question 5 (Engineering Context):**
A company is considering a new machinery investment. The project requires borrowing funds. Explain how a rise in the overall interest rate in the economy, possibly due to changes in money supply, would affect the company's decision-making process for this investment.

**Answer:**
A rise in the interest rate would directly increase the company's **cost of capital** or the cost of borrowing. This means the project will have to generate higher returns to be profitable. The company will need to re-evaluate the project's Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR). If the projected returns on the machinery investment, when discounted at the higher interest rate, fall below the required threshold (e.g., IRR < Cost of Capital or NPV < 0), the company may decide to abandon or postpone the investment.

## 7. Important Points to Remember

*   **Money** is more than just currency; it's any asset generally accepted as a medium of exchange.
*   The **functions of money** (medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account, standard of deferred payment) are essential for a functioning economy.
*   **Monetary aggregates (M0, M1, M2, M3)** are different measures of the money supply, categorized by liquidity.
*   **Central banks** play a crucial role in managing the money supply through monetary policy tools like open market operations, reserve requirements, and the discount rate.
*   The **demand for money** arises from transactions, precautionary, and speculative motives.
*   **Interest rates** are determined by the supply and demand for money (Liquidity Preference Theory).
*   Changes in **money supply and interest rates** have significant implications for economic activity, inflation, and investment decisions, particularly in engineering economy.
*   The **money multiplier** explains how fractional reserve banking can expand the money supply.
