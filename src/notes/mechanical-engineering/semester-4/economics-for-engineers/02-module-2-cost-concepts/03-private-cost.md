### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions:

#### 2.1. What is Private Cost?

**Definition:** Private cost, also known as **Accounting Cost** or **Explicit Cost**, refers to the direct monetary outlay or expenditure incurred by a firm to produce a good or service. These are the costs that appear in the firm's accounting records and are readily measurable in monetary terms.

*   **Focus:** Directly attributable to the production process.
*   **Nature:** Tangible and observable outlays.
*   **Importance:** Essential for calculating a firm's profit and for financial reporting.

#### 2.2. Components of Private Cost:

Private costs can be broadly categorized into the following:

*   **Explicit Costs:** These are the actual, out-of-pocket payments made by a firm for the use of factors of production and other inputs.

    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Wages and Salaries:** Payment to labor.
        *   **Raw Material Costs:** Expenses for materials used in production.
        *   **Rent:** Payment for the use of land or buildings.
        *   **Interest Payments:** Cost of borrowing capital.
        *   **Utilities:** Electricity, water, gas expenses.
        *   **Marketing and Advertising:** Expenses for promoting products.
        *   **Transportation Costs:** Costs of moving raw materials and finished goods.
        *   **Maintenance and Repairs:** Costs to keep machinery and equipment in working order.
        *   **Depreciation (as an accounting charge):** While not a direct cash outlay in a specific period, it represents the gradual wearing out of assets.

*   **Implicit Costs (Opportunity Costs) - *Brief Mention for Contrast*:**
    While the focus of this topic is *private* cost (explicit), it's important to distinguish it from implicit costs. Implicit costs represent the opportunity cost of using resources that the firm already owns. They are not recorded in accounting books but are crucial for economic decision-making. For instance, the salary the owner of a business could have earned by working elsewhere is an implicit cost. However, for the purpose of understanding private cost, we focus on the explicit monetary outflows.

#### 2.3. Classification of Private Costs:

Private costs can also be classified based on their relationship with the level of output:

*   **Fixed Costs (FC):** Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run. These costs are incurred even if the firm produces nothing.
    *   **Examples:** Rent of the factory, salaries of permanent staff, insurance premiums.
    *   **Key Characteristic:** FC exist even at zero output.
    *   *Referenced in:* Thuesen, Fabrycky (1966) and Paneerselvam (2012) extensively discuss fixed costs in the context of engineering project evaluation.

*   **Variable Costs (VC):** Costs that vary directly with the level of output. If output increases, variable costs increase, and vice-versa.
    *   **Examples:** Raw material costs, direct labor wages, fuel costs.
    *   **Key Characteristic:** VC are zero at zero output.
    *   *Referenced in:* Ghosh and Chodhury (2015) emphasize the direct relationship between variable costs and production volume.

*   **Total Cost (TC):** The sum of fixed costs and variable costs at a given level of output.
    *   **Formula:** TC = FC + VC
    *   **Importance:** Represents the overall private cost of production.

#### 2.4. Cost Curves:

Understanding the behavior of fixed and variable costs leads to the concept of cost curves, which are graphical representations of how costs change with output.

*   **Total Cost (TC) Curve:**
    *   Typically upward sloping, reflecting increasing total costs as output rises.
    *   It starts at the level of fixed costs at zero output and increases with variable costs.

*   **Average Fixed Cost (AFC) Curve:**
    *   **Formula:** AFC = FC / Q (where Q is the quantity of output)
    *   **Shape:** Continuously declines as output increases because the fixed cost is spread over a larger number of units. It approaches zero but never reaches it.

*   **Average Variable Cost (AVC) Curve:**
    *   **Formula:** AVC = VC / Q
    *   **Shape:** Typically U-shaped. It initially falls due to increasing efficiency (specialization of labor, better utilization of capital) and then rises due to diminishing marginal returns to variable inputs.
    *   *Referenced in:* Park (2001) provides detailed graphical analysis of AVC and its U-shaped nature.

*   **Average Total Cost (ATC) Curve:**
    *   **Formula:** ATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC
    *   **Shape:** Also typically U-shaped. It is the sum of AFC (declining) and AVC (U-shaped). The minimum point of the ATC curve is higher than the minimum point of the AVC curve.
    *   *Referenced in:* Geetika, Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015) dedicate significant attention to the derivation and interpretation of the ATC curve.

*   **Marginal Cost (MC) Curve:**
    *   **Definition:** The additional cost incurred by producing one more unit of output.
    *   **Formula:** MC = ΔTC / ΔQ (Change in Total Cost divided by Change in Quantity) or the derivative of the Total Cost function with respect to Q.
    *   **Shape:** Typically U-shaped and intersects both the AVC and ATC curves at their minimum points. This intersection point is a critical concept for profit maximization.
    *   *Referenced in:* Newman and Lavelle (2002) explain the relationship between MC and AVC/ATC curves, highlighting their importance in production decisions.

#### 2.5. Cost in Engineering Decision Making:

*   **Relevance:** Understanding private costs is fundamental for engineers when making decisions related to:
    *   **Project Feasibility:** Calculating the cost of producing a product or implementing a project.
    *   **Cost-Benefit Analysis:** Comparing the costs incurred with the benefits derived.
    *   **Break-Even Analysis:** Determining the output level at which total revenue equals total cost (no profit, no loss).
    *   **Make-or-Buy Decisions:** Evaluating whether to produce a component in-house or purchase it from an external supplier.
    *   **Process Optimization:** Identifying cost-saving opportunities by analyzing the behavior of different cost components.
    *   **Capital Budgeting:** Estimating future costs associated with investment proposals.
    *   *Referenced in:* Blank and Tarquin (2007) and Thuesen, Fabrycky (1966) provide numerous examples of cost analysis in engineering economic evaluations.

### 3. Examples:

**Example 1: Calculating Total Private Cost**

A small manufacturing firm produces widgets.
*   **Fixed Costs (per month):**
    *   Rent for the factory: $5,000
    *   Salaries for administrative staff: $10,000
    *   Depreciation on machinery: $2,000
    *   **Total Fixed Costs (TFC) = $5,000 + $10,000 + $2,000 = $17,000**
*   **Variable Costs (per widget):**
    *   Raw materials: $5
    *   Direct labor wages: $8
    *   Utilities (power per widget): $2
    *   **Total Variable Cost per widget (TVC/Q) = $5 + $8 + $2 = $15**

If the firm produces 1,000 widgets in a month:
*   **Total Variable Costs (TVC) = $15/widget * 1,000 widgets = $15,000**
*   **Total Cost (TC) = TFC + TVC = $17,000 + $15,000 = $32,000**

**Example 2: Cost Curves Behavior**

Consider a firm with the following cost structure:
*   Fixed Costs (FC) = $100 (regardless of output)
*   Variable Costs (VC) = $10 * Q$ (where Q is the number of units produced)

Let's calculate costs for different output levels:

| Quantity (Q) | Fixed Cost (FC) | Variable Cost (VC) = 10*Q | Total Cost (TC) = FC + VC | Average Fixed Cost (AFC) = FC/Q | Average Variable Cost (AVC) = VC/Q | Average Total Cost (ATC) = TC/Q | Marginal Cost (MC) = ΔTC/ΔQ |
| :----------- | :-------------- | :------------------------ | :------------------------ | :------------------------------ | :--------------------------------- | :------------------------------ | :-------------------------- |
| 0            | $100            | $0                        | $100                      | -                               | -                                  | -                               | -                           |
| 1            | $100            | $10                       | $110                      | $100.00                         | $10.00                             | $110.00                         | $10                         |
| 2            | $100            | $20                       | $120                      | $50.00                          | $10.00                             | $60.00                          | $10                         |

**Observation:** In this simplified linear VC example, AVC and MC are constant ($10). In reality, VC often increase at an increasing rate beyond a certain output, leading to U-shaped AVC and MC curves.

### 4. Practice Questions/Exercises:

**Question 1:**
Define private cost and list three common examples of explicit costs incurred by a manufacturing firm.

**Question 2:**
A company has total fixed costs of $20,000 per month. If it produces 500 units in a month, what is its Average Fixed Cost (AFC)? If production increases to 1,000 units, what will the AFC be?

**Question 3:**
Given the following data for a firm:
*   Total Fixed Costs = $15,000
*   Variable Costs for producing 100 units = $5,000
*   Variable Costs for producing 200 units = $12,000

Calculate the Total Cost for producing 100 units and 200 units. Also, calculate the Marginal Cost of increasing production from 100 units to 200 units.

**Question 4:**
Explain why Average Fixed Cost (AFC) always declines as output increases.

---

### 5. Answers to Practice Questions:

**Answer 1:**
Private cost is the direct monetary outlay or expenditure incurred by a firm to produce a good or service. It represents the explicit costs that appear in accounting records.
Examples of explicit costs include:
1.  Wages and salaries paid to employees.
2.  Cost of raw materials.
3.  Rent paid for factory premises.

**Answer 2:**
AFC = FC / Q
*   For 500 units: AFC = $20,000 / 500 units = $40 per unit.
*   For 1,000 units: AFC = $20,000 / 1,000 units = $20 per unit.

**Answer 3:**
*   **For 100 units:**
    *   Total Cost (TC) = Total Fixed Costs (TFC) + Variable Costs (VC)
    *   TC = $15,000 + $5,000 = $20,000
*   **For 200 units:**
    *   TC = $15,000 + $12,000 = $27,000
*   **Marginal Cost (MC):**
    *   MC = Change in Total Cost / Change in Quantity
    *   MC = ($27,000 - $20,000) / (200 units - 100 units)
    *   MC = $7,000 / 100 units = $70 per unit

**Answer 4:**
Average Fixed Cost (AFC) is calculated by dividing Total Fixed Costs (TFC) by the quantity of output (Q). Since Total Fixed Costs remain constant in the short run, as the quantity of output (Q) increases, the fixed cost is spread over a larger number of units. This results in a continuously decreasing AFC. Mathematically, AFC = TFC / Q. As Q increases, the denominator grows, causing the value of AFC to fall.

### 6. Important Points to Remember:

*   **Private cost = Explicit cost = Accounting cost.**
*   It represents direct monetary outlays.
*   Key components are Fixed Costs (FC) and Variable Costs (VC).
*   **TC = FC + VC**.
*   AFC always declines with output.
*   AVC and ATC are typically U-shaped due to the law of diminishing marginal returns.
*   MC intersects AVC and ATC at their lowest points.
*   Understanding private costs is fundamental for most engineering economic decisions, including cost-benefit analysis and break-even analysis.
