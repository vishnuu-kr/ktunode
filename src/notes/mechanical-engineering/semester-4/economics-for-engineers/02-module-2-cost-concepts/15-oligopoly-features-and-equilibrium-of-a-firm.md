### 1. Introduction to Oligopoly

Oligopoly is a market structure characterized by a **small number of large firms** that dominate the market. These firms are interdependent, meaning the actions of one firm significantly impact the others. This interdependence is the defining feature of oligopoly.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Few Sellers:** The market is dominated by a handful of firms.
*   **Homogeneous or Differentiated Products:** Products can be identical (e.g., steel, oil) or differentiated (e.g., automobiles, soft drinks).
*   **High Barriers to Entry:** Significant obstacles prevent new firms from entering the market, such as economies of scale, patents, brand loyalty, high capital requirements, and government regulations.
*   **Interdependence:** Firms must consider the reactions of their rivals when making pricing, output, or marketing decisions.
*   **Non-Price Competition:** Firms often compete through advertising, product differentiation, customer service, and innovation rather than solely through price cuts.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015):** This book will provide a comprehensive overview of market structures, including oligopoly, with detailed explanations of its characteristics and implications for firm behavior.

### 2. Features of Oligopoly

Let's delve deeper into the defining characteristics:

*   **Dominance of a Few Firms:** A small number of firms control a substantial majority of the market share.
    *   *Example:* The global automobile industry is dominated by a few major manufacturers. The smartphone operating system market is largely controlled by iOS and Android.
*   **High Barriers to Entry:**
    *   **Economies of Scale:** Existing large firms benefit from lower average costs, making it difficult for new, smaller firms to compete.
    *   **Patents and Proprietary Technology:** Control over crucial technologies can prevent entry.
    *   **Brand Loyalty:** Strong customer recognition and preference for established brands create a barrier.
    *   **Capital Requirements:** Significant investment is needed to establish production facilities and marketing efforts.
    *   **Government Regulations and Licenses:** Certain industries require specific permits and approvals.
*   **Product Differentiation (Often):** While some oligopolies have homogeneous products, many feature differentiated products that appeal to different customer segments.
    *   *Example:* In the soft drink market, Coca-Cola and Pepsi differentiate their products through branding, taste variations, and marketing campaigns.
*   **Mutual Interdependence and Strategic Behavior:** This is the most crucial feature. Firms are like chess players, constantly anticipating and reacting to their opponents' moves.
    *   **"Kinked Demand Curve" Theory:** This model, popularized by Sweezy, suggests that firms in an oligopoly face a demand curve that is relatively elastic for price increases (as rivals won't match) and relatively inelastic for price decreases (as rivals will match to avoid losing market share). This can lead to price rigidity.
    *   **Game Theory:** Oligopoly behavior is often analyzed using game theory, which studies strategic interactions between rational decision-makers. Concepts like Nash Equilibrium are vital here.
*   **Non-Price Competition:** To avoid destructive price wars, oligopolistic firms often engage in non-price competition.
    *   **Advertising and Sales Promotion:** Building brand awareness and customer loyalty.
    *   **Product Improvement and Innovation:** Offering new features and technologies.
    *   **Customer Service and Warranties:** Enhancing the customer experience.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   The degree of interdependence varies. In some oligopolies, firms might tacitly collude, while in others, they engage in fierce competition.
*   The strategic nature of decision-making makes predicting firm behavior challenging.

### 3. Equilibrium of a Firm in Oligopoly

Unlike perfect competition or monopoly, there isn't a single, universally applicable model for oligopoly equilibrium. The outcome depends on the specific assumptions made about how firms react to each other. Here are some prominent models:

#### 3.1. Cournot Duopoly Model

*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Two firms (duopoly) compete on output.
    *   Each firm assumes the other firm's output is fixed when making its own output decision.
    *   Homogeneous product.
    *   Firms aim to maximize profit.
*   **Mechanism:** Each firm determines its profit-maximizing output by considering the output of its rival. This leads to a **reaction curve** for each firm, showing its optimal output for every possible output of the other firm. The intersection of these reaction curves is the **Cournot-Nash equilibrium**.
*   **Equilibrium:** In Cournot equilibrium, both firms produce at the output level where their reaction curves intersect. Neither firm has an incentive to unilaterally change its output. The price is determined by the total market output.
*   **Textbook References:**
    *   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015):** This textbook will likely explain the Cournot model in detail, including graphical representation of reaction curves and derivation of equilibrium output and price.
    *   **Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam (2012):** May offer simplified explanations or applications relevant to engineering decision-making.

#### 3.2. Bertrand Duopoly Model

*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Two firms (duopoly) compete on price.
    *   Homogeneous product.
    *   Firms set prices simultaneously.
    *   Consumers buy from the firm with the lowest price.
*   **Mechanism:** Firms will continuously undercut each other's prices until the price reaches the marginal cost (MC).
*   **Equilibrium:** In the Bertrand model with homogeneous products, the equilibrium price is equal to the marginal cost (P = MC), and both firms earn zero economic profit. This is similar to the outcome in perfect competition, even with only two firms.
*   **Important Point:** If products are differentiated, the Bertrand equilibrium price will be above marginal cost.

#### 3.3. Stackelberg Duopoly Model (Sequential Cournot)

*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Two firms, but one is a **leader** and the other is a **follower**.
    *   The leader firm moves first, setting its output, and the follower firm observes the leader's output and then sets its own output to maximize its profit, given the leader's output.
    *   Homogeneous product.
*   **Mechanism:** The leader firm anticipates the follower's reaction and incorporates the follower's reaction curve into its own profit maximization problem. The leader chooses its output to maximize its profit, knowing how the follower will respond.
*   **Equilibrium:** The Stackelberg leader produces more and earns higher profits than in the Cournot model. The follower produces less and earns lower profits.
*   **Textbook References:**
    *   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015):** Will likely discuss this model as a variation of the Cournot model, highlighting the strategic advantage of being a first-mover.

#### 3.4. Kinked Demand Curve Model (Sweezy Model)

*   **Assumptions:**
    *   Firms are hesitant to change prices due to the fear of competitor reactions.
    *   If a firm raises its price, rivals will not follow, and the firm will lose significant market share (elastic demand above the current price).
    *   If a firm lowers its price, rivals will also lower their prices, leading to a price war where the firm gains little market share (inelastic demand below the current price).
*   **Mechanism:** This creates a "kink" in the firm's perceived demand curve at the current price. The marginal revenue (MR) curve is discontinuous at this kink.
*   **Equilibrium:** A firm operating at the kink will produce at the output level where its MC curve intersects the discontinuous part of the MR curve. This model suggests that firms in an oligopoly tend to maintain stable prices even when their costs change, as long as the MC curve remains within the gap in the MR curve.
*   **Limitations:** This model explains price rigidity but doesn't explain how the initial price is set or what happens when costs change significantly.

### 4. Application of Cost and Revenue Concepts in Oligopoly Equilibrium

To determine the equilibrium output and price for a firm in an oligopoly, we use the fundamental economic principle of **profit maximization**: producing at the output level where **Marginal Revenue (MR) equals Marginal Cost (MC)**.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Total Revenue (TR):** Price (P) x Quantity (Q)
*   **Average Revenue (AR):** TR / Q = P (The demand curve represents the AR)
*   **Marginal Revenue (MR):** Change in TR from selling one more unit.
*   **Total Cost (TC):** Fixed Costs (FC) + Variable Costs (VC)
*   **Average Total Cost (ATC):** TC / Q
*   **Marginal Cost (MC):** Change in TC from producing one more unit.

**Procedure for Finding Equilibrium (General Approach):**

1.  **Determine the Demand Curve:** This is tricky in oligopoly due to interdependence. The shape of the demand curve depends on the strategic assumptions about competitors' reactions.
2.  **Derive the Marginal Revenue (MR) Curve:** Based on the demand curve.
3.  **Determine the Marginal Cost (MC) Curve:** Based on the firm's cost structure. This will be similar to other market structures.
4.  **Find the Profit-Maximizing Output:** Set MR = MC.
5.  **Determine the Equilibrium Price:** From the demand curve (AR curve) at the profit-maximizing output.
6.  **Calculate Profit:** Profit = (Price - ATC) x Quantity.

**Textbook References:**

*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015):** Essential for understanding the derivation of MR from different demand curve shapes and applying the MR=MC rule.
*   **Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam (2012):** Can provide practical insights into calculating costs (MC, ATC) relevant for these decisions.
*   **Engineering Economics by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky (1966):** While older, foundational concepts of cost behavior and optimization are still relevant.
*   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park (2001):** Offers an engineering perspective on economic decision-making, which can be applied to firm-level analysis in any market structure.

**Example:**

Consider a duopoly where Firm A believes Firm B will produce 100 units. Firm A's demand curve (and MR curve) will depend on this assumption. If Firm A's MC curve is known, it can find its profit-maximizing output by setting its MR equal to its MC. However, Firm B will be doing the same, and the interaction leads to the equilibrium.

### 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**

Which of the following is NOT a key feature of oligopoly?
a) A small number of dominant firms.
b) Significant barriers to entry.
c) Firms are price takers.
d) Interdependence among firms.

**Answer:** c) Firms are price takers. In an oligopoly, firms have some market power and are not price takers.

**Question 2:**

In the Cournot duopoly model, each firm:
a) Assumes the other firm's price is fixed.
b) Assumes the other firm's output is fixed.
c) Sets its price to match the competitor's price.
d) Tries to drive the competitor out of the market.

**Answer:** b) Assumes the other firm's output is fixed. This is the core assumption of the Cournot model.

**Question 3:**

The Kinked Demand Curve model suggests that firms in an oligopoly are most likely to:
a) Engage in frequent price wars.
b) Radically change their prices often.
c) Maintain stable prices, even with cost fluctuations.
d) Act as price takers in the market.

**Answer:** c) Maintain stable prices, even with cost fluctuations. The kinked demand curve explains price rigidity.

**Question 4 (Application):**

Suppose a firm in a duopoly faces the following demand and cost functions:
Demand: $P = 100 - Q$ (where Q is the total market output, $Q = q_A + q_B$)
Firm A's Cost: $TC_A = 10 \cdot q_A$

Assume Firm B's output is fixed at $q_B = 20$.

a) Determine Firm A's demand curve, total revenue (TR_A), and marginal revenue (MR_A).
b) Determine Firm A's marginal cost (MC_A).
c) Calculate Firm A's profit-maximizing output ($q_A$) and the resulting price (P).

**Solution:**

a) **Firm A's Demand Curve:**
Given $q_B = 20$, Firm A's demand is $P = 100 - (q_A + 20) = 80 - q_A$.
This is Firm A's perceived demand curve.

**Firm A's Total Revenue (TR_A):**
$TR_A = P \cdot q_A = (80 - q_A) \cdot q_A = 80q_A - q_A^2$.

**Firm A's Marginal Revenue (MR_A):**
Differentiating TR_A with respect to $q_A$:
$MR_A = \frac{d(TR_A)}{dq_A} = 80 - 2q_A$.

b) **Firm A's Marginal Cost (MC_A):**
Given $TC_A = 10 \cdot q_A$, the marginal cost is the derivative of the total cost with respect to $q_A$:
$MC_A = \frac{d(TC_A)}{dq_A} = 10$.

c) **Profit-Maximizing Output and Price:**
Set $MR_A = MC_A$:
$80 - 2q_A = 10$
$70 = 2q_A$
$q_A = 35$ units.

Now, find the price using Firm A's perceived demand curve:
$P = 80 - q_A = 80 - 35 = 45$.

**Equilibrium:** Firm A's profit-maximizing output is 35 units, and the price is $45, assuming Firm B produces 20 units.

### 6. Important Points to Remember:

*   **Interdependence is King:** The defining characteristic of oligopoly is how firms react to each other's actions.
*   **No Single Solution:** Unlike simpler market structures, there isn't one definitive model. The equilibrium depends on the assumptions made about competitor behavior.
*   **Strategic Decision-Making:** Firms must constantly think ahead and consider the consequences of their decisions on rivals.
*   **Barriers to Entry:** These are crucial for maintaining the oligopolistic structure.
*   **Non-Price Competition:** A common strategy to avoid price wars and build brand loyalty.
*   **Game Theory:** A powerful tool for analyzing strategic interactions in oligopolies.
