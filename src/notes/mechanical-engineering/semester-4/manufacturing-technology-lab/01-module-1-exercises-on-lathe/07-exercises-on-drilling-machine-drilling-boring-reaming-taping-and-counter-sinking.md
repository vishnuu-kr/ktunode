# Manufacturing Technology Lab: Module 1 - Exercises on Drilling Machine

This module focuses on the fundamental operations performed on a drilling machine, crucial for creating holes and modifying existing ones in various manufacturing processes.

## 1. Introduction to the Drilling Machine

The drilling machine (also known as a drill press or pillar drill) is a machine tool used for drilling holes in metals, plastics, and other materials. It operates by rotating a cutting tool (drill bit) and feeding it into a stationary workpiece.

**Key Components of a Drilling Machine:**
*   **Base:** Provides stability to the machine.
*   **Column:** Supports the drilling head.
*   **Drilling Head:** Contains the spindle, motor, and feed mechanisms.
*   **Spindle:** Rotates the cutting tool.
*   **Chuck:** Holds the drill bit.
*   **Drill Table:** Supports the workpiece.
*   **Feed Mechanism:** Controls the rate at which the drill bit enters the workpiece (manual or automatic).
*   **Motor:** Provides rotational power to the spindle.

**Types of Drilling Machines:**
*   **Bench Drill:** Smaller, mounted on a workbench.
*   **Pedestal Drill:** Larger, floor-mounted.
*   **Radial Drill:** Versatile with an adjustable arm.
*   **Gang Drill:** Multiple spindles for simultaneous operations.

**Reference:**
*   *Workshop Technology Part I* by W. A. J. Chapman provides a detailed explanation of the construction and working principles of drilling machines.

## 2. Drilling

Drilling is the process of producing a cylindrical hole of a specific diameter in a workpiece using a rotating cutting tool called a drill bit.

**Drill Bits:**
*   **Twist Drill:** The most common type, with helical flutes for chip removal.
    *   **Point Angle:** The angle at the tip of the drill, typically 118° for general-purpose drilling in mild steel. Other angles are used for different materials (e.g., 90° for soft materials, 135° for harder materials).
    *   **Helix Angle:** The angle of the flutes, affecting chip evacuation and cutting action.
    *   **Web Thickness:** The central portion of the drill that supports the cutting edges.
*   **Center Drill:** Used to create a starting point and chamfer for subsequent drilling or turning operations.

**Drilling Process:**
1.  **Workpiece Setup:** Secure the workpiece firmly to the drill table using clamps or a vise. Ensure the drilling location is accurately marked.
2.  **Tool Selection:** Choose the appropriate drill bit size and type.
3.  **Spindle Speed (RPM):** Select the correct spindle speed based on the drill bit diameter, material being drilled, and cutting fluid used. A general formula for cutting speed is:
    $V = \frac{\pi D N}{1000}$
    where:
    *   $V$ = Cutting speed (m/min)
    *   $D$ = Drill diameter (mm)
    *   $N$ = Spindle speed (RPM)
    **Important Point:** Lower speeds are generally used for larger diameter drills and harder materials.
4.  **Feed Rate:** Control the rate at which the drill advances into the workpiece. This is often done manually using the handwheel.
    **Important Point:** Excessive feed can lead to tool breakage or poor hole quality.
5.  **Cutting Fluid:** Apply cutting fluid (e.g., soluble oil, cutting oil) to cool the drill bit and workpiece, lubricate the cutting action, and flush away chips.
6.  **Drilling:** Engage the drill and feed it into the workpiece. For deeper holes, perform "peck drilling," where the drill is periodically withdrawn to clear chips.

**Learning Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1:** Operating the drilling machine, selecting appropriate drill bits, and setting speeds/feeds to produce holes of desired quality.
*   **CO2:** Understanding the relationship between spindle speed, feed, and tool diameter for efficient drilling.

**Example:** Drilling a 10mm diameter hole in a mild steel block.
*   **Material:** Mild Steel
*   **Drill Diameter (D):** 10 mm
*   **Recommended Cutting Speed (V):** 20-30 m/min (Refer to material cutting data charts)
*   **Spindle Speed (N):**
    $N = \frac{1000 \times V}{\pi \times D} = \frac{1000 \times 25}{\pi \times 10} \approx 796$ RPM.
    Select a nearby speed setting on the drilling machine, e.g., 800 RPM.

**Practice Question:** What spindle speed should be used to drill a 6mm diameter hole in aluminum if the recommended cutting speed is 40 m/min?
**Answer:**
$N = \frac{1000 \times V}{\pi \times D} = \frac{1000 \times 40}{\pi \times 6} \approx 2122$ RPM.

## 3. Boring

Boring is the process of enlarging an existing hole to a precise diameter and improving its surface finish and accuracy. It uses a single-point cutting tool.

**Purpose of Boring:**
*   Achieve accurate hole diameter.
*   Improve hole surface finish.
*   Correctly position a hole relative to other features.
*   Enlarge a previously drilled or cast hole.

**Boring Tools:**
*   Boring bars with single-point cutting tools.
*   Adjustable boring heads for fine diameter adjustments.

**Boring Process:**
1.  **Existing Hole:** A pilot hole (smaller than the final diameter) must exist.
2.  **Tool Setup:** Mount the boring tool in the drilling machine spindle (or a dedicated boring head). The cutting tool should be set at the correct height and extend the correct amount.
3.  **Workpiece Setup:** Secure the workpiece to the drill table.
4.  **Spindle Speed and Feed:** Select appropriate speed and feed rates, typically lower than drilling for precision.
5.  **Boring:** Feed the boring tool into the existing hole, rotating it to remove material and enlarge the hole to the desired size. Multiple passes might be required for large diameter increases.

**Learning Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1:** Producing accurate and smooth internal features.

**Example:** Enlarging a 15mm drilled hole to 20mm using a boring bar.
*   The drill bit used for the pilot hole would be slightly smaller than 15mm (e.g., 14.5mm).
*   The boring tool is set to cut from 15mm up to 20mm, often in several passes to avoid excessive cutting forces and ensure accuracy.

**Important Point:** Boring is a finishing operation for holes, focusing on accuracy and surface finish rather than just material removal.

## 4. Reaming

Reaming is a finishing operation used to enlarge a pre-drilled or bored hole to its final precise size and to improve its surface finish and accuracy. It uses a multi-fluted cutting tool called a reamer.

**Purpose of Reaming:**
*   Achieve very close dimensional tolerance.
*   Produce a smooth, polished internal surface.
*   Improve roundness and straightness of the hole.

**Reamers:**
*   **Hand Reamer:** Operated manually.
*   **Machine Reamer:** Designed for use in drilling machines or other machine tools.
    *   **Types:** Straight fluted, spiral fluted, expansion reamer, adjustable reamer.
    *   **Cutting Action:** Reamers remove a very small amount of material (a few thousandths of an inch) with a shearing action, not a continuous chip.

**Reaming Process:**
1.  **Pre-drilled/Bored Hole:** A hole that is slightly undersized (typically 0.05mm to 0.2mm smaller than the final size) is required.
2.  **Tool Selection:** Choose the correct size and type of reamer.
3.  **Workpiece Setup:** Secure the workpiece.
4.  **Spindle Speed:** Use a significantly **slower** spindle speed than for drilling (typically 1/3 to 1/2 the drilling speed).
5.  **Feed Rate:** Use a steady, consistent feed rate, often manual.
6.  **Reaming:** Feed the reamer into the hole. **Do not rotate the workpiece.** The reamer should be fed straight into the hole. Avoid reversing the reamer, as this can damage the hole or the reamer.
7.  **Cutting Fluid:** Use cutting fluid for lubrication and cooling.

**Learning Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1:** Achieving high accuracy and surface finish in hole production.

**Example:** Reaming a 20mm hole to a final tolerance of ±0.01mm.
*   The hole would first be drilled to, say, 19.8mm.
*   Then, it might be bored to 19.95mm for better surface finish before reaming.
*   The 20mm reamer is then used at a slow speed to achieve the final size and finish.

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   Reaming is a finishing operation; it is not for removing large amounts of material.
*   Use slow speeds and steady feed.
*   Do not reverse a reamer in a hole.
*   Ensure the hole is properly sized and accurately positioned before reaming.

## 5. Tapping

Tapping is the process of creating internal screw threads within a pre-drilled hole.

**Purpose of Tapping:**
*   To create threaded holes for fastening components with bolts or screws.

**Taps:**
*   **Taper Tap:** Has a chamfered end for starting.
*   **Plug Tap:** Less chamfered than a taper tap.
*   **Bottoming Tap:** Has a square end to thread a hole to the bottom.
*   **Machine Tap:** Designed for use in a machine tool, often with a straight shank or spiral flutes for chip evacuation.

**Tapping Process (Using a Drilling Machine):**
1.  **Drilled Hole:** A hole of the correct diameter (tap drill size) must be drilled first. The tap drill size is critical for creating good threads.
    *   Formula for tap drill size: $D_{drill} = D_{thread} - P_{thread}$ (where $D_{thread}$ is the major diameter and $P_{thread}$ is the pitch). For standard threads, pitch can be found in thread charts.
2.  **Workpiece Setup:** Secure the workpiece.
3.  **Tap Setup:** Mount the tap in the drill chuck. It's highly recommended to use a **tap holder with a floating shank** or a **tap collet** to prevent tap breakage due to misalignment.
4.  **Spindle Speed:** Use **very slow** spindle speeds, often the slowest setting available.
5.  **Lubrication:** Apply a suitable lubricant (tapping fluid) for better thread formation and tool life.
6.  **Tapping:** Gently feed the tap into the hole. For manual control, use the handwheel. Rotate the tap into the hole until it engages the threads. Then, rotate it forward about 3/4 to 1 full turn, and then **backwards 1/2 to 3/4 turn** to break the chip and clear the flutes. Repeat this process until the desired thread depth is achieved.
    **Important Point:** The back-and-forth motion is crucial to prevent tap breakage.
7.  **Chamfering:** The top of the tapped hole is usually chamfered to facilitate screw insertion.

**Learning Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1:** Creating threaded features for assembly.

**Example:** Tapping a M8 x 1.25 pitch thread in steel.
*   **Major Diameter (D):** 8 mm
*   **Pitch (P):** 1.25 mm
*   **Tap Drill Size:** $8 - 1.25 = 6.75$ mm. A standard 6.7mm or 6.8mm drill bit would be used.
*   The tapping operation would be performed at a very slow speed, with intermittent backing out to clear chips.

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   Always use the correct tap drill size.
*   Use a tap holder with a floating shank to prevent breakage.
*   Use slow speeds and lubricant.
*   Perform the back-and-forth tapping motion to break chips.

## 6. Countersinking

Countersinking is a machining process used to create a conical enlargement at the opening of a drilled hole. This is done to allow the head of a countersunk screw or bolt to sit flush with or below the surface of the workpiece.

**Purpose of Countersinking:**
*   To recess the head of a countersunk screw or bolt.
*   To provide a bearing surface for the screw head.
*   To deburr the edge of a hole.

**Countersinks:**
*   **Countersink Cutters:** Tools with multiple cutting edges, typically with a 60°, 82°, or 90° included angle. The angle chosen depends on the type of screw being used.

**Countersinking Process:**
1.  **Drilled Hole:** A hole must already exist.
2.  **Workpiece Setup:** Secure the workpiece.
3.  **Countersink Tool Setup:** Mount the countersink in the drill chuck.
4.  **Spindle Speed:** Use a moderate spindle speed, generally **slower than drilling**, as the cutting edges are more delicate.
5.  **Feed Rate:** Feed the countersink into the hole using the handwheel. Apply steady pressure.
6.  **Depth Control:** The depth of the countersink is controlled by how far the tool is fed into the hole. It's important to achieve the correct depth for the screw head to sit properly. A stop mechanism on the drilling machine or careful manual control is used.

**Learning Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1:** Creating specific geometric features for component assembly.

**Example:** Creating a recess for a 90° countersunk screw in a metal plate.
*   A hole of the appropriate clearance diameter for the screw shank is drilled first.
*   A 90° countersink cutter is then used to create the conical recess to a suitable depth.

**Important Point:** The angle of the countersink must match the angle of the screw head.

## 7. Counterboring

Counterboring is a machining process used to enlarge the entrance of a drilled hole to a specific diameter and depth, creating a flat-bottomed recess. This is typically done to allow the head of a socket head cap screw or bolt to sit flush with or below the surface.

**Purpose of Counterboring:**
*   To recess the head of socket head cap screws or bolts.
*   To create a flat bearing surface for the screw head.

**Counterbores:**
*   **Counterbore Cutters:** Tools with a pilot that fits into the existing hole and a cutting diameter that creates the larger recess. They have multiple cutting edges and produce a flat bottom.

**Counterboring Process:**
1.  **Drilled Hole:** A pilot hole of the same diameter as the counterbore's pilot must exist.
2.  **Workpiece Setup:** Secure the workpiece.
3.  **Counterbore Tool Setup:** Mount the counterbore in the drill chuck.
4.  **Spindle Speed:** Use moderate spindle speeds, similar to countersinking, but be mindful of the cutting load.
5.  **Feed Rate:** Feed the counterbore into the hole. The pilot guides the tool. Apply steady pressure.
6.  **Depth Control:** The depth of the counterbore is usually pre-set by the tool itself or controlled by a stop.

**Learning Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1:** Creating specific geometric features for component assembly.

**Example:** Creating a recess for a socket head cap screw in a metal bracket.
*   A clearance hole for the screw shank is drilled first.
*   A counterbore tool with a pilot matching the drilled hole diameter and a cutting diameter suitable for the screw head is used to create the flat-bottomed recess.

**Important Point:** Counterboring creates a flat-bottomed recess, unlike countersinking which creates a conical recess.

## 8. Numerical Control (NC) and CNC Drilling Machines (Brief Introduction)

While this module focuses on manual drilling machine operations, it's important to note the evolution towards automated processes.

*   **Numerical Control (NC):** A system that uses numerical codes to control the machine tool's movements and operations.
*   **Computer Numerical Control (CNC):** An advancement of NC, where a dedicated computer controls the machine. CNC drilling machines can automatically perform drilling, reaming, tapping, countersinking, and complex path drilling based on programmed instructions.

**Learning Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO3:** This module provides the fundamental understanding of hole-making operations that are then programmed and executed on CNC machines for complex profile manufacturing.

**Reference:**
*   *Numerical Control of Machine Tools* by Yoram Koren discusses the principles and applications of NC and CNC machining, including drilling.

## 9. Safety Precautions for Drilling Machines

*   **Eye Protection:** Always wear safety glasses.
*   **Clothing:** Wear appropriate workshop attire, avoid loose clothing, jewelry, and tie back long hair.
*   **Workpiece Security:** Ensure the workpiece is securely clamped to the table. Never hold small workpieces by hand.
*   **Tool Security:** Make sure the drill bit or other cutting tool is securely held in the chuck.
*   **Chucks Keys:** Remove chuck keys immediately after use.
*   **Chips:** Do not remove chips with your hands; use a brush.
*   **Moving Parts:** Keep hands and fingers away from rotating parts.
*   **Tool Breakage:** Be aware of the possibility of tool breakage, especially with small drills or taps.
*   **Emergency Stop:** Know the location of the emergency stop button.

## Practice Exercises

1.  **Drilling:** Given a mild steel workpiece, drill a 10mm diameter hole. Specify the recommended spindle speed and explain the importance of using cutting fluid.
2.  **Reaming:** You need to ream a hole to a final diameter of 25mm with a tolerance of $\pm$0.005mm. What would be the approximate size of the hole before reaming, and what spindle speed would you recommend compared to drilling?
3.  **Tapping:** Describe the process of tapping a 1/4" UNC thread in an aluminum block using a drilling machine. What are the critical steps to prevent tap breakage?
4.  **Countersinking vs. Counterboring:** Explain the difference between countersinking and counterboring and when each process would be used.

---

**Answers to Practice Exercises:**

1.  **Drilling:**
    *   **Spindle Speed:** Referencing a chart for mild steel and a 10mm drill bit, a cutting speed of 25 m/min is typical.
        $N = \frac{1000 \times 25}{\pi \times 10} \approx 796$ RPM. So, approximately 800 RPM would be suitable.
    *   **Cutting Fluid:** Cutting fluid is important for:
        *   Cooling the drill bit and workpiece, preventing overheating and tool wear.
        *   Lubricating the cutting edges, reducing friction and improving surface finish.
        *   Flushing away chips from the cutting zone, preventing chip recutting and tool damage.

2.  **Reaming:**
    *   **Pre-reaming Size:** The hole would typically be about 0.05mm to 0.2mm undersized. For a 25mm hole, a good pre-reaming size might be around 24.85mm or 24.9mm.
    *   **Spindle Speed:** The spindle speed for reaming should be significantly slower than for drilling, typically 1/3 to 1/2 of the drilling speed. If drilling at 800 RPM, reaming might be done at around 250-400 RPM.

3.  **Tapping:**
    *   **Process:**
        1.  Drill a hole of the correct tap drill size (for 1/4" UNC, the major diameter is approx 6.35mm, and the pitch is approx 1.06mm; tap drill size is around 5.29mm, so a 5.3mm or 5.4mm drill bit would be used).
        2.  Secure the aluminum block to the drill table.
        3.  Mount the tap in a tap holder with a floating shank in the drill chuck.
        4.  Set the drilling machine to its slowest spindle speed.
        5.  Apply tapping fluid to the tap and the hole.
        6.  Carefully feed the tap into the hole, using the handwheel. Rotate the tap about 3/4 to 1 full turn, then back it out 1/2 to 3/4 turn to break the chip. Repeat this sequence until the desired thread depth is achieved.
    *   **Preventing Tap Breakage:**
        *   Use the correct tap drill size.
        *   Use a floating tap holder.
        *   Use slow speeds and lubricant.
        *   Perform the back-and-forth tapping motion regularly to clear chips.
        *   Ensure the tap is entering the hole perpendicularly.

4.  **Countersinking vs. Counterboring:**
    *   **Countersinking:** Creates a **conical** recess at the opening of a hole. It is used for screws with conical heads (e.g., 82° or 90° countersunk screws) so that the screw head sits flush with or slightly below the surface. The angle of the countersink tool matches the angle of the screw head.
    *   **Counterboring:** Creates a **flat-bottomed** cylindrical recess at the opening of a hole. It is used for screws with cylindrical heads (e.g., socket head cap screws or bolts) to allow the head to sit flush with or below the surface. The counterbore tool has a pilot to guide it into the existing hole and creates a specific diameter and depth of recess.
