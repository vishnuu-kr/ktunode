# TECHNOLOGY MANAGEMENT: MODULE 3 - IMPLICATIONS IN TECHNOLOGY CHANGES: PRODUCTION FUNCTION AND TECHNOLOGY CHANGE

## TOPIC: EROSION OF COMPETITIVE MISMATCH IN DEVELOPING COUNTRIES

---

### **1. Introduction: Understanding Competitive Mismatch**

*   **Definition:** Competitive mismatch occurs when a firm's existing competencies, resources, or organizational structures are poorly aligned with the demands of a new technological paradigm or market shift. This misalignment leads to a loss of competitive advantage, even for historically successful firms.
*   **Context in Developing Countries:** Developing countries often face unique challenges and opportunities related to technological adoption. They might be leapfrogging older technologies, directly adopting advanced ones, or struggling with the implications of global technological shifts.
*   **Key Idea:** Technological change is not neutral; it often favors new entrants or firms that can adapt their internal "production function" (how they create value) to match the new technological possibilities.

---

### **2. Technology Changes and the Production Function**

*   **Production Function (Classical Economics):** A mathematical relationship between inputs (labor, capital, materials) and output (goods/services).
*   **Production Function (Technology Management Perspective):** Encompasses not just physical inputs and outputs, but also:
    *   **Processes:** How value is created, from R&D to manufacturing to delivery.
    *   **Knowledge & Skills:** The human capital and expertise required.
    *   **Organizational Structures:** How teams are organized and information flows.
    *   **Infrastructure:** The physical and digital systems supporting operations.
*   **Impact of Technology Change:** New technologies fundamentally alter the *optimal* way to combine these elements. They can:
    *   **Increase efficiency:** Produce more output with fewer inputs.
    *   **Create new types of output:** Goods or services previously impossible.
    *   **Require new skills and knowledge:** Demanding a shift in human capital.
    *   **Necessitate new organizational structures:** E.g., agile teams for rapid product development.
    *   **Render existing infrastructure obsolete.**

### **3. Erosion of Competitive Mismatch in Developing Countries: Key Drivers**

This section directly addresses **CO4** (Critically assess significant technological changes...effects on human needs, while exploring dimensions of technology management and its importance in business planning and competition) and **CO5** (Synthesize the implications of technological changes, including production function dynamics...and competitive landscapes...).

*   **Rapid Technological Adoption (Leapfrogging):**
    *   **Concept:** Developing countries can sometimes bypass intermediate technologies and adopt the latest ones directly (e.g., mobile banking instead of traditional branch banking).
    *   **Mismatch Erosion:** This can erode the advantage of established firms in developed countries that are still invested in older infrastructure and business models. For developing countries, this offers an opportunity to build competitive advantage quickly.
    *   **Example (Narayanan, 2002):** The rapid adoption of mobile telephony in many African nations, bypassing landline infrastructure, created new business models and eroded the dominance of traditional telecom giants.

*   **Globalized Supply Chains and Outsourcing:**
    *   **Concept:** Multinational corporations (MNCs) leverage technological advancements to optimize global production. They can establish manufacturing or service centers in developing countries, bringing with them new technologies and processes.
    *   **Mismatch Erosion:**
        *   **For Host Countries:** Local firms that can align with these global standards and integrate into these supply chains can rapidly upgrade their capabilities and reduce their "mismatch."
        *   **For Developed Country Firms:** If they fail to adapt to efficient global production models or lose access to skilled labor in developing nations, they can face a mismatch.
    *   **Example (Rastogi, 2009):** The rise of IT-enabled services (BPO, KPO) in India and the Philippines allowed these countries to build expertise in areas where Western firms might have had legacy systems or higher labor costs, leading to a shift in competitive advantage.

*   **Emergence of New Business Models:**
    *   **Concept:** Technology enables entirely new ways of delivering value, often with lower barriers to entry.
    *   **Mismatch Erosion:** Firms that are locked into traditional business models based on older technologies find themselves at a disadvantage.
    *   **Example (Burgelman, Christensen, Wheelright, 2009):** E-commerce platforms have disrupted traditional retail. Developing country entrepreneurs, unburdened by legacy brick-and-mortar operations, can leverage these new models more effectively. Consider the rise of online marketplaces in Southeast Asia.

*   **Access to Information and Knowledge:**
    *   **Concept:** The internet and digital platforms provide unprecedented access to knowledge, best practices, and technical expertise.
    *   **Mismatch Erosion:** Firms in developing countries can learn from global leaders and adopt best practices much faster than in the past. This reduces the knowledge gap that historically contributed to competitive mismatch.
    *   **Example (Trott, 2009):** Online courses, technical forums, and open-source software allow engineers and managers in developing countries to acquire skills and knowledge that were once the exclusive domain of developed-country firms.

*   **Adaptability and Agility:**
    *   **Concept:** Smaller, more agile firms or those unburdened by legacy systems often find it easier to adapt to technological change.
    *   **Mismatch Erosion:** This is particularly relevant in developing countries where entrepreneurial ecosystems might be more dynamic, or where firms are starting from scratch and can build capabilities around new technologies from the outset.
    *   **Example (Tushman & Anderson, 2004):** Disruptive technologies often emerge from outside the established industry. Start-ups in developing countries, unhindered by the "tyranny of success" of incumbent firms, can exploit these disruptions. Think of fintech startups in Nigeria bypassing traditional banking infrastructure.

### **4. Implications for Production Function and Competitive Advantage in Developing Countries**

This section builds on **CO4** and **CO5** by linking technology change, production function, and competitive advantage.

*   **Redefining "Best Practice":** What constitutes an efficient production function is no longer static or solely defined by developed countries. Developing countries can define their own "best practices" based on their unique technological adoption patterns and market needs.
*   **Focus on Capabilities:** Instead of solely focusing on physical assets, firms in developing countries can build competitive advantage through:
    *   **Technological absorption capabilities:** The ability to acquire, adapt, and utilize new technologies effectively (as per **CO5**).
    *   **Learning and adaptation agility.**
    *   **Customer insight in emerging markets.**
*   **Shifting from Factor-Cost Advantage to Technology-Driven Advantage:** While low labor costs have historically been an advantage, sustained competitiveness increasingly depends on the ability to leverage technology to improve productivity, quality, and innovation.
*   **The Role of Government and Policy:**
    *   **Policy Instruments (CO5):** Governments can foster technology adoption through policies like tax incentives for R&D, investments in digital infrastructure, and educational reforms to build a skilled workforce.
    *   **Intellectual Property Rights (CO3):** Balancing IP protection with the need for technology transfer and diffusion is crucial for developing countries.

### **5. Overcoming and Preventing Mismatch: Strategies for Developing Countries**

This section relates to **CO2** (Analyse and apply various management processes...) and **CO3** (Evaluate the role...of financial management, and intellectual property rights...).

*   **Continuous Learning and Skill Development:**
    *   **Action:** Invest in training, upskilling, and reskilling of the workforce to match evolving technological requirements.
    *   **Example:** Government-sponsored vocational training programs in digital skills, collaboration between universities and industries for curriculum development.

*   **Strategic Technology Adoption:**
    *   **Action:** Proactively identify and adopt technologies that align with long-term strategic goals and market opportunities. Avoid adopting technologies simply because they are new.
    *   **Example:** A manufacturing firm in Vietnam identifying Industry 4.0 technologies like IoT and automation to improve export competitiveness.

*   **Fostering Innovation Ecosystems:**
    *   **Action:** Create environments that encourage entrepreneurship, R&D, and collaboration between firms, universities, and research institutions.
    *   **Example:** Setting up innovation hubs, incubators, and science parks.

*   **Building Flexible Organizational Structures:**
    *   **Action:** Design organizations that can adapt quickly to changing technological landscapes and market demands.
    *   **Example:** Adopting agile methodologies in software development or project management.

*   **Leveraging Financial Management:**
    *   **Action:** Secure appropriate funding for technology upgrades, R&D, and human capital development.
    *   **Example:** Utilizing venture capital, government grants, or strategic partnerships for technology investment.

*   **Developing Strong IP Strategies:**
    *   **Action:** Understand and strategically manage intellectual property to protect innovations while facilitating access to necessary technologies.
    *   **Example:** Patenting novel processes, licensing technologies, or engaging in joint R&D.

### **6. Key Concepts and Definitions Recap**

*   **Competitive Mismatch:** A state where a firm's capabilities are misaligned with the demands of a new technological paradigm.
*   **Production Function:** The process and resources used to create value, including inputs, processes, knowledge, skills, and organizational structures.
*   **Technological Leapfrogging:** Adopting advanced technologies directly, skipping intermediate stages.
*   **Technological Absorption:** The capability of a firm or country to effectively acquire, adapt, and utilize new technologies.
*   **Disruptive Technologies:** Innovations that create new markets and value networks, eventually displacing established market-leading firms, products, and alliances. (Tushman & Anderson, 2004)
*   **Capabilities:** The ability of an organization to perform a particular task or function. (Narayanan, 2002)

---

### **7. Important Points to Remember**

*   Technological change is a primary driver of competitive advantage and disadvantage.
*   Developing countries are not passive recipients of technology; they can actively shape their competitive landscape by strategically adopting and adapting technologies.
*   The erosion of competitive mismatch in developing countries is facilitated by globalized knowledge transfer, new business models, and the potential for leapfrogging.
*   A firm's "production function" must evolve alongside technological changes to maintain or gain competitive advantage.
*   Focus on building absorptive capacity, fostering innovation, and developing agile organizational structures.

### **8. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1 (CO4, CO5):** Explain how the concept of "leapfrogging" can contribute to the erosion of competitive mismatch for developing countries in the context of digital technologies. Provide a specific example.

**Answer:** Leapfrogging allows developing countries to bypass legacy infrastructure and adopt the latest digital technologies directly. This can erode the competitive advantage of firms in developed countries that are heavily invested in older systems. For example, many African nations adopted mobile banking (e.g., M-Pesa) directly, bypassing the need for extensive landline infrastructure and traditional bank branches. This allowed them to build competitive financial service models rapidly, eroding the dominance of established Western banks in offering accessible financial services.

**Question 2 (CO2, CO3):** A manufacturing company in India is considering adopting advanced robotics and AI for its production line. Discuss two management processes (e.g., planning, organizing) that this company needs to effectively manage this technological change to prevent competitive mismatch. Also, how might effective financial management support this transition?

**Answer:**
*   **Planning:** The company needs to engage in strategic planning to assess the feasibility of robotics and AI, forecast its impact on production efficiency, identify potential workforce displacement, and plan for necessary training. This also involves financial planning for the significant capital investment required.
*   **Organizing:** The company must reorganize its production processes, potentially creating new roles (e.g., AI specialists, robotics technicians) and retraining existing staff. It may need to restructure teams and reporting lines to integrate these new technologies effectively.
*   **Financial Management:** Sound financial management is crucial for securing funding (loans, investment), budgeting for the capital expenditure, managing operational costs associated with the new technology, and evaluating the return on investment (ROI) to ensure the adoption enhances, rather than detracts from, competitiveness.

**Question 3 (Multiple Choice, CO1, CO5):** Which of the following is LEAST likely to contribute to the erosion of competitive mismatch in developing countries?

    a) Increased access to global R&D networks.
    b) Adoption of legacy technologies with established infrastructure.
    c) Emergence of new digital business models.
    d) Development of local technological absorption capabilities.

**Answer:** b) Adoption of legacy technologies with established infrastructure.

**Explanation:** While adopting technologies is key, adopting *legacy* technologies that are already being phased out in developed markets is unlikely to erode competitive mismatch and may even perpetuate it. The other options represent factors that enable developing countries to gain ground or bypass older systems.
