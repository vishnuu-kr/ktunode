# TURBO MACHINERY: Module 3 - Hydraulic Turbines: Classification

## Introduction to Hydraulic Turbines

Hydraulic turbines are rotary machines that convert the hydraulic energy of a fluid (typically water) into mechanical energy, which is then usually converted into electrical energy by a generator. They are essential components in hydroelectric power plants.

**Key Concept:** The fundamental principle behind hydraulic turbines is the transfer of energy from the moving fluid to the rotating runner through the action of pressure and/or kinetic energy.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   Dixon, S.I. (1999). *Fluid Mechanics and Thermodynamics of Turbomachinery*. Discusses the fundamental principles of energy conversion in turbomachines.
*   Venkanna, B.K. (2009). *Fundamentals of Turbo Machinery*. Provides a foundational understanding of turbomachinery operation.
*   Yahya, S.H. (1996). *Turbines, Compressor and Fans*. Introduces the basic types and operation of turbines.

## Classification of Hydraulic Turbines

Hydraulic turbines can be classified based on various criteria. The most common and important classifications are based on:

1.  **Nature of Hydraulic Energy at Inlet:**
    *   **Impulse Turbines:** The fluid strikes the runner vanes at atmospheric pressure, and the pressure remains constant throughout the fluid's passage through the runner. The entire available energy is converted into kinetic energy in the nozzle before striking the runner.
    *   **Reaction Turbines:** The fluid enters the runner under pressure, and both the pressure and velocity of the fluid change as it passes through the runner. The runner is completely filled with fluid.

2.  **Direction of Flow Through the Runner:**
    *   **Radial Flow Turbines:** The fluid flows radially outwards or inwards through the runner.
    *   **Axial Flow Turbines:** The fluid flows parallel to the axis of rotation of the runner.
    *   **Mixed Flow Turbines:** The fluid enters radially and leaves axially, or vice versa.

3.  **Head Under Which They Operate:**
    *   **High Head Turbines:** Operate under large heads (typically > 250 m).
    *   **Medium Head Turbines:** Operate under medium heads (typically 60 m to 250 m).
    *   **Low Head Turbines:** Operate under low heads (typically < 60 m).

4.  **Specific Speed:**
    *   Specific speed is a dimensionless parameter that characterizes the type of turbine suitable for a given head and flow rate. It is defined as the speed at which a geometrically similar turbine would run to produce unit power under unit head.

**Learning Outcome Covered:** Understanding different types of hydraulic turbines based on their operating characteristics and fluid flow. This directly supports **CO3: Select an appropriate power generating turbomachine for a particular application**.

### 1. Classification Based on Nature of Hydraulic Energy at Inlet

#### 1.1. Impulse Turbines

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Pressure Energy to Kinetic Energy Conversion:** The entire available energy of the fluid is converted into kinetic energy in a nozzle.
*   **Atmospheric Pressure Operation:** The fluid jet strikes the runner vanes at atmospheric pressure, and the pressure remains constant throughout the runner.
*   **Breaking Jet:** Used for quick stopping of the runner.
*   **Runner Vanes:** Designed to receive the impact of the fluid jet and impart rotation to the runner.

**Types of Impulse Turbines:**

*   **Pelton Turbine:**
    *   **Description:** The most common type of impulse turbine, used for very high heads and relatively low flow rates. It consists of a runner with buckets (double hemispherical buckets are common) mounted on a shaft. One or more nozzles direct high-velocity water jets onto the buckets.
    *   **Working Principle:** The water jet strikes the splitter in the center of the bucket, dividing it into two halves and deflecting it sideways, minimizing friction and maximizing momentum transfer.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:** (Imagine a wheel with buckets around its periphery, with a nozzle directing a jet of water onto the buckets.)
    *   **Key Features:** Buckets have a splitter ridge. Discharge is axial. Casing is not watertight.
    *   **Head Range:** Very High Head (250 m to 2000 m and above)
    *   **Specific Speed Range:** Low (0.1 to 0.5)
    *   **Applications:** High-head hydroelectric power plants.

    **Textbook Reference:**
    *   Dixon, S.I. (1999). *Fluid Mechanics and Thermodynamics of Turbomachinery*. Provides detailed analysis of energy transfer in Pelton turbines.
    *   Yahya, S.H. (1996). *Turbines, Compressor and Fans*. Covers the construction and working of Pelton turbines.

*   **Turgo Turbine:**
    *   **Description:** Similar to a Pelton turbine but the jet strikes the runner at an angle (typically 20 degrees) to the plane of rotation. This allows for a smaller runner diameter for the same power output compared to a Pelton turbine.
    *   **Working Principle:** The jet strikes the runner at an oblique angle. Both kinetic and some pressure energy contribute to the runner's rotation.
    *   **Key Features:** Jet strikes at an angle. Casing is not watertight.
    *   **Head Range:** High Head (around 50 m to 300 m)
    *   **Specific Speed Range:** Medium-low (0.5 to 1.5)
    *   **Applications:** Medium to high head applications where the flow rate is higher than what a Pelton turbine would efficiently handle.

    **Reference Book:**
    *   Gambini, M., & Vellini, M. (2021). *Turbomachinery – Fundamentals, Selection and Preliminary Design*. Might discuss Turgo turbines as an alternative to Pelton for specific applications.

*   **Cross-Flow Turbine (or Over-Shot Turbine for certain designs):**
    *   **Description:** Used for low heads and relatively high flow rates. The water flows through the runner blades twice, first from the outside and then from the inside.
    *   **Working Principle:** The water jet passes through the buckets, imparting energy.
    *   **Key Features:** Simple construction, can handle debris well.
    *   **Head Range:** Low to Medium Head (2m to 200m, depending on the specific design variant)
    *   **Specific Speed Range:** Medium
    *   **Applications:** Small hydro power plants, particularly where there is significant variation in flow rate and head.

    **Textbook Reference:**
    *   Venkanna, B.K. (2009). *Fundamentals of Turbo Machinery*. Might cover simpler turbine types for educational purposes.

#### 1.2. Reaction Turbines

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Pressure and Kinetic Energy Conversion:** Both pressure energy and kinetic energy of the fluid are utilized to produce mechanical energy.
*   **Pressure Drop Across Runner:** The pressure of the fluid decreases as it passes through the runner.
*   **Water-tight Casing:** The casing is completely filled with water, and it encloses the runner.
*   **Draft Tube:** A diverging pipe connected to the outlet of the runner to recover some of the kinetic energy and discharge the water to the tailrace at a low velocity, thereby increasing the effective head.

**Types of Reaction Turbines:**

*   **Francis Turbine:**
    *   **Description:** A widely used radial flow reaction turbine, suitable for medium heads and medium flow rates. It consists of a spiral casing (volute), stay vanes, guide vanes, a runner with curved vanes, and a draft tube.
    *   **Working Principle:** Water enters tangentially through the guide vanes, which control the flow rate and direct it onto the runner. The runner blades are shaped to extract both kinetic and pressure energy. The water flows radially inwards and then axially outwards.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:** (Imagine a casing with inward-pointing vanes directing water onto a runner with curved vanes. Water flows inwards radially then exits axially.)
    *   **Key Features:** Guide vanes are adjustable to control flow. Runner is completely submerged.
    *   **Head Range:** Medium Head (30 m to 600 m)
    *   **Specific Speed Range:** Medium (0.5 to 4.0)
    *   **Applications:** Most common type of turbine in hydroelectric power plants.

    **Textbook Reference:**
    *   Dixon, S.I. (1999). *Fluid Mechanics and Thermodynamics of Turbomachinery*. Provides in-depth analysis of velocity triangles and efficiency calculations for Francis turbines.
    *   Yahya, S.H. (1996). *Turbines, Compressor and Fans*. Covers the design and operation of Francis turbines.

*   **Kaplan Turbine:**
    *   **Description:** An axial flow reaction turbine, best suited for low heads and large flow rates. It is essentially a propeller turbine with adjustable blades.
    *   **Working Principle:** Water flows axially through the runner. The adjustable blades allow for efficient operation over a wide range of flow rates.
    *   **Diagrammatic Representation:** (Imagine a propeller-like runner with adjustable blades, with water flowing axially through it.)
    *   **Key Features:** Adjustable runner blades for efficiency at varying flow rates.
    *   **Head Range:** Low Head (2 m to 40 m)
    *   **Specific Speed Range:** High (1.5 to 10.0)
    *   **Applications:** Low-head hydroelectric power plants, run-of-river power plants.

    **Textbook Reference:**
    *   Dixon, S.I. (1999). *Fluid Mechanics and Thermodynamics of Turbomachinery*. Analyzes the axial flow patterns and blade design for Kaplan turbines.
    *   Venkanna, B.K. (2009). *Fundamentals of Turbo Machinery*. Discusses the operation of axial flow turbines like Kaplan.

*   **Propeller Turbine:**
    *   **Description:** A fixed-blade axial flow reaction turbine, used for low heads and large flow rates. It is similar to a Kaplan turbine but with fixed blades.
    *   **Working Principle:** Water flows axially through the runner, imparting energy to the fixed blades.
    *   **Key Features:** Fixed runner blades, less efficient than Kaplan at varying flow rates.
    *   **Head Range:** Low Head (2 m to 40 m)
    *   **Specific Speed Range:** High (1.5 to 10.0)
    *   **Applications:** Low-head power generation where flow rates are relatively constant.

    **Reference Book:**
    *   Dick, E. (2022). *Fundamentals of Turbomachines*. Likely covers propeller turbines as a simpler axial flow design.

*   **Deriaz Turbine:**
    *   **Description:** A diagonal flow reaction turbine, operating between Francis and Kaplan turbines in terms of head and specific speed.
    *   **Working Principle:** Water flows diagonally across the runner blades.
    *   **Key Features:** Combines features of Francis and Kaplan turbines.
    *   **Head Range:** Medium to Low Head
    *   **Specific Speed Range:** Medium-high

### 2. Classification Based on Direction of Flow Through the Runner

This classification is largely covered within the types mentioned above:

*   **Radial Flow:** Francis Turbine (inward radial flow).
*   **Axial Flow:** Kaplan Turbine, Propeller Turbine.
*   **Mixed Flow:** Some variations of Francis turbines exhibit mixed flow characteristics.

### 3. Classification Based on Head

This is a crucial practical aspect for selecting the right turbine for a given site.

*   **High Head Turbines (H > 250 m):**
    *   **Suitable Turbine:** Pelton Turbine.
    *   **Reasoning:** At high heads, the velocity of the water is very high. Impulse turbines like the Pelton are efficient because they convert this high kinetic energy into mechanical work using a jet, without requiring a pressure-tight casing. Reaction turbines would require extremely strong casings to withstand the high pressure, making them impractical and expensive.
    *   **Example:** Hoover Dam Power Plant uses multiple Pelton turbines.

*   **Medium Head Turbines (60 m < H < 250 m):**
    *   **Suitable Turbine:** Francis Turbine.
    *   **Reasoning:** Francis turbines are versatile and efficient for this range of heads. They effectively utilize both pressure and kinetic energy.
    *   **Example:** Grand Coulee Dam Power Plant utilizes Francis turbines.

*   **Low Head Turbines (H < 60 m):**
    *   **Suitable Turbines:** Kaplan Turbine, Propeller Turbine.
    *   **Reasoning:** At low heads, the flow rates are typically very high to generate significant power. Axial flow turbines are ideal as they can handle large volumes of water efficiently. Kaplan turbines, with their adjustable blades, provide excellent efficiency across a range of flow rates.
    *   **Example:** Run-of-river power plants often use Kaplan turbines.

### 4. Classification Based on Specific Speed (Ns)

**Key Concept: Specific Speed (Ns)**

Specific speed is a parameter that indicates the type of turbine best suited for a particular application. It is defined as the speed of a geometrically similar turbine that would produce unit power under a unit head.

The formula for specific speed is:

$N_s = \frac{N \sqrt{P}}{H^{5/4}}$

Where:
*   $N_s$ = Specific speed (usually in rpm or rad/s, but units need to be consistent)
*   $N$ = Rotational speed of the turbine (e.g., rpm)
*   $P$ = Power developed by the turbine (e.g., kW or MW)
*   $H$ = Net head on the turbine (e.g., meters)

**Typical Specific Speed Ranges and Turbine Types:**

| Specific Speed Range (Ns) | Turbine Type   | Head Range | Flow Rate |
| :------------------------ | :------------- | :--------- | :-------- |
| 0.1 - 0.5                 | Pelton Turbine | Very High  | Low       |
| 0.5 - 1.5                 | Turgo Turbine  | High       | Medium    |
| 0.5 - 4.0                 | Francis Turbine | Medium     | Medium    |
| 1.5 - 10.0                | Kaplan Turbine | Low        | High      |

**Important Note on Units for Specific Speed:** The units of $N_s$ depend on the units chosen for $N$, $P$, and $H$. It is crucial to use consistent units. A common set of units is:
*   $N$ in rpm
*   $P$ in kW
*   $H$ in meters

In this case, $N_s$ is often referred to as the "speed index." If $N$ is in rpm, $P$ in HP, and $H$ in feet, a different numerical value is obtained. It is always best to state the units used for $N_s$.

**How Specific Speed Helps in Selection:**
*   **Low Specific Speed:** Indicates a turbine that runs fast for a given head and power, typically having a smaller diameter and fewer vanes. This is characteristic of impulse turbines (Pelton).
*   **High Specific Speed:** Indicates a turbine that runs slower for a given head and power, typically having a larger diameter and more vanes, designed to handle large volumes of flow. This is characteristic of axial flow turbines (Kaplan).
*   **Medium Specific Speed:** Indicates a compromise, like the Francis turbine, which balances head and flow rate.

**Learning Outcome Covered:** This directly supports **CO3: Select an appropriate power generating turbomachine for a particular application** by providing a quantitative basis for selection.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Energy Conversion:** Hydraulic turbines convert hydraulic energy (potential + kinetic) into mechanical energy.
*   **Impulse vs. Reaction:** The key difference lies in how pressure energy is utilized. Impulse turbines convert all head to kinetic energy before striking the runner, while reaction turbines utilize both pressure and kinetic energy changes across the runner.
*   **Head and Flow Rate are Key:** Turbine selection is primarily dictated by the available head and the required flow rate.
*   **Specific Speed is a Selection Tool:** It acts as a characteristic parameter to match turbine type to site conditions.
*   **Draft Tube:** Essential for reaction turbines to recover kinetic energy from the outflow, increasing the overall efficiency.
*   **Nozzle:** Used in impulse turbines to create a high-velocity jet.
*   **Guide Vanes/Spear:** Used in reaction turbines to control the flow rate and angle of entry to the runner.

### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
A hydroelectric power plant is to be designed for a head of 300 meters and a flow rate of 5 m³/s. Which type of turbine would be most suitable, and why? Justify your answer based on head, flow rate, and specific speed.

**Answer 1:**
For a head of 300 meters, the turbine suitable is either a Pelton turbine or a Francis turbine.
*   **Pelton Turbine:** Suitable for very high heads (> 250m) and low flow rates.
*   **Francis Turbine:** Suitable for medium heads (30m to 600m) and medium flow rates.
Given the flow rate of 5 m³/s, which is not exceptionally low, and a head of 300m, a **Francis turbine** is generally the more appropriate choice. A Pelton turbine would be used if the head was significantly higher (e.g., > 600m) or the flow rate significantly lower. Francis turbines can handle this head and flow rate combination efficiently. The specific speed for a Francis turbine in this range would be moderate, indicating its suitability for a balance of head and flow.

**Question 2:**
What is the primary function of a draft tube in a reaction turbine?

**Answer 2:**
The primary function of a draft tube in a reaction turbine is to recover the kinetic energy of the water leaving the runner and convert it into pressure energy. This increases the effective head available to the turbine and consequently improves its overall efficiency. It also allows the turbine to be set above the tailwater level without a significant loss in head.

**Question 3:**
Calculate the specific speed of a turbine operating under a head of 50m at a speed of 300 rpm, developing 10,000 kW of power. (Use N in rpm, P in kW, H in meters).

**Calculation:**
$N_s = \frac{N \sqrt{P}}{H^{5/4}}$
$N_s = \frac{300 \times \sqrt{10000}}{50^{5/4}}$
$N_s = \frac{300 \times 100}{(50^{1.25})}$
$N_s = \frac{30000}{133.97}$ (approx.)
$N_s \approx 223.9$ rpm (if using N in rpm, P in kW, H in meters).

**Question 4:**
Differentiate between impulse and reaction turbines, highlighting the key differences in their working principle and operating conditions.

**Answer 4:**
| Feature           | Impulse Turbine (e.g., Pelton)                                    | Reaction Turbine (e.g., Francis, Kaplan)                               |
| :---------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Energy at Inlet** | All available energy is converted to kinetic energy in a nozzle. | Both pressure energy and kinetic energy are available.                 |
| **Pressure**      | Fluid strikes the runner at atmospheric pressure. Pressure remains constant throughout the runner. | Fluid enters and leaves the runner under pressure. Pressure drops across the runner. |
