# COMPOSITE MATERIALS - Module 2: Polymer Matrix Composites (PMCs)

## Topic: Moulding Methods for PMCs

This module focuses on Polymer Matrix Composites (PMCs), specifically the various moulding methods used to fabricate them. We will explore techniques like pressure bagging, bag moulding, autoclave-based processing with prepregs, pultrusion, and filament winding.

**Course Outcomes Addressed:**
*   **CO2:** To know about polymer matrix composites, classification, properties, characteristics and applications, manufacturing methods. (Knowledge Level: K1, K2)

---

### 1. Introduction to PMC Moulding Methods

Polymer matrix composites (PMCs) are materials where a polymer acts as the matrix, binding reinforcing fibers (e.g., carbon, glass, aramid) together. The choice of moulding method significantly influences the composite's properties, cost, and production volume. These methods aim to consolidate the reinforcement and matrix, cure the polymer, and achieve the desired shape and performance.

**Key Concept:** Consolidation refers to the process of removing voids and ensuring good contact between the reinforcement and the matrix. Curing is the process of solidifying the polymer matrix, often through chemical reactions (cross-linking).

---

### 2. Bag Moulding Methods

Bag moulding methods utilize a flexible mold (bag) to apply pressure to the laminate during curing. This offers advantages like lower tooling costs and the ability to produce complex shapes.

#### 2.1. Bag Moulding (Vacuum Bagging)

**Definition:** Vacuum bagging is a process where a vacuum is drawn on a laminate laid up on a mold. The atmospheric pressure outside the bag then compresses the laminate against the mold surface.

**Process Steps:**
1.  **Mold Preparation:** The mold surface is prepared with a release agent.
2.  **Lay-up:** Reinforcement (e.g., woven fabric, prepreg) and matrix (e.g., resin infusion, prepreg) are laid up onto the mold.
3.  **Bagging:** A flexible bag (often made of plastic film) is placed over the laid-up laminate. Sealant tape is used to create an airtight seal between the bag and the mold flange.
4.  **Vacuum Application:** A vacuum pump is connected to the bag, drawing out air.
5.  **Curing:** The laminate is cured, typically in an oven or at room temperature, under vacuum. The vacuum pressure consolidates the laminate and removes excess resin and entrapped air.

**Properties and Characteristics:**
*   **Consolidation:** Effective in consolidating the laminate and reducing void content.
*   **Surface Finish:** Can produce a good surface finish on the mold side.
*   **Pressure:** Applies uniform pressure (up to atmospheric pressure, ~0.1 MPa or 14.7 psi).
*   **Complexity:** Suitable for moderately complex shapes.
*   **Cost:** Relatively low tooling costs compared to rigid molds.
*   **Part Thickness:** Typically limited to thinner laminates.

**Applications:**
*   Aerospace components (secondary structures)
*   Automotive parts
*   Marine components
*   Prototypes and low-volume production

**Textbook Reference:** *Chawla, K. K. (2013) discusses vacuum bagging as a common composite manufacturing technique that relies on atmospheric pressure for consolidation.*

#### 2.2. Pressure Bagging

**Definition:** Pressure bagging is an advancement of vacuum bagging where a pressurized gas (usually air or nitrogen) is used to apply higher pressure to the laminate compared to atmospheric pressure alone.

**Process Steps:**
1.  **Mold Preparation & Lay-up:** Similar to vacuum bagging.
2.  **Bagging:** A flexible bag is sealed over the laminate and mold.
3.  **Pressure Application:** The bag is connected to a pressurized gas source, which inflates the bag and presses the laminate against the mold.
4.  **Curing:** The laminate is cured under this elevated pressure.

**Properties and Characteristics:**
*   **Higher Consolidation Pressure:** Can achieve pressures significantly higher than vacuum bagging (e.g., 0.3 - 0.7 MPa or 45-100 psi).
*   **Improved Quality:** Leads to better consolidation, lower void content, and potentially higher fiber volume fractions.
*   **Reduced Resin Exudation:** Higher pressure can help keep excess resin within the laminate rather than squeezing it out excessively.
*   **Tooling:** Requires a robust mold and bagging setup to withstand the higher pressure.

**Applications:**
*   High-performance aerospace components
*   Applications requiring superior mechanical properties and minimal voids

**Textbook Reference:** *Mallicak, P. K. (1988) may detail pressure bagging as a method to enhance laminate quality over simple vacuum bagging, particularly for demanding applications.*

### 3. Autoclave-Based Processing with Prepregs

**Definition:** Autoclave processing involves curing composite laminates under controlled high pressure and temperature within an autoclave. Prepregs, which are pre-impregnated sheets of fiber with a partially cured resin system (B-stage), are commonly used.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Prepreg:** Reinforcement material pre-impregnated with a controlled amount of resin.
*   **B-stage:** The partially cured state of the resin, where it is solid or semi-solid and tacky but not fully cross-linked.
*   **Autoclave:** A pressurized, heated vessel used for curing.

**Process Steps:**
1.  **Mold Preparation:** A rigid mold (often made of metal or composite) is prepared with a release agent.
2.  **Prepreg Lay-up:** Layers of prepreg are cut to shape and precisely laid up onto the mold in the desired orientation. This is often done in a clean room environment.
3.  **Bagging:** A series of bagging materials are applied over the prepreg lay-up:
    *   **Release Film:** Prevents sticking to the laminate.
    *   **Bleeder/Breather Ply:** Absorbs excess resin and allows for airflow during curing.
    *   **Vacuum Bag:** Creates a vacuum seal.
4.  **Autoclave Cycle:** The bagged assembly is placed inside the autoclave. A specific curing cycle is followed, involving:
    *   **Vacuum Pull:** To remove entrapped air and volatile byproducts.
    *   **Pressurization:** With inert gas (nitrogen) to a specified pressure (typically 0.5 - 0.7 MPa or 75-100 psi).
    *   **Heating:** To the resin cure temperature.
    *   **Curing Dwell:** Holding at the cure temperature and pressure.
    *   **Cooling:** Under pressure.

**Properties and Characteristics:**
*   **High Quality:** Produces laminates with excellent mechanical properties, low void content, and high fiber volume fractions due to high pressure and temperature.
*   **Dimensional Accuracy:** Rigid molds and precise lay-up contribute to good dimensional control.
*   **Surface Finish:** Can achieve a very smooth surface on the mold side.
*   **Cost:** High tooling costs (rigid molds) and high equipment costs (autoclave).
*   **Production Rate:** Relatively slow due to complex lay-up and curing cycles.
*   **Suitable for:** Complex geometries and critical structural components.

**Applications:**
*   Aerospace primary structures (wings, fuselage, control surfaces)
*   High-performance sporting goods
*   Automotive structural components (e.g., chassis)

**Textbook Reference:** *Chawla (2013) extensively covers autoclave curing, emphasizing its role in achieving high-performance composites with prepregs due to the controlled application of heat and pressure.* *Jones (1998) also likely details the process for achieving optimal matrix cure and consolidation.*

### 4. Pultrusion

**Definition:** Pultrusion is a continuous process for manufacturing composite profiles with a constant cross-section. Reinforcing fibers are pulled through a resin bath and then through a heated die that shapes and cures the resin.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Continuous Process:** Produces long, uniform parts.
*   **Constant Cross-section:** Ideal for structural shapes like rods, beams, and tubes.
*   **Pulling Mechanism:** Friction drive or caterpillar-type pullers are used.

**Process Steps:**
1.  **Fiber Collimation:** Reinforcement materials (rovings of continuous fibers, mats, or fabrics) are guided and arranged into a desired cross-sectional pattern.
2.  **Resin Impregnation:** Fibers are pulled through a resin bath containing the polymer matrix (thermoset resins are most common).
3.  **Pre-forming:** Fibers pass through pre-forming guides to consolidate them and initiate initial shaping.
4.  **Die:** The impregnated tow passes through a heated die. The die has a precisely shaped cavity that conforms to the final cross-section. The heat initiates and completes the polymerization (curing) of the resin.
5.  **Pulling & Cutting:** As the cured profile emerges from the die, it is gripped by a pulling mechanism (e.g., caterpillar puller) and cut to the desired length by a flying saw.

**Properties and Characteristics:**
*   **High Fiber Volume Fraction:** Efficient impregnation and consolidation lead to high fiber content, resulting in excellent strength and stiffness along the pulling direction.
*   **Directionality:** Properties are highly anisotropic, with superior mechanical performance in the axial direction.
*   **Cost-Effective:** Continuous process suitable for high-volume production of standard profiles.
*   **Geometric Limitations:** Limited to constant cross-sectional shapes.
*   **Surface Finish:** Can vary depending on the die finish.

**Applications:**
*   Structural components (rods, beams, channels, angles) for construction and infrastructure (e.g., electrical insulators, bridge components).
*   Poles (flagpoles, fishing rods, tent poles).
*   Window frames.
*   Golf club shafts.
*   Reinforcing bars for concrete.

**Textbook Reference:** *Chawla (2013) likely describes pultrusion as a cost-effective method for producing continuous, structurally efficient profiles with excellent axial properties.* *Matthews & Rawlings (1994) would offer detailed insights into the process parameters and material behavior in pultruded composites.*

### 5. Filament Winding

**Definition:** Filament winding is a process for fabricating composite structures with continuous, curvilinear fiber paths, typically cylindrical or spherical shapes. Reinforcing fibers are impregnated with resin and then wound onto a rotating mandrel.

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Mandrel:** A rotating mold (tool) that defines the internal shape of the composite part.
*   **Programmed Winding:** The winding head precisely controls the fiber path and tension.
*   **Curing:** Can be done on the mandrel or after removal, depending on the resin system.

**Process Steps:**
1.  **Mandrel Preparation:** The mandrel surface is prepared with a release agent.
2.  **Fiber Impregnation:** Reinforcement fibers (typically glass or carbon fiber) are pulled through a resin bath or a die containing the resin.
3.  **Winding:** The impregnated fibers are wound onto the rotating mandrel by a movable winding head. The winding pattern (angles, number of passes) is programmed to achieve the desired mechanical properties and structural integrity.
4.  **Curing:** The wound part is cured, either by heating the mandrel (if heated) or by removing the part from the mandrel and curing it in an oven.
5.  **Mandrel Removal:** Once cured, the part is removed from the mandrel.

**Properties and Characteristics:**
*   **High Fiber Volume Fraction:** Achieved due to controlled tension and impregnation.
*   **Anisotropic Properties:** Properties are tailored by the winding pattern, allowing optimization for specific load cases.
*   **Good Consolidation:** Uniform winding tension ensures good consolidation.
*   **Geometric Limitations:** Primarily suited for axisymmetric or rotationally symmetric shapes.
*   **Cost-Effective:** For high-volume production of specific shapes.

**Applications:**
*   Pressure vessels (e.g., gas cylinders, rocket motor casings).
*   Pipes and tubes.
*   Aircraft and missile components (e.g., fuselage sections, engine casings).
*   Drive shafts.
*   Wind turbine blades (using advanced winding techniques).

**Textbook Reference:** *Chawla (2013) describes filament winding as an excellent method for producing hollow, cylindrical, or spherical composite structures with highly controlled fiber placement and excellent mechanical properties.* *Mallicak (1988) would detail the winding parameters and their impact on the final composite properties.*

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Pressure is Key:** Most moulding methods rely on applied pressure for consolidation, void reduction, and resin impregnation. The level of pressure (atmospheric, pressurized gas, internal pressure) dictates the quality and cost.
*   **Tooling Costs:** Rigid molds (autoclave) are expensive but produce high-quality parts. Flexible bagging methods have lower tooling costs but may yield lower quality for demanding applications.
*   **Process Control:** Temperature, pressure, time, and fiber placement are critical parameters for achieving desired composite properties in all these methods.
*   **Prepregs vs. Wet Lay-up:** Prepregs offer better control over resin content and fiber orientation, leading to higher performance, but are more expensive. Wet lay-up (infusing resin into dry fibers) is often used with vacuum bagging or RTM.
*   **Continuous vs. Discrete:** Pultrusion and filament winding are continuous or semi-continuous processes suitable for high-volume production of specific shapes. Bagging and autoclave methods are typically used for discrete parts.
*   **Fiber Orientation:** Filament winding allows for precise control of fiber orientation, critical for pressure vessels. Pultrusion excels in axial strength.

### Practice Questions & Answers:

**Question 1:** Which moulding method is most suitable for producing long, composite structural beams with a constant cross-section?
    a) Autoclave Processing
    b) Pressure Bagging
    c) Pultrusion
    d) Filament Winding

**Answer 1:** c) Pultrusion. Pultrusion is specifically designed for continuous profiles with a constant cross-section.

**Question 2:** What is the primary advantage of using an autoclave in composite manufacturing compared to simple vacuum bagging?
    a) Lower tooling costs
    b) Higher consolidation pressure and temperature control
    c) Ability to produce complex organic shapes easily
    d) Faster production cycles

**Answer 2:** b) Higher consolidation pressure and temperature control. Autoclaves provide a precisely controlled environment with high pressure and temperature, leading to superior laminate quality.

**Question 3:** Define "prepreg" and explain its role in autoclave processing.
**Answer 3:** A prepreg is a reinforcement material (like carbon or glass fiber) that has been pre-impregnated with a controlled amount of resin in a partially cured state (B-stage). In autoclave processing, prepregs are used because they offer better control over fiber-to-resin ratio, fiber orientation, and lead to higher quality laminates with fewer voids due to the precise curing cycle in the autoclave.

**Question 4:** Describe the fundamental principle behind vacuum bagging.
**Answer 4:** Vacuum bagging utilizes atmospheric pressure to consolidate the composite laminate. A flexible bag is sealed around the laminate laid up on a mold, and air is evacuated from within the bag. The external atmospheric pressure then presses the laminate against the mold, squeezing out excess resin and entrapped air.

**Question 5:** For a pressure vessel requiring high burst strength and controlled anisotropic properties, which manufacturing method would be most appropriate and why?
**Answer 5:** Filament winding would be the most appropriate method. This is because filament winding allows for precise control over the fiber orientation and tension, enabling the designer to tailor the composite's mechanical properties to withstand the internal pressure in specific directions, leading to high strength and efficiency.
