### **Introduction (K2 - CO1, CO3)**

Steam turbines are essential prime movers in thermal power plants, converting the thermal energy of steam into mechanical rotational energy. This mechanical energy is then used to drive generators for electricity production, or other machinery like pumps and compressors. Their development marked a significant advancement in power generation technology, enabling higher efficiencies and power outputs compared to reciprocating steam engines.

**Key Concept:** The fundamental principle behind a steam turbine is the expansion of high-pressure, high-temperature steam through a series of stationary and rotating blades. This expansion causes the steam to accelerate, and as it passes through the rotating blades, it exerts a force, producing torque and hence rotational work.

---

### **Classification of Steam Turbines (K2 - CO1, CO3)**

Steam turbines can be classified based on several criteria, each highlighting different aspects of their design and application.

#### **1. Based on the Method of Steam Expansion:**

*   **Impulse Turbines:**
    *   **Princ:** Steam expands in stationary nozzles (nozzles are part of the casing or diaphragm), converting its thermal energy into kinetic energy. The high-velocity steam jet then strikes the moving blades, imparting momentum and causing rotation. Pressure drop occurs only in the nozzles, not across the moving blades.
    *   **Characteristics:**
        *   Constant pressure across the moving blades.
        *   Velocity of steam decreases as it passes through moving blades.
        *   Requires a high-velocity steam jet.
        *   Often simpler construction.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Curtis Turbine:** A velocity-compounded impulse turbine with multiple rows of moving blades and stationary nozzles. Designed for high pressure and temperature drop.
        *   **Rateau Turbine:** A pressure-compounded impulse turbine, where the total pressure drop is divided into stages, with each stage consisting of a set of nozzles and a row of moving blades.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil, Chapter 12, "Steam Turbines" (specifically discusses impulse turbine principles and types).

*   **Reaction Turbines:**
    *   **Princ:** Steam expands in both stationary (nozzle or guide vanes) and moving blades. The stationary blades direct the steam onto the moving blades, and a partial pressure drop occurs across both the stationary and moving blades. The expansion in the moving blades creates a reactive force (thrust) that drives the rotor.
    *   **Characteristics:**
        *   Pressure drops across both stationary and moving blades.
        *   Velocity of steam increases as it passes through moving blades.
        *   Requires carefully designed blade profiles to achieve efficient expansion.
        *   Generally more efficient for a given stage compared to impulse turbines.
    *   **Examples:**
        *   **Parsons Turbine:** The classic reaction turbine, where the moving blades are essentially partial nozzles themselves, designed for continuous expansion of steam.
    *   **Reference:** P. K. Nag, Chapter 10, "Steam Turbines" (provides a detailed explanation of reaction turbine working and Parsons turbine).

*   **Impulse-Reaction Turbines (Mixed Flow Turbines):**
    *   **Princ:** Combine features of both impulse and reaction turbines. Typically, the high-pressure stages are impulse-driven, and the low-pressure stages are reaction-driven. This allows for optimization of efficiency across different pressure ranges.
    *   **Application:** Common in large-scale power generation for better overall efficiency.

#### **2. Based on the Direction of Steam Flow:**

*   **Axial Flow Turbines:**
    *   **Princ:** Steam flows parallel to the axis of the turbine shaft. This is the most common type for high-power applications.
    *   **Characteristics:** High power output for a given size, suitable for high rotational speeds.
    *   **Example:** Most large-scale power plant turbines are axial flow.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil, Chapter 12, discusses the mechanics of axial flow and blade design.

*   **Radial Flow Turbines (Radial Inflow Turbines):**
    *   **Princ:** Steam flows radially inward or outward, perpendicular to the turbine shaft. Less common in large power plants but used in specific applications.
    *   **Characteristics:** Can handle high steam velocities and pressures effectively.

*   **Mixed Flow Turbines:**
    *   **Princ:** Steam flows at an angle to the axis of the shaft, combining axial and radial components.

#### **3. Based on the Arrangement of Cylinders:**

*   **Single Cylinder Turbine:** All stages are housed within a single casing. Suitable for smaller units.
*   **Multi-Cylinder Turbine:** The turbine is divided into multiple cylinders (e.g., High-Pressure (HP), Intermediate-Pressure (IP), Low-Pressure (LP)) to handle different pressure and temperature ranges more efficiently. This is typical for large power plants.
    *   **Arrangements:**
        *   **Tandem Compound:** HP, IP, and LP turbines are mounted on the same shaft.
        *   **Cross Compound:** HP, IP, and LP turbines are mounted on separate shafts, driving separate generators or coupled through gears.

#### **4. Based on Steam Exhaust:**

*   **Condensing Turbines:** Steam is exhausted into a condenser at sub-atmospheric pressure, maximizing the pressure drop and thus work output. This is the standard for power generation.
*   **Back-Pressure Turbines (Non-Condensing Turbines):** Steam is exhausted at atmospheric pressure or above. Used in industrial power plants where exhaust steam is required for process heating (cogeneration).
*   **Extraction Turbines (Bleeder Turbines):** Steam is extracted from various stages of the turbine to supply process heating or feedwater heating.

### **Key Components of a Steam Turbine (K2 - CO1, CO3)**

A typical steam turbine consists of the following major components:

*   **Rotor (Shaft and Discs/Blades):**
    *   **Shaft:** Transmits the rotational mechanical power to the driven equipment (generator).
    *   **Discs/Wheels:** Mounted on the shaft, carrying the rows of moving blades.
    *   **Moving Blades (Buckets):** Attached to the discs, they are acted upon by the steam jet (impulse) or expand the steam (reaction) to produce torque. Their shape is crucial for efficiency.
    *   **Reference:** P. K. Nag, Chapter 10, provides detailed descriptions and diagrams of rotor construction.

*   **Casing (Cylinder):**
    *   **Function:** Encloses the rotor and stationary blades. It contains the steam and directs its flow.
    *   **Types:** Often split into upper and lower halves for maintenance. HP and IP casings are robust due to high pressures and temperatures. LP casings are larger due to the high volume of low-pressure steam.

*   **Stationary Blades (Nozzles or Guide Vanes):**
    *   **Function:** Attached to the casing or diaphragms, they direct the steam onto the moving blades and, in some designs, cause steam expansion.
    *   **Nozzles:** Used in impulse turbines for accelerating steam.
    *   **Guide Vanes:** Used in reaction turbines to guide steam flow and contribute to expansion.

*   **Diaphragms:**
    *   **Function:** Fixed partitions that divide the casing into stages and support the stationary blades (nozzles). They also prevent steam leakage between stages.

*   **Bearings:**
    *   **Function:** Support the rotor shaft and allow it to rotate freely with minimal friction.
    *   **Types:** Journal bearings (support radial loads) and thrust bearings (support axial loads).

*   **Seals:**
    *   **Function:** Prevent steam leakage along the shaft and between stages.
    *   **Types:** Labyrinth seals, brush seals, carbon seals are commonly used. Effective sealing is critical for turbine efficiency.
    *   **Reference:** El Wakil, Chapter 12, discusses various sealing techniques and their impact on performance.

*   **Governor:**
    *   **Function:** Regulates the steam flow to the turbine to maintain a constant speed under varying load conditions. Modern governors are electronic or hydraulic.

*   **Throttle Valve:**
    *   **Function:** Controls the initial steam flow from the boiler to the turbine.

### **Working Principle and Thermodynamics (K3 - CO3)**

The operation of a steam turbine is governed by the principles of thermodynamics and fluid mechanics.

1.  **Steam Admission:** High-pressure, high-temperature steam from the boiler enters the turbine through the throttle valve.
2.  **Expansion and Acceleration (Impulse Turbine):** Steam expands through stationary nozzles, converting thermal energy into kinetic energy. This results in a high-velocity jet.
3.  **Momentum Transfer (Impulse Turbine):** The high-velocity steam jet impinges on the moving blades, transferring its momentum and causing the rotor to rotate.
4.  **Expansion in Moving Blades (Reaction Turbine):** Steam expands as it passes through the passages formed by the moving blades, generating a reactive force that drives the rotor.
5.  **Multi-staging:** To extract maximum energy from the steam, turbines are designed with multiple stages. Each stage consists of a set of stationary blades (nozzles/guide vanes) and a row of moving blades. The pressure and temperature of the steam decrease progressively across the stages.
6.  **Exhaust:** After passing through the last row of moving blades, the steam is exhausted to a condenser (in condensing turbines) to create a vacuum, thereby maximizing the expansion ratio and work output.

**Thermodynamic Cycle:** The process within a steam turbine is part of the Rankine cycle. The ideal process within the turbine is isentropic expansion (constant entropy), represented by a vertical line on a T-s diagram.

**Power Developed (CO3):**
The power developed by a turbine stage depends on the steam flow rate, the change in enthalpy across the stage, and the efficiency of the stage.

*   **Isentropic Work per unit mass:** $W_{is} = h_{in} - h_{out,is}$
*   **Actual Work per unit mass:** $W_{actual} = \eta_{turbine} \times W_{is}$
*   **Total Power:** $P = \dot{m} \times W_{actual} = \dot{m} \times \eta_{turbine} \times (h_{in} - h_{out,is})$

Where:
*   $\dot{m}$ is the mass flow rate of steam.
*   $\eta_{turbine}$ is the isentropic efficiency of the turbine (or stage).
*   $h_{in}$ is the enthalpy of steam at the inlet of the stage.
*   $h_{out,is}$ is the enthalpy of steam at the outlet of the stage if the expansion were isentropic.

**Reference:** P. K. Nag, Chapter 10, provides detailed thermodynamic analysis of turbine stages and efficiency calculations.

### **Blade Geometry and Aerodynamics (K3 - CO3)**

The design of turbine blades is critical for efficient energy transfer.

*   **Blade Velocity (U):** The tangential speed of the rotating blades.
*   **Steam Velocity (V):** The velocity of the steam jet.
*   **Angle of Attack:** The angle at which the steam strikes the blade.
*   **Blade Shape:** Aerofoil shapes are used to maximize energy transfer and minimize losses due to friction and turbulence.
*   **Velocity Diagrams:** These are graphical representations used to analyze the velocity, momentum, and energy changes of the steam as it flows through the blades. They are essential for determining the work done and efficiency.
    *   **Absolute Velocity (V):** Velocity of steam relative to the casing.
    *   **Relative Velocity (Vr):** Velocity of steam relative to the moving blades.
    *   **Blade Velocity (U):** Tangential velocity of the blades.

**Impulse Stage Velocity Diagram:**
*   Steam enters the moving blade with absolute velocity $V_1$ at angle $\alpha_1$ to the direction of blade motion.
*   Relative velocity $V_{r1}$ at the blade inlet.
*   After passing through the blade, the steam leaves with relative velocity $V_{r2}$ at angle $\beta_2$ and absolute velocity $V_2$ at angle $\alpha_2$.
*   Work done per unit mass is proportional to $U \times (V_{w1} + V_{w2})$, where $V_{w1}$ and $V_{w2}$ are the whirl components of the steam velocity (tangential component).

**Reaction Stage Velocity Diagram:**
*   Similar to impulse stages, but the pressure drop across the moving blades means the steam accelerates relative to the blades. This leads to a higher relative velocity at the outlet ($V_{r2} > V_{r1}$ for a typical reaction stage).
*   Work done per unit mass is proportional to $U \times (V_{w1} + V_{w2})$.

**Reference:** El Wakil, Chapter 12, and P. K. Nag, Chapter 10, extensively cover velocity diagrams and their application in blade design.

### **Turbine Efficiency and Losses (K3 - CO3)**

The actual performance of a steam turbine is always less than its ideal (isentropic) performance due to various losses.

*   **Isentropic Efficiency ($\eta_{is}$):**
    *   Ratio of actual work output to the isentropic work output.
    *   $\eta_{is} = \frac{\text{Actual Work}}{\text{Isentropic Work}}$

*   **Mechanical Efficiency ($\eta_{mech}$):**
    *   Ratio of power delivered at the turbine shaft to the power developed at the blades. Accounts for bearing friction, shaft losses, etc.

*   **Overall Efficiency ($\eta_{overall}$):**
    *   Product of isentropic efficiency and mechanical efficiency (and other efficiencies like volumetric).
    *   $\eta_{overall} = \eta_{is} \times \eta_{mech}$

*   **Major Losses in Steam Turbines:**
    *   **Nozzle Losses (Impulse Turbines):** Friction during acceleration in nozzles.
    *   **Blade Friction Losses:** Friction between steam and moving/stationary blade surfaces.
    *   **Leakage Losses:** Steam leaking past seals on the rotor and casing.
    *   **Carry-over Losses (Leaving Losses):** Kinetic energy remaining in the steam after it leaves the last row of moving blades. This is often reduced by designing the last stage blades for high exit velocity and expanding to a lower exhaust pressure.
    *   **Throttling Losses:** Occur in governing stages where steam is throttled.
    *   **Reheat Factor:** In multi-stage turbines, the steam is reheated slightly between stages due to the internal irreversibilities of expansion. This slightly increases the enthalpy drop and thus the work output. The reheat factor ($R$) is greater than 1, and actual work is approximately $R \times W_{is}$.

**Reference:** El Wakil, Chapter 12, provides a thorough analysis of various loss mechanisms. P. K. Nag also details these losses and their impact on overall efficiency.

### **Steam Turbine Governing (K2 - CO1, CO3)**

Governing is the process of controlling the speed of the turbine by regulating the steam flow to match the load demand.

*   **Throttle Governing:**
    *   **Method:** Steam flow is controlled by partially closing a throttle valve. This is simple but leads to throttling losses at part loads, reducing efficiency.
    *   **Application:** Small turbines or when efficiency at part load is not critical.

*   **Nozzle or Steam Chest Governing:**
    *   **Method:** Steam is admitted to only a few nozzles at a time. As load decreases, entire nozzles are shut off. This reduces throttling losses compared to throttle governing.
    *   **Application:** Medium-sized turbines.

*   **Deflector or Bypass Governing:**
    *   **Method:** A deflector plate or valve diverts a portion of the steam flow away from the first stage moving blades, effectively bypassing them. This is more efficient at part loads than throttle governing.

*   **All-Steam Governing:**
    *   **Method:** Steam is admitted to all nozzles, but the quantity of steam admitted is regulated by a valve that controls the steam pressure entering the nozzles. This minimizes throttling losses.
    *   **Application:** Large power plant turbines, particularly when efficiency at varying loads is crucial.

**Reference:** El Wakil, Chapter 12, discusses different governing systems and their thermodynamic implications.

### **Applications of Steam Turbines (K2 - CO1)**

Steam turbines are widely used in various sectors:

*   **Thermal Power Plants:**
    *   **Primary Function:** Driving electric generators to produce electricity.
    *   **Types:** Condensing turbines are used in conventional thermal power plants (coal, gas, oil fired).
*   **Nuclear Power Plants:** Similar to thermal power plants, steam from the nuclear reactor drives turbines to generate electricity.
*   **Cogeneration (Combined Heat and Power - CHP):**
    *   **Function:** Used in industrial facilities and district heating systems. Back-pressure or extraction turbines supply process steam for heating or industrial processes while simultaneously generating electricity.
*   **Marine Propulsion:** Large ships are often powered by steam turbines.
*   **Industrial Drives:** Driving pumps, compressors, and other rotating machinery in industries.

**Reference:** P. K. Nag, Chapter 10, provides an overview of the application spectrum of steam turbines.

### **Design Considerations (K2 - CO1, CO3)**

The design of a steam turbine involves several critical considerations:

*   **Steam Conditions:** Inlet temperature, pressure, and exhaust pressure significantly influence the turbine design and efficiency.
*   **Power Output Requirement:** Dictates the size and number of stages.
*   **Rotational Speed:** Influences blade design, material selection, and bearing requirements.
*   **Efficiency Requirements:** Balancing design complexity with desired efficiency.
*   **Material Selection:** Blades and casing must withstand high temperatures, pressures, and stresses. Special alloys are used for high-temperature sections.
*   **Blade Tip Speed:** Must be limited to prevent excessive centrifugal stress.
*   **Blade Vibrations:** Aerodynamic forces can induce vibrations, requiring careful blade design and balancing.
*   **Thermal Expansion:** Turbine components expand significantly with temperature changes, requiring careful design to accommodate this expansion without causing damage.
*   **Cost:** Balancing performance with manufacturing and operational costs.

### **Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:**
Differentiate between impulse and reaction turbines, highlighting the key differences in steam expansion and blade design.

**Answer 1:**
| Feature             | Impulse Turbine                                     | Reaction Turbine                                       |
| :------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Steam Expansion** | Occurs entirely in stationary nozzles.              | Occurs in both stationary and moving blades.           |
| **Pressure Change** | Pressure drops only across nozzles.                 | Pressure drops across both stationary and moving blades. |
| **Velocity Change** | Steam accelerates in nozzles; velocity reduces across moving blades. | Steam accelerates across moving blades.                 |
| **Blade Shape**     | Blades are buckets designed to intercept high-velocity steam. | Blades are airfoils with nozzle-like passages for expansion. |
| **Force**           | Primarily due to change in momentum (impact).     | Primarily due to reaction force from steam acceleration. |
| **Efficiency**      | Generally lower efficiency per stage compared to reaction. | Generally higher efficiency per stage.                 |
| **Construction**    | Can be simpler.                                     | Requires more precise blade shaping.                   |

**Question 2:**
What are the main types of losses in a steam turbine, and how do they affect its overall efficiency?

**Answer 2:**
The main losses in a steam turbine include:
1.  **Nozzle Losses:** Friction in nozzles reduces steam velocity.
2.  **Blade Friction Losses:** Friction between steam and blade surfaces reduces kinetic energy.
3.  **Leakage Losses:** Steam bypassing the blades through seals reduces the amount of steam doing useful work.
4.  **Carry-over Losses (Leaving Losses):** Kinetic energy remaining in the exhaust steam that could have been converted to work.
5.  **Throttling Losses:** Occur during governing by throttling, where steam pressure is reduced without doing work.
6.  **Mechanical Losses:** Friction in bearings and glands.

These losses reduce the actual work output compared to the ideal isentropic work, thereby decreasing the turbine's overall efficiency ($\eta_{overall}$).

**Question 3:**
A steam turbine stage has an isentropic enthalpy drop of 50 kJ/kg. If the isentropic efficiency of the stage is 85%, calculate the actual work done per kg of steam.

**Answer 3:**
Given:
Isentropic enthalpy drop ($\Delta h_{is}$) = 50 kJ/kg
Isentropic efficiency ($\eta_{is}$) = 85% = 0.85

Actual work done per kg of steam ($W_{actual}$) = $\eta_{is} \times \Delta h_{is}$
$W_{actual} = 0.85 \times 50 \text{ kJ/kg}$
$W_{actual} = 42.5 \text{ kJ/kg}$

**Question 4:**
Explain the purpose of governing in a steam turbine and describe one method of governing.

**Answer 4:**
Governing in a steam turbine is the process of regulating the steam flow to control the turbine's speed and maintain it at a desired level under varying load conditions. This ensures that the generator connected to the turbine produces electricity at a stable frequency.

**Method of Governing: Throttle Governing**
In throttle governing, a throttle valve is used to control the amount of steam entering the turbine. As the load decreases, the throttle valve is partially closed, reducing the steam flow rate and pressure, thus slowing down the turbine. As the load increases, the valve is opened further, increasing steam flow and speed. This method is simple but leads to throttling losses at part loads, reducing efficiency because the steam entering the first stage is at a lower pressure and temperature than it could be if expansion occurred in stages.

### **Important Points to Remember:**

*   Steam turbines are the prime movers in most thermal power plants, converting steam's thermal and kinetic energy into mechanical work.
*   The two main types are **impulse** (expansion in stationary nozzles) and **reaction** (expansion in both stationary and moving blades).
*   **Axial flow turbines** are most common for power generation due to their high efficiency and power output.
*   **Multi-cylinder designs** (HP, IP, LP) are used in large power plants for better efficiency across different steam conditions.
*   Key components include the rotor (shaft and blades), casing, stationary blades, diaphragms, bearings, and seals.
*   **Velocity diagrams** are crucial for analyzing blade performance and calculating work output.
*   Turbine efficiency is reduced by various **losses**, including blade friction, leakage, and leaving losses.
*   **Governing systems** are essential for maintaining stable turbine speed under changing load conditions.
*   Steam turbines are also used in **cogeneration** (CHP) and other industrial applications.
