### **Module 4: Energy Storage - Introduction**

Energy storage is a critical component in modern power systems, enabling better integration of renewable energy sources, improving grid stability, and enhancing power plant operational flexibility. This module focuses on two key methods of energy storage: Thermal Energy Storage (TES) and Chemical Energy Storage (CES).

### **1. Thermal Energy Storage (TES)**

Thermal Energy Storage (TES) is a technology that stores thermal energy (heat or cold) generated at one time for use at a later time. This allows for decoupling of energy supply and demand, making it particularly useful for solar thermal power plants, combined heat and power (CHP) systems, and for shifting electricity generation from peak to off-peak hours.

**1.1 Key Concepts and Definitions:**

*   **Thermal Energy Storage (TES):** The process of capturing thermal energy for later use.
*   **Sensible Heat Storage:** Storing heat by changing the temperature of a storage medium. The amount of heat stored is proportional to the mass of the medium, its specific heat capacity, and the temperature difference.
    *   *Equation:* $Q = m \times c_p \times \Delta T$
        *   $Q$: Heat stored (kJ)
        *   $m$: Mass of storage medium (kg)
        *   $c_p$: Specific heat capacity of the medium (kJ/kg°C)
        *   $\Delta T$: Temperature difference (°C)
*   **Latent Heat Storage:** Storing heat by changing the phase of a storage medium (e.g., melting, solidifying, vaporization, condensation). This method can store a large amount of energy at a nearly constant temperature.
    *   *Equation:* $Q = m \times L$
        *   $Q$: Heat stored (kJ)
        *   $m$: Mass of storage medium (kg)
        *   $L$: Latent heat of phase change (kJ/kg)
*   **Thermochemical Heat Storage:** Storing heat through reversible chemical reactions. The heat is absorbed during an endothermic reaction and released during the reverse exothermic reaction. This method offers high energy density and long-term storage capabilities.

**1.2 Types of TES Systems Based on Storage Medium:**

#### **1.2.1 Sensible Heat Storage Systems**

These systems store heat by changing the temperature of the storage material.

*   **Solid Media:**
    *   **Materials:** Rocks, concrete, ceramics, bricks, sand, water (though often grouped separately due to its high specific heat).
    *   **Advantages:** High temperature stability, low cost, readily available.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Lower energy density compared to latent heat or thermochemical storage, thermal conductivity can be an issue.
    *   **Applications:** Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) plants, district heating systems, industrial process heat.
    *   *Example (El Wakil):* El Wakil's "Power Plant Technology" might describe packed beds of rocks or ceramic materials used in CSP plants where hot molten salt or air flows through the bed to heat it. This heat is then extracted later to generate steam.

*   **Liquid Media:**
    *   **Materials:** Water, molten salts, oils (mineral oil, synthetic oil).
    *   **Advantages:** High specific heat capacity (water), ability to operate at high temperatures (molten salts, oils), good thermal conductivity.
    *   **Disadvantages:** Water has a limited temperature range (boiling point), oils can be flammable, molten salts can be corrosive at high temperatures.
    *   **Applications:**
        *   **Water/Steam:** Domestic hot water, solar water heaters, district heating.
        *   **Molten Salts:** CSP plants (e.g., parabolic trough or solar tower systems) are a prime example. They can store heat at temperatures up to 565°C. The stored heat is used to melt the salt and then to generate steam for turbines. (Nag discusses molten salt storage in detail for CSP applications).
        *   **Oils:** Used in some CSP systems and for industrial heat transfer.

#### **1.2.2 Latent Heat Storage Systems (Phase Change Materials - PCMs)**

These systems store energy during a phase transition.

*   **Materials:** Paraffins, fatty acids, salt hydrates, metallic alloys.
    *   **Paraffins:** Organic compounds, good thermal stability, low cost, but low thermal conductivity and can be flammable.
    *   **Fatty Acids:** Similar to paraffins, better thermal stability, but more expensive.
    *   **Salt Hydrates:** High latent heat, good volumetric energy density, low cost, but can undergo phase segregation (undesirable separation of solid salt and liquid water), leading to reduced performance over time. Encapsulation is often used to mitigate this.
    *   **Metallic Alloys:** High thermal conductivity, but expensive and can have melting point issues.
*   **Advantages:** High energy density at a nearly constant temperature, reduced storage volume.
*   **Disadvantages:** Latent heat values can vary, potential for phase segregation (salt hydrates), often lower thermal conductivity than sensible heat storage materials.
*   **Applications:** Building climate control (e.g., embedding PCMs in wall materials to absorb heat during the day and release it at night), thermal management in electronic devices, solar thermal applications.
*   *Example (Nagpal & Sharma):* Nagpal and Sharma might detail the use of salt hydrates in building materials for passive solar heating and cooling. The PCM in the wall melts when the room gets too hot, absorbing heat, and solidifies when it gets too cool, releasing heat.

#### **1.2.3 Thermochemical Heat Storage Systems**

These systems store heat using reversible chemical reactions.

*   **Principles:** An endothermic reaction absorbs heat, and the products are stored. The reverse exothermic reaction releases the stored heat when needed.
*   **Materials:** Metal hydrides, salt hydrates (dehydration/hydration), organic compounds (e.g., methane hydrate decomposition/formation), carbonate salts.
*   **Advantages:** Very high energy density (often higher than PCMs), long-term storage capability with minimal heat loss, ability to store heat at high temperatures.
*   **Disadvantages:** Complex system design, catalyst requirements, potential for side reactions, cost can be high.
*   **Applications:** Long-term solar energy storage, industrial waste heat recovery, hydrogen storage (which can be linked to energy storage).
*   *Example (Gupta):* Gupta's "Power Plant Engineering" might discuss the use of reversible chemical reactions like metal hydrides for heat storage, similar to hydrogen storage applications. For example, a metal absorbing hydrogen (exothermic) and releasing it (endothermic), with the heat released or absorbed being the stored energy.

**1.3 Components of a TES System:**

*   **Storage Medium:** The material that stores the thermal energy.
*   **Heat Exchanger:** Transfers heat to and from the storage medium.
*   **Storage Container/Tank:** Insulated vessel to hold the storage medium.
*   **Circulation System:** Pumps and piping to move the heat transfer fluid.
*   **Control System:** Manages charging and discharging cycles.

**1.4 Applications of TES in Power Plants:**

*   **Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) Plants:**
    *   TES is crucial for CSP to provide dispatchable solar power. It allows electricity generation even when the sun is not shining (e.g., at night or during cloudy periods).
    *   Molten salt is commonly used as the storage medium. The solar field heats the molten salt, which is then pumped to an insulated storage tank (hot salt tank). When power is needed, hot salt is pumped to a heat exchanger to generate steam for the turbine. Cooler salt is returned to a cold salt tank.
*   **District Heating and Cooling:** TES systems can store surplus heat or cold generated during off-peak hours or from waste heat sources and deliver it to buildings for heating or cooling.
*   **Enhancing Grid Stability:** TES can provide grid services like frequency regulation and voltage support by absorbing or releasing thermal energy quickly.

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   TES is essential for making intermittent renewable energy sources like solar thermal more dispatchable.
*   Sensible heat storage is the simplest and most common, but has lower energy density.
*   Latent heat storage (PCMs) offers higher energy density at constant temperature.
*   Thermochemical storage offers the highest energy density and long-term storage potential but is more complex.
*   Molten salts are a key TES medium for CSP plants due to their high operating temperatures.

### **2. Chemical Energy Storage (CES)**

Chemical Energy Storage (CES) involves storing energy in the chemical bonds of compounds. Energy is released when these compounds undergo chemical reactions. While not always directly integrated into the thermal cycle of a conventional power plant, it plays a crucial role in energy systems, particularly for large-scale, long-duration storage and in the context of fuels.

**2.1 Key Concepts and Definitions:**

*   **Chemical Energy Storage (CES):** The storage of energy in the form of chemical potential energy within molecules.
*   **Charge/Discharge Process:**
    *   **Charging:** Energy is supplied to the system to create or transform chemical compounds.
    *   **Discharging:** The stored chemical energy is released through a chemical reaction, typically producing heat or electricity directly.
*   **Energy Density:** The amount of energy stored per unit mass or volume. CES systems generally have high energy densities.
*   **Cycle Life:** The number of charge-discharge cycles a system can undergo before significant degradation.

**2.2 Types of CES Systems:**

#### **2.2.1 Electrochemical Storage (Batteries)**

Batteries store energy through reversible electrochemical reactions. While often considered a separate category, their chemical basis is fundamental to CES.

*   **Types:**
    *   **Lead-Acid Batteries:** Mature technology, used in vehicles and backup power systems.
    *   **Lithium-ion Batteries:** High energy density, widely used in portable electronics and electric vehicles, becoming more common for grid-scale storage. Different chemistries (Li-NMC, Li-FePO4) offer varying characteristics.
    *   **Flow Batteries (e.g., Vanadium Redox Flow Batteries):** Electrolytes are stored in external tanks, allowing for independent scaling of power and energy capacity. Good for long-duration grid storage.
    *   **Sodium-Sulfur (NaS) Batteries:** Operate at high temperatures, suitable for grid applications.
*   **Advantages:** High efficiency, rapid charge/discharge rates, modularity.
*   **Disadvantages:** Cost, lifespan limitations, safety concerns (for some chemistries), material availability.
*   **Applications:** Grid-scale energy storage (for grid stability, renewable integration), electric vehicles, backup power.
*   *Example (El Wakil):* El Wakil's text might broadly categorize batteries as chemical storage, emphasizing their role in providing stored electrical energy. He could mention the basic principles of ion movement between electrodes.

#### **2.2.2 Hydrogen Storage**

Hydrogen can be produced (e.g., via electrolysis powered by renewable energy) and stored, then used in fuel cells to generate electricity or burned directly.

*   **Production:** Electrolysis of water ($2H_2O \rightarrow 2H_2 + O_2$).
*   **Storage Methods:**
    *   **Compressed Gas:** Stored in high-pressure tanks (e.g., 350-700 bar).
    *   **Liquefied Hydrogen:** Stored at very low temperatures (-253°C). High energy density by volume, but energy-intensive to liquefy and requires specialized tanks.
    *   **Material-based Storage:** Absorption into metal hydrides, adsorption onto porous materials (e.g., carbon nanotubes), or chemical storage in compounds like ammonia ($NH_3$) or methanol ($CH_3OH$).
*   **Discharge:**
    *   **Fuel Cells:** Electrochemical conversion of hydrogen and oxygen into electricity, water, and heat ($2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + Energy$). Fuel cells offer high efficiency.
    *   **Combustion:** Burning hydrogen in turbines or engines (less efficient than fuel cells and can produce NOx emissions if not managed).
*   **Advantages:** High gravimetric energy density (hydrogen itself), no carbon emissions at point of use (when produced renewably), can be used for long-duration storage.
*   **Disadvantages:** Storage challenges (volume, pressure, temperature), production costs (electrolysis), infrastructure development, safety.
*   **Applications:** Grid-scale storage, transportation fuel, industrial feedstock.
*   *Example (Nag):* Nag's "Power Plant Engineering" would likely discuss hydrogen as a fuel for future power generation, including its production via electrolysis (potentially powered by off-peak nuclear or renewable energy) and its use in fuel cells for electricity generation or in gas turbines.

#### **2.2.3 Chemical Looping**

This is a more advanced CES concept that uses reversible chemical reactions, often involving solid materials, to store and release heat or to facilitate combustion with inherent CO2 capture.

*   **Principle:** A reactive material cycles between oxidized and reduced states, driven by heat. For example, metal oxides can react with a fuel (like natural gas) to release heat and produce $CO_2$ while oxidizing. The reduced metal oxide can then be re-oxidized in a separate stream of air, releasing the stored heat.
*   **Advantages:** Potential for inherent CO2 capture, improved combustion efficiency.
*   **Disadvantages:** Complex systems, material degradation.
*   **Applications:** Advanced power cycles, industrial process heating.

#### **2.2.4 Other Chemical Storage Methods:**

*   **Synthetic Fuels:** Producing fuels like methane ($CH_4$) or ammonia ($NH_3$) from renewable electricity and carbon sources (e.g., $CO_2$ and water) for easier storage and transport.
*   **Biochemical Storage:** Energy stored in biomass through photosynthesis.

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   CES is crucial for long-duration energy storage and for decoupling energy production from consumption.
*   Batteries are a key CES technology for grid-scale applications, offering flexibility and fast response.
*   Hydrogen is a promising CES medium with a role in decarbonizing sectors and for long-duration storage, but faces infrastructure and cost challenges.
*   The "charging" of a CES system often involves consuming electrical or thermal energy to create chemical bonds, and "discharging" involves breaking these bonds to release energy.

### **3. Integrating Energy Storage with Power Plants (CO1, CO4)**

Energy storage systems can significantly enhance the performance and economic viability of various power plant types.

*   **Steam Power Plants (Coal, Gas):**
    *   TES can be used in conjunction with Combined Heat and Power (CHP) plants to store surplus heat during low demand periods for later use in district heating.
    *   Batteries can provide grid services (frequency regulation, peak shaving) to improve the overall economics of gas-fired power plants.
*   **Gas Turbine Power Plants:**
    *   Compressed Air Energy Storage (CAES) is a form of TES where air is compressed and stored underground (e.g., in caverns) during off-peak hours, then heated and expanded through a gas turbine during peak demand.
    *   Battery storage can be used for "peak shaving," reducing the need for the gas turbine to ramp up and down frequently.
*   **Hydroelectric Power Plants:**
    *   Pumped-storage hydropower (PSH) is the most established form of large-scale energy storage. During off-peak hours, electricity is used to pump water from a lower reservoir to an upper reservoir. During peak hours, water is released from the upper reservoir to drive turbines and generate electricity. This is a form of mechanical energy storage, but it's a significant power plant integration example.
*   **Nuclear Power Plants:**
    *   Nuclear power provides stable baseload electricity. TES can be used to store surplus heat from the nuclear reactor for district heating or industrial processes, improving the overall efficiency of the nuclear plant.
    *   Hydrogen production via electrolysis using nuclear power is a way to store this reliable energy chemically for later use.
*   **Renewable Energy Integration (Solar PV, Wind):**
    *   Batteries and TES are essential for stabilizing the output of intermittent renewables, smoothing out fluctuations and providing reliable power when the source is unavailable.

**CO1 Alignment:** Understanding TES and CES helps explain the working of "various energy storage systems" which are often integrated with different types of power plants to enhance their operation and grid integration.

### **Practice Questions and Exercises:**

**Question 1:**
(CO4: K2)
Describe the fundamental difference between sensible heat storage and latent heat storage. Provide one example of a storage medium and application for each.

**Answer 1:**
*   **Sensible Heat Storage:** Stores heat by changing the temperature of a material. The amount of heat stored is proportional to the mass, specific heat capacity, and temperature change ($Q = m \times c_p \times \Delta T$).
    *   **Medium Example:** Water, Rocks.
    *   **Application Example:** Solar water heaters (water), packed bed thermal storage in CSP plants (rocks).
*   **Latent Heat Storage:** Stores heat by changing the phase of a material (e.g., melting, solidifying) at a nearly constant temperature. The heat stored is proportional to the mass and latent heat of fusion ($Q = m \times L$).
    *   **Medium Example:** Paraffins, Salt Hydrates.
    *   **Application Example:** Phase change materials in building walls for temperature regulation, thermal management in electronics.

---

**Question 2:**
(CO4: K2)
Explain the role of molten salt in concentrated solar power (CSP) plants. What type of TES does it primarily utilize?

**Answer 2:**
Molten salt in CSP plants acts as a thermal energy storage medium. It is heated by the concentrated solar radiation and then stored in insulated tanks. When electricity is needed, hot molten salt is pumped through a heat exchanger to generate steam, which drives a turbine. This allows the CSP plant to generate electricity even when the sun is not shining. Molten salt primarily utilizes **sensible heat storage** as its temperature changes to store and release heat. However, the phase change from solid to liquid (melting) is also an aspect of its use, though the primary storage mechanism is the temperature rise.

---

**Question 3:**
(CO4: K2)
What are the main advantages and disadvantages of storing energy using hydrogen compared to lithium-ion batteries for grid-scale applications?

**Answer 3:**
**Hydrogen Storage:**
*   **Advantages:**
    *   **Long-Duration Storage:** Can store energy for days or weeks with minimal loss.
    *   **High Energy Density (Gravimetric):** Hydrogen has a high energy content per unit mass.
    *   **Potential for Decarbonization:** Can be used to decarbonize sectors beyond electricity (transportation, industry).
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Lower Energy Density (Volumetric):** Requires high pressure or low temperatures for storage, leading to larger storage volumes or energy penalties.
    *   **Lower Round-trip Efficiency:** The overall process of producing, storing, and converting hydrogen back to electricity typically has lower efficiency (e.g., 30-50%) compared to batteries.
    *   **Infrastructure Costs:** Significant investment needed for production (electrolyzers), storage, and delivery infrastructure.
    *   **Storage Challenges:** Managing high pressures or cryogenic temperatures.

**Lithium-ion Batteries:**
*   **Advantages:**
    *   **High Round-trip Efficiency:** Typically 80-90%.
    *   **Fast Response Time:** Can rapidly charge and discharge.
    *   **Mature Technology:** For short-to-medium duration storage (hours).
    *   **Modularity:** Easily scalable for grid applications.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   **Limited Duration:** Primarily suited for short-to-medium duration storage (hours) due to cost and degradation mechanisms.
    *   **Material Availability and Cost:** Concerns about the supply chains of lithium, cobalt, etc.
    *   **Degradation:** Capacity fades over time and with cycles.
    *   **Safety Concerns:** Some chemistries can be prone to thermal runaway.

---

**Question 4:**
(CO4: K2)
Give an example of a TES system that uses **latent heat storage** and explain how it works for thermal management.

**Answer 4:**
An example of a TES system using latent heat storage is **Phase Change Materials (PCMs) embedded in building materials** (e.g., gypsum boards or concrete).

*   **How it works:** During the day, if the indoor temperature rises above the melting point of the PCM (e.g., a salt hydrate melting at 24°C), the PCM absorbs the excess heat from the room by melting. This process keeps the room cooler. At night, when the room temperature drops below the PCM's freezing point, the PCM solidifies, releasing the stored latent heat back into the room, providing passive heating and stabilizing the temperature. This reduces the need for active heating and cooling systems.
