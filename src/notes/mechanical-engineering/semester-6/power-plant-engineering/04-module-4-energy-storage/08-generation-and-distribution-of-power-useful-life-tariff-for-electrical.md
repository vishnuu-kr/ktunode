# POWER PLANT ENGINEERING - Module 4: Energy Storage

## Topic: Generation and Distribution of Power, Useful Life, Tariff for Electrical Energy

This topic delves into the fundamental aspects of how electrical power is produced, transmitted, and distributed, along with the economic considerations that govern its pricing and the lifespan of power generation equipment. While it touches upon energy storage (Module 4's overarching theme), it primarily focuses on the preceding and subsequent stages in the power delivery chain.

### 1. Generation of Electrical Power

Electrical power is generated from various primary energy sources through different power plant technologies. This section will briefly recap the generation aspect as it relates to the overall power system.

*   **Primary Energy Sources:**
    *   **Fossil Fuels:** Coal, natural gas, oil (used in thermal power plants).
    *   **Renewable Sources:** Hydroelectric potential (water), solar radiation, wind energy, geothermal energy, biomass.
    *   **Nuclear Fuels:** Uranium, Thorium (used in nuclear power plants).

*   **Power Plant Types (Brief Recap - primarily covered in CO1):**
    *   **Thermal Power Plants (Coal, Gas, Oil):** Convert thermal energy from fuel combustion into mechanical energy (turbines) and then into electrical energy (generators).
        *   *Key Cycle:* Rankine Cycle (for steam turbines).
    *   **Hydroelectric Power Plants:** Convert potential energy of water into kinetic energy (turbines) and then into electrical energy (generators).
    *   **Nuclear Power Plants:** Utilize nuclear fission to generate heat, which produces steam to drive turbines and generators.
    *   **Renewable Power Plants (Solar, Wind, Geothermal):** Directly convert renewable energy forms into electrical energy, often with different conversion mechanisms than thermal plants.

*   **Key Components in Generation:**
    *   **Prime Mover:** Turbine (steam, gas, hydraulic, wind) or engine.
    *   **Generator:** Converts mechanical energy into electrical energy (based on electromagnetic induction).
    *   **Auxiliary Equipment:** Boilers, condensers, pumps, cooling towers, fuel handling systems, control systems, etc.

### 2. Distribution of Power

The generated electrical power needs to be efficiently transported from the power plant to the consumers. This involves a system of transmission and distribution networks.

*   **Transmission:**
    *   **Purpose:** To carry large amounts of power over long distances from generating stations to load centers.
    *   **Voltage Level:** High voltages (e.g., 132 kV, 220 kV, 400 kV, 765 kV) are used to minimize transmission losses.
    *   **Components:**
        *   **Step-up Transformers:** At the generating station to increase voltage for transmission.
        *   **Transmission Lines:** Conductors (usually aluminum or copper) supported by towers.
        *   **Substations:** For voltage transformation and switching.
        *   **Step-down Transformers:** At load centers to reduce voltage for distribution.

*   **Distribution:**
    *   **Purpose:** To deliver power to individual consumers within a locality.
    *   **Voltage Level:** Lower voltages (e.g., 11 kV, 4.15 kV, 440 V, 230 V) are used.
    *   **Components:**
        *   **Primary Distribution Lines:** Carry power from substations to local distribution transformers.
        *   **Distribution Transformers:** Step down the voltage for residential and commercial use.
        *   **Secondary Distribution Lines:** Carry power from distribution transformers to consumers.
        *   **Service Mains:** Connect to individual consumer premises.
    *   **Types of Distribution Systems:**
        *   **Radial System:** Simple and economical, but a single fault can disrupt supply to a large area.
        *   **Loop System:** Provides reliability as power can be supplied from both ends.
        *   **Network System:** Most reliable, with interconnected feeders, but complex and expensive.

**Key Concept:** **Power Loss in Transmission and Distribution:**
*   Power loss ($P_{loss}$) in a conductor is given by $P_{loss} = I^2 R$, where $I$ is the current and $R$ is the resistance of the conductor.
*   Increasing the voltage ($V$) for a given power ($P$) reduces the current ($I = P/V$).
*   Therefore, transmitting power at higher voltages significantly reduces $I^2R$ losses.

**Example:** Transmitting 100 MW at 132 kV requires a current of approximately 437 A. Transmitting the same power at 400 kV requires a current of approximately 144 A. The loss reduction is significant due to the squared relationship with current.

### 3. Useful Life of Power Plant Equipment

The economic viability of a power plant is heavily influenced by the lifespan of its critical components. Understanding useful life helps in planning for replacements, maintenance, and depreciation.

*   **Definition of Useful Life:** The period during which a piece of equipment is expected to remain functional and economically efficient in its intended operation.
*   **Factors Affecting Useful Life:**
    *   **Material Degradation:** Corrosion, erosion, fatigue, creep, oxidation due to high temperatures, pressures, and chemical environments.
    *   **Operating Conditions:** Load variations, start-stop cycles, operating temperature and pressure, quality of fuel and water.
    *   **Maintenance Practices:** Regular and preventive maintenance can significantly extend useful life.
    *   **Technological Advancements:** Obsolescence due to newer, more efficient technologies.
    *   **Design and Manufacturing Quality:** Initial quality of materials and construction.
    *   **Environmental Factors:** Exposure to pollutants, weather conditions.

*   **Typical Useful Life of Key Power Plant Components (Estimates):**
    *   **Boilers:** 20-40 years (can be extended with refurbishing).
    *   **Steam Turbines:** 25-50 years (blades may need replacement more frequently).
    *   **Generators:** 30-50 years.
    *   **Gas Turbines:** 15-30 years (depending on operating hours and duty cycle).
    *   **Pumps and Fans:** 15-30 years.
    *   **Transformers:** 25-40 years.
    *   **Cooling Towers:** 20-30 years.
    *   **Nuclear Reactor Vessels:** Very long life, often exceeding 50 years, but subject to strict safety inspections and potential refits.

*   **Importance of Useful Life:**
    *   **Financial Planning:** For capital recovery, depreciation, and future investments.
    *   **Maintenance Strategy:** Differentiating between preventive, corrective, and predictive maintenance.
    *   **Replacement Planning:** To avoid service disruptions and ensure operational efficiency.
    *   **Economic Analysis:** Cost-benefit analysis of upgrades or retrofits versus replacement.

**Important Point to Remember:** Useful life is an estimate, and actual service life can vary significantly. Regular condition monitoring and life assessment studies are crucial.

### 4. Tariff for Electrical Energy

Tariff refers to the structure of charges for electrical energy supplied to consumers. It aims to recover the cost of generation, transmission, distribution, and provide a reasonable return on investment, while also encouraging efficient energy usage.

*   **Objectives of Tariff:**
    *   **Cost Recovery:** To cover all expenses incurred by the utility.
    *   **Fairness:** To ensure that each consumer class pays its fair share of the costs.
    *   **Revenue Generation:** To meet financial obligations and allow for expansion.
    *   **Demand Management:** To influence consumption patterns and reduce peak loads.
    *   **Conservation:** To encourage efficient use of electricity.

*   **Factors Influencing Tariff:**
    *   **Cost of Service:**
        *   **Fixed Costs:** Capital costs (plant construction, transmission lines), salaries, interest on loans, depreciation.
        *   **Variable Costs:** Fuel costs, operating and maintenance expenses that vary with output.
    *   **Load Factor:** The ratio of average load to the maximum demand over a period. A higher load factor leads to lower cost per unit of energy as fixed costs are spread over more units.
    *   **Diversity Factor:** The ratio of the sum of individual maximum demands of various consumers to the maximum demand of the system. A higher diversity factor reduces the overall capacity required, thus lowering costs.
    *   **Demand Factor:** The ratio of the maximum demand of a consumer to the total connected load of that consumer.
    *   **Nature of Load:** Industrial loads are often steadier than residential loads.
    *   **Cost of Energy Storage:** If storage is used, its capital and operating costs must be factored in.
    *   **Government Policies and Regulations:** Subsidies, taxes, environmental regulations.

*   **Types of Tariffs:**

    1.  **Simple Tariff:**
        *   **Description:** A flat rate per unit of energy consumed, regardless of the magnitude or time of consumption.
        *   **Formula:** Total Charge = Rate per unit × Energy consumed.
        *   **Pros:** Simple to understand and administer.
        *   **Cons:** Does not differentiate between costly peak demand and cheap off-peak demand, discouraging load factor improvement.

    2.  **Flat Rate Tariff:**
        *   **Description:** A fixed charge per unit of energy based on the connected load or demand, irrespective of the energy consumed. Often used for specific categories like street lighting or agriculture.
        *   **Formula:** Total Charge = (Rate per kW of connected load) × Connected Load.
        *   **Pros:** Simple for certain applications.
        *   **Cons:** Does not reward energy conservation.

    3.  **Block Tariff:**
        *   **Description:** The rate per unit of energy changes in blocks of consumption. The rate decreases as consumption increases.
        *   **Example:** First 100 units @ Rs. 5/unit, next 200 units @ Rs. 4/unit, above 300 units @ Rs. 3/unit.
        *   **Pros:** Encourages conservation by penalizing higher consumption.
        *   **Cons:** Can be complex to administer if many blocks are used.

    4.  **Two-Part Tariff:**
        *   **Description:** The charge has two components: a fixed charge (based on maximum demand or connected load) and a variable charge (based on energy consumed).
        *   **Formula:** Total Charge = (Fixed Charge per kW of Demand) × Maximum Demand + (Variable Charge per unit of Energy) × Energy Consumed.
        *   **Pros:** Accurately reflects the costs associated with both demand and energy. Encourages reduction of maximum demand.
        *   **Cons:** Requires accurate measurement of maximum demand, which can be complex.

    5.  **Maximum Demand Tariff (or Demand Tariff):**
        *   **Description:** The charge is based on the consumer's maximum demand during the billing period.
        *   **Formula:** Total Charge = (Rate per kW of Maximum Demand) × Maximum Demand.
        *   **Pros:** Directly addresses the cost of providing capacity.
        *   **Cons:** Requires demand meters.

    6.  **Sliding Scale Tariff:**
        *   **Description:** Similar to block tariff, but the rate varies based on the cost of fuel.
        *   **Example:** If the cost of coal increases, the tariff rate also increases proportionally.
        *   **Pros:** Allows utilities to pass on increased fuel costs to consumers.
        *   **Cons:** Can lead to fluctuating bills for consumers.

    7.  **Time-of-Use (TOU) Tariff:**
        *   **Description:** Rates vary depending on the time of day or season, reflecting the varying costs of generation and demand. Peak hours have higher rates, off-peak hours have lower rates.
        *   **Pros:** Encourages shifting of load from peak to off-peak periods, improving load factor and reducing the need for expensive peak generation capacity or storage.
        *   **Cons:** Requires smart meters and complex billing systems.

    8.  **Power Factor Tariff:**
        *   **Description:** An additional charge or discount is applied based on the consumer's power factor. A low power factor increases reactive power demand and system losses.
        *   **Formula:** Penalty if Power Factor < Threshold, or Discount if Power Factor > Threshold.
        *   **Pros:** Encourages consumers to improve their power factor, leading to better system efficiency.
        *   **Cons:** Requires power factor monitoring.

**Key Concepts in Tariff Calculation:**
*   **Load Factor (LF) = Average Load / Maximum Demand**
*   **Diversity Factor (DF) = Sum of Individual Max Demands / System Max Demand (DF ≥ 1)**
*   **Demand Factor (DemF) = Max Demand of Consumer / Total Connected Load of Consumer (DemF ≤ 1)**

### Practice Questions and Exercises:

**Question 1:**
A thermal power plant has a connected load of 200 MW and a maximum demand of 160 MW. The plant operates for 8000 hours in a year. The total annual energy generated is $1.28 \times 10^6$ MWh.
(a) Calculate the load factor.
(b) If the cost of generation is Rs. 4 per kWh, calculate the total annual revenue if a simple tariff is used.
(c) If a two-part tariff is used with a fixed charge of Rs. 1000 per kW of maximum demand per annum and a running charge of Rs. 2 per kWh, calculate the total annual revenue.

**Answer 1:**
(a)
Average Load = Total Energy Generated / Total Operating Hours
Average Load = $1.28 \times 10^6$ MWh / 8000 h = 160 MW

Load Factor (LF) = Average Load / Maximum Demand
LF = 160 MW / 160 MW = 1

(b)
Cost per kWh = Rs. 4
Total Energy Consumed = $1.28 \times 10^6$ MWh = $1.28 \times 10^9$ kWh

Total Annual Revenue (Simple Tariff) = Cost per kWh × Total Energy Consumed
Total Annual Revenue = Rs. 4/kWh × $1.28 \times 10^9$ kWh = Rs. $5.12 \times 10^9$

(c)
Fixed Charge = Rs. 1000/kW/annum
Maximum Demand = 160 MW = 160,000 kW

Total Annual Fixed Charge = Fixed Charge × Maximum Demand
Total Annual Fixed Charge = Rs. 1000/kW/annum × 160,000 kW = Rs. $1.6 \times 10^8$

Running Charge = Rs. 2/kWh
Total Annual Energy Consumed = $1.28 \times 10^9$ kWh

Total Annual Running Charge = Running Charge × Total Energy Consumed
Total Annual Running Charge = Rs. 2/kWh × $1.28 \times 10^9$ kWh = Rs. $2.56 \times 10^9$

Total Annual Revenue (Two-Part Tariff) = Total Annual Fixed Charge + Total Annual Running Charge
Total Annual Revenue = Rs. $1.6 \times 10^8$ + Rs. $2.56 \times 10^9$ = Rs. $2.72 \times 10^9$

**Question 2:**
A power utility is considering implementing a Time-of-Use (TOU) tariff. The cost of generation during peak hours (6 PM - 10 PM) is Rs. 6/kWh, and during off-peak hours (all other times) is Rs. 3/kWh. If a consumer shifts 50 kWh of their demand from peak to off-peak hours, what is the potential saving for the consumer per day?

**Answer 2:**
Saving per kWh = Cost during peak - Cost during off-peak
Saving per kWh = Rs. 6/kWh - Rs. 3/kWh = Rs. 3/kWh

Total Daily Saving = Saving per kWh × Energy Shifted
Total Daily Saving = Rs. 3/kWh × 50 kWh = Rs. 150

The consumer would save Rs. 150 per day by shifting 50 kWh of demand.

**Question 3:**
List and briefly explain any three types of tariffs used for electrical energy supply.

**Answer 3:**
1.  **Simple Tariff:** A single rate is charged for every unit of energy consumed, irrespective of the time or magnitude of consumption. It is easy to administer but doesn't incentivize load factor improvement.
2.  **Two-Part Tariff:** Consists of a fixed charge (based on maximum demand) and a variable charge (based on energy consumed). It accounts for both demand-related costs and energy-related costs, encouraging consumers to reduce their peak demand.
3.  **Time-of-Use (TOU) Tariff:** Charges different rates for energy consumption depending on the time of day or season. Peak hour consumption is charged at a higher rate, while off-peak hour consumption is charged at a lower rate, promoting load shifting and better utilization of power generation and storage assets.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Voltage and Losses:** Higher transmission voltages significantly reduce $I^2R$ losses.
*   **Load Factor and Cost:** A higher load factor generally leads to lower per-unit cost of electricity.
*   **Useful Life:** Influenced by material science, operating conditions, and maintenance. It's crucial for economic planning.
*   **Tariff Design:** Aims to balance cost recovery, fairness, and load management. TOU tariffs are becoming increasingly important with the integration of energy storage and distributed generation.
*   **Energy Storage Integration:** While this topic focuses on core generation/distribution economics, energy storage technologies (covered in the broader module) will increasingly influence tariff structures by enabling load shifting, peak shaving, and providing grid services.
