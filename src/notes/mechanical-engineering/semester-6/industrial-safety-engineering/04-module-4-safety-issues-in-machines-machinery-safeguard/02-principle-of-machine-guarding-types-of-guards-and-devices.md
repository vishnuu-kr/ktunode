# Industrial Safety Engineering: Module 4 - Safety Issues in Machines: Machinery Safeguarding

## Topic: Principles of Machine Guarding - Types of Guards and Devices

This module focuses on ensuring safety when working with machinery, a critical aspect of Industrial Safety Engineering. Understanding and implementing effective machine guarding is paramount to preventing accidents and protecting workers.

### **1. Introduction to Machine Guarding**

Machine guarding refers to the **physical barriers or devices** that are installed on machinery to prevent workers from coming into contact with hazardous moving parts. Its primary objective is to eliminate or reduce the risk of injury from:

*   **Moving parts:** Rotating, reciprocating, transverse, or revolving parts.
*   **Flying chips, sparks, or debris:** Generated during cutting, grinding, or other operations.
*   **Sharp edges or points:** Exposed parts of the machine.
*   **Hot surfaces:** Heated components of the machine.
*   **Electrical hazards:** Exposed electrical wiring or components.

**Key Concept:** The core principle of machine guarding is to **isolate the hazard from the operator**.

**Importance of Machine Guarding:**

*   **Prevents Injuries:** Significantly reduces the incidence of severe injuries like amputations, lacerations, abrasions, and crush injuries.
*   **Compliance with Regulations:** Meets legal and regulatory requirements (e.g., OSHA in the US, similar bodies globally).
*   **Increased Productivity:** Workers feel safer and can operate machinery more confidently, leading to better output.
*   **Reduced Downtime:** Fewer accidents mean less disruption to production schedules.

**Reference:**
*   **Jain, R.K. (2000).** *Industrial Safety, Health and Environment Management Systems.* Emphasizes the role of engineering controls like guarding in a comprehensive safety management system.
*   **Grimaldi & Simonds (2001).** *Safety Management.* Highlights guarding as a fundamental element of preventing accidents caused by mechanical hazards.

### **2. Fundamental Principles of Machine Guarding**

Effective machine guarding adheres to several key principles to ensure worker safety and operational efficiency.

**Key Principles:**

*   **Permanent and Fixed:** Guards should be a permanent part of the machine and not easily removable by the operator. They should be securely attached.
*   **Enclose the Hazard:** The guard should completely enclose the hazardous moving parts, preventing any body part from reaching the danger zone.
*   **No New Hazards:** The guard itself should not create new hazards, such as sharp edges, pinch points, or tripping hazards.
*   **Permit Lubrication and Maintenance:** Guards should allow for routine lubrication and maintenance without complete removal, if possible. Access ports with interlocks are often used.
*   **Adequate Visibility:** Operators should be able to see the work area and the operation clearly through or around the guard.
*   **Durable Construction:** Guards should be made of materials strong enough to withstand impact and wear.
*   **Proper Ventilation:** Guards should not impede necessary ventilation for the machine's operation.
*   **No Obstruction to Work:** Guards should not interfere with the operator's ability to perform their work efficiently.

**Example:** A fixed barrier guard on a rotating shaft prevents accidental contact while still allowing the operator to observe the process.

**Important Point to Remember:** The primary goal is to **prevent access to the danger zone** during operation.

### **3. Classification of Machine Guards**

Machine guards can be classified based on their function, design, or how they are attached.

**Key Classifications:**

**A. Based on Function/Design:**

*   **Fixed Guards:**
    *   **Description:** These are permanently attached barriers that enclose a hazardous area. They are typically made of metal, plastic, or wire mesh. They require tools for removal.
    *   **Examples:** Cages around rotating flywheels, solid enclosures around gearboxes, mesh guards on fan blades.
    *   **Pros:** Highly effective, permanent solution.
    *   **Cons:** Can hinder access for maintenance or cleaning if not designed with access points.
    *   **Reference:** **Blake, R.P. (1973).** *Industrial Safety.* Discusses fixed guards as the most common and reliable type.

*   **Interlocked Guards:**
    *   **Description:** These guards are connected to the machine's control system. When the guard is opened or removed, the machine automatically stops or is prevented from starting. When the guard is closed and secured, the machine can operate.
    *   **Examples:** Guards on radial drills, presses, or CNC machines that stop the spindle when the door is opened.
    *   **Pros:** Ensures safety during operation and provides easier access for maintenance when the machine is off.
    *   **Cons:** Can be complex and expensive, and may be bypassed if not properly designed or maintained.
    *   **Reference:** **Anton, T.J. (1989).** *Occupational Safety and Health Management.* Covers interlocked guards as an advanced safety feature.

*   **Adjustable Guards:**
    *   **Description:** These guards can be adjusted to accommodate different workpiece sizes or operational needs. They are designed to maintain a protective barrier at all times.
    *   **Examples:** Guards on table saws that can be raised or lowered as the material thickness changes, adjustable chip deflectors.
    *   **Pros:** Versatile for varying operations.
    *   **Cons:** Can be less secure if not properly adjusted or maintained.
    *   **Reference:** **Krishnan, N.V. (1997).** *Safety Management in Industry.* Mentions adjustable guards for their flexibility.

*   **Self-Adjusting Guards:**
    *   **Description:** These guards automatically adjust their position based on the movement of the machine or the workpiece. They often use springs or counterweights.
    *   **Examples:** Guards on power presses that retract as the ram descends and return as it rises, guards on band saws that follow the material.
    *   **Pros:** Provide continuous protection without operator intervention.
    *   **Cons:** Can be subject to mechanical failure or malfunction.

**B. Based on Location:**

*   **Enclosing Guards:**
    *   **Description:** These completely enclose the hazardous moving parts.
    *   **Examples:** Full enclosures around a milling machine or lathe.

*   **Point-of-Operation Guards:**
    *   **Description:** These guards are specifically designed to protect the operator from the point where the work is actually being performed (e.g., where cutting, shaping, or forming occurs).
    *   **Examples:** Guards on power presses, circular saws, band saws, drill presses.

*   **Power Transmission Guards:**
    *   **Description:** These guards protect parts of the machine that transmit power, such as gears, belts, pulleys, flywheels, and chains.
    *   **Examples:** Covers over belt and pulley systems, enclosures for gear trains.

### **4. Types of Machine Guarding Devices**

Beyond fixed barriers, specialized devices are employed to enhance machine safety.

**Key Devices:**

*   **Presence-Sensing Devices:**
    *   **Description:** These devices detect the presence of a person in or near a danger zone and stop the machine immediately. They are often used for equipment that has a short stopping time.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Photoelectric (Light Curtain) Sensors:** Emit a beam of light; if the beam is broken, the machine stops.
        *   **Capacitive Sensors:** Detect the presence of a conductive body (human body) near the sensing field.
        *   **Radio Frequency (RF) Sensors:** Similar to capacitive sensors, detecting presence through RF fields.
    *   **Examples:** Light curtains on automated machinery, safety mats around presses.
    *   **Pros:** Provide non-contact sensing, allowing for easier access to the machine when it's safe.
    *   **Cons:** Can be sensitive to dust, debris, or environmental conditions. Require careful setup and maintenance.
    *   **Reference:** **AIChE/CCPS (1992).** *Guidelines for Hazard Evaluation Procedures.* Discusses safety instrumented systems which can incorporate presence-sensing principles.

*   **Pullback Devices:**
    *   **Description:** These devices use cables or straps connected to the operator's hands or body and to moving parts of the machine. As the hazardous motion begins, the cables pull the operator's hands away from the danger zone.
    *   **Examples:** Primarily used on presses and similar machines where the operator manually positions the workpiece.
    *   **Pros:** Actively removes the operator's hands from the danger zone.
    *   **Cons:** Relies on proper adjustment and can be uncomfortable.

*   **Restraint Devices:**
    *   **Description:** These devices limit the operator's reach to the danger zone.
    *   **Examples:** Wrist straps connected to a fixed point, preventing the operator from reaching into the machine's operating area.
    *   **Pros:** Prevents access to the danger zone.
    *   **Cons:** Can restrict movement and be inconvenient.

*   **Safety Trip Controls:**
    *   **Description:** Devices that the operator can activate to stop the machine immediately in an emergency.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Emergency Stop Buttons (E-stops):** Clearly marked buttons that, when pressed, cut power to the machine.
        *   **Safety Trip Wires/Bars:** A wire or bar that, when pulled or depressed, stops the machine.
    *   **Examples:** E-stop buttons on conveyors, emergency trip wires along the length of a machine.
    *   **Pros:** Provide a quick and direct way to stop a machine in an emergency.
    *   **Cons:** Effectiveness depends on the operator's quick reaction and the machine's stopping time.

*   **Two-Hand Controls:**
    *   **Description:** Require the operator to use both hands simultaneously to activate a machine cycle. This ensures that both hands are away from the danger zone during the hazardous part of the operation.
    *   **Examples:** Common on presses, stamping machines, and assembly operations.
    *   **Pros:** Keeps hands clear of the point of operation.
    *   **Cons:** Can slow down the operation and may not be suitable for all tasks.

### **5. Guarding Techniques and Materials**

The selection of guarding techniques and materials is crucial for effectiveness and durability.

**Common Guarding Techniques:**

*   **Barriers:** Physical barriers (fixed, interlocked, adjustable) are the most common.
*   **Enclosures:** Fully enclosing hazardous areas.
*   **Shields:** Transparent or translucent shields to allow visibility.
*   **Shielding of Rotating Shafts:** Guarding with solid covers or mesh.
*   **Belt and Pulley Guards:** Covers that prevent access to the nip points between belts and pulleys.
*   **Gear Guards:** Enclosures for meshing gears.
*   **Flywheel Guards:** Barriers around rotating flywheels.
*   **Fan Guards:** Mesh or perforated covers for rotating fan blades.

**Guard Materials:**

*   **Metal:**
    *   **Steel:** Strong and durable, suitable for heavy-duty applications.
    *   **Aluminum:** Lighter than steel, good corrosion resistance.
    *   **Wire Mesh:** Provides visibility and ventilation, often used for larger enclosures. Mesh openings must be appropriately sized to prevent fingers from passing through.
*   **Plastic:**
    *   **Polycarbonate:** Strong, transparent, and impact-resistant. Excellent for guards where visibility is critical.
    *   **Acrylic:** Transparent but more brittle than polycarbonate.
*   **Other:**
    *   **Fiberglass Reinforced Plastic (FRP):** Offers good strength and chemical resistance.

**Important Considerations for Material Selection:**

*   **Strength and Durability:** Must withstand operational forces and potential impacts.
*   **Visibility:** Transparent materials are preferred when visual monitoring is required.
*   **Corrosion Resistance:** Important in certain environments.
*   **Flammability:** Material should not be easily flammable.
*   **Cost and Maintainability:** Practical considerations for implementation.

### **6. Guarding Specific Machine Types (Examples)**

Understanding how guards are applied to different machinery is key.

*   **Power Presses:**
    *   **Hazards:** Crushing, shearing, cutting at the point of operation.
    *   **Guards:** Full enclosures, light curtains, two-hand controls, barrier guards, die guards.
*   **Lathes:**
    *   **Hazards:** Entanglement with rotating chuck, leadscrew, or workpiece; ejection of chips.
    *   **Guards:** Chuck guards, chip guards, carriage limit stops, emergency stops.
*   **Milling Machines:**
    *   **Hazards:** Entanglement with rotating cutter; contact with moving table; ejection of chips.
    *   **Guards:** Cutter guards, chip shields, enclosures for moving parts.
*   **Circular Saws:**
    *   **Hazards:** Contact with rotating blade, kickback, ejection of material.
    *   **Guards:** Blade guard (retracting or fixed), anti-kickback devices, splitters.
*   **Drill Presses:**
    *   **Hazards:** Entanglement with rotating spindle or chuck; contact with rotating workpiece.
    *   **Guards:** Chuck guards, spindle guards, motor guards.
*   **Grinding Wheels:**
    *   **Hazards:** Bursting wheel, entanglement, contact with abrasive surface, ejected particles.
    *   **Guards:** Wheel guards (cup guards), machine enclosures, eye shields, splash guards.
    *   **Reference:** **Blake, R.P. (1973).** *Industrial Safety.* Provides detailed discussions on guarding for various specific machines.

### **7. Practice Questions and Exercises**

**Question 1:**
What is the primary objective of machine guarding?
a) To increase machine speed
b) To isolate hazardous moving parts from the operator
c) To improve the aesthetics of the machine
d) To reduce the need for operator training

**Answer:** b) To isolate hazardous moving parts from the operator

**Question 2:**
Describe the difference between a fixed guard and an interlocked guard. Provide an example for each.

**Answer:**
*   **Fixed Guard:** A permanent barrier that requires tools to remove. Example: A solid metal enclosure around the gears of a conveyor system.
*   **Interlocked Guard:** Connected to the machine's control system; opening it stops the machine. Example: A safety door on a CNC machine that stops the spindle when opened.

**Question 3:**
Name two types of presence-sensing devices used in machine guarding.

**Answer:**
1.  Photoelectric (Light Curtain) Sensors
2.  Capacitive Sensors
3.  (Other correct answers include RF sensors)

**Question 4:**
Why are two-hand controls often used on power presses?

**Answer:** Two-hand controls ensure that the operator's hands are away from the point of operation during the hazardous stroke of the press, thus preventing crush injuries.

**Question 5:**
A guard made of wire mesh with openings large enough for a finger to pass through would be considered effective. True or False? Justify your answer.

**Answer:** False. Effective guards must have mesh openings small enough to prevent a finger or other body parts from reaching the danger zone. The specific size of the opening depends on the distance from the hazard.

**Exercise:**
Imagine you are designing a safety system for a new automated packaging machine. List at least three types of hazards associated with its moving parts and suggest appropriate guarding measures (guards or devices) for each hazard.

**Example Solution for Exercise:**

1.  **Hazard:** Entanglement with rotating rollers.
    **Guard/Device:** Fixed enclosure guard around the rollers, or an interlocked guard that stops the machine if accessed.
2.  **Hazard:** Pinch point between a moving gripper arm and a stationary part.
    **Guard/Device:** A self-adjusting guard that follows the gripper arm or a presence-sensing device (light curtain) to stop the arm if a hand is detected.
3.  **Hazard:** Ejection of small packaging components during high-speed operation.
    **Guard/Device:** A transparent polycarbonate shield to contain any ejected components, allowing visibility of the operation.

### **8. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Guard all hazardous moving parts.** Never assume a part is too fast or too small to cause injury.
*   **Guards must be kept in place** and not removed or bypassed.
*   **Regular inspection and maintenance** of guards are crucial.
*   **Training** is essential for operators on the proper use and importance of machine guards.
*   **Consider the entire machine lifecycle:** Guarding needs to be considered from design through operation and maintenance.
*   **Hierarchy of Controls:** Guarding is an **engineering control**, which is generally preferred over administrative controls or PPE for machine safety.
*   **Refer to relevant standards and regulations** (e.g., national safety standards, manufacturer guidelines) for specific requirements.
*   **Human factors** play a role; guards should be designed to be as user-friendly as possible without compromising safety.
