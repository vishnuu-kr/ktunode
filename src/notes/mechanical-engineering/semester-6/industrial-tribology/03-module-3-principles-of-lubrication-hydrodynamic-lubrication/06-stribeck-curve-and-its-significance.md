# Industrial Tribology: Module 3: Principles of Lubrication: Hydrodynamic Lubrication

## Topic: The Stribeck Curve and its Significance

---

### 1. Introduction to Lubrication Regimes and the Stribeck Curve

**Objective:** To understand the different lubrication regimes and how the Stribeck curve illustrates the transition between them. (K2 - CO1)

*   **Lubrication:** The process of reducing friction and wear between surfaces in relative motion by the interposition of a lubricating film.
*   **Tribological System:** Consists of contacting surfaces, a lubricant, and the environment.
*   **Lubrication Regimes:** Different states of contact based on the effectiveness of the lubricant film separating the surfaces. These regimes are characterized by the level of solid-to-solid contact.

The **Stribeck curve** is a fundamental graphical representation in tribology that plots the **friction coefficient ($\mu$)** against a **dimensionless speed parameter**. It effectively maps the different lubrication regimes encountered under varying operating conditions.

#### Key Concepts:

*   **Friction Coefficient ($\mu$):** The ratio of the frictional force to the normal force between two surfaces.
*   **Dimensionless Speed Parameter:** A parameter that combines speed, load, and lubricant viscosity to characterize the operating conditions. A common form is:

    $S = \frac{\eta N}{P}$

    Where:
    *   $\eta$ (eta): Dynamic viscosity of the lubricant (Pa·s or N·s/m²)
    *   $N$: Relative speed of the surfaces (m/s or rad/s, depending on context)
    *   $P$: Applied pressure or load per unit area (Pa or N/m²)

#### The Stribeck Curve: A Visual Representation

The Stribeck curve typically exhibits three distinct regions:

1.  **Boundary Lubrication Region:**
    *   **Characteristics:** Very low speeds and/or high loads. The lubricant film is very thin, and there is significant direct solid-to-solid contact between the asperities of the surfaces.
    *   **Friction:** High friction coefficient.
    *   **Wear:** Significant wear due to metal-to-metal contact.
    *   **Lubricant Mechanism:** Relies on adsorbed layers of lubricant molecules and boundary additives to provide a degree of protection.

2.  **Mixed Lubrication Region:**
    *   **Characteristics:** Intermediate speeds and loads. Both the lubricant film and asperity contact contribute to friction and wear. The lubricant film is partially effective, but some asperities still penetrate it.
    *   **Friction:** Friction coefficient decreases as speed increases and the lubricant film becomes more effective.
    *   **Wear:** Moderate wear, a combination of abrasive, adhesive, and fatigue wear.
    *   **Lubricant Mechanism:** A balance between hydrodynamic effects and boundary lubrication.

3.  **Hydrodynamic Lubrication Region (or Full Film Lubrication):**
    *   **Characteristics:** High speeds and/or low loads. The lubricant film is thick enough to completely separate the surfaces, preventing direct solid-to-solid contact.
    *   **Friction:** Relatively low and constant friction coefficient. Friction is primarily due to viscous shear within the lubricant film.
    *   **Wear:** Minimal wear, often termed "running-in" wear if initial surface irregularities are smoothed out.
    *   **Lubricant Mechanism:** The wedge-shaped film of lubricant is continuously generated by the relative motion of the surfaces, supporting the load.

#### **Visualizing the Stribeck Curve:**

```
Friction Coefficient (μ)
      ^
      |       /
      |      / Boundary Lubrication
      |     /
      |    /
      |   /
      |  /
      | /---------- Mixed Lubrication
      |/ \
      |   \
      |    \
      |     \ Hydrodynamic Lubrication
      |      \
      +--------------------------------> Dimensionless Speed Parameter (S)
```

**Important Point:** The shape of the Stribeck curve is sensitive to surface roughness, lubricant properties, and operating conditions. (K2 - CO1)

### 2. The Physics Behind the Lubrication Regimes

**Objective:** To explain the physical mechanisms responsible for the observed friction behavior in each lubrication regime. (K2 - CO1)

#### 2.1 Boundary Lubrication

*   **Asperity Contact:** Under low speeds and high loads, the lubricant film is too thin to fully separate the microscopic peaks (asperities) of the opposing surfaces.
*   **Direct Contact:** Asperities interlock and slide against each other, leading to high friction and adhesive wear.
*   **Role of Lubricant Film:** The lubricant, though thin, provides a barrier. Polar lubricant molecules (e.g., fatty acids) can adsorb onto metal surfaces, forming a thin, protective layer.
*   **Boundary Additives:** Many lubricants contain specific additives (e.g., anti-wear agents like ZDDP, extreme pressure additives like sulfur/phosphorus compounds) that react with the metal surfaces under high localized pressure and temperature to form sacrificial films that prevent catastrophic welding.
*   **Friction Source:** Primarily solid-solid friction between asperities, augmented by shear forces within the adsorbed molecular layers.

#### 2.2 Mixed Lubrication

*   **Partial Separation:** As speed increases or load decreases, the hydrodynamic pressure within the lubricant film starts to build up, providing some separation.
*   **Intermittent Contact:** The lubricant film is not consistently thick enough to prevent all asperity interactions. There are periods of direct contact and periods of fluid film separation.
*   **Transition Zone:** This regime represents the transition from boundary to hydrodynamic lubrication.
*   **Friction Source:** A combination of:
    *   Viscous shear in the lubricant film.
    *   Solid-solid friction at points of asperity contact.
    *   Deformation of asperities.

#### 2.3 Hydrodynamic Lubrication (Full Film Lubrication)

*   **Hydrodynamic Wedge:** Relative motion between the surfaces draws the lubricant into a converging gap (a "wedge"). This motion generates a pressure build-up within the lubricant film, which supports the applied load.
*   **Complete Separation:** The generated hydrodynamic pressure creates a fluid film thick enough to completely separate the mating surfaces. This thickness is typically on the order of lubricant molecules to micrometers, depending on the operating conditions.
*   **No Solid Contact:** Friction is solely due to the internal resistance of the lubricant to flow (viscous shear).
*   **Newton's Law of Viscosity:** The shear stress ($\tau$) in a Newtonian fluid is proportional to the shear rate ($\dot{\gamma}$): $\tau = \eta \dot{\gamma}$.
*   **Friction Source:** Viscous shear within the lubricant film. This friction is generally lower than in boundary or mixed lubrication.

**Reference:**
*   Stachowiak & Batchelor (2000) extensively discuss these regimes and the formation of hydrodynamic pressure in Chapter 3.
*   Bhushan (2013) also provides a thorough explanation of the mechanisms in Chapter 4.

**Important Point:** The "minimum film thickness" that separates the surfaces is critical. If this minimum thickness falls below a certain threshold (often related to the combined surface roughness), wear and increased friction will occur. (K2 - CO1)

### 3. Significance of the Stribeck Curve in Industrial Applications

**Objective:** To understand why the Stribeck curve is a vital tool for tribologists and engineers in selecting lubricants and designing tribological systems. (K3 - CO5, K2 - CO4)

The Stribeck curve is indispensable for:

1.  **Lubricant Selection:**
    *   By knowing the operating conditions (speed, load, temperature) and the corresponding dimensionless speed parameter ($S$), engineers can choose a lubricant that operates the tribological system in the desired regime.
    *   For example, in a gear system experiencing high loads and moderate speeds, a lubricant with good boundary or EP additives might be necessary to prevent wear in the mixed lubrication regime.
    *   In a high-speed bearing, the goal is to operate in the full hydrodynamic regime to minimize friction and wear. (K3 - CO5)

2.  **System Design and Operation:**
    *   The Stribeck curve helps in understanding the operational limits of a tribological system. If operating conditions are changed (e.g., increased load, decreased speed), the system might transition to a less desirable regime (e.g., from hydrodynamic to mixed or boundary).
    *   It guides engineers on how to adjust operating parameters (speed, load) to maintain optimal lubrication.
    *   **Example:** In a journal bearing, increasing the rotational speed (N) or decreasing the load (P) will increase the $S$ value, moving the system towards the hydrodynamic regime, which is generally preferred for low friction and wear. (K3 - CO5)

3.  **Troubleshooting and Diagnostics:**
    *   If a machine is experiencing premature wear or excessive friction, analyzing the operating conditions and comparing them to the expected position on the Stribeck curve can help diagnose the problem.
    *   The issue might be an inappropriate lubricant, insufficient lubricant supply, incorrect operating speed or load, or surface damage that has altered the lubrication characteristics. (K3 - CO5)

4.  **Understanding Surface Interactions:**
    *   The curve implicitly reflects the role of surface roughness and the effectiveness of lubricant additives in each regime.
    *   Changes in surface topography (due to wear or surface treatments) will alter the Stribeck curve. (K2 - CO2)

5.  **Predicting Wear and Friction:**
    *   While the Stribeck curve primarily illustrates friction, it is strongly correlated with wear. The boundary and mixed lubrication regimes are associated with significant wear, whereas the hydrodynamic regime has minimal wear.
    *   The transition points on the curve indicate the onset of increased wear. (K2 - CO3)

#### **Example Application:**

Consider a journal bearing in an engine.
*   **Starting up/Idling:** Low speed, engine still building pressure. System likely operates in **boundary** or **mixed lubrication**. Boundary additives are crucial here to protect against wear.
*   **Cruising:** High speed, moderate load. System ideally operates in **hydrodynamic lubrication**, where friction is low and wear is minimal.
*   **Under heavy acceleration:** High load, moderate speed. System may enter the **mixed lubrication** regime. The lubricant must be able to sustain adequate film thickness and have sufficient boundary protection.

The Stribeck curve helps engineers select an oil with the right viscosity and additive package to ensure good performance across all these conditions. (K3 - CO5)

**Reference:**
*   Williams (2005) emphasizes the practical implications of the Stribeck curve for selecting lubricants and understanding bearing performance.
*   Hutchings (2017) discusses how the curve relates to wear mechanisms in different lubrication regimes.

**Important Point:** The Stribeck curve is a powerful conceptual tool that allows engineers to visualize and analyze the complex behavior of lubricating films under varying operating conditions. (K3 - CO5)

### 4. Factors Influencing the Stribeck Curve

**Objective:** To identify and understand how various factors modify the shape and position of the Stribeck curve. (K2 - CO1)

The Stribeck curve is not a universal constant but is influenced by several parameters:

1.  **Viscosity ($\eta$):**
    *   **Higher Viscosity:** A higher viscosity lubricant will generate more hydrodynamic pressure at a given speed. This shifts the curve towards **lower $S$ values** for the onset of hydrodynamic lubrication. It generally leads to thicker film thicknesses and potentially lower friction in the hydrodynamic regime.
    *   **Lower Viscosity:** Results in thinner films and shifts the curve towards higher $S$ values for hydrodynamic lubrication.
    *   **Temperature Dependence:** Viscosity decreases significantly with increasing temperature. This is why operating temperature is a critical factor.

2.  **Load ($P$):**
    *   **Higher Load:** Requires greater hydrodynamic pressure to support the load. This means higher speeds are needed to achieve full film lubrication. The transition to hydrodynamic lubrication occurs at **higher $S$ values**. Thicker films are generally harder to maintain under higher loads.
    *   **Lower Load:** Less pressure is required, leading to hydrodynamic lubrication at lower $S$ values and potentially thinner films.

3.  **Speed ($N$):**
    *   **Higher Speed:** Contributes to generating higher hydrodynamic pressure. This allows for hydrodynamic lubrication at **lower $S$ values** and promotes thicker film formation.
    *   **Lower Speed:** Requires higher viscosity or lower load to achieve hydrodynamic lubrication, shifting the transition to higher $S$ values.

4.  **Surface Roughness:**
    *   **Smoother Surfaces:** Can sustain thinner lubricant films and will exhibit hydrodynamic lubrication at lower $S$ values. The friction coefficient in the hydrodynamic regime might be slightly lower.
    *   **Rougher Surfaces:** Require thicker lubricant films to achieve full separation. This shifts the transition to hydrodynamic lubrication to **higher $S$ values**. Rougher surfaces can lead to higher friction in the mixed regime as asperities are more prone to contact.
    *   **Reference:** Stachowiak & Batchelor (2000) dedicate a chapter to surface topography and its impact.

5.  **Lubricant Properties (Additives):**
    *   **Boundary Additives (e.g., ZDDP, fatty acids):** Improve performance in the boundary and mixed lubrication regimes by forming protective films. This can lead to a **lower friction coefficient** and **reduced wear** in these regions, effectively "lifting" the friction curve in the boundary and mixed zones.
    *   **Extreme Pressure (EP) Additives:** Form sacrificial films under very high contact pressures, preventing metal welding. Crucial for high-load applications.
    *   **Viscosity Modifiers:** Improve the lubricant's viscosity-temperature behavior.

6.  **Temperature:**
    *   As mentioned, temperature directly affects viscosity. Increased temperature leads to decreased viscosity, which shifts the Stribeck curve towards higher $S$ values for hydrodynamic lubrication and can potentially reduce film thickness. (K2 - CO4)

7.  **Contamination:**
    *   Solid contaminants (e.g., wear debris, dirt) can significantly alter the lubrication regime, often promoting wear and increasing friction by causing abrasive action and interrupting the lubricant film.

**Important Point:** The Stribeck curve is a map of the tribological system's behavior under changing conditions, and modifying any of these influencing factors will redraw that map. (K2 - CO1)

### 5. Stribeck Curve vs. Other Lubrication Regimes

**Objective:** To differentiate the Stribeck curve from other specific lubrication regimes and understand their relationships. (K2 - CO1)

While the Stribeck curve plots friction across *all* regimes, it's important to be precise about the definitions:

*   **Boundary Lubrication:** Governed by surface chemistry and adsorbed layers. Friction is high and sensitive to surface treatments and additive packages.
*   **Hydrodynamic Lubrication (Full Film):** Governed by fluid mechanics and the generation of a pressure wedge. Friction is low and dictated by lubricant viscosity and shear.
*   **Mixed Lubrication:** A combination of both, where the film is insufficient to prevent all contact, and both surface interactions and fluid shear contribute to friction.

Other related lubrication concepts:

*   **Elastohydrodynamic Lubrication (EHL):** Occurs in highly loaded contacts with elastic deformation of surfaces (e.g., rolling element bearings, cams). The lubricant film is very thin (sub-micron) but sufficient to separate surfaces. EHL films are resilient and can withstand high pressures. The Stribeck curve applies to EHL as well, but the specific $S$ parameter and film thickness calculations differ slightly due to the elastic deformation.
    *   **Reference:** Stachowiak & Batchelor (2000) discuss EHL in detail.

*   **Hydrostatic Lubrication:** Load is supported by an externally pressurized lubricant, not by the motion of the surfaces. This is common in precision machinery. The Stribeck curve is typically associated with *hydrodynamic* and *mixed* lubrication where motion generates pressure.

*   **Squeeze Film Lubrication:** Lubricant film is generated by the surfaces moving closer together, trapping the fluid. This contributes to damping vibrations.

**Key Distinction:** The Stribeck curve is a *framework* that illustrates how friction *transitions* between the boundary, mixed, and hydrodynamic (and EHL) regimes as operating conditions ($S$) change. It's not a regime itself but a depiction of how regimes manifest in terms of friction.

**Important Point:** Understanding the specific regime (boundary, mixed, hydrodynamic, EHL) is crucial for selecting the right lubricant and predicting performance within that regime, while the Stribeck curve shows how to navigate between them. (K2 - CO1)

### 6. Surface Characterization and Wear Measurement in Relation to Stribeck Curve

**Objective:** To connect surface characterization and wear measurement techniques to the Stribeck curve. (K2 - CO2, K2 - CO3)

#### 6.1 Surface Characterization Techniques

*   **Surface Roughness Measurement (e.g., profilometry, AFM):**
    *   **Significance:** Directly influences the minimum lubricant film thickness required for full separation. Rougher surfaces necessitate thicker films, pushing the transition to hydrodynamic lubrication to higher $S$ values.
    *   **Application:** Used to assess the "waviness" and "roughness" parameters that affect the lubrication regime. Higher roughness can mean more asperity contact in the mixed regime.
    *   **Reference:** Bhushan (2013) covers various surface characterization methods.

*   **Surface Texture Analysis (e.g., bearing ratio curves, correlation functions):**
    *   **Significance:** Provides a more detailed understanding of the surface topography than just average roughness. Helps predict how asperities will interact with the lubricant film.
    *   **Application:** Understanding the "worst-case" asperity contact and its impact on friction and wear in the mixed regime.

#### 6.2 Wear Measurement Techniques

*   **Wear Scar Measurement (e.g., optical microscopy, profilometry):**
    *   **Significance:** Quantifies the material loss due to friction and contact. The size and morphology of wear scars are indicative of the lubrication regime.
    *   **Application:** In the boundary and mixed regimes, significant wear scars are expected. In the hydrodynamic regime, wear is minimal. Measuring wear scars helps validate Stribeck curve predictions about wear severity.
    *   **Reference:** Hutchings (2017) details wear measurement methods.

*   **Mass Loss Measurement (e.g., gravimetric analysis):**
    *   **Significance:** Direct measurement of material removed.
    *   **Application:** Can be used to determine the overall wear rate of components operating under different conditions, helping to confirm the wear characteristics associated with each region of the Stribeck curve.

*   **Friction Force Measurement (as plotted on the Stribeck Curve):**
    *   **Significance:** Friction itself is a form of energy dissipation and can lead to material degradation.
    *   **Application:** The Stribeck curve is fundamentally a plot of friction coefficient versus operating parameter. Changes in friction directly indicate transitions between lubrication regimes and potential changes in wear mechanisms. (K2 - CO3)

**Important Point:** Surface topography directly dictates the film thickness requirements for each regime, and wear measurements confirm the predicted material degradation occurring in the boundary and mixed lubrication regimes as depicted by the Stribeck curve. (K2 - CO2, K2 - CO3)

### 7. Practice Questions and Answers

**Objective:** To test understanding of the Stribeck curve and its implications. (K2, K3 - CO1, CO4, CO5)

**Question 1:**
The Stribeck curve plots the friction coefficient ($\mu$) against which of the following dimensionless parameters?
a) Load divided by viscosity
b) Viscosity-speed product divided by load
c) Load divided by speed
d) Viscosity divided by (load x speed)

**Answer 1:**
b) Viscosity-speed product divided by load ($S = \frac{\eta N}{P}$)

---

**Question 2:**
In which region of the Stribeck curve is there significant direct solid-to-solid contact between asperities, leading to high friction and wear?
a) Hydrodynamic Lubrication
b) Mixed Lubrication
c) Boundary Lubrication
d) EHL Lubrication

**Answer 2:**
c) Boundary Lubrication

---

**Question 3:**
What is the primary mechanism responsible for load support in the Hydrodynamic Lubrication region of the Stribeck curve?
a) Adsorption of lubricant molecules on surfaces
b) Elastic deformation of surfaces
c) Generation of lubricant pressure due to the wedge effect
d) Chemical reaction of additives with the metal surface

**Answer 3:**
c) Generation of lubricant pressure due to the wedge effect

---

**Question 4:**
If you observe a sudden increase in friction and wear in a bearing system, and you know the load has increased significantly while the speed remained constant, how would this typically be represented on a Stribeck curve analysis? (Assume you started in the hydrodynamic regime).

**Answer 4:**
An increase in load ($P$) while speed ($N$) is constant would decrease the dimensionless speed parameter ($S = \frac{\eta N}{P}$). This would shift the operating point to the left on the Stribeck curve, potentially moving the system from the hydrodynamic regime into the mixed or even boundary lubrication regime, leading to increased friction and wear.

---

**Question 5:**
A design engineer needs to select a lubricant for a critical component that operates under widely varying loads and speeds. Based on the Stribeck curve concept, what are two key properties the lubricant should possess to ensure reliable performance across these conditions? (Relates to CO4)

**Answer 5:**
1.  **Good Viscosity-Temperature Characteristics (e.g., high Viscosity Index):** This ensures the viscosity doesn't drop too drastically with expected temperature increases, helping to maintain adequate film thickness and prevent a premature shift to boundary lubrication.
2.  **Effective Boundary and/or Extreme Pressure (EP) Additives:** These additives are crucial for providing protection during low-speed, high-load conditions or transient states where full hydrodynamic lubrication is not achieved, preventing excessive wear and friction.

### 8. Summary and Important Points to Remember

*   The **Stribeck curve** plots friction coefficient ($\mu$) vs. a dimensionless speed parameter ($S = \frac{\eta N}{P}$).
*   It illustrates three primary lubrication regimes: **Boundary**, **Mixed**, and **Hydrodynamic (Full Film)**.
*   **Boundary Lubrication:** High friction, significant wear, relies on adsorbed layers and additives. Low $S$.
*   **Mixed Lubrication:** Intermediate friction and wear, combination of asperity contact and fluid film. Moderate $S$.
*   **Hydrodynamic Lubrication:** Low friction, minimal wear, surfaces fully separated by a lubricant film generated by motion. High $S$.
*   The curve is influenced by **viscosity, load, speed, surface roughness, temperature, and lubricant additives.**
*   The Stribeck curve is a vital tool for **lubricant selection, system design, and troubleshooting** in industrial applications. (K3 - CO5)
*   **Surface characterization** (roughness) and **wear measurements** are essential for validating the predictions made using the Stribeck curve framework. (K2 - CO2, K2 - CO3)
*   Understanding the Stribeck curve helps engineers achieve desired performance by operating in the optimal lubrication regime for their specific application. (K3 - CO5)
