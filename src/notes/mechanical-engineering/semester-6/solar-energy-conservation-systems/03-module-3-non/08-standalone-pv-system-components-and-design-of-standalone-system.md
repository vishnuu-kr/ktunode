## Module 3: Non-Standalone PV Systems

### Topic: Components and Design of Standalone PV Systems

---

**Module Overview:** This module focuses on photovoltaic (PV) systems that operate independently of the main electricity grid. We will delve into the essential components of these systems and the fundamental principles guiding their design to meet specific energy demands.

### Key Concepts and Definitions:

*   **Standalone PV System (Off-Grid PV System):** A photovoltaic system that is not connected to the utility electricity grid. It is self-sufficient and typically relies on battery storage to provide power when solar generation is insufficient.
*   **Load:** The total energy demand of the appliances and devices that the PV system will power.
*   **Solar Irradiance:** The amount of solar power received per unit area, typically measured in Watts per square meter (W/m²).
*   **Peak Sun Hours (PSH):** The equivalent number of hours per day during which the solar irradiance averages 1000 W/m². This is a key parameter for sizing PV arrays.
*   **Battery Bank:** A collection of batteries connected together to store electrical energy generated by the PV array.
*   **Charge Controller:** A device that regulates the voltage and current coming from the solar panels to the batteries, preventing overcharging and deep discharge.
*   **Inverter:** A device that converts the direct current (DC) electricity produced by the solar panels and stored in the batteries into alternating current (AC) electricity, which is used by most household appliances.
*   **Balance of System (BOS):** All components of a PV system other than the PV modules themselves, including batteries, charge controllers, inverters, wiring, mounting structures, and safety devices.

### 1. Components of a Standalone PV System:

A typical standalone PV system consists of the following key components:

*   **Photovoltaic (PV) Array:**
    *   **Function:** Converts sunlight directly into DC electricity through the photovoltaic effect.
    *   **Description:** Composed of multiple solar panels (modules) connected in series and/or parallel to achieve the desired voltage and current.
    *   **Key Considerations:** Panel efficiency, voltage, current, temperature coefficient, and durability.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Sukhatme (Chapter on Solar Photovoltaic Systems), Goswami et al. (Chapter on Photovoltaic Devices).

*   **Battery Bank:**
    *   **Function:** Stores the electrical energy generated by the PV array for use during periods of low sunlight or high demand.
    *   **Description:** Typically uses deep-cycle batteries, such as lead-acid (gel or AGM) or lithium-ion, designed for repeated charging and discharging.
    *   **Key Considerations:**
        *   **Capacity (Ampere-hours - Ah):** Determines the amount of energy the battery can store.
        *   **Voltage (V):** System voltage (e.g., 12V, 24V, 48V).
        *   **Depth of Discharge (DoD):** The percentage of the battery's capacity that is discharged. Lower DoD extends battery life.
        *   **Autonomy Days:** The number of days the system can operate without any solar input.
        *   **Battery Chemistry:** Lead-acid (cheaper, heavier, shorter lifespan), Lithium-ion (more expensive, lighter, longer lifespan, higher efficiency).
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Messenger & Ventre (Chapter on Energy Storage), Solanki (Chapter on Storage Devices).

*   **Charge Controller:**
    *   **Function:** Manages the flow of energy from the PV array to the battery bank, protecting the batteries from overcharging, deep discharge, and reverse current flow at night.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **PWM (Pulse Width Modulation):** Simple and cost-effective, suitable for smaller systems. It effectively matches the panel voltage to the battery voltage.
        *   **MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking):** More sophisticated and efficient, especially in variable conditions (cloudy skies, varying temperatures). It actively finds the optimal voltage and current combination from the PV array to maximize power output.
    *   **Key Considerations:** Voltage and current ratings, MPPT vs. PWM, battery type compatibility, system voltage.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Sukhatme (Section on charge controllers), Solanki (Chapter on Charge Controllers).

*   **Inverter:**
    *   **Function:** Converts the DC power from the PV array and battery bank into AC power, which is compatible with most standard appliances and electronics.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Modified Sine Wave:** Less expensive, but can cause issues with sensitive electronics, motors, and appliances with electronic controls.
        *   **Pure Sine Wave:** Produces clean AC power, similar to grid power, and is suitable for all types of appliances.
    *   **Key Considerations:**
        *   **Continuous Power Rating (Watts):** The steady power output the inverter can provide.
        *   **Surge Power Rating (Watts):** The peak power the inverter can deliver for short durations (e.g., when starting motors).
        *   **Input Voltage (DC):** Must match the battery bank voltage.
        *   **Output Voltage and Frequency (AC):** Standard is 120V/240V at 60Hz (in North America) or 230V at 50Hz (in Europe/Asia).
        *   **Efficiency:** The percentage of DC power converted to AC power.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Messenger & Ventre (Chapter on Inverters), Boxwell (Chapter on Inverters).

*   **Mounting Structure:**
    *   **Function:** Supports the PV array and positions it at an optimal angle and orientation to maximize solar energy capture.
    *   **Types:** Ground mounts, roof mounts, pole mounts.
    *   **Key Considerations:** Structural integrity, wind load resistance, tilt angle, azimuth (orientation), durability.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Sukhatme (Section on mounting structures), Tiwari (Chapter on Structural Aspects).

*   **Wiring and Protection Devices:**
    *   **Function:** Connects all the system components and provides safety.
    *   **Components:** DC and AC cables, connectors (MC4), fuses, circuit breakers, surge protectors, disconnect switches.
    *   **Key Considerations:** Proper wire sizing to minimize voltage drop, correct polarity, protection against overcurrent and short circuits.
    *   **Textbook Reference:** Solanki (Chapter on Safety and Protection), Messenger & Ventre (Section on wiring).

### 2. Design of Standalone PV Systems:

Designing a standalone PV system involves a systematic process to ensure it reliably meets the energy needs of the user.

#### 2.1. Load Assessment:

This is the **most critical first step**. You need to determine the total daily energy consumption in Watt-hours (Wh) or kilowatt-hours (kWh).

*   **Steps:**
    1.  **List all appliances/loads:** Identify every device that will be powered by the system.
    2.  **Determine power rating (Watts) of each appliance:** Found on the device's label.
    3.  **Estimate daily usage hours for each appliance:** How many hours per day will it be used?
    4.  **Calculate daily energy consumption per appliance:** Power (W) × Usage Hours (h) = Energy (Wh).
    5.  **Sum daily energy consumption for all appliances:** This gives the total daily load in Wh.
    6.  **Account for inverter efficiency:** The inverter is not 100% efficient. You need to generate slightly more DC energy to compensate. Multiply the total AC load by the inverter's efficiency (e.g., 0.90 for 90% efficiency).
    7.  **Account for battery charging/discharging efficiency:** Batteries also have losses. Multiply by battery efficiency (e.g., 0.85 for 85%).
    8.  **Factor in system losses:** These include wiring losses, temperature effects, and module degradation. A typical factor is around 1.2-1.3.

*   **Example:**
    *   1 light bulb (10W) used for 5 hours/day = 50 Wh
    *   1 mobile phone charger (5W) used for 3 hours/day = 15 Wh
    *   1 TV (50W) used for 4 hours/day = 200 Wh
    *   **Total AC Load:** 50 Wh + 15 Wh + 200 Wh = 265 Wh/day
    *   **DC Load (considering inverter efficiency of 90%):** 265 Wh / 0.90 = 294.4 Wh/day
    *   **Daily Energy Requirement (considering battery efficiency of 85% and other losses of 20%):** 294.4 Wh / (0.85 * 0.80) = **~433 Wh/day**

*   **Textbook Reference:** Messenger & Ventre (Chapter on System Design), Solanki (Chapter on System Sizing).

#### 2.2. Solar Resource Assessment:

You need to know how much solar energy is available at your location.

*   **Key Parameter:** Peak Sun Hours (PSH) for the least sunny month of the year. This ensures reliable power year-round.
*   **Sources:** Online databases (e.g., NREL PVWatts, meteorological data), local climate data.
*   **Example:** If your location receives an average of 4 PSH in the least sunny month, you have 4 hours of equivalent 1000 W/m² irradiance.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Sukhatme (Chapter on Solar Radiation), Goswami et al. (Chapter on Solar Resource Assessment).

#### 2.3. PV Array Sizing:

This determines the total capacity of the solar panels needed.

*   **Formula:**
    **PV Array Size (Wp) = Daily Energy Requirement (Wh/day) / Peak Sun Hours (h/day)**
    *(Note: Wp stands for Watt-peak, the output of a module under Standard Test Conditions (STC).)*

*   **Example (Continuing from above):**
    *   Daily Energy Requirement = 433 Wh/day
    *   Peak Sun Hours (least sunny month) = 4 PSH
    *   **PV Array Size = 433 Wh / 4 h = 108.25 Wp**
    *   You would then select panels to meet this requirement, e.g., two 60Wp panels or one 120Wp panel. It's often good practice to oversize slightly (e.g., by 10-20%) to account for system degradation and less-than-ideal conditions. So, a 120-150 Wp array might be chosen.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Messenger & Ventre (Chapter on PV Array Sizing), Solanki (Chapter on PV Array Sizing).

#### 2.4. Battery Bank Sizing:

This ensures you have enough energy storage for periods without sunlight.

*   **Key Factors:**
    *   **Daily Energy Requirement (Wh/day):** From load assessment.
    *   **Depth of Discharge (DoD):** The maximum percentage of the battery's capacity that can be safely discharged to prolong its life. (e.g., 50% for lead-acid, 80% for lithium-ion).
    *   **Autonomy Days:** The number of days the system can run without solar charging. (e.g., 2-3 days for critical loads).
    *   **Battery Efficiency:** The energy lost during charging and discharging.

*   **Formula:**
    **Battery Capacity (Ah) = (Daily Energy Requirement (Wh/day) × Autonomy Days) / (DoD (%) × Battery Voltage (V) × Battery Efficiency)**

*   **Example (Continuing from above):**
    *   Daily Energy Requirement = 433 Wh/day
    *   Autonomy Days = 2
    *   DoD = 50% (0.50) for lead-acid batteries
    *   Battery Voltage = 12V
    *   Battery Efficiency = 85% (0.85)
    *   **Battery Capacity (Ah) = (433 Wh/day × 2 days) / (0.50 × 12V × 0.85) = 866 Wh / 5.1 V = ~169.8 Ah**
    *   You would select a battery bank with a capacity of at least 170 Ah at 12V. If using a 24V system, you would need two 12V batteries in series, or a single 24V battery with half the Ah rating.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Messenger & Ventre (Chapter on Battery Sizing), Solanki (Chapter on Battery Sizing).

#### 2.5. Charge Controller Sizing:

The charge controller must be able to handle the maximum current from the PV array and the system voltage.

*   **Current Rating:**
    **Charge Controller Current (A) ≥ PV Array Short-Circuit Current (Isc) × 1.25** (A safety factor is applied).
*   **Voltage Rating:** Must be equal to or greater than the PV array's open-circuit voltage (Voc).

*   **Textbook Reference:** Solanki (Chapter on Charge Controller Sizing).

#### 2.6. Inverter Sizing:

The inverter's continuous power rating must be sufficient for the total simultaneous AC load, and its surge rating must accommodate starting surges of motors.

*   **Continuous Power:** Sum of the power ratings of all AC loads that will be running simultaneously.
*   **Surge Power:** Identify the appliance with the highest starting surge (e.g., refrigerator, pump) and ensure the inverter's surge capacity covers it.

*   **Example:** If you expect to run a 50W TV and a 20W fan simultaneously, the continuous load is 70W. If the TV has a 500W surge, the inverter must handle at least that. A 300-500W inverter might be chosen.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Boxwell (Chapter on Inverter Sizing).

### 3. System Configuration and Protection:

*   **Series/Parallel Connections:**
    *   **Series:** Increases voltage, current remains the same. Used to match the PV array voltage to the battery bank voltage or charge controller input voltage.
    *   **Parallel:** Increases current, voltage remains the same. Used to increase the total current output from the array.
*   **Wiring:** Use appropriately sized cables to minimize voltage drop and energy loss. Follow local electrical codes.
*   **Fuses and Circuit Breakers:** Essential for protecting components from overcurrent and short circuits. Place fuses/breakers between the array and controller, controller and battery, and battery and inverter.
*   **Disconnect Switches:** Allow for safe isolation of components for maintenance or troubleshooting.
*   **Grounding:** Proper grounding is crucial for safety and lightning protection.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Solanki (Chapter on Wiring and Protection), Messenger & Ventre (Chapter on System Protection).

---

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Load Assessment is Paramount:** An accurate assessment of energy needs is the foundation of a successful standalone PV system design.
*   **Worst-Case Scenario:** Design for the least sunny month to ensure year-round reliability.
*   **Efficiency Matters:** Account for the inefficiencies of batteries and inverters in your calculations.
*   **Battery Health:** Proper battery management (avoiding deep discharge, overcharging) is crucial for extending battery lifespan.
*   **MPPT vs. PWM:** MPPT controllers are generally preferred for efficiency, especially in variable conditions, but PWM is adequate for simpler, smaller systems.
*   **Pure Sine Wave Inverters:** Necessary for most modern electronics and sensitive appliances.
*   **Safety First:** Always use proper wiring, protection devices, and follow installation guidelines.

### Practice Questions:

**Question 1:**
A remote cabin requires 500 Wh of energy per day. The location receives an average of 3.5 Peak Sun Hours (PSH) during the winter months (when energy demand is highest). The inverter efficiency is 88%, and battery charging/discharging efficiency is 80%.
a) Calculate the total DC energy requirement per day, accounting for inverter losses.
b) Calculate the total daily energy that needs to be generated by the PV array, accounting for battery losses.
c) What is the minimum Watt-peak (Wp) rating for the PV array?

**Answer 1:**
a) **DC Energy Requirement = AC Load / Inverter Efficiency**
   DC Energy Requirement = 500 Wh / 0.88 = 568.18 Wh/day

b) **Total PV Generation = DC Energy Requirement / Battery Efficiency**
   Total PV Generation = 568.18 Wh / 0.80 = 710.23 Wh/day

c) **PV Array Size (Wp) = Total PV Generation / PSH**
   PV Array Size (Wp) = 710.23 Wh/day / 3.5 h/day = **202.92 Wp**
   A PV array of approximately 200-220 Wp would be suitable.

---

**Question 2:**
You have calculated that your standalone PV system needs to provide 600 Wh per day. You plan to use deep-cycle lead-acid batteries with a maximum Depth of Discharge (DoD) of 50% and a system voltage of 24V. You want 2 days of autonomy. Assume battery efficiency of 85%.
a) Calculate the required battery capacity in Ampere-hours (Ah).
b) If you are using 12V batteries, how many Ampere-hour rated batteries would you need and how would you connect them to achieve the required 24V system voltage?

**Answer 2:**
a) **Battery Capacity (Ah) = (Daily Energy Requirement (Wh/day) × Autonomy Days) / (DoD (%) × Battery Voltage (V) × Battery Efficiency)**
   Battery Capacity (Ah) = (600 Wh/day × 2 days) / (0.50 × 24V × 0.85)
   Battery Capacity (Ah) = 1200 Wh / 10.2 V = **117.65 Ah**
   You would need a battery bank with a capacity of approximately 118 Ah at 24V.

b) To achieve a 24V system using 12V batteries, you need to connect two 12V batteries in **series**.
   If you need a total capacity of 118 Ah at 24V, and your individual batteries are 12V, you would need two 12V batteries, each with a capacity of at least 118 Ah (since series connection keeps the Ah rating the same). So, you would need **two 12V, 118Ah (or higher) batteries connected in series.**

**Question 3:**
What is the primary function of a charge controller in a standalone PV system?
a) To convert DC to AC power.
b) To store electrical energy.
c) To regulate the voltage and current from the PV array to the batteries.
d) To mount the solar panels.

**Answer 3:**
c) To regulate the voltage and current from the PV array to the batteries.
