## 1. Materials for Flat Belts

The selection of appropriate belt material is critical for ensuring durability, efficiency, and reliability of the belt drive. The ideal material should possess high tensile strength, flexibility, good wear resistance, and resistance to environmental factors.

### Key Concepts & Properties:

*   **Tensile Strength:** The ability of the belt material to withstand pulling forces without breaking. High tensile strength allows for transmitting more power with a smaller cross-section.
*   **Flexibility:** The ability of the belt to bend easily around pulleys. Poor flexibility leads to increased bending stresses and reduced belt life.
*   **Wear Resistance:** The ability to withstand abrasion from pulleys and environmental particles.
*   **Coefficient of Friction:** A measure of the belt's ability to grip the pulley surface.
*   **Elasticity/Stiffness:** The tendency of the belt to return to its original shape after deformation.
*   **Resistance to Environmental Factors:** Ability to withstand heat, oil, moisture, and chemicals depending on the operating environment.
*   **Cost:** An important consideration for economic viability.

### Common Belt Materials:

#### **1.1 Leather Belts**

*   **Description:** Historically, leather has been a very popular choice due to its excellent strength, flexibility, and grip. It's typically made from steer hides, tanned to improve durability.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   High tensile strength.
    *   Good flexibility.
    *   Excellent grip on pulleys.
    *   Durable and long-lasting if properly maintained.
    *   Relatively quiet operation.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Expensive compared to some synthetic materials.
    *   Susceptible to moisture, oil, and heat damage if not treated.
    *   Requires regular maintenance (oiling).
    *   Belt thickness can be uniform or variable (e.g., single, double, or triple ply), affecting flexibility.
*   **Types:** Single ply (for lighter loads), Double ply (for moderate loads), Triple ply (for heavy loads).
*   **Textbook Reference:** Norton (Pearson Education) often discusses traditional materials like leather, highlighting their properties. Bhandari (Tata McGraw Hill) may also cover leather belts in detail.

#### **1.2 Rubber Belts**

*   **Description:** These belts are constructed with layers of fabric (like cotton, nylon, or polyester) embedded in rubber compounds. The fabric provides tensile strength, while the rubber provides flexibility and friction.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   Good flexibility and resistance to abrasion.
    *   Can be manufactured to be resistant to oil, heat, and moisture.
    *   Relatively inexpensive.
    *   Good grip.
    *   Can be made in various lengths and widths.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Tensile strength is generally lower than leather or high-strength synthetic belts.
    *   Susceptible to degradation by oil and heat if not specifically designed for it.
    *   Can stretch over time.
*   **Construction:** Often consist of a carcass (multiple layers of fabric) and rubber covers. The carcass provides the strength, while the rubber provides the necessary grip and protection.
*   **Textbook Reference:** Bhandari and Sharma & Aggarwal are likely to provide detailed information on rubber belt construction and their applications.

#### **1.3 Fabric Belts (Cotton, Nylon, Polyester)**

*   **Description:** These belts are made from woven or braided fabrics, often impregnated with rubber or other materials for improved performance.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   High tensile strength (especially nylon and polyester).
    *   Lightweight and flexible.
    *   Good resistance to stretching.
    *   Can be made oil-resistant and heat-resistant.
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   May have lower friction compared to leather or rubber unless treated.
    *   Can be susceptible to fraying if not properly constructed or protected.
*   **Examples:** Conveyor belts (often heavy-duty fabric), V-belts (often reinforced with synthetic cords like polyester or rayon). While this module focuses on flat belts, the underlying material principles for synthetic cords are relevant.
*   **Textbook Reference:** Bhandari and Sharma & Aggarwal will likely cover these types, especially for applications requiring high strength and low stretch.

#### **1.4 Synthetic Belts (e.g., Polyurethane, PVC)**

*   **Description:** Modern belts made from advanced synthetic polymers offer excellent performance characteristics.
*   **Advantages:**
    *   High tensile strength.
    *   Excellent flexibility.
    *   Good resistance to abrasion, oil, and chemicals.
    *   Low stretch, leading to more consistent speed ratios.
    *   Can be joined seamlessly (e.g., by welding).
*   **Disadvantages:**
    *   Can be more expensive than rubber belts.
    *   Some may have lower friction coefficients than leather.
*   **Textbook Reference:** Juvinall & Marshek, and Budynas & Nisbett might touch upon modern material advancements in belt drives.

## 2. Slip of Belts

Slip is a phenomenon where there is a relative sliding motion between the belt and the pulley surface, occurring even under normal operating conditions.

### Key Concepts & Definitions:

*   **Slip:** The relative sliding of the belt on the pulley surface.
*   **Types of Slip:**
    *   **Positive Slip (or Velocity Slip):** Occurs due to the elasticity of the belt material. As the belt passes over the driving pulley, it stretches slightly due to tension. As it moves to the driven pulley, it contracts slightly. This elasticity causes a difference in the arc length covered by the belt and the pulley circumference per revolution, leading to a reduction in the speed of the driven pulley. This is inherent and cannot be eliminated.
    *   **Negative Slip (or Creep):** This is a misnomer, as it refers to the effect of creep, not actual negative slip. It's often discussed alongside slip.
    *   **Actual Slip (or Sliding):** Occurs when the tangential force exerted by the belt exceeds the maximum friction force between the belt and the pulley. This happens when the tension ratio is too high for the arc of contact and coefficient of friction.
*   **Causes of Slip:**
    *   **Elasticity of the Belt:** As mentioned above, the stretching and contraction of the belt material.
    *   **Insufficient Friction:** If the coefficient of friction between the belt and pulley is low or the arc of contact is small, the belt may not transmit enough force, leading to actual sliding.
    *   **Overtensioning:** Excessive tension can also contribute to slip.
    *   **Worn Pulley Surfaces:** Smooth or worn pulley surfaces reduce friction.
    *   **Contaminated Belt/Pulley Surfaces:** Oil, grease, or dirt reduce friction.

### Effect of Slip:

*   **Reduced Speed Ratio:** The actual speed ratio between the driving and driven pulleys will be lower than the geometric speed ratio (ratio of pulley diameters).
*   **Reduced Power Transmission Efficiency:** Energy is lost due to friction and heat generated by the sliding.
*   **Increased Wear:** Excessive slip can lead to accelerated wear of the belt and pulley surfaces.
*   **Loss of Synchronization:** In applications requiring precise speed ratios, slip can be detrimental.

### Calculation of Slip:

Let:
*   $N_1$ = Speed of the driving pulley (e.g., in rpm)
*   $N_2$ = Speed of the driven pulley (e.g., in rpm)
*   $D_1$ = Diameter of the driving pulley
*   $D_2$ = Diameter of the driven pulley

Ideally, without slip, the velocity ratio is:
$$ \frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{D_2}{D_1} $$

Due to slip, the actual speed of the driven pulley ($N_2$) is less than the ideal speed.
Let $s$ be the slip percentage.
The actual speed of the driven pulley is given by:
$$ N_2 = N_1 \left(1 - s/100 \right) \frac{D_1}{D_2} $$

Alternatively, if $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the slips on the driving and driven pulleys respectively (expressed as a fraction of speed), then:
$$ V_1 = \pi D_1 N_1 $$ (Belt speed at driving pulley)
$$ V_2 = \pi D_2 N_2 $$ (Belt speed at driven pulley)

The slip on the driving pulley is $V_1 - V_{belt\_leaving\_driver}$.
The slip on the driven pulley is $V_{belt\_entering\_driver} - V_2$.

If the slip is primarily due to elasticity (positive slip), the belt leaves the driving pulley slightly slower than $V_1$ and arrives at the driven pulley slightly faster than $V_2$.
A more practical way to express slip is as a percentage reduction in the driven pulley's speed relative to the driving pulley's speed:

$$ \text{Slip \%} = \frac{N_{1,ideal} - N_{2,actual}}{N_{1,ideal}} \times 100 $$
where $N_{1,ideal}$ is the ideal speed of the driven pulley if there were no slip, which is $N_1 \times (D_1/D_2)$.

So,
$$ \text{Slip \%} = \frac{N_1 \frac{D_1}{D_2} - N_2}{N_1 \frac{D_1}{D_2}} \times 100 $$
This can be rearranged to:
$$ N_2 = N_1 \frac{D_1}{D_2} (1 - s/100) $$

*   **Example:** A flat belt drive has a driving pulley of 200 mm diameter running at 1000 rpm and a driven pulley of 400 mm diameter. If the driven pulley runs at 480 rpm, calculate the slip.
    *   Ideal speed of driven pulley ($N_{2,ideal}$) = $N_1 \times (D_1/D_2) = 1000 \times (200/400) = 500$ rpm.
    *   Actual speed of driven pulley ($N_{2,actual}$) = 480 rpm.
    *   Slip = $N_{2,ideal} - N_{2,actual} = 500 - 480 = 20$ rpm.
    *   Slip percentage = $\frac{20}{500} \times 100 = 4\%$.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Bhandari (Tata McGraw Hill) and Sharma & Aggarwal (S.K. Kataria & Sons) provide detailed explanations and derivations for slip calculations.

## 3. Creep

Creep is a phenomenon in belt drives where the belt material undergoes a continuous deformation under sustained tensile stress. This leads to a gradual change in belt length.

### Key Concepts & Definitions:

*   **Creep:** The progressive elongation of the belt material under continuous load due to the gradual slipping of fibers over each other.
*   **Causes of Creep:**
    *   **Material Properties:** The inherent viscoelastic nature of belt materials, especially rubber and fabric, causes them to deform over time under tension.
    *   **Tensile Stress:** The tension applied to the belt is the primary driving force for creep.
    *   **Temperature:** Elevated temperatures can accelerate the creep rate.
*   **Mechanism:** When the belt passes over the pulleys, it experiences varying tensions. On the tight side, it's under high tension, and on the slack side, it's under lower tension. This difference in tension causes a flow of material within the belt, leading to a gradual increase in its length. The belt continuously moves from the high-tension side to the low-tension side, and this slow movement of material is creep.

### Effect of Creep:

*   **Reduced Belt Tension:** As the belt elongates due to creep, the initial tension decreases, which can lead to increased slip and reduced power transmission.
*   **Loss of Speed Ratio:** Creep contributes to the difference between the ideal and actual speed ratios, similar to slip.
*   **Requirement for Take-up Devices:** Belt drives often incorporate take-up devices (e.g., tensioning pulleys or adjustable motor bases) to compensate for creep and maintain adequate belt tension.
*   **Belt Slackening:** Over time, creep can cause the belt to become slack, requiring frequent readjustment.

### Relation to Slip:

Creep and slip are often discussed together because they both contribute to the loss of speed and efficiency in a belt drive. While slip involves instantaneous sliding, creep is a time-dependent phenomenon involving material deformation. Both result in the driven pulley rotating at a slower speed than ideally expected.

Some sources may use the term "creep" to refer to the overall effect of reduced speed in the driven pulley, encompassing both elastic slip and material creep. However, in a strict sense, creep refers to the material deformation.

*   **Textbook Reference:** Bhandari (Tata McGraw Hill) and Sharma & Aggarwal (S.K. Kataria & Sons) are good resources for understanding the concept of creep and its impact on belt drives.

## 4. Centrifugal Tension

Centrifugal tension is the tension induced in a belt due to its rotation at a certain speed. This tension acts outwards from the center of rotation.

### Key Concepts & Definitions:

*   **Centrifugal Tension ($T_c$):** The tension developed in a rotating belt due to centrifugal force. It acts along the belt, pulling it outwards from the center of rotation.
*   **Centrifugal Force:** The outward force experienced by a mass moving in a circular path.

### Calculation of Centrifugal Tension:

Let:
*   $m$ = mass of the belt per unit length (kg/m)
*   $v$ = belt speed (m/s)
*   $T_c$ = centrifugal tension (N)

Consider a small element of the belt of length $dl$. Its mass is $dm = m \cdot dl$.
When this element moves with a linear velocity $v$ in a circular path (effectively, around the pulley), it experiences a centrifugal force:
$$ dF_c = \frac{dm \cdot v^2}{R} $$
where $R$ is the radius of the pulley.

However, it's more common to directly relate centrifugal tension to the belt's mass per unit length and its speed. The centrifugal force acting on the entire belt as it rotates is distributed along its length. The centrifugal tension is the force required to keep the belt moving in a circular path.

A simpler derivation considers the force acting on an arc of the belt subtending an angle $d\theta$ at the center of the pulley.
*   Mass of the arc element: $dm = m \cdot R \cdot d\theta$
*   Centrifugal force on the arc element: $dF_c = \frac{dm \cdot v^2}{R} = \frac{m \cdot R \cdot d\theta \cdot v^2}{R} = m \cdot v^2 \cdot d\theta$

This centrifugal force acts radially outwards. The component of this force tangential to the belt at its ends is what contributes to the centrifugal tension along the belt. Alternatively, and more commonly, the centrifugal tension ($T_c$) itself can be calculated as the force acting on the belt due to its inertia as it rotates.

$$ T_c = m \cdot v^2 $$

where:
*   $m$ is the mass of the belt per unit length (kg/m).
*   $v$ is the belt speed (m/s).

*   **Derivation Insight (from Bhandari):** Consider a belt of length $L$ and mass $M$ rotating at speed $\omega$ rad/s. The belt is assumed to be a flexible chain of mass $M$ moving with velocity $v = \omega R$. The centrifugal force acting on the entire belt is $F_c = \frac{M}{L} L (\omega R)^2 = m L v^2$. This centrifugal force is distributed uniformly along the belt's circumference, and the tension required to counteract this force is the centrifugal tension $T_c$. If we consider a section of belt of length $dL$, its mass is $dm = m \cdot dL$. The centrifugal force on this mass is $dF_c = \frac{dm \cdot v^2}{R} = \frac{m \cdot dL \cdot v^2}{R}$. The centrifugal tension $T_c$ is effectively the force that keeps this mass $dm$ moving in a circle of radius $R$. Thus, $T_c = \frac{dm \cdot v^2}{R} = \frac{m \cdot dL \cdot v^2}{R}$. Since $dL = R \cdot d\theta$, $T_c = \frac{m \cdot R \cdot d\theta \cdot v^2}{R} = m \cdot v^2 \cdot d\theta$. However, this $T_c$ is the tangential force component. The simpler and more directly usable formula is $T_c = m v^2$.

### Effect of Centrifugal Tension on Belt Tension:

Centrifugal tension is always present when the belt is in motion. It adds to the tensions on both the tight side ($T_1$) and the slack side ($T_2$).

*   **Effective Tension:** The net tension available to transmit power is the difference between the tight side and slack side tensions: $T_{effective} = T_1 - T_2$.
*   **Actual Tensions:** When centrifugal tension is considered, the actual tensions on the tight and slack sides are:
    *   Actual Tight Side Tension ($T_{1,actual}$) = $T_1 + T_c$
    *   Actual Slack Side Tension ($T_{2,actual}$) = $T_2 + T_c$

Where $T_1$ and $T_2$ are the tensions due to power transmission requirements and initial tension.
The power transmitted is proportional to $T_1 - T_2$.

$$ P = (T_1 - T_2) v $$

The belt must be strong enough to withstand the maximum tension, which is the actual tight side tension: $T_{1,actual} = T_1 + T_c$.

### Important Considerations:

*   **Belt Speed:** Centrifugal tension increases with the square of the belt speed ($v^2$). This means it becomes significant at higher speeds.
*   **Belt Mass:** Belts with higher mass per unit length will develop higher centrifugal tension.
*   **Belt Strength:** The maximum permissible tension in the belt is limited by its material strength. If $T_{1,actual}$ exceeds this limit, the belt will fail.
*   **Power Transmission Capacity:** For a given belt material and cross-section (which determines maximum allowable tension $T_{max}$), the maximum power that can be transmitted is limited by the belt speed. As speed increases, $T_c$ increases, reducing the available difference $(T_1 - T_2)$ that can be sustained before $T_1 + T_c$ reaches $T_{max}$.
    *   The maximum tension is $T_{1,actual} = T_1 + T_c \le T_{max}$.
    *   The power transmitted is $P = (T_1 - T_2) v$.
    *   From the belt friction equation (or velocity ratio equation), $T_1/T_2 = e^{\mu \theta}$, so $T_1 = T_2 e^{\mu \theta}$.
    *   Substituting $T_1$: $T_2 e^{\mu \theta} + T_c \le T_{max}$.
    *   $T_2 (e^{\mu \theta} + 1) \le T_{max} + T_c$. This is incorrect.
    *   Let's re-evaluate. The initial tension is set such that when running, tensions are $T_1$ and $T_2$. The belt has a strength limit. The tensions on the tight and slack sides *during operation* are $T_{1,actual} = T_1 + T_c$ and $T_{2,actual} = T_2 + T_c$.
    *   The *available* tension for power transmission is $(T_{1,actual} - T_{2,actual}) = (T_1 + T_c) - (T_2 + T_c) = T_1 - T_2$.
    *   The belt must be strong enough to withstand $T_{1,actual}$. So, $T_1 + T_c \le T_{max}$.
    *   We also know $T_1 = T_2 e^{\mu \theta}$.
    *   Substitute $T_1$: $T_2 e^{\mu \theta} + T_c \le T_{max}$.
    *   The power transmitted is $P = (T_1 - T_2) v = T_2 (e^{\mu \theta} - 1) v$.
    *   From $T_2 e^{\mu \theta} + T_c \le T_{max}$, we get $T_2 \le \frac{T_{max} - T_c}{e^{\mu \theta}}$.
    *   So, $P \le \left(\frac{T_{max} - T_c}{e^{\mu \theta}}\right) (e^{\mu \theta} - 1) v$.
    *   $P \le (T_{max} - T_c) \left(1 - \frac{1}{e^{\mu \theta}}\right) v$.

    This equation shows that as speed ($v$) increases, centrifugal tension ($T_c$) increases, reducing the maximum power transmission capacity. There exists an optimal belt speed for maximum power transmission.

*   **Example:** A flat belt has a mass of 0.5 kg/m and is used to transmit power at a speed of 20 m/s. Calculate the centrifugal tension.
    *   $m = 0.5$ kg/m
    *   $v = 20$ m/s
    *   $T_c = m \cdot v^2 = 0.5 \times (20)^2 = 0.5 \times 400 = 200$ N.

*   **Textbook Reference:** All major textbooks (Norton, Bhandari, Sharma & Aggarwal, Shigley) will cover centrifugal tension as it's a fundamental concept in belt drive design. Bhandari often provides detailed mathematical derivations.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Belt Material Selection:** Consider strength, flexibility, friction, and environmental resistance. Leather, rubber, and synthetics are common.
*   **Slip:** Reduces speed ratio and efficiency. Caused by elasticity and insufficient friction.
*   **Creep:** Gradual elongation due to sustained tension. Leads to slackening and reduced tension.
*   **Centrifugal Tension ($T_c = mv^2$):** Develops due to rotation, adds to belt tension. Significant at high speeds.
*   **Maximum Tension:** The belt must withstand $T_1 + T_c$.
*   **Power Transmission:** Limited by belt strength, speed, friction, and arc of contact. Centrifugal tension reduces the power capacity at higher speeds.
*   **Design Process:** Involves selecting a suitable belt material, calculating required tensions based on power and speed, accounting for slip and creep, and ensuring that the maximum belt tension does not exceed the belt's material strength, including the effect of centrifugal tension.

### Practice Questions/Exercises:

**Question 1:**
A flat belt drive is used to transmit 10 kW power at a belt speed of 25 m/s. The belt has a mass of 0.8 kg/m. If the angle of contact is 180 degrees (π radians) and the coefficient of friction is 0.35, what is the maximum tension that the belt can withstand if the centrifugal tension is 400 N?

**Solution 1:**
*   Given: Power ($P$) = 10 kW = 10,000 W, Belt speed ($v$) = 25 m/s, Mass per unit length ($m$) = 0.8 kg/m, Centrifugal Tension ($T_c$) = 400 N, Angle of contact ($\theta$) = 180° = π radians, Coefficient of friction ($\mu$) = 0.35.
*   Calculate Centrifugal Tension:
    $T_c = m v^2 = 0.8 \times (25)^2 = 0.8 \times 625 = 500$ N.
    *(Correction: The problem stated $T_c = 400$ N. Let's assume the problem meant *required* $T_c$ or there was a typo. If we calculate it, it's 500N. Let's re-read. "if the centrifugal tension is 400 N" suggests this is a given parameter. So, we use $T_c = 400$ N as given).*

*   Effective Tension ($T_1 - T_2$):
    $P = (T_1 - T_2) v$
    $10000 = (T_1 - T_2) \times 25$
    $T_1 - T_2 = \frac{10000}{25} = 400$ N.

*   Velocity ratio from belt friction:
    $\frac{T_1}{T_2} = e^{\mu \theta}$
    $\frac{T_1}{T_2} = e^{0.35 \times \pi} = e^{1.0995} \approx 3.003$

*   Solve for $T_1$ and $T_2$:
    $T_1 = 400 + T_2$
    $400 + T_2 = 3.003 T_2$
    $400 = 2.003 T_2$
    $T_2 = \frac{400}{2.003} \approx 199.7$ N.
    $T_1 = 400 + 199.7 = 599.7$ N.

*   Calculate maximum tension (including centrifugal tension):
    Maximum Tension = $T_1 + T_c$
    Maximum Tension = $599.7$ N + $400$ N = $999.7$ N.

    *Note: If we used the calculated $T_c = 500$ N:*
    *Maximum Tension = $599.7$ N + $500$ N = $1099.7$ N.*
    Since the question specifies $T_c=400$N, we use that.

*   **Answer:** The maximum tension the belt can withstand (under these operating conditions) is approximately **999.7 N**. This value would be compared against the belt's material strength to ensure safety.

**Question 2:**
A flat belt drive has a driving pulley of 300 mm diameter running at 600 rpm and a driven pulley of 600 mm diameter. If the driven pulley runs at 295 rpm, calculate the slip in the belt drive.

**Solution 2:**
*   Given: $D_1 = 300$ mm, $N_1 = 600$ rpm, $D_2 = 600$ mm, $N_2 = 295$ rpm.
*   Ideal speed of the driven pulley ($N_{2,ideal}$):
    $N_{2,ideal} = N_1 \times \frac{D_1}{D_2} = 600 \times \frac{300}{600} = 600 \times 0.5 = 300$ rpm.
*   Actual speed of the driven pulley ($N_{2,actual}$) = 295 rpm.
*   Slip (in rpm) = $N_{2,ideal} - N_{2,actual} = 300 - 295 = 5$ rpm.
*   Slip percentage:
    Slip % = $\frac{N_{2,ideal} - N_{2,actual}}{N_{2,ideal}} \times 100$
    Slip % = $\frac{5}{300} \times 100 = \frac{500}{300} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67\%$

*   **Answer:** The slip in the belt drive is approximately **1.67%**.

**Question 3:**
A belt running at 30 m/s has a mass of 0.7 kg per meter length. Calculate the centrifugal tension in the belt. What will be the effect on the power transmission capacity if the belt speed is doubled?

**Solution 3:**
*   Given: Belt speed ($v$) = 30 m/s, Mass per unit length ($m$) = 0.7 kg/m.
*   Calculate Centrifugal Tension ($T_c$):
    $T_c = m v^2 = 0.7 \times (30)^2 = 0.7 \times 900 = 630$ N.
*   **Answer Part 1:** The centrifugal tension is **630 N**.

*   Effect on power transmission if speed is doubled:
    Let the new speed be $v' = 2v = 2 \times 30 = 60$ m/s.
    New Centrifugal Tension ($T_c'$) = $m (v')^2 = 0.7 \times (60)^2 = 0.7 \times 3600 = 2520$ N.
    The centrifugal tension becomes $2520 / 630 = 4$ times its original value.

    Recall the power transmission equation: $P \le (T_{max} - T_c) \left(1 - \frac{1}{e^{\mu \theta}}\right) v$.
    For a constant $T_{max}$, $\mu$, and $\theta$, the power transmission capacity is proportional to $(T_{max} - T_c) v$.
    Let $C = \left(1 - \frac{1}{e^{\mu \theta}}\right) v$. The power capacity is approximately $P \propto (T_{max} - T_c) v$.

    At $v = 30$ m/s, $T_c = 630$ N. Power capacity $\propto (T_{max} - 630) \times 30$.
    At $v = 60$ m/s, $T_c = 2520$ N. Power capacity $\propto (T_{max} - 2520) \times 60$.

    Since $T_{max}$ is a constant (material property), the term $(T_{max} - T_c)$ decreases significantly when speed increases. Doubling the speed quadruples the centrifugal tension. This drastically reduces the allowable tension available for power transmission ($T_1 - T_2$). The term $(T_{max} - T_c)$ decreases. Furthermore, the term $v$ is multiplied by this reduced tension difference.

    The overall effect is that doubling the belt speed will **significantly decrease** the power transmission capacity of the belt drive, even though the velocity term itself has doubled, because the reduction in the allowable tension difference is much more pronounced. The power capacity might even become zero if $T_c$ approaches $T_{max}$.

*   **Answer Part 2:** Doubling the belt speed will **drastically reduce** the power transmission capacity due to the quadrupling of centrifugal tension, which significantly diminishes the allowable tension difference available for transmitting power.
