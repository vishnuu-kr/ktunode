# THERMAL ENGINEERING: Module 3: Fundamentals of IC Engines - Air Standard Cycles

This module delves into the fundamental concepts of Internal Combustion (IC) engines, focusing on air-standard cycles as an idealization to understand their thermodynamic performance. We will explore the workings of reciprocating SI (Spark Ignition) and CI (Compression Ignition) engines, examining their ideal and actual cycles, and understanding the factors that cause deviations from the ideal.

## **Module 3: Fundamentals of IC Engines: Air Standard Cycles**

### **Topic: Reciprocating Type SI and CI Engines: Ideal and Actual Cycle for IC Engines – Deviation from Ideal Cycle and Associated Factors**

---

### **1. Introduction to IC Engines**

*   **Definition:** An Internal Combustion (IC) engine is a heat engine where the combustion of a fuel occurs with an oxidizer (usually air) in a combustion chamber that is an integral part of the working fluid's system. The expansion of the high-temperature and high-pressure gases produced by combustion applies direct force to some component of the engine, such as pistons or turbine blades, resulting in useful work.
*   **Reciprocating Engines:** These engines use pistons that move back and forth in cylinders to convert pressure into rotating motion. They are the most common type of IC engine.
*   **Types of Reciprocating IC Engines:**
    *   **Spark Ignition (SI) Engines:** Ignite the fuel-air mixture using a spark plug. Typically run on gasoline or petrol. (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 12)
    *   **Compression Ignition (CI) Engines:** Ignite the fuel due to high temperatures generated by compressing air. Typically run on diesel. (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 13)

---

### **2. Ideal Cycles: Air-Standard Cycles**

*   **Purpose:** To simplify the analysis of IC engine performance by making several assumptions. This allows for a theoretical maximum efficiency calculation, providing a benchmark for actual engine performance.
*   **Key Assumptions of Air-Standard Cycles:**
    1.  **Working Fluid:** The working fluid is assumed to be air, which behaves as an ideal gas throughout the cycle.
    2.  **Constant Specific Heats:** The specific heats of air ($c_p$ and $c_v$) are assumed to be constant at room temperature. (R.K Rajput, Chapter 10)
    3.  **Combustion Replaced by Heat Addition:** Combustion process is replaced by heat addition from an external source at constant volume or constant pressure.
    4.  **Exhaust Replaced by Heat Rejection:** Exhaust process is replaced by heat rejection to an external sink at constant volume or constant pressure.
    5.  **Isentropic Processes:** All processes involving compression and expansion are assumed to be isentropic (reversible adiabatic).
    6.  **No Heat Losses:** No heat transfer occurs between the working fluid and the surroundings during compression and expansion.
    7.  **No Friction:** No mechanical friction within the engine.

### **3. Ideal Cycles for SI Engines: The Otto Cycle**

*   **Description:** The Otto cycle is the theoretical cycle for SI engines. It consists of four strokes, but the thermodynamic cycle is represented by four idealized processes. (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 12; R.K Rajput, Chapter 10)
*   **Processes of the Ideal Otto Cycle:**
    1.  **1-2: Isentropic Compression:** Air is compressed adiabatically from state 1 to state 2.
    2.  **2-3: Constant Volume Heat Addition:** Heat is added at constant volume, simulating ignition and combustion.
    3.  **3-4: Isentropic Expansion:** The hot gases expand adiabatically, performing work.
    4.  **4-1: Constant Volume Heat Rejection:** Heat is rejected at constant volume, simulating the exhaust process.

*   **P-V Diagram:** (Sketch a P-V diagram showing the four processes)
*   **T-s Diagram:** (Sketch a T-s diagram showing the four processes)
*   **Efficiency of the Ideal Otto Cycle:**
    *   Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{Otto}$) = $1 - \frac{T_1}{T_2} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_v^{(\gamma-1)}}$
    *   Where:
        *   $T_1$: Initial temperature
        *   $T_2$: Temperature after compression
        *   $r_v$: Compression ratio ($V_1/V_2$)
        *   $\gamma$: Adiabatic index ($c_p/c_v$)
*   **Key Concept:** The efficiency of the Otto cycle depends only on the compression ratio ($r_v$) and the specific heat ratio ($\gamma$). Higher compression ratios lead to higher efficiencies. (V Ganesan, Chapter 5)

### **4. Ideal Cycles for CI Engines: The Diesel Cycle**

*   **Description:** The Diesel cycle is the theoretical cycle for CI engines. It is similar to the Otto cycle, but heat addition occurs at constant pressure. (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 13; R.K Rajput, Chapter 10)
*   **Processes of the Ideal Diesel Cycle:**
    1.  **1-2: Isentropic Compression:** Air is compressed adiabatically.
    2.  **2-3: Constant Pressure Heat Addition:** Heat is added at constant pressure, representing fuel injection and initial combustion.
    3.  **3-4: Isentropic Expansion:** The hot gases expand adiabatically.
    4.  **4-1: Constant Volume Heat Rejection:** Heat is rejected at constant volume, representing exhaust.

*   **P-V Diagram:** (Sketch a P-V diagram showing the four processes, with a portion of heat addition at constant pressure)
*   **T-s Diagram:** (Sketch a T-s diagram showing the four processes)
*   **Efficiency of the Ideal Diesel Cycle:**
    *   Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{Diesel}$) = $1 - \frac{1}{r_v^{(\gamma-1)}} \left[ \frac{r_c^\gamma - 1}{\gamma(r_c - 1)} \right]$
    *   Where:
        *   $r_v$: Compression ratio ($V_1/V_2$)
        *   $r_c$: Cut-off ratio ($V_3/V_2$)
        *   $\gamma$: Adiabatic index
*   **Key Concept:** The efficiency of the Diesel cycle depends on the compression ratio ($r_v$), the cut-off ratio ($r_c$), and the specific heat ratio ($\gamma$). For the same compression ratio, the Diesel cycle is less efficient than the Otto cycle because of the constant pressure heat addition which is less efficient than constant volume heat addition in terms of work output per unit heat input. (V Ganesan, Chapter 5)

### **5. Dual Cycle (Semi-Diesel Cycle)**

*   **Description:** This cycle combines features of both the Otto and Diesel cycles. Heat addition occurs partially at constant volume and partially at constant pressure. It is a more realistic representation of CI engines. (R.K Rajput, Chapter 10)
*   **Processes of the Ideal Dual Cycle:**
    1.  **1-2: Isentropic Compression.**
    2.  **2-3: Constant Volume Heat Addition.**
    3.  **3-4: Constant Pressure Heat Addition.**
    4.  **4-5: Isentropic Expansion.**

### **6. Actual Cycle of IC Engines vs. Ideal Cycle**

*   **Deviation from Ideal Cycles:** The actual performance of IC engines deviates significantly from the ideal air-standard cycles due to various irreversibilities and other factors. (J.B. Heywood, Chapter 3; V Sajith and Shijo Thomas, Chapter 4)
*   **Factors Causing Deviations:**

    *   **Variable Specific Heats:** The specific heats of air increase with temperature, unlike the assumption of constant specific heats in ideal cycles. This leads to lower actual work output and efficiency. (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 12/13)
    *   **Heat Losses to Surroundings:** In an actual engine, there are heat losses through the cylinder walls, cylinder head, and piston to the cooling medium and the atmosphere. This reduces the work output. (R.K Rajput, Chapter 10)
    *   **Incomplete Combustion:** Not all fuel burns completely during the combustion process, leading to unburnt fuel in the exhaust, which reduces the heat released and thus the work output. (V Sajith and Shijo Thomas, Chapter 4)
    *   **Non-Isentropic Processes:**
        *   **Friction:** Mechanical friction between moving parts (piston rings, bearings) consumes some of the work produced.
        *   **Fluid Friction:** Friction within the fluid itself during flow causes pressure drops, especially during intake and exhaust strokes.
        *   **Heat Transfer during Compression and Expansion:** Compression and expansion strokes are not perfectly adiabatic; there is some heat transfer to and from the cylinder walls, making them deviate from isentropic.
    *   **Time Delays in Combustion:**
        *   **Ignition Delay (SI Engines):** The time lag between spark ignition and the start of actual combustion.
        *   **Ignition Delay (CI Engines):** The time lag between the start of fuel injection and the start of combustion. This delay is a significant factor in CI engines.
    *   **Valve Timing and Overlap:** The actual opening and closing of intake and exhaust valves are not instantaneous and can overlap, affecting the charge of fresh air and the expulsion of exhaust gases. (V Ganesan, Chapter 6)
    *   **Throttling Losses (SI Engines):** In SI engines, the throttle valve controls the amount of air-fuel mixture entering the cylinder. Throttling causes a pressure drop, leading to volumetric efficiency losses. (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 12)
    *   **Formation of By-products:** Formation of harmful by-products and pollutants reduces the overall efficiency and can affect the combustion process. (V Sajith and Shijo Thomas, Chapter 4)
    *   **Blow-by:** Leakage of gases past the piston rings into the crankcase.

*   **Fuel-Air Cycles:** A more realistic analysis than air-standard cycles is the **fuel-air cycle**, which considers the chemical reactions of combustion and the variable properties of the products of combustion. (J.B. Heywood, Chapter 3)

### **7. Performance Parameters and their Evaluation**

*   **Thermal Efficiency:** The ratio of net work output to the heat input.
    *   **Indicated Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{ith}$):** Ratio of indicated work (work done on the piston) to heat input.
    *   **Brake Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{bth}$):** Ratio of brake work (work delivered at the crankshaft) to heat input. $\eta_{bth} = \eta_{mech} \times \eta_{ith}$, where $\eta_{mech}$ is mechanical efficiency. (CO3)
*   **Volumetric Efficiency ($\eta_{vol}$):** The ratio of the actual volume of air drawn into the cylinder during the intake stroke to the swept volume. Crucial for SI engines. (CO3)
*   **Specific Fuel Consumption (SFC):** The mass of fuel consumed per unit of brake power output per unit time. Lower SFC indicates better fuel economy. (CO3)
*   **Heat Balance Sheet:** Accounts for all the energy inputs and outputs from the engine, including work output, heat rejected to cooling water, heat rejected in exhaust gases, and heat lost due to radiation and convection. (CO3)

### **9. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Air-standard cycles** are **idealizations** for analyzing IC engine thermodynamics.
*   The **Otto cycle** is for **SI engines** (constant volume heat addition).
*   The **Diesel cycle** is for **CI engines** (constant pressure heat addition).
*   The **Dual cycle** is a more realistic model for CI engines.
*   **Efficiency increases with compression ratio** in both Otto and Diesel cycles.
*   For the same compression ratio, the **Otto cycle is more efficient** than the Diesel cycle.
*   **Actual cycles deviate from ideal cycles** due to variable specific heats, heat losses, incomplete combustion, friction, and fluid effects.
*   **Fuel-air cycles** offer a more accurate analysis than air-standard cycles.

### **10. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** What are the main assumptions made in an air-standard cycle?
**Answer:** The main assumptions are: working fluid is air behaving as an ideal gas, constant specific heats, combustion replaced by heat addition, exhaust replaced by heat rejection, isentropic compression/expansion, no heat losses, and no friction.

**Question 2:** How does the thermal efficiency of the ideal Otto cycle vary with the compression ratio?
**Answer:** The thermal efficiency of the ideal Otto cycle increases with the compression ratio. The formula $\eta_{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_v^{(\gamma-1)}}$ clearly shows this inverse relationship.

**Question 3:** State two major reasons why the actual thermal efficiency of an SI engine is lower than that of its ideal Otto cycle.
**Answer:**
1.  **Heat Losses:** Heat is lost to the cylinder walls and cooling system during the cycle.
2.  **Incomplete Combustion:** Not all the fuel burns completely.
    (Other valid answers include variable specific heats, friction, throttling losses).

**Question 4:** For a given compression ratio and heat input, which cycle, Otto or Diesel, produces more work output? Explain briefly.
**Answer:** For a given compression ratio and heat input, the **Otto cycle** produces more work output. This is because heat addition at constant volume (Otto) is thermodynamically more efficient in converting heat to work than heat addition at constant pressure (Diesel), as it results in a higher peak temperature and pressure for the same heat input.

**Question 5:** Calculate the ideal efficiency of an Otto cycle with a compression ratio of 8 and $\gamma = 1.4$.
**Answer:**
Given: $r_v = 8$, $\gamma = 1.4$
$\eta_{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_v^{(\gamma-1)}} = 1 - \frac{1}{8^{(1.4-1)}} = 1 - \frac{1}{8^{0.4}}$
$8^{0.4} \approx 2.297$
$\eta_{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{2.297} \approx 1 - 0.435 = 0.565$
So, the ideal efficiency is approximately **56.5%**.
