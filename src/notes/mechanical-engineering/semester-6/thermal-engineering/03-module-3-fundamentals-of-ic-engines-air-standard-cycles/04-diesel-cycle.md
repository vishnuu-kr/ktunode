# Thermal Engineering: Module 3 - Fundamentals of IC Engines: Air Standard Cycles
## Topic: Diesel Cycle

---

### 1. Introduction and Relevance

The Diesel cycle is a fundamental air-standard cycle that represents the thermodynamic process occurring in a **compression-ignition (CI) engine**, commonly known as a **diesel engine**. Unlike the Otto cycle (used in spark-ignition engines), the diesel engine ignites the fuel through the heat generated by compressing air to a high temperature. This topic is crucial for understanding the operation and performance of a significant class of internal combustion engines.

**Relevance to Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO1 (K4):** Understanding the Diesel cycle involves applying basic thermodynamic principles (heat transfer, work, energy conservation) to analyze the cycle's processes and calculate its performance parameters.
*   **CO3 (K3):** This topic directly addresses the identification and evaluation of performance parameters of IC engines, particularly diesel engines.

---

### 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

*   **Air Standard Cycle:** A theoretical thermodynamic cycle used to analyze the performance of internal combustion engines. It makes several simplifying assumptions:
    *   The working fluid is air, treated as an ideal gas.
    *   All heat transfers occur at constant volume or constant pressure.
    *   All processes are reversible.
    *   There is no friction.
    *   Specific heats of air are constant, or they vary with temperature. (In basic analysis, they are often assumed constant).
*   **Diesel Engine:** An internal combustion engine that uses compression ignition to ignite fuel. High compression ratios lead to high air temperatures, which then ignite the injected fuel.
*   **Compression Ratio ($r_c$):** The ratio of the volume of the cylinder at the bottom dead center (BDC) to the volume at the top dead center (TDC).
    $r_c = \frac{V_1}{V_2}$
    where $V_1$ is the volume at BDC and $V_2$ is the volume at TDC.
*   **Cut-off Ratio ($r_o$):** The ratio of the volume at the end of heat addition (constant pressure) to the volume at the beginning of heat addition (constant volume).
    $r_o = \frac{V_3}{V_2}$
*   **Compression Stroke:** The stroke where the air is compressed.
*   **Heat Addition:** In the ideal diesel cycle, this occurs at constant pressure.
*   **Expansion (Power) Stroke:** The stroke where the hot gases expand, doing work. This is a polytropic process.
*   **Heat Rejection:** In the ideal diesel cycle, this occurs at constant volume.

---

### 3. The Ideal Diesel Cycle: Processes

The ideal diesel cycle consists of four reversible processes:

*   **Process 1-2: Isentropic Compression**
    *   Air is compressed reversibly and adiabatically (isentropically) from state 1 to state 2.
    *   **Description:** Piston moves from BDC to TDC. Volume decreases, pressure and temperature increase.
    *   **Thermodynamic Relation:** $PV^{\gamma} = \text{constant}$ or $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{constant}$
    *   **Work Done (on gas):** $W_{1-2} = \frac{P_1V_1 - P_2V_2}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{mR(T_2 - T_1)}{\gamma - 1}$
    *   **Heat Transfer:** $Q_{1-2} = 0$ (adiabatic)

*   **Process 2-3: Isobaric Heat Addition**
    *   Heat is added to the air at constant pressure from state 2 to state 3.
    *   **Description:** Fuel is injected and burns at constant pressure. Piston starts moving from TDC. Volume and temperature increase.
    *   **Thermodynamic Relation:** $P = \text{constant}$
    *   **Heat Added ($Q_{in}$):** $Q_{in} = m C_p (T_3 - T_2)$
    *   **Work Done (by gas):** $W_{2-3} = P_2(V_3 - V_2) = mR(T_3 - T_2)$

*   **Process 3-4: Isentropic Expansion**
    *   The hot gases expand reversibly and adiabatically (isentropically) from state 3 to state 4.
    *   **Description:** Piston moves from near TDC (after heat addition) to BDC. Volume increases, pressure and temperature decrease.
    *   **Thermodynamic Relation:** $PV^{\gamma} = \text{constant}$ or $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{constant}$
    *   **Work Done (by gas):** $W_{3-4} = \frac{P_3V_3 - P_4V_4}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{mR(T_3 - T_4)}{\gamma - 1}$
    *   **Heat Transfer:** $Q_{3-4} = 0$ (adiabatic)

*   **Process 4-1: Isochoric Heat Rejection**
    *   Heat is rejected from the air at constant volume from state 4 to state 1.
    *   **Description:** Piston is at BDC. Cylinder volume is cleared of exhaust gases. Volume and temperature decrease.
    *   **Thermodynamic Relation:** $V = \text{constant}$
    *   **Heat Rejected ($Q_{out}$):** $Q_{out} = m C_v (T_4 - T_1)$
    *   **Work Done:** $W_{4-1} = 0$ (constant volume)

### 5. Derivation of Thermal Efficiency ($\eta_{th}$)

The thermal efficiency of any cycle is defined as:
$\eta_{th} = \frac{\text{Net Work Done}}{\text{Heat Added}} = \frac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}}$

For the diesel cycle:
$W_{net} = W_{2-3} + W_{3-4} - W_{1-2} \quad (\text{Note: } W_{4-1}=0)$
$Q_{in} = m C_p (T_3 - T_2)$
$Q_{out} = m C_v (T_4 - T_1)$

From the First Law of Thermodynamics for a cycle: $W_{net} = Q_{in} - Q_{out}$

Therefore, $\eta_{th} = \frac{Q_{in} - Q_{out}}{Q_{in}} = 1 - \frac{Q_{out}}{Q_{in}}$

Substituting the expressions for $Q_{in}$ and $Q_{out}$:
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{m C_v (T_4 - T_1)}{m C_p (T_3 - T_2)} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2}$

Now, we need to express $T_4$ in terms of other temperatures using the isentropic relations:

*   **From process 1-2 (Isentropic Compression):**
    $\frac{T_2}{T_1} = (\frac{V_1}{V_2})^{\gamma-1} = r_c^{\gamma-1}$
    $T_2 = T_1 r_c^{\gamma-1}$

*   **From process 2-3 (Isobaric Expansion):**
    $\frac{V_3}{V_2} = \frac{T_3}{T_2} = r_o$
    $T_3 = T_2 r_o$
    Substituting $T_2$: $T_3 = T_1 r_c^{\gamma-1} r_o$

*   **From process 3-4 (Isentropic Expansion):**
    $\frac{T_3}{T_4} = (\frac{V_4}{V_3})^{\gamma-1}$
    We know that $V_4 = V_1$ and $V_3$ is the volume at the end of heat addition.
    Also, $V_2$ is the volume at the end of compression (TDC).
    $V_1 = V_2 \cdot r_c$
    $V_3 = V_2 \cdot r_o$
    So, $\frac{V_4}{V_3} = \frac{V_1}{V_3} = \frac{V_2 \cdot r_c}{V_2 \cdot r_o} = \frac{r_c}{r_o}$
    Therefore, $\frac{T_3}{T_4} = (\frac{r_c}{r_o})^{\gamma-1}$
    $T_4 = T_3 \left(\frac{r_o}{r_c}\right)^{\gamma-1}$
    Substituting $T_3$: $T_4 = T_1 r_c^{\gamma-1} r_o \left(\frac{r_o}{r_c}\right)^{\gamma-1}$
    $T_4 = T_1 r_c^{\gamma-1} r_o \frac{r_o^{\gamma-1}}{r_c^{\gamma-1}} = T_1 r_o^\gamma$

Now substitute $T_2$ and $T_4$ into the efficiency equation:
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{T_1 r_o^\gamma - T_1}{T_1 r_c^{\gamma-1} r_o - T_1 r_c^{\gamma-1}}$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{T_1 (r_o^\gamma - 1)}{T_1 r_c^{\gamma-1} (r_o - 1)}$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{r_o^\gamma - 1}{r_c^{\gamma-1} (r_o - 1)}$

This is the thermal efficiency of the ideal diesel cycle.

**Key Observation:** The efficiency depends on the compression ratio ($r_c$) and the cut-off ratio ($r_o$).

**Effect of $r_c$ and $r_o$ on efficiency:**
*   **Increasing $r_c$:** Increases efficiency (similar to Otto cycle).
*   **Increasing $r_o$:** Decreases efficiency. This is because a higher $r_o$ means more heat is added at a higher volume, leading to higher temperatures and more work extracted during expansion, but also a higher initial temperature for heat rejection which reduces the net gain from heat addition relative to heat rejection.

### 6. Comparison with Otto Cycle

| Feature               | Otto Cycle                               | Diesel Cycle                               |
| :-------------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| **Ignition**          | Spark Ignition                           | Compression Ignition                       |
| **Fuel**              | Gasoline                                 | Diesel                                     |
| **Heat Addition**     | Constant Volume                          | Constant Pressure                          |
| **Compression Ratio** | Lower (typically 6-12)                   | Higher (typically 14-25)                   |
| **Cut-off ratio ($r_o$)** | $r_o = 1$ (no constant pressure process) | $r_o > 1$                                  |
| **Efficiency**        | $\eta_{th, Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_c^{\gamma-1}}$ | $\eta_{th, Diesel} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{r_o^\gamma - 1}{r_c^{\gamma-1} (r_o - 1)}$ |
| **Comparison**        | For same $r_c$, Diesel is less efficient than Otto if $r_o$ were 1. | For same $r_c$, Diesel efficiency is generally higher than Otto due to higher $r_c$. |
| **Specific Heat**     | Specific heats assumed constant          | Specific heats often assumed variable for better accuracy, especially for higher temperature ranges. |
| **Fuel Knock**        | More prone to knock if compression ratio is too high. | Less prone to knock due to lower pre-ignition temperatures. |
| **Power Output**      | Higher power output for same displacement. | Lower power output for same displacement, but higher torque. |

**Textbook Reference:** Rudra Moorthy (Chapter 15: Air Standard Cycles) and R.K. Rajput (Chapter 16: Air Standard Cycles) provide detailed comparisons and derivations. V. Ganesan and H.N. Gupta also cover these aspects extensively.

### 7. Mean Effective Pressure (MEP)

*   **Definition:** The hypothetical constant pressure that, if acting on the piston during the entire power stroke, would produce the same net work as the cycle.
*   **Formula:** $P_m = \frac{W_{net}}{V_d}$
    where $V_d$ is the swept volume ($V_1 - V_2$).

$W_{net} = Q_{in} - Q_{out}$
$W_{net} = m C_p (T_3 - T_2) - m C_v (T_4 - T_1)$

$P_m = \frac{m C_v (T_1 - T_4) + m C_p (T_3 - T_2) - m C_v (T_4 - T_1)}{V_1 - V_2}$
This can be further simplified by substituting derived temperature relations and volume ratios. A common form involves the initial pressure and temperatures:
$P_m = \frac{P_1}{\gamma-1} [r_c^{\gamma-1}(r_o + (1-r_o)\frac{T_4}{T_3}) - 1] - \frac{P_1}{\gamma-1} [r_c - 1]$ (This is a more complex derivation and might not be expected for basic understanding).

A simpler approach derived from work per cycle:
$W_{net} = (P_3V_3 - P_4V_4)/(\gamma-1) + P_2(V_3-V_2) - (P_1V_1 - P_2V_2)/(\gamma-1)$
$W_{net} = m(C_p(T_3-T_2) - C_v(T_4-T_1))$
$V_d = V_1 - V_2 = V_2(r_c - 1)$

$P_m = \frac{mC_v(T_1-T_4) + mC_p(T_3-T_2)}{V_2(r_c-1)}$

*   **Effect of parameters on MEP:**
    *   **Higher $r_c$:** Increases MEP.
    *   **Higher $r_o$:** Increases MEP (as it means more heat is added, leading to higher peak pressures and more work).
    *   **Higher initial pressure ($P_1$) and temperature ($T_1$):** Increases MEP.

**Textbook Reference:** Textbooks like V. Sajith and Shijo Thomas (Chapter 5: Air Standard Cycles) provide detailed derivations of MEP.

### 8. Volumetric Efficiency

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the mass of air actually drawn into the cylinder during the intake stroke to the mass of air that would occupy the cylinder volume at the intake conditions (pressure and temperature).
*   **Formula:** $\eta_{vol} = \frac{m_{actual}}{m_{theoretical}} = \frac{\rho_{actual}}{\rho_{theoretical}}$
    where $\rho$ is the density of air.
    $\eta_{vol} = \frac{P_{in}T_{std}}{P_{std}T_{in}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma \frac{R}{287} T_{in}}} \cdot \frac{1}{V_d} \cdot \frac{C_d A_v}{\sqrt{2 g h_{eff}}}$ (This is a more detailed formula involving valve coefficients).

A simplified conceptual understanding is that as compression ratio increases, the clearance volume increases proportionally, which leads to a higher residual gas fraction in the cylinder. This residual gas heats up the incoming fresh charge, reducing its density and thus decreasing volumetric efficiency. However, the overall performance benefit of higher compression ratios in diesel engines usually outweighs this effect.

### 9. Limitations of the Ideal Diesel Cycle (Real Cycle Considerations)

The ideal diesel cycle is a simplification. Real diesel engines deviate due to:

*   **Heat Loss:** Heat transfer to cylinder walls, piston, and cylinder head.
*   **Friction:** Mechanical friction between moving parts.
*   **Incomplete Combustion:** Fuel may not burn completely.
*   **Variable Specific Heats:** Specific heats of air and combustion products change with temperature.
*   **Finite Rate of Combustion:** Fuel injection and combustion take a finite time, not instantaneous.
*   **Valve Overlap:** Periods where both intake and exhaust valves are open.
*   **Specific Heat Variation:** The ratio $\gamma$ is not constant throughout the cycle.
*   **Fuel Properties:** Diesel fuel is not an ideal gas.

**Correction for Variable Specific Heats:**
When specific heats are considered variable (which is more realistic for high temperatures), the efficiency formula changes. For example, the ratio of specific heats $\gamma$ might be taken as an average value. Textbooks often present analyses with variable specific heats, showing a slight reduction in efficiency compared to the constant specific heat assumption.

**Textbook Reference:** Heywood's "Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals" provides extensive details on real cycle analysis and factors affecting performance.

### 10. Practice Questions and Answers

**Q1. Define the cut-off ratio for a diesel cycle and state its significance.**

**Answer:** The cut-off ratio ($r_o$) is the ratio of the volume at the end of heat addition (constant pressure process) to the volume at the beginning of heat addition (constant volume process). It signifies how much the volume expands during the constant pressure combustion phase. A higher cut-off ratio implies more fuel burned at constant pressure, leading to higher peak temperatures and pressures, and generally higher thermal efficiency (up to a point).

**Q2. For an ideal diesel cycle, what happens to the thermal efficiency if the compression ratio ($r_c$) increases?**

**Answer:** The thermal efficiency of an ideal diesel cycle increases with an increase in the compression ratio ($r_c$). This is evident from the efficiency formula:
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{r_o^\gamma - 1}{r_c^{\gamma-1} (r_o - 1)}$
As $r_c$ increases, the term $r_c^{\gamma-1}$ in the denominator increases, making the fraction smaller and thus increasing the overall efficiency.

**Q3. An ideal diesel cycle has a compression ratio of 16 and a cut-off ratio of 2. If the specific heat ratio $\gamma = 1.4$, calculate the thermal efficiency.**

**Solution:**
Given:
$r_c = 16$
$r_o = 2$
$\gamma = 1.4$

Thermal efficiency formula for ideal diesel cycle:
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \frac{r_o^\gamma - 1}{r_c^{\gamma-1} (r_o - 1)}$

$\gamma - 1 = 1.4 - 1 = 0.4$

$r_c^{\gamma-1} = 16^{0.4} \approx 3.0314$

$r_o^\gamma = 2^{1.4} \approx 2.6390$

$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{1.4} \frac{2.6390 - 1}{3.0314 (2 - 1)}$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1}{1.4} \frac{1.6390}{3.0314 \times 1}$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1.6390}{1.4 \times 3.0314}$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - \frac{1.6390}{4.24396}$
$\eta_{th} = 1 - 0.38616$
$\eta_{th} \approx 0.6138$ or $61.38\%$

**Q4. How does a diesel engine differ from a petrol engine (Otto cycle) in terms of fuel injection and ignition process?**

**Answer:**
*   **Petrol Engine (Otto Cycle):** Fuel and air are mixed *before* entering the cylinder (in a carburetor or port injection) or injected into the cylinder during the intake stroke. The mixture is then compressed, and ignition is initiated by an electric spark from a spark plug at a precise moment.
*   **Diesel Engine:** Only air is drawn into the cylinder and compressed to a very high temperature. Diesel fuel is then injected directly into this hot, compressed air at or near the end of the compression stroke. The high temperature of the air causes the fuel to auto-ignite. There is no spark plug.

### 11. Important Points to Remember

*   **Diesel Cycle is for CI Engines:** Always associate it with diesel engines and compression ignition.
*   **Key Processes:** Isentropic compression, isobaric heat addition, isentropic expansion, isochoric heat rejection.
*   **Efficiency Formula:** Remember the formula involving $r_c$, $r_o$, and $\gamma$.
*   **Higher $r_c$ = Higher Efficiency:** A major advantage of diesel engines.
*   **$r_o$ affects Efficiency:** For a given $r_c$, increasing $r_o$ generally decreases efficiency.
*   **Comparison with Otto:** Understand the fundamental differences in fuel injection, ignition, compression ratios, and resulting efficiencies.
*   **Real World Deviations:** Be aware of the limitations of the ideal cycle and the factors affecting actual engine performance.
