# Thermal Engineering: Module 3 - Fundamentals of IC Engines: Air Standard Cycles

## Topic: Engine Testing and Performance of SI and CI Engines: Torque

This module focuses on understanding the performance of Internal Combustion (IC) engines, specifically the concept of torque. We will explore how torque is measured and what it signifies in the context of engine operation, aligning with Course Outcome CO3: "Identify the performance parameters of IC engines and evaluate their performance." (Knowledge Level: K3).

---

### 1. Introduction to Engine Performance Parameters

**1.1 What is Engine Performance?**

Engine performance refers to how well an engine fulfills its intended function. This includes:

*   **Power Output:** The rate at which the engine can do work.
*   **Efficiency:** The ratio of useful work output to the energy input.
*   **Fuel Consumption:** The amount of fuel required to produce a certain amount of work.
*   **Emissions:** The pollutants released into the atmosphere.
*   **Reliability and Durability:** The engine's ability to operate consistently over time without failure.
*   **Torque:** A rotational force produced by the engine.

**1.2 Importance of Torque in IC Engines**

Torque is a fundamental measure of an engine's rotational effort. It is directly related to the force produced by the expanding gases on the piston and its subsequent translation into rotational motion at the crankshaft. Understanding torque is crucial for:

*   **Vehicle Acceleration:** Higher torque generally means better acceleration.
*   **Load Carrying Capacity:** Engines with higher torque can handle heavier loads.
*   **Towing and Hauling:** Essential for vehicles designed for these purposes.
*   **Engine Design and Optimization:** Torque characteristics influence engine tuning and design choices.

---

### 2. Understanding Torque

**2.1 Definition of Torque**

Torque ($\tau$) is the rotational equivalent of linear force. It is defined as the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot point (axis of rotation) to the line of action of the force.

**Formula:**
$\tau = F \times r$

Where:
*   $\tau$ = Torque (typically measured in Newton-meters (Nm) or pound-feet (lb-ft))
*   $F$ = Force (typically measured in Newtons (N) or pounds (lb))
*   $r$ = Perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation to the point where the force is applied (lever arm, typically measured in meters (m) or feet (ft))

**2.1.1 Torque in an IC Engine**

In an IC engine, torque is generated by the force exerted by the expanding combustion gases on the piston. This force is transmitted through the connecting rod to the crankshaft, causing it to rotate.

**Key Components involved in Torque generation:**

*   **Piston:** Receives the force from combustion.
*   **Connecting Rod:** Transmits the force from the piston to the crankshaft.
*   **Crankshaft:** The rotating shaft that converts linear motion into rotational motion.
*   **Crank Radius (or Crank Throw):** The distance from the center of the crankshaft to the center of the crankpin. This acts as the lever arm.

**2.1.2 Relationship between Force, Torque, and Crankshaft Geometry**

The force acting on the piston is converted into torque at the crankshaft. The maximum torque is produced when the connecting rod is perpendicular to the crankshaft (i.e., when the crank angle is 90 degrees relative to the line of stroke).

**Formula for Torque in an IC Engine:**

$\tau = \frac{P_m \times V_d}{2\pi} \times K$

Where:
*   $\tau$ = Torque
*   $P_m$ = Mean Effective Pressure (MEP) - the average pressure acting on the piston during the power stroke.
*   $V_d$ = Displacement Volume of the engine (bore area $\times$ stroke).
*   $K$ = A constant factor depending on the number of cylinders and the crank mechanism. For a single-cylinder engine, the relationship is often derived as: $\tau = \frac{F_{net} \times L}{2}$ where $F_{net}$ is the net force on the piston and L is the stroke. A more practical approach relates torque to MEP and displacement.

A more commonly used and direct relationship derived from the definition of work done per revolution is:

$\tau = \frac{P_m \times V_d}{4\pi}$ (for a four-stroke engine per cylinder, considering torque as the average torque)

However, for practical engine testing, torque is directly measured.

### 3. Engine Testing and Measurement of Torque

**3.1 Purpose of Engine Testing**

Engine testing is crucial for:

*   **Performance Evaluation:** Assessing power, torque, and efficiency.
*   **Calibration:** Ensuring the engine operates as designed.
*   **Durability and Reliability:** Identifying potential failure points.
*   **Emission Control:** Measuring and analyzing exhaust gases.
*   **Research and Development:** Optimizing engine design and combustion strategies.

**3.2 Dynamometers: The Primary Tool for Torque Measurement**

A dynamometer (or "dyno") is a device used to measure the torque output of an engine. It essentially applies a controlled load to the engine's output shaft and measures the resulting torque.

**3.2.1 Types of Dynamometers**

Several types of dynamometers are used for engine testing, each with its own operating principle:

*   **Absorption Dynamometers:** These absorb the engine's output power and dissipate it as heat. They are commonly used for measuring torque.
    *   **Prony Brake Dynamometer:** An older, simpler type. It consists of a band or blocks clamped around a rotating drum attached to the engine shaft. The friction between the band and the drum creates a braking torque. A lever arm attached to the band measures the force required to hold it stationary, allowing torque calculation.
        *   **Principle:** Measures the braking force required to prevent the rotating drum from turning.
        *   **Torque Calculation:** $\tau = F \times L$ (where F is the force measured by a scale at the end of the lever arm, and L is the length of the lever arm).
        *   **Power Calculation:** $P = \tau \times \omega$ (where $\omega$ is the angular velocity in rad/s).

    *   **Hydraulic Dynamometer (Water Brake or Hydrokinetic Dynamometer):** This type uses the resistance of a fluid (water or oil) to absorb power. The engine drives a rotor within a casing filled with the fluid. The resistance to fluid flow creates a torque.
        *   **Principle:** Fluid friction between a rotor and stator.
        *   **Measurement:** The casing is mounted on trunnions and held stationary by a force transducer (load cell).
        *   **Torque Calculation:** $\tau = F \times L$ (where F is the force measured by the load cell, and L is the effective lever arm).
        *   **Advantages:** Can handle high power outputs, efficient heat dissipation.

    *   **Eddy-Current Dynamometer:** This type uses the principle of electromagnetic induction. The engine drives a rotor made of a conductive material (like steel or aluminum) within a magnetic field. The rotating rotor induces eddy currents within it, which oppose its motion, creating a braking torque. The strength of the magnetic field can be adjusted to control the load.
        *   **Principle:** Electromagnetic induction and eddy current braking.
        *   **Measurement:** Similar to hydraulic dynos, the casing is supported by a load cell.
        *   **Torque Calculation:** $\tau = F \times L$.
        *   **Advantages:** Good controllability, relatively compact.

*   **Transmission Dynamometers:** These are inserted in the transmission line and measure the torque being transmitted. They do not absorb power.

**3.2.2 Measurement of Rotational Speed (RPM)**

To determine power, the rotational speed of the engine crankshaft is also required. This is typically measured using:

*   **Tachometers:** Electronic devices that measure the rate of rotation.
*   **Speed Sensors:** Often magnetic or optical sensors that count pulses from a toothed wheel on the crankshaft.

**3.3 Torque Measurement Procedure**

A typical engine testing procedure using a dynamometer involves:

1.  **Mounting the Engine:** Securely attach the engine to the dynamometer.
2.  **Connecting Instrumentation:** Connect sensors for RPM, throttle position, engine temperature, fuel flow, etc.
3.  **Starting the Engine:** Run the engine at idle.
4.  **Applying Load:** Gradually increase the load applied by the dynamometer.
5.  **Recording Data:** At various engine speeds and loads, record:
    *   Torque output
    *   Engine speed (RPM)
    *   Fuel consumption
    *   Other relevant parameters (e.g., manifold pressure, exhaust gas temperature).
6.  **Varying Engine Parameters:** Repeat the process for different throttle openings, fuel-air ratios, and potentially ignition timing settings.

### 4. Performance Curves and Torque Analysis

**4.1 Torque Curve**

A torque curve is a graph that plots the engine's torque output against its rotational speed (RPM). This is a crucial indicator of an engine's performance characteristics.

**Key features of a typical SI/CI engine torque curve:**

*   **Peak Torque:** The maximum torque the engine can produce. This usually occurs at a relatively low to mid-range engine speed.
*   **Torque Band:** The range of RPM where the engine produces a significant portion of its peak torque. A wider torque band generally indicates more flexible engine performance.
*   **Torque Drop-off:** At very high RPMs, torque typically decreases due to factors like reduced volumetric efficiency, increased friction, and combustion limitations.

**Example of a Torque Curve (Conceptual):**

Imagine a graph with RPM on the x-axis and Torque (Nm) on the y-axis. The curve would typically:
*   Start low at low RPMs.
*   Rise steeply.
*   Reach a peak at a certain RPM (e.g., 2500-3500 RPM for many passenger car engines).
*   Gradually decrease as RPM increases further.

**4.2 Relationship between Torque, Power, and RPM**

Power ($P$) is the rate at which work is done. In rotational systems, power is the product of torque and angular velocity.

**Formula:**
$P = \tau \times \omega$

Where:
*   $P$ = Power (typically measured in Watts (W) or Kilowatts (kW))
*   $\tau$ = Torque (typically measured in Newton-meters (Nm))
*   $\omega$ = Angular velocity (radians per second (rad/s))

**Converting RPM to rad/s:**
$\omega = \frac{2\pi \times RPM}{60}$

**Therefore, in SI units (kW, Nm, RPM):**
$P_{kW} = \frac{\tau_{Nm} \times RPM}{9549}$

**4.3 Power Curve**

A power curve plots the engine's power output against its rotational speed. It is derived from the torque curve.

**Key features of a typical Power Curve:**

*   **Peak Power:** The maximum power the engine can produce, usually at a higher RPM than peak torque.
*   **Power Band:** The range of RPM where the engine delivers substantial power.

**Important Observation:**
Since $P = \tau \times \omega$, and $\omega$ increases linearly with RPM, while torque might not always increase proportionally, the power curve typically continues to rise even after the torque curve starts to fall, until the engine reaches its maximum governed speed.

**4.4 Specific Torque**

Specific torque is the torque produced per unit of engine displacement. It provides a measure of how effectively an engine's displacement is utilized to generate torque.

**Formula:**
Specific Torque = $\frac{\text{Torque}}{\text{Displacement Volume}}$

This is useful for comparing engines of different sizes.

### 5. Torque in SI vs. CI Engines

**5.1 Spark Ignition (SI) Engines (Gasoline Engines)**

*   **Torque Characteristics:** SI engines generally produce peak torque at lower to mid-range RPMs. They have a relatively good torque curve for everyday driving, offering flexibility.
*   **Factors Affecting Torque:**
    *   Throttle opening (air-fuel mixture intake)
    *   Fuel-air ratio
    *   Ignition timing
    *   Volumetric efficiency
    *   Compression ratio
*   **Example:** A naturally aspirated gasoline engine might peak around 3000-4000 RPM. Turbocharged and supercharged SI engines can achieve higher torque at lower RPMs due to forced induction.

**5.2 Compression Ignition (CI) Engines (Diesel Engines)**

*   **Torque Characteristics:** CI engines typically produce higher torque at lower RPMs compared to SI engines of similar displacement. They often have a flatter torque curve over a broader RPM range, making them suitable for heavy-duty applications.
*   **Factors Affecting Torque:**
    *   Fuel injection timing and quantity
    *   Turbocharger boost pressure (if present)
    *   Compression ratio
    *   Air-fuel ratio (often run lean)
*   **Example:** A diesel engine often produces its peak torque around 1500-2500 RPM and maintains a significant portion of this torque over a wider range, providing strong pulling power.

---

### 6. Practical Implications and Torque

*   **Drivability:** A wider torque band leads to better drivability, as the driver doesn't need to constantly shift gears to keep the engine in its optimal operating range.
*   **Towing and Hauling:** Engines with high low-end torque are essential for vehicles that need to tow trailers or carry heavy loads.
*   **Fuel Economy:** While peak power is important for high-speed performance, torque characteristics, especially at lower loads and RPMs, play a significant role in overall fuel efficiency.
*   **Engine Responsiveness:** A good torque response to throttle input makes the engine feel more lively and responsive.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
An engine produces a torque of 200 Nm at 3000 RPM. Calculate the power output in kW.

**Answer:**
Given:
Torque ($\tau$) = 200 Nm
Engine Speed (RPM) = 3000 RPM

Using the formula: $P_{kW} = \frac{\tau_{Nm} \times RPM}{9549}$
$P_{kW} = \frac{200 \times 3000}{9549}$
$P_{kW} = \frac{600000}{9549}$
$P_{kW} \approx 62.83$ kW

**Question 2:**
A single-cylinder four-stroke engine has a bore of 80 mm and a stroke of 90 mm. If the mean effective pressure (MEP) is 6 bar and the engine speed is 1500 RPM, estimate the torque produced by the engine. (Assume a conversion factor related to MEP and displacement for estimation).

*Hint: You can use the relationship where approximate torque is proportional to MEP and displacement.*
A commonly used approximate formula for torque (in Nm) is $\tau \approx \frac{MEP_{Pa} \times V_{d, m^3}}{4\pi}$.
1 bar = $10^5$ Pa
Vd = $\pi/4 \times D^2 \times L$

**Answer:**
Given:
Bore (D) = 80 mm = 0.08 m
Stroke (L) = 90 mm = 0.09 m
MEP = 6 bar = $6 \times 10^5$ Pa
Engine Speed = 1500 RPM

1.  **Calculate Displacement Volume ($V_d$):**
    $V_d = \frac{\pi}{4} \times D^2 \times L$
    $V_d = \frac{\pi}{4} \times (0.08)^2 \times 0.09$
    $V_d = \frac{\pi}{4} \times 0.0064 \times 0.09$
    $V_d \approx 0.004524$ m$^3$

2.  **Estimate Torque using the formula:**
    $\tau \approx \frac{MEP_{Pa} \times V_{d, m^3}}{4\pi}$
    $\tau \approx \frac{(6 \times 10^5) \times 0.004524}{4\pi}$
    $\tau \approx \frac{2714.4}{12.566}$
    $\tau \approx 216$ Nm

*(Note: This is an estimation, as the actual formula involves more detailed consideration of forces and geometry. The MEP itself is often derived from torque and displacement.)*

**Question 3:**
Which type of dynamometer uses fluid friction to absorb engine power?
(a) Prony Brake Dynamometer
(b) Eddy-Current Dynamometer
(c) Hydraulic Dynamometer
(d) Transmission Dynamometer

**Answer:**
(c) Hydraulic Dynamometer

**Question 4:**
Why is peak torque usually achieved at a lower RPM than peak power?

**Answer:**
Peak torque occurs when the engine's ability to generate a strong rotational force is maximized. This is often related to factors like the efficiency of combustion and the geometry of the crank mechanism. Peak power, on the other hand, is the product of torque and angular velocity ($P = \tau \times \omega$). Even if torque starts to decrease at higher RPMs, the increasing angular velocity can cause power to continue rising until very high speeds. Factors limiting high-RPM torque include reduced volumetric efficiency (less air enters the cylinder), increased friction losses, and limitations in the combustion process itself (e.g., flame propagation speed).

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Torque** is the rotational equivalent of force.
*   In an IC engine, torque is generated by the force on the piston transmitted to the crankshaft.
*   **Dynamometers** are used to measure engine torque by applying a controlled load.
*   The **Prony brake, hydraulic, and eddy-current dynamometers** are common types used for measuring torque.
*   **Torque curves** illustrate an engine's rotational effort across its speed range, with **peak torque** indicating the engine's maximum pulling power at a specific RPM.
*   **Power** is the product of torque and angular velocity ($P = \tau \times \omega$).
*   CI (Diesel) engines generally produce higher torque at lower RPMs compared to SI (Gasoline) engines.
*   A **wider torque band** improves drivability and engine flexibility.
