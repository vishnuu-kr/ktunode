# Thermal Engineering: Module 2 - Steam Turbines: Classification - Efficiency

This module focuses on the performance and efficiency of steam turbines, crucial components in thermal power plants. Understanding efficiency is key to analyzing and improving the overall performance of a steam power system, directly contributing to CO1 and CO2.

## 1. Introduction to Steam Turbine Efficiency

Steam turbines are thermodynamic machines that convert the thermal energy of steam into mechanical work. Their efficiency dictates how much of the available energy in the steam is effectively converted into useful output.

**Key Concept:** **Efficiency** in a steam turbine is a measure of how well it utilizes the energy supplied by the steam. A higher efficiency means more mechanical work is produced for the same amount of steam input.

## 2. Types of Efficiencies in Steam Turbines

There are several types of efficiencies considered when analyzing steam turbine performance. These help pinpoint where energy losses occur and guide improvement strategies.

### 2.1. Isentropic Efficiency (or Adiabatic Efficiency)

This is the most fundamental efficiency measure for a steam turbine. It compares the actual work output to the maximum possible work output under ideal (isentropic) expansion.

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the actual work done by the turbine to the work that would be done if the expansion of steam through the turbine were isentropic.

*   **Formula:**
    $$ \eta_{isentropic} = \frac{\text{Actual Work Output}}{\text{Isentropic Work Output}} = \frac{W_{actual}}{W_{isentropic}} $$

*   **Explanation:**
    *   **Actual Work Output ($W_{actual}$):** The work done by the steam as it expands through the turbine blades, accounting for all irreversible losses (friction, leakage, etc.).
    *   **Isentropic Work Output ($W_{isentropic}$):** The maximum possible work that can be extracted from the steam if it expands isentropically (reversibly and adiabatically) from the initial state to the final pressure. This represents the theoretical maximum work.

*   **Graphical Representation (T-s Diagram):**
    *   The initial state of steam is represented by point 1.
    *   The final pressure is represented by a horizontal line at $P_2$.
    *   Isentropic expansion is represented by a vertical line (constant entropy) from 1 to 2s.
    *   Actual expansion is represented by a curve from 1 to 2, which deviates from the vertical line due to irreversibilities.
    *   Isentropic work output is proportional to the area under the isentropic expansion line on a T-s diagram (or $h_1 - h_{2s}$).
    *   Actual work output is proportional to the area under the actual expansion curve (or $h_1 - h_2$).

*   **Relation to Enthalpy:**
    $$ \eta_{isentropic} = \frac{h_1 - h_2}{h_1 - h_{2s}} $$
    Where:
    *   $h_1$: Enthalpy of steam at the inlet.
    *   $h_2$: Enthalpy of steam at the actual outlet.
    *   $h_{2s}$: Enthalpy of steam at the outlet if expansion were isentropic.

*   **Example (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 10, Section 10.2):** If steam enters a turbine at 40 bar, 400°C and expands to 0.1 bar. The isentropic expansion results in steam at 0.1 bar with an enthalpy of 2300 kJ/kg. The actual expansion results in steam at 0.1 bar with an enthalpy of 2450 kJ/kg, and the inlet enthalpy is 3214 kJ/kg.
    $$ \eta_{isentropic} = \frac{3214 - 2450}{3214 - 2300} = \frac{764}{914} \approx 0.836 \text{ or } 83.6\% $$

### 2.2. Mechanical Efficiency

This efficiency accounts for losses in the mechanical components of the turbine, such as bearings and seals, which reduce the power delivered to the output shaft.

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the power delivered to the output shaft to the power developed by the steam in the turbine stages.

*   **Formula:**
    $$ \eta_{mechanical} = \frac{\text{Shaft Power}}{\text{Developed Power}} $$

*   **Explanation:**
    *   **Shaft Power:** The net mechanical power delivered by the turbine to the external load.
    *   **Developed Power:** The total mechanical power generated by the steam expanding through the turbine blades before accounting for mechanical friction.

### 2.3. Volumetric Efficiency

While not a primary efficiency measure for energy conversion, volumetric efficiency is relevant in the context of steam flow, particularly in impulse turbines with partial admission or when considering the impact of blade design on steam passage. It relates to how effectively the turbine passages are filled with steam.

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the actual volume flow rate of steam through the turbine to the theoretical volume flow rate.

*   **Relevance:** More pertinent to the design and flow characteristics within specific turbine stages rather than overall energy conversion.

### 2.4. Overall Efficiency (or Thermal Efficiency)

This is the most comprehensive efficiency measure, relating the net useful work output to the total energy supplied by the steam.

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the net mechanical power output of the turbine to the enthalpy drop of the steam.

*   **Formula:**
    $$ \eta_{overall} = \frac{\text{Shaft Power}}{\text{Isentropic Enthalpy Drop}} = \frac{W_{shaft}}{m \cdot (h_1 - h_{2s})} $$
    Where:
    *   $m$: mass flow rate of steam.

*   **Relationship between Efficiencies:**
    $$ \eta_{overall} = \eta_{isentropic} \times \eta_{mechanical} $$
    This simplification assumes that the isentropic efficiency accounts for all thermodynamic losses, and mechanical efficiency accounts for all mechanical losses.

*   **Example (Continuing from above):** If the shaft power delivered is 10 MW and the isentropic enthalpy drop per kg of steam is 914 kJ/kg, with a steam flow rate of 20 kg/s.
    $$ \eta_{overall} = \frac{10 \text{ MW}}{20 \text{ kg/s} \times 914 \text{ kJ/kg}} = \frac{10 \times 10^6 \text{ J/s}}{18280 \times 10^3 \text{ J/kg}} \approx 0.547 $$
    If the isentropic efficiency was 83.6%, then the mechanical efficiency would be:
    $$ \eta_{mechanical} = \frac{\eta_{overall}}{\eta_{isentropic}} = \frac{0.547}{0.836} \approx 0.654 \text{ or } 65.4\% $$

### 2.5. Stage Efficiency

This refers to the efficiency of a single stage of the turbine, which consists of a set of nozzles and a set of moving blades.

*   **Definition:** The ratio of the work done by the steam in a single stage to the isentropic work that could be done in that stage.

*   **Relevance:** Useful for analyzing the performance of individual turbine components and identifying stages with significant losses.

### 2.6. Mechanical Losses in Steam Turbines

These are significant contributors to the difference between developed power and shaft power.

*   **Friction and Leakage Losses:**
    *   **Friction:** Occurs between the steam and the rotating blades, and between the rotating discs and the stationary casing (windage losses).
    *   **Leakage:** Steam can leak past the glands and seals at the shaft ends and between stages, bypassing the blades and reducing the work output.

*   **Bearing Friction:** Friction in the turbine bearings.

*   **Axial Thrust:** While not directly a loss, it needs to be balanced and can affect the overall design and efficiency.

**Important Point to Remember (R.K Rajput, Chapter 11, Section 11.7):** The isentropic efficiency is a measure of the thermodynamic effectiveness of the expansion process, while mechanical efficiency deals with the conversion of this expanded energy into usable shaft power. Both are critical for overall turbine performance.

## 3. Factors Affecting Steam Turbine Efficiency

Several factors influence the efficiency of a steam turbine, often interconnected.

### 3.1. Steam Conditions

*   **Inlet Pressure and Temperature:** Higher inlet pressure and temperature generally lead to higher available energy and thus higher potential efficiency. Superheated steam reduces moisture content in later stages, minimizing erosion and improving efficiency.
*   **Exhaust Pressure:** Lower exhaust pressure increases the pressure drop across the turbine, leading to greater work extraction and higher efficiency.

### 3.2. Turbine Design and Construction

*   **Blade Design:** Aerodynamic efficiency of blade profiles, angle of incidence, and blade tip clearance significantly impact steam flow and work output.
*   **Nozzle Design:** Efficient nozzle design ensures optimal conversion of thermal energy into kinetic energy of the steam jet.
*   **Stage Design:** Proper matching of nozzle and blade velocities, and efficient inter-stage sealing.
*   **Materials:** High-strength, erosion-resistant materials are crucial for maintaining efficiency over time.
*   **Number of Stages:** Multi-stage turbines are generally more efficient than single-stage turbines as they allow for a more gradual and controlled expansion of steam, minimizing losses.

### 3.3. Operating Conditions

*   **Load:** Turbines are designed for optimal efficiency at a specific load. Operating significantly above or below this design point can reduce efficiency.
*   **Steam Velocity and Blade Velocity:** The ratio of blade velocity to steam velocity (velocity ratio) is crucial for achieving maximum work output.
*   **Partial Admission:** In impulse turbines, if only a portion of the circumference is supplied with steam, it can lead to losses due to steam jet divergence and reheating of idle blades.

### 3.4. Steam Leakage and Sealing

*   **Gland Leakage:** Steam leaking through the shaft seals reduces the mass flow rate through the blades.
*   **Blade Tip Leakage:** Steam leaking over the tips of the moving blades bypasses the work-producing sections.

### 3.5. Wet Steam Losses

*   **Moisture Erosion:** In low-pressure stages, steam can become wet. Water droplets are denser and harder than steam, causing erosion of blades, which reduces their aerodynamic efficiency.
*   **Friction with Droplets:** The presence of water droplets increases friction within the steam flow, leading to energy losses.

**Example (Mahesh Rathore, Chapter 9, Section 9.4):** The effect of moisture content on the efficiency of low-pressure turbine stages is substantial. Even a small percentage of moisture can decrease efficiency by several percent due to increased friction and blade erosion.

## 4. Methods to Improve Steam Turbine Efficiency

Improving efficiency is a continuous effort in steam turbine design and operation.

### 4.1. Enhancing Thermodynamic Performance (Improving Isentropic Efficiency)

*   **Superheating Steam:** Reduces moisture content in low-pressure stages, mitigating erosion and friction losses.
*   **Reheating:** After partial expansion in high-pressure stages, steam is reheated before expanding through low-pressure stages. This increases the average temperature during expansion and reduces wetness in the final stages. (Relates to Rankine cycle improvements, CO1).
*   **Regenerative Feedwater Heating:** A portion of steam is extracted from intermediate stages to preheat the feedwater entering the boiler. This increases the overall thermal efficiency of the power plant, indirectly impacting turbine performance by allowing for more optimal steam conditions. (Relates to cycle analysis, CO1).
*   **Optimized Blade and Nozzle Design:** Advanced aerodynamic profiling, reduced tip clearances, and improved stage matching.

### 4.2. Minimizing Mechanical Losses

*   **Improved Sealing:** Advanced labyrinth seals, carbon seals, and improved gland sealing to minimize steam leakage.
*   **Reduced Friction:** Use of low-friction bearings and effective lubrication.
*   **Balancing:** Proper rotor balancing to minimize vibrations and associated losses.

### 4.3. Operational Improvements

*   **Load Optimization:** Operating the turbine close to its design load.
*   **Regular Maintenance:** Cleaning of blades, inspection of seals, and replacement of worn components.
*   **Condition Monitoring:** Using sensors to monitor vibration, temperature, and steam conditions to detect potential issues affecting efficiency.

**Example (Rudra Moorthy, Chapter 10, Section 10.12):** The implementation of modern blade profiles with optimized aerodynamics and reduced tip clearances has been instrumental in achieving isentropic efficiencies in large modern turbines exceeding 90%.

## 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:** Define isentropic efficiency of a steam turbine and express it in terms of enthalpy.
**Answer:** Isentropic efficiency ($\eta_{isentropic}$) is the ratio of actual work done by the turbine to the work that would be done if the steam expanded isentropically from inlet to exhaust pressure.
$$ \eta_{isentropic} = \frac{\text{Actual Work Output}}{\text{Isentropic Work Output}} = \frac{h_1 - h_2}{h_1 - h_{2s}} $$

**Question 2:** What are the main types of mechanical losses in a steam turbine?
**Answer:** Main mechanical losses include friction in bearings, windage losses (friction between rotating discs and casing), and steam leakage past seals and glands.

**Question 3:** Steam enters a turbine at 40 bar, 400°C and expands to 0.2 bar.
If the isentropic enthalpy drop is 950 kJ/kg and the actual enthalpy drop is 800 kJ/kg, calculate the isentropic efficiency of the turbine.
**Answer:**
Given:
Isentropic enthalpy drop ($h_1 - h_{2s}$) = 950 kJ/kg
Actual enthalpy drop ($h_1 - h_2$) = 800 kJ/kg
$$ \eta_{isentropic} = \frac{\text{Actual Enthalpy Drop}}{\text{Isentropic Enthalpy Drop}} = \frac{800 \text{ kJ/kg}}{950 \text{ kJ/kg}} \approx 0.842 $$
So, the isentropic efficiency is approximately 84.2%.

**Question 4:** How does the presence of moisture in the steam affect the efficiency of a low-pressure turbine stage? (CO2)
**Answer:** Moisture in steam, particularly in low-pressure stages, leads to increased friction due to the density of water droplets and causes erosion of turbine blades. Both these factors reduce the aerodynamic efficiency of the blades, thus decreasing the overall efficiency of the stage and the turbine.

**Question 5:** Explain the concept of overall efficiency and its relationship with isentropic and mechanical efficiencies. (CO2)
**Answer:** Overall efficiency is the ratio of the net mechanical power output to the total energy supplied to the turbine. It represents the combined effect of thermodynamic and mechanical losses. It can be expressed as:
$$ \eta_{overall} = \eta_{isentropic} \times \eta_{mechanical} $$
This relationship highlights that both the quality of the expansion process (isentropic efficiency) and the effectiveness of converting developed power to shaft power (mechanical efficiency) are crucial for the overall performance.

**Question 6:** List two methods to improve the efficiency of a steam turbine. (CO2)
**Answer:**
1.  **Superheating steam:** Reduces moisture content in low-pressure stages, minimizing erosion and friction.
2.  **Improved blade design:** Using aerodynamically optimized blade profiles and minimizing tip clearances to reduce leakage and improve steam flow.

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Isentropic efficiency** is a fundamental measure of the thermodynamic quality of the expansion process.
*   **Mechanical efficiency** accounts for non-thermodynamic losses that reduce shaft power.
*   **Overall efficiency** combines both thermodynamic and mechanical performance.
*   **Moisture content** is a significant factor in the efficiency of low-pressure stages, leading to erosion and increased friction.
*   **Superheating and reheating** are key thermodynamic cycle improvements that enhance turbine efficiency.
*   **Advanced blade design and sealing technologies** are crucial for minimizing losses and maximizing turbine performance.
