### Introduction to Harmonics

Harmonics are sinusoidal voltages or currents having frequencies that are integer multiples of the fundamental power frequency (e.g., 50 Hz or 60 Hz). They are generated by non-linear loads, which draw current that is not sinusoidal in shape, even when supplied with a sinusoidal voltage. The presence of harmonics distorts the sinusoidal waveform of the voltage and current in a power system, leading to various power quality issues.

**Key Concept:** A non-linear load is any load where the relationship between voltage and current is not linear, meaning it cannot be described by Ohm's law ($V=IR$).

**Reference:** Dugan, Me Granaghen, Beaty (2012) extensively discusses the concept of non-linear loads and their impact on power quality, including the generation of harmonics.

### 1. Harmonic Sources: Switching Devices

Switching devices, particularly those used in power electronic converters and controllers, are a major source of harmonics. These devices rapidly switch current on and off, creating non-sinusoidal current waveforms.

#### 1.1 Types of Switching Devices and Harmonic Generation

*   **Thyristors (SCRs - Silicon Controlled Rectifiers):** These are unidirectional switches that conduct when triggered by a gate pulse and continue to conduct until the current through them drops below a holding current or the voltage reverses.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** By controlling the firing angle ($\alpha$), the conduction period of the thyristor is altered, chopping the sinusoidal waveform. This introduces significant odd and even harmonics, depending on the firing angle and the circuit configuration.
    *   **Example:** Phase-controlled rectifiers used in DC motor drives, battery chargers, and high-voltage DC (HVDC) transmission systems.
    *   **Reference:** Sankaran (2002) provides detailed explanations and circuit diagrams illustrating how thyristor-based converters produce harmonic currents.

*   **Diodes:** While seemingly simple, diodes used in uncontrolled rectifiers also introduce harmonics. They conduct only when forward-biased and block when reverse-biased.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** The abrupt switching of current from on to off creates a pulsating DC output, which requires filtering to approximate a pure DC. The AC input current drawn by these converters is non-sinusoidal.
    *   **Example:** Uncontrolled bridge rectifiers used in power supplies for electronic equipment.
    *   **Reference:** Bollen (1999) discusses uncontrolled rectifiers as fundamental sources of harmonics in electronic power supplies.

*   **Insulated Gate Bipolar Transistors (IGBTs), Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors (MOSFETs), and Gate Turn-Off Thyristors (GTOs):** These are modern, fast-switching semiconductor devices used extensively in variable frequency drives (VFDs), switched-mode power supplies (SMPS), and active filters.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) is a common technique used with these devices. By rapidly switching the devices on and off at high frequencies, the output voltage or current waveform is synthesized. The switching pattern, duty cycle, and switching frequency determine the harmonic content. While PWM can be designed to push harmonics to higher frequencies (easier to filter), it still generates significant harmonic distortion, particularly at lower switching frequencies.
    *   **Example:** AC motor drives controlled by VFDs, uninterruptible power supplies (UPSs), and electric vehicle charging stations.
    *   **Reference:** Singh, Chandra, Al-Haddad (2015) offers in-depth analysis of harmonic generation by PWM converter topologies and the impact of switching strategies.

#### 1.2 Harmonic Spectrum from Switching Devices

The harmonic spectrum generated by switching devices depends on:

*   **Switching mechanism:** Controlled (thyristors, IGBTs) vs. uncontrolled (diodes).
*   **Circuit topology:** Rectifiers, inverters, converters.
*   **Control strategy:** Firing angle (for thyristors), PWM modulation scheme (for IGBTs/MOSFETs).
*   **Load characteristics:** Resistive, inductive, or mixed.

**Important Point to Remember:** The switching frequency and its multiples, along with lower-order harmonics, are characteristic of these devices. For PWM converters, the dominant harmonics are often around the switching frequency and its sidebands.

### 2. Harmonic Sources: Arcing Devices

Arcing devices generate harmonics due to the intermittent and chaotic nature of electric arcs. An electric arc is a sustained plasma discharge between two electrodes.

#### 2.1 Types of Arcing Devices and Harmonic Generation

*   **Electric Arc Furnaces (EAFs):** Used in steel production, EAFs draw very large currents and create intense electric arcs between electrodes and the metallic charge.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** The arc is inherently unstable and fluctuates rapidly. As the arc length and current change, the resistance of the arc varies non-linearly. This rapid variation in resistance causes the current to deviate significantly from a sinusoid, producing a broad spectrum of harmonics, often rich in lower-order harmonics.
    *   **Example:** Steel foundries and recycling plants.
    *   **Reference:** Dugan, Me Granaghen, Beaty (2012) dedicates a section to arc furnaces as significant sources of voltage distortion and harmonics due to their highly variable arc characteristics.

*   **Arc Welders:** Similar to EAFs, arc welding processes involve creating and maintaining an electric arc for melting and joining metals.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** The arc's instability and the intermittent nature of the welding process lead to non-sinusoidal current waveforms and harmonic generation.
    *   **Example:** Manufacturing, construction, and repair workshops.

*   **Lightning Arresters (Surge Arresters):** While designed for transient overvoltage protection, some types, particularly older or malfunctioning metal oxide varistors (MOVs) or spark gaps, can exhibit arcing behavior under certain conditions, contributing to harmonic distortion.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** Non-linear voltage-current characteristics and potential temporary arcing can introduce distortion.
    *   **Example:** Electrical substation equipment protection.

#### 2.2 Harmonic Spectrum from Arcing Devices

*   **Broadband Harmonic Spectrum:** Arcing devices typically produce a wide range of harmonics, often with a significant presence of lower-order harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th, etc.).
*   **Time-Varying Harmonics:** The harmonic content can fluctuate significantly as the arc conditions change.
*   **High Magnitude Harmonics:** Due to the extreme non-linearity of the arc, the magnitude of these harmonics can be substantial, leading to significant voltage distortion.

**Important Point to Remember:** The chaotic and unstable nature of electric arcs is the primary reason for their significant harmonic contribution.

### 3. Harmonic Sources: Saturable Devices

Saturable devices are components whose magnetic flux density no longer increases linearly with the applied magnetic field strength (or magnetizing current) once the magnetic material reaches its saturation point.

#### 3.1 Types of Saturable Devices and Harmonic Generation

*   **Transformers:** The magnetic core of a transformer can saturate under certain conditions.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** When a transformer core is operated with a high flux density, especially near or at saturation, the magnetizing current becomes highly non-sinusoidal. The magnetizing current waveform contains a significant amount of the 3rd harmonic, and also 5th and 7th harmonics, depending on the core material and design. This non-sinusoidal magnetizing current is drawn from the supply, even when the primary voltage is sinusoidal.
    *   **Conditions leading to saturation:**
        *   **Over-excitation:** Applying a voltage higher than the rated voltage.
        *   **DC offset in AC voltage:** Caused by switching faults or other power system disturbances.
        *   **Energizing a transformer:** The initial inrush current when energizing a transformer can have a DC component that drives the core into saturation, leading to large harmonic currents.
        *   **Deep voltage sags:** Can lead to over-fluxing when voltage recovers.
    *   **Example:** Power transformers in substations and distribution systems.
    *   **Reference:** Dugan, Me Granaghen, Beaty (2012) dedicates a significant portion to transformer saturation and its contribution to harmonic currents, particularly the third harmonic.

*   **Inductors:** Inductors with ferromagnetic cores can also saturate.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** Similar to transformers, if the current through an inductor with a saturable core becomes too large, the core saturates. This leads to a non-linear relationship between current and flux, and consequently, a non-sinusoidal current waveform containing harmonics.
    *   **Example:** Inductors used in filters for power electronic converters or in current limiting circuits.

*   **Motors (Induction and Synchronous):** The magnetic circuits in electric machines can saturate under high load conditions or when operating with distorted voltages.
    *   **How they generate harmonics:** Saturation of the stator or rotor iron can lead to non-linear flux-current relationships, contributing to harmonic currents drawn by the motor.
    *   **Example:** Large industrial induction motors operating at or above their rated capacity.

#### 3.2 Harmonic Spectrum from Saturable Devices

*   **Dominant 3rd Harmonic:** Transformers, particularly when saturated, are well-known for generating a significant third harmonic component in their magnetizing current.
*   **Odd Harmonics:** Generally, saturation tends to produce odd harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, etc.), with the lower-order odd harmonics being most prominent.
*   **Magnitude Depends on Degree of Saturation:** The severity of harmonic generation is directly related to how deeply the magnetic material is driven into saturation.

**Important Point to Remember:** Transformer saturation, especially due to over-excitation or DC offset, is a primary source of the third harmonic in the power system.

### Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
c) An electric arc furnace
**Explanation:** Arcing devices like EAFs are known for their instability, leading to a broad harmonic spectrum, often with significant lower-order components. VFDs produce harmonics related to their switching frequency, simple rectifiers produce specific sets of harmonics, and linear resistors do not generate harmonics.

**Answer 2:**
b) 3rd Harmonic
**Explanation:** Operating a transformer at a voltage higher than its rating leads to over-excitation and core saturation, which is a primary cause of injecting the third harmonic into the system.

**Answer 3:**
b) Harmonics concentrated around the switching frequency
**Explanation:** PWM techniques intentionally switch at high frequencies. While they can be designed to shift harmonics, the resulting distortion often manifests as significant components at or around the switching frequency and its multiples.

**Answer 4:**
b) It becomes highly distorted and contains harmonic frequencies.
**Explanation:** When a magnetic material saturates, its inductive properties change non-linearly with the current, causing the current waveform itself to become distorted and rich in harmonics.

**Answer 5:**
c) The unstable and fluctuating nature of the electric arc.
**Explanation:** The electric arc is an inherently unstable phenomenon where the resistance changes rapidly and non-linearly with arc length and current, leading to significant harmonic generation.

### Important Points to Remember

*   **Non-linearity is Key:** All harmonic sources are inherently non-linear loads.
*   **Switching Devices:** Semiconductor devices (thyristors, IGBTs, MOSFETs, diodes) in power electronic converters are major harmonic sources, with PWM techniques contributing harmonics around the switching frequency.
*   **Arcing Devices:** The instability of electric arcs (e.g., in EAFs) creates a broad harmonic spectrum, often with substantial low-order harmonics.
*   **Saturable Devices:** Magnetic saturation in transformers and inductors, especially due to over-excitation or DC offset, is a significant source of the third harmonic.
*   **Harmonic Spectrum Varies:** The specific harmonic frequencies and their magnitudes depend heavily on the type of device, its operating conditions, and the control strategy employed.
*   **Impact on System:** Harmonics can lead to overheating, increased losses, equipment malfunction, and interference with sensitive electronic devices.
