# Power Quality: Module 2 - Harmonics

## Topic: Harmonics - Mechanism of Harmonic Generation and Triplen Harmonics

This module delves into the phenomenon of harmonics, a significant contributor to power quality issues. We will explore how these non-fundamental frequency components are generated in electrical systems and pay special attention to the peculiar behavior of triplen harmonics.

## 1. Introduction to Harmonics

Harmonics are sinusoidal voltages or currents that have frequencies that are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency of the power system.

*   **Fundamental Frequency:** The nominal frequency of the power system, typically 50 Hz or 60 Hz.
*   **Harmonic Frequency:** A frequency that is an integer multiple of the fundamental frequency. For a fundamental frequency $f$, harmonic frequencies are $2f, 3f, 4f, 5f, \ldots$.
*   **Order of Harmonics:** The integer multiple itself (e.g., the 3rd harmonic has an order of 3).
*   **Harmonic Distortion:** The presence of harmonic frequencies in the system, causing the waveform to deviate from a pure sinusoid.

**Reference:** Dugan, Me Granaghen, & Beaty (2012) defines harmonics as sinusoidal components at frequencies that are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. Sankaran (2002) emphasizes that harmonics are a major cause of power quality problems.

## 2. Mechanism of Harmonic Generation

Harmonics are primarily generated by **non-linear loads**. A linear load is one where the current is directly proportional to the voltage, and the waveform of the current is the same as the waveform of the voltage (only scaled). A non-linear load, however, draws current that is not proportional to the voltage, resulting in a current waveform that is distorted and contains harmonic components.

### 2.1. What Makes a Load Non-Linear?

Non-linearity arises from the presence of **semiconductor switching devices** in loads. These devices, such as diodes, thyristors (SCRs), and transistors, have switching characteristics that are not linear. They conduct current only when a certain voltage condition is met and then switch off when another condition is met, creating abrupt changes in current.

**Examples of Non-Linear Loads:**

*   **Switch-Mode Power Supplies (SMPS):** Found in computers, chargers, and many electronic devices. They rectify AC to DC, and then chop the DC at a high frequency to produce regulated DC outputs.
*   **Adjustable Speed Drives (ASDs) / Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs):** Used to control the speed of AC motors. They typically involve rectification, DC bus smoothing, and then inversion to create a variable frequency AC output.
*   **Rectifiers:** Convert AC to DC.
    *   **Phase-Controlled Rectifiers:** Used in industrial applications (e.g., welding, electroplating) where voltage and current can be controlled.
    *   **Uncontrolled Rectifiers (e.g., 6-pulse bridge):** Common in DC power supplies.
*   **Arc Furnaces:** Used in steel production, characterized by a fluctuating current due to the arc.
*   **Discharge Lighting:** Fluorescent lamps, high-intensity discharge (HID) lamps, and compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) contain ballasts that often use switching elements or gas discharge mechanisms that introduce non-linearity.
*   **Switching Devices in Power Electronics:** Thyristors, IGBTs, MOSFETs, etc., when used for power conversion or control.

**Reference:** Bollen (1999) extensively discusses non-linear loads as the primary source of harmonic currents. Singh, Chandra, & Al-Haddad (2015) provide detailed analyses of harmonic generation in various power electronic converters.

### 2.2. How Non-Linear Loads Generate Harmonics

The fundamental principle is that a non-linear load draws current that is not sinusoidal, even when supplied with a sinusoidal voltage. This distorted current waveform can be mathematically decomposed into a fundamental frequency component and its integer multiples (harmonics) using the Fourier Series.

**Fourier Series Decomposition:**

Any periodic, non-sinusoidal waveform $f(t)$ can be represented as an infinite sum of sinusoids:

$f(t) = A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \cos(n\omega t + \phi_n)$

where:
*   $A_0$ is the DC component (average value).
*   $A_n$ is the amplitude of the $n^{th}$ harmonic.
*   $\omega$ is the fundamental angular frequency ($2\pi f$).
*   $n$ is the harmonic order.

For AC systems, we are primarily interested in the AC components, so we often consider the Fourier series in the form:

$f(t) = c_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n \cos(n\omega t) + b_n \sin(n\omega t))$

The amplitude of the $n^{th}$ harmonic component is given by:
$F_n = \sqrt{a_n^2 + b_n^2}$

The Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) is a measure of the overall harmonic content:

$THD = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} F_n^2}}{F_1} \times 100\%$

**Mechanism Example: Half-Wave Rectifier**

Consider a simple half-wave rectifier (a single diode). When a sinusoidal voltage $v(t) = V_m \sin(\omega t)$ is applied, the diode only conducts for positive half-cycles. The resulting current waveform is a series of positive half-sine waves separated by zero current periods. This waveform is inherently non-sinusoidal and rich in harmonics.

*   **Waveform:** A series of positive half-sine waves.
*   **Harmonics Present:** Contains the fundamental (50/60 Hz), and odd harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th, etc.). Notably, even harmonics are absent due to the symmetry of the positive half-cycle.
*   **Fourier Analysis:** Decomposing this waveform reveals significant amplitudes for odd harmonics.

**Reference:** Dugan, Me Granaghen, & Beaty (2012) and Sankaran (2002) provide detailed explanations of how the switching action of diodes and thyristors leads to distorted current waveforms and the generation of harmonics. They often use examples of rectifiers and converters.

### 2.3. Impact of Harmonic Generation

*   **Increased RMS Current:** Even if the voltage waveform is pure, the distorted current drawn by non-linear loads increases the RMS current in the system.
*   **Overheating:** Increased RMS current leads to higher $I^2R$ losses in conductors, transformers, and rotating machines, causing them to overheat and reducing their lifespan.
*   **Equipment Malfunction:** Sensitive electronic equipment, control systems, and communication devices can be affected by harmonic distortion, leading to erratic operation, false tripping of protective relays, and data corruption.
*   **Reduced System Efficiency:** Extra losses due to harmonics reduce the overall efficiency of the power system.
*   **Transformer Saturation:** In some cases, the fundamental component might cause transformer saturation, leading to even more harmonic generation.
*   **Resonance:** Harmonics can interact with system impedances and capacitances to create resonant conditions, amplifying harmonic voltages and currents to dangerous levels.

**Reference:** All textbooks emphasize the detrimental effects of harmonics. Dugan, Me Granaghen, & Beaty (2012) dedicate sections to the impact on transformers, motors, and electronic loads.

## 3. Triplen Harmonics (3rd, 9th, 15th, etc.)

Triplen harmonics are a specific group of odd harmonics whose frequencies are multiples of three times the fundamental frequency (i.e., 3rd, 9th, 15th, 21st, etc.).

### 3.1. Characteristics of Triplen Harmonics

*   **Phase Relationship:** In a three-phase system, the characteristic of triplen harmonics is that they are **in-phase** in all three phases.
*   **Generation Sources:** They are commonly generated by non-linear loads that exhibit certain symmetrical current waveforms, such as:
    *   **Single-phase loads connected to three-phase systems:** Many single-phase non-linear loads (e.g., computers, VFDs) are connected to a three-phase supply via a single phase. These loads can generate triplen harmonics.
    *   **Phase-controlled rectifiers:** Particularly those with a certain firing angle control.
    *   **Transformer excitation:** Transformer cores can produce triplen harmonics during magnetization, although typically at low levels unless the transformer is heavily loaded or designed poorly.
    *   **Six-pulse rectifiers:** These produce 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th, etc., harmonics, but not the 3rd harmonic itself from the rectifier switching sequence. However, if a single-phase load causing 3rd harmonic is connected to one phase of a 3-phase system, the 3rd harmonic will be generated.

**Reference:** Sankaran (2002) and Singh, Chandra, & Al-Haddad (2015) specifically discuss the behavior of triplen harmonics in three-phase systems.

### 3.2. Impact of Triplen Harmonics in Three-Phase Systems

The in-phase nature of triplen harmonics in a three-phase system has significant implications:

1.  **Neutral Wire Current:**
    *   In a balanced three-phase system with pure sinusoidal waveforms, the sum of the fundamental currents in the three phases is zero ($I_A + I_B + I_C = 0$). Therefore, the neutral wire carries no current (or only very small zero-sequence components).
    *   However, when triplen harmonics are present, they are in-phase across all three phases. This means the sum of the triplen harmonic currents in the three phases is not zero; instead, it adds up.
    *   **$I_{N} = I_{A3} + I_{B3} + I_{C3}$**
    *   If $I_{A3}$, $I_{B3}$, and $I_{C3}$ are in phase, then $I_N = 3 \times I_{A3}$ (assuming balanced triplen harmonic generation).
    *   **Consequence:** The neutral wire can carry a significant current, potentially equal to or even greater than the current in the phase conductors, especially if there are many single-phase non-linear loads connected.

2.  **Overheating of Neutral Conductors:**
    *   If the neutral conductor is not adequately sized to handle this triplen harmonic current, it can overheat, posing a fire hazard.
    *   Historically, neutrals were often sized for the fundamental current, assuming they would carry little current. This assumption is invalid with modern non-linear loads.

3.  **Transformer Issues:**
    *   **Delta-Wye (Y-Δ) Transformers:** In Y-Δ transformers, the delta winding provides a path for zero-sequence currents, including triplen harmonics. This can help to trap and circulate these harmonics within the delta winding, potentially leading to overheating of the transformer's delta winding if not properly managed.
    *   **Wye-Wye (Y-Y) Transformers (without tertiary winding):** In a Y-Y connection without a tertiary winding, triplen harmonics generated in the primary cannot flow into the secondary and vice-versa. This can lead to voltage distortion in both windings.
    *   **Wye-Wye Transformers with Tertiary Winding:** A delta tertiary winding is often added to Y-Y transformers specifically to provide a path for zero-sequence currents, including triplen harmonics, thereby mitigating their effect on the main windings.
    *   **Standard Wye (Y-Y) Connected Loads:** If a three-phase load connected to a Y-Y transformer has a neutral connection (four-wire system), triplen harmonics generated by the load will flow into the neutral.

4.  **Voltage Distortion:**
    *   The presence of triplen harmonic currents flowing through system impedances (lines, transformers) causes voltage drops at these harmonic frequencies, leading to voltage distortion.

**Example:**

Consider a three-phase four-wire system supplying power to three identical single-phase non-linear loads, each drawing a current with a significant 3rd harmonic component.

*   Phase A current: $I_A = I_{A1}\sin(\omega t) + I_{A3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3)$
*   Phase B current: $I_B = I_{B1}\sin(\omega t - 120^\circ) + I_{B3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3)$
*   Phase C current: $I_C = I_{C1}\sin(\omega t - 240^\circ) + I_{C3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3)$

If the loads are identical and the 3rd harmonic generation is consistent, then $I_{A3}$, $I_{B3}$, and $I_{C3}$ will be in phase.

$I_A + I_B + I_C = (I_{A1}\sin(\omega t) + I_{B1}\sin(\omega t - 120^\circ) + I_{C1}\sin(\omega t - 240^\circ)) + (I_{A3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3) + I_{B3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3) + I_{C3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3))$

Assuming balanced fundamental currents ($I_{A1} + I_{B1} + I_{C1} = 0$), and balanced triplen harmonic currents ($I_{A3} = I_{B3} = I_{C3}$), then:

$I_A + I_B + I_C = 0 + 3 \times I_{A3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3)$

The neutral current ($I_N$) will be $3 \times I_{A3}\sin(3\omega t + \phi_3)$, which can be significant.

**Reference:** Dugan, Me Granaghen, & Beaty (2012) highlights the issue of neutral current in modern distribution systems due to single-phase loads and triplen harmonics. Sankaran (2002) also provides a good explanation of this phenomenon and its impact on transformers.

### 3.3. Mitigation of Triplen Harmonics

*   **Proper Neutral Sizing:** Ensure neutral conductors are adequately sized to handle expected triplen harmonic currents (often sized at 150-200% of phase conductor capacity).
*   **Harmonic Filters:** Install active or passive filters designed to absorb or divert triplen harmonics.
*   **K-rated Transformers:** Use transformers specifically designed for harmonic loads, often with a K-factor rating. K-rated transformers have a larger neutral conductor and are designed to withstand the increased heating due to harmonic currents.
*   **Delta Tertiary Windings:** For Y-Y connected transformers, a delta tertiary winding can provide a path for triplen harmonics.
*   **Isolation Transformers:** Can isolate sensitive equipment from harmonic distortion.
*   **Harmonic Mitigating Transformers:** Special transformer designs exist that can significantly reduce harmonic currents.
*   **Controlled Rectifiers with Different Pulse Numbers:** Using 6-pulse, 12-pulse, or 18-pulse rectifiers can reduce or eliminate certain harmonic orders. For example, a 12-pulse rectifier (formed by combining a 6-pulse rectifier with a phase-shifting transformer) cancels out the 3rd, 9th, 15th, etc., harmonics.

**Reference:** Singh, Chandra, & Al-Haddad (2015) offers a comprehensive review of various harmonic mitigation techniques, including those specifically for triplen harmonics.

## 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **Non-linear loads are the primary source of harmonics.**
*   **Semiconductor switching devices are the key to non-linearity.**
*   **Harmonics cause increased RMS current, overheating, equipment malfunction, and reduced efficiency.**
*   **Triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th, 15th...) are in-phase in a three-phase system.**
*   **Triplen harmonics cause significant neutral current and can overheat neutral conductors.**
*   **The neutral conductor must be sized appropriately for modern loads.**
*   **K-rated transformers and delta tertiary windings are common solutions for harmonic issues.**
*   **Harmonic mitigation techniques include filtering, special transformer designs, and using higher pulse number converters.**

## 5. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1 (K2 - CO1):**
What is the primary reason for harmonic generation in power systems?
a) Overloaded transformers
b) Induction motors
c) Non-linear loads
d) Long transmission lines

**Answer:** c) Non-linear loads

**Question 2 (K2 - CO1):**
Which of the following is NOT a common source of harmonics?
a) Switch-Mode Power Supplies (SMPS)
b) Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs)
c) Incandescent light bulbs
d) Arc furnaces

**Answer:** c) Incandescent light bulbs (These are generally considered linear loads).

**Question 3 (K2 - CO1):**
A non-linear load draws a current waveform that is:
a) Always sinusoidal, but with a phase shift
b) Not proportional to the voltage waveform
c) A pure DC component
d) Always a lagging power factor

**Answer:** b) Not proportional to the voltage waveform

**Question 4 (K2 - CO1):**
What is a key characteristic of triplen harmonics (e.g., 3rd, 9th) in a balanced three-phase system?
a) They are 120 degrees out of phase with each other.
b) They are in phase with each other across the three phases.
c) They are always cancelled out by the neutral conductor.
d) They are only found in single-phase systems.

**Answer:** b) They are in phase with each other across the three phases.

**Question 5 (K2 - CO1):**
What is a significant problem caused by triplen harmonics in a four-wire three-phase system?
a) Reduced voltage on phase conductors
b) Increased capacitance in the system
c) Significant current in the neutral conductor
d) Increased inductive reactance

**Answer:** c) Significant current in the neutral conductor

**Question 6 (K3 - CO2 precursor):**
Explain, in your own words, how the switching action of a diode in a rectifier creates harmonic currents. Use the concept of Fourier analysis in your explanation.

**Answer:** A diode acts as a switch, allowing current to flow only when the applied voltage is in a certain direction (e.g., positive). This abrupt switching on and off, rather than a smooth variation, creates a current waveform that is not a pure sine wave. According to Fourier analysis, any periodic, non-sinusoidal waveform can be decomposed into a sum of a fundamental sine wave and its integer multiples (harmonics). The switching action of the diode generates these harmonic frequency components in the current drawn from the supply. For instance, a half-wave rectifier produces odd harmonics.

**Question 7 (K2 - CO1):**
Why are K-rated transformers used in systems with significant harmonic distortion?

**Answer:** K-rated transformers are specifically designed to handle the increased heating effects caused by harmonic currents. They have larger neutral conductors to carry significant triplen harmonic currents and are constructed to withstand the higher RMS currents and eddy current losses associated with harmonic distortion, thereby preventing overheating and ensuring a longer lifespan compared to standard transformers.
