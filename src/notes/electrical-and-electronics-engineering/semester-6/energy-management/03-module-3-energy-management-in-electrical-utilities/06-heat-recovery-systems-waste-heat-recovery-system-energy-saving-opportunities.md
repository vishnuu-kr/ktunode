## Energy Management: Module 3 - Energy Management in Electrical Utilities: Heat Recovery Systems

This module delves into the critical area of heat recovery systems within electrical utilities, focusing on waste heat recovery and its associated energy-saving opportunities. Understanding these concepts is vital for efficient energy management in the utility sector.

### 1. Introduction to Heat Recovery and Waste Heat

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Heat Recovery:** The process of capturing and reusing heat that would otherwise be lost to the environment. This recovered heat can be used for various purposes, such as preheating feedwater, generating electricity, or providing space heating.
*   **Waste Heat:** Thermal energy that is released as a byproduct of industrial processes, power generation, or other activities, and is not utilized for its intended purpose.
*   **Thermodynamic Efficiency:** The ratio of useful output energy to the total input energy in a process. Waste heat represents a loss of thermodynamic efficiency.

**Importance in Electrical Utilities:**

Electrical utilities, particularly power generation plants, are significant sources of waste heat. This heat is often rejected to the atmosphere or cooling water, representing a substantial loss of potential energy. Effective heat recovery can significantly improve overall plant efficiency, reduce fuel consumption, and lower operational costs.

**Reference:**
*   "Energy Management and Conservation Handbook" by D. Yogi Goswami, Frank Kreith (CRC Press, 2007) emphasizes the importance of recovering heat from various industrial processes, including power generation, for enhanced energy efficiency.

### 2. Sources of Waste Heat in Electrical Utilities

Waste heat can be generated from various stages and components within electrical utility operations.

**Common Sources:**

*   **Flue Gases from Boilers/Furnaces:** High-temperature gases exiting combustion chambers contain significant thermal energy.
    *   **Example:** In thermal power plants, flue gases from coal or gas combustion are a primary source of waste heat.
*   **Exhaust Gases from Gas Turbines/Engines:** Hot exhaust gases from prime movers are a substantial source of thermal energy.
    *   **Example:** In combined cycle power plants, the exhaust from gas turbines is used to generate steam in a Heat Recovery Steam Generator (HRSG).
*   **Cooling Water from Condensers:** Heat rejected from the working fluid (e.g., steam) in the condenser to the cooling medium (water or air).
    *   **Example:** In steam power plants, the latent heat of condensation is transferred to cooling water, which is then often discharged at a higher temperature.
*   **Hot Process Water/Liquids:** Heated water or other fluids used in auxiliary processes that are not fully utilized.
*   **Steam Vents and Blowdown:** Steam released from pressure relief valves or from boiler blowdown systems.
*   **Waste Heat from Transformers and Electrical Equipment:** Although typically lower in temperature, heat generated by transformers and other electrical equipment can be recovered for specific applications.

**Reference:**
*   "Industrial Energy Conservation" by Charles M. Gottschalk (John Wiley & Sons, 1996) details various industrial processes and their associated waste heat streams, many of which are relevant to utility operations.

### 3. Waste Heat Recovery Systems (WHRS)

WHRS are designed to capture and utilize waste heat, transforming a loss into a valuable energy resource.

**Types of WHRS:**

*   **Economizers:** Preheating boiler feedwater using the heat from flue gases before it enters the boiler. This reduces the fuel required to generate steam.
*   **Air Preheaters:** Preheating combustion air using the heat from flue gases before it enters the furnace. This improves combustion efficiency and reduces fuel consumption.
    *   **Types:**
        *   **Recuperative Air Preheaters:** Heat is transferred directly from flue gas to combustion air through a separating wall (e.g., tubular, plate types).
        *   **Regenerative Air Preheaters:** Heat is transferred indirectly using a thermal storage medium (e.g., Ljungström air preheater with rotating elements).
*   **Waste Heat Boilers (WHBs):** Generating steam from high-temperature waste gases. This steam can be used for process heating or to drive turbines for electricity generation.
*   **Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) Systems:** Utilizing waste heat to vaporize an organic fluid with a low boiling point, which then drives a turbine to produce electricity. This is suitable for lower-grade waste heat (typically below 300°C).
*   **Heat Pumps:** Using a small amount of high-grade energy to upgrade low-grade waste heat to a higher temperature for useful application.
*   **Heat Exchangers:** General devices for transferring heat between two fluids without direct contact. Various types are used for different applications (e.g., shell-and-tube, plate-and-frame).

**Reference:**
*   "Energy Management and Conservation Handbook" by D. Yogi Goswami, Frank Kreith (CRC Press, 2007) provides comprehensive details on various waste heat recovery technologies and their applications.

### 4. Energy Saving Opportunities through Waste Heat Recovery

The implementation of WHRS offers significant energy savings and economic benefits.

**Key Opportunities:**

*   **Improved Thermal Efficiency:** By preheating combustion air and feedwater, the overall thermal efficiency of power generation cycles is increased, leading to less fuel consumption per unit of electricity generated.
*   **Reduced Fuel Consumption:** Directly translates to lower operating costs and reduced reliance on primary energy sources.
*   **Cogeneration (Combined Heat and Power - CHP):** Utilizing waste heat to generate both electricity and useful thermal energy (steam or hot water) for industrial processes or district heating. This significantly increases overall energy utilization efficiency.
    *   **Example:** A power plant using its waste heat to supply steam to an adjacent industrial facility.
*   **Electricity Generation from Low-Grade Heat:** ORC systems enable the generation of electricity from heat sources that are too low in temperature for conventional steam cycles.
*   **Reduced Greenhouse Gas Emissions:** Lower fuel consumption directly leads to a reduction in the emission of greenhouse gases like CO2.
*   **Improved Environmental Performance:** Reduced thermal pollution of cooling water discharges.
*   **Potential for New Revenue Streams:** Selling surplus heat or electricity generated from waste heat.

**Impact on Course Outcomes:**

*   **CO1 (Analyse the significance of energy management and auditing):** Waste heat recovery is a prime example of how identifying and utilizing lost energy contributes to overall energy management strategy.
*   **CO2 (Discuss the energy efficiency and management of electrical loads):** While this topic focuses on generation-side, efficient generation impacts the overall energy landscape and can reduce the need for inefficient load shedding.
*   **CO4 (Explain the energy management opportunities in industries):** Power plants are large industrial facilities, and waste heat recovery is a significant energy management opportunity within them.
*   **CO5 (Compute the economic feasibility of the energy conservation measures):** Quantifying savings from reduced fuel consumption and potential revenue from selling recovered energy is crucial for economic feasibility studies.

**Important Point to Remember:** The economic viability of a WHRS depends on the temperature of the waste heat, the quantity of heat available, the intended use of the recovered heat, and the cost of the recovery equipment.

### 5. Economic Feasibility of Heat Recovery Systems

The decision to implement a WHRS hinges on its economic viability.

**Factors to Consider:**

*   **Capital Cost:** Initial investment for the recovery equipment (e.g., economizers, air preheaters, WHBs, ORC units).
*   **Operating and Maintenance (O&M) Costs:** Ongoing expenses for running and maintaining the system.
*   **Energy Savings:** Quantifiable reduction in fuel costs or increase in electricity generation.
*   **Salvage Value:** Potential resale value of the equipment at the end of its lifespan.
*   **Payback Period:** The time required for the accumulated energy savings to equal the initial investment.
*   **Internal Rate of Return (IRR):** The discount rate at which the net present value of all cash flows from the project equals zero.
*   **Net Present Value (NPV):** The difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a period of time.
*   **Government Incentives and Subsidies:** Availability of tax credits or grants for energy-efficient technologies.

**Calculation of Savings:**

*   **For Boiler Feedwater Preheating:**
    *   `Energy Saved (kJ/hr) = Mass flow rate of feedwater (kg/hr) * Specific heat of water (kJ/kg°C) * Temperature Rise (°C)`
    *   `Fuel Saved = Energy Saved / (Fuel Calorific Value * Boiler Efficiency)`
*   **For Air Preheating:**
    *   `Energy Saved (kJ/hr) = Mass flow rate of combustion air (kg/hr) * Specific heat of air (kJ/kg°C) * Temperature Rise (°C)`
    *   `Fuel Saved = Energy Saved / (Fuel Calorific Value * Boiler Efficiency)`

**Example Calculation (Simplified):**

A power plant has flue gases at 200°C and wants to preheat combustion air from 20°C to 150°C using a recuperative air preheater.
*   Mass flow rate of air = 100,000 kg/hr
*   Specific heat of air ≈ 1.005 kJ/kg°C
*   Temperature rise = 150°C - 20°C = 130°C

*   **Energy gained by air:**
    `Q_air = 100,000 kg/hr * 1.005 kJ/kg°C * 130°C = 13,065,000 kJ/hr`

If the boiler efficiency is 85% and the fuel calorific value is 40,000 kJ/kg, and the fuel cost is $0.02/kg:

*   **Fuel saved:**
    This calculation requires knowing the heat lost to the flue gas per unit of fuel burned, which is not directly provided in this simplified scenario. However, the energy gained by the air directly reduces the amount of fuel needed to achieve the desired boiler output.

### 6. Case Studies and Best Practices

*   **Combined Cycle Power Plants:** HRSGs are a standard component, recovering heat from gas turbine exhaust to generate steam for a steam turbine, significantly boosting overall efficiency.
*   **Cogeneration in Industrial Power Plants:** Utilizing waste steam from turbines for industrial processes.
*   **Flue Gas Heat Recovery in Coal-Fired Power Plants:** Economizers and air preheaters are common to improve combustion and reduce fuel consumption.

**Best Practices:**

*   **Conduct Thorough Waste Heat Audits:** Identify all potential sources and quantities of waste heat.
*   **Match Recovery Technology to Heat Source Characteristics:** Select appropriate systems based on temperature, flow rate, and contaminants.
*   **Consider the End-Use of Recovered Heat:** Ensure a viable application for the recovered energy.
*   **Integrate WHRS with Existing Systems:** Design for seamless integration and minimal disruption.
*   **Regular Monitoring and Maintenance:** Ensure optimal performance and longevity of the WHRS.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
What are the primary benefits of implementing waste heat recovery systems in electrical utilities? (Relates to CO1, CO4)

**Answer:**
The primary benefits include improved thermal efficiency, reduced fuel consumption, lower operational costs, reduced greenhouse gas emissions, and potential for cogeneration or additional electricity generation.

**Question 2:**
Describe two types of air preheaters commonly used in power plants and explain how they save energy. (Relates to CO4)

**Answer:**
1.  **Recuperative Air Preheaters (e.g., Tubular, Plate):** These preheat combustion air by direct heat transfer from hot flue gases through a separating wall. This higher air temperature leads to more efficient combustion, reducing the amount of fuel needed to achieve the same heat output.
2.  **Regenerative Air Preheaters (e.g., Ljungström):** These use a rotating mass (usually metal elements) that absorbs heat from flue gases and then transfers it to the incoming combustion air. This cyclic process also preheats the air, improving combustion efficiency and saving fuel.

**Question 3:**
A thermal power plant generates 500 MW of electricity. The flue gases leaving the boiler are at 180°C. If these gases are used to preheat boiler feedwater from 100°C to 140°C using an economizer, and the mass flow rate of feedwater is 2000 tonnes/hour, calculate the approximate energy saved per hour (in GJ). Assume the specific heat of water is 4.18 kJ/kg°C. (Relates to CO5)

**Given:**
*   Mass flow rate of feedwater = 2000 tonnes/hour = 2000 * 1000 kg/hour = 2,000,000 kg/hour
*   Initial feedwater temperature = 100°C
*   Final feedwater temperature = 140°C
*   Temperature rise = 140°C - 100°C = 40°C
*   Specific heat of water = 4.18 kJ/kg°C

**Calculation:**
*   **Energy gained by feedwater:**
    `Q = mass flow rate * specific heat * temperature rise`
    `Q = 2,000,000 kg/hour * 4.18 kJ/kg°C * 40°C`
    `Q = 334,400,000 kJ/hour`

*   **Convert to GJ:**
    `1 GJ = 10^9 J = 10^6 kJ`
    `Energy Saved = 334,400,000 kJ/hour / 10^6 kJ/GJ`
    `Energy Saved = 334.4 GJ/hour`

**Answer:** The approximate energy saved per hour is **334.4 GJ**.

**Question 4:**
Discuss the role of Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) systems in waste heat recovery within electrical utilities. (Relates to CO4)

**Answer:**
ORC systems are employed to recover energy from low-grade waste heat sources (typically below 300°C) that are not suitable for conventional steam Rankine cycles. They use an organic fluid with a low boiling point that is vaporized by the waste heat. This vapor then expands through a turbine, generating electricity. ORCs offer an efficient way to generate additional electricity from waste heat that would otherwise be lost, improving the overall energy utilization of a plant.

**Question 5:**
What are the key financial metrics used to assess the economic feasibility of a waste heat recovery project? (Relates to CO5)

**Answer:**
Key financial metrics include:
*   **Payback Period:** Time to recover the initial investment.
*   **Internal Rate of Return (IRR):** The discount rate at which NPV is zero.
*   **Net Present Value (NPV):** Present value of future savings minus the initial investment.
*   **Return on Investment (ROI):** Profitability of the investment.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Waste heat is a lost opportunity:** Effectively recovering and utilizing it is a cornerstone of efficient energy management.
*   **Temperature is a key factor:** The higher the temperature of the waste heat, the more versatile and efficient the recovery options.
*   **Economic viability is paramount:** Thorough financial analysis is crucial before implementing any WHRS.
*   **Integration is key:** WHRS should be designed to complement and enhance existing plant operations.
*   **Cogeneration offers significant advantages:** Combining heat and power generation from waste heat dramatically improves overall energy efficiency.
*   **Sustainability:** Waste heat recovery contributes to reduced fossil fuel consumption and lower environmental impact.
