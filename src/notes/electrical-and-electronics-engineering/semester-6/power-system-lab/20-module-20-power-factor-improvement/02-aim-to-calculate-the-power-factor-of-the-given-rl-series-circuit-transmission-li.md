# Power System Lab - Module 20: Power Factor Improvement

## Topic: Aim: To calculate the power factor of the given RL series circuit (transmission line) and design the capacitance required to improve the power factor to the desired value.

### Introduction

Power factor is a crucial parameter in AC power systems that indicates the efficiency of power utilization. A low power factor signifies that a significant portion of the apparent power is reactive power, which does not contribute to useful work but still flows through the system. This leads to increased current, higher losses, and reduced system capacity. This module focuses on understanding and improving the power factor of an RL series circuit, representative of a transmission line.

### Learning Outcomes Covered:

*   **Calculate the power factor of the given RL series circuit (transmission line):** This involves understanding the components of power and how they relate to the power factor.
*   **Design the capacitance required to improve the power factor to the desired value:** This involves applying the principles of reactive power compensation to determine the appropriate capacitor size.

## 1. Key Concepts and Definitions

### 1.1. Power in AC Circuits

In an AC circuit, power can be categorized into three types:

*   **Real Power (P):** Also known as active power or true power. It is the power that performs useful work (e.g., turning a motor, lighting a bulb).
    *   **Unit:** Watts (W)
    *   **Formula:** $P = V \cdot I \cdot \cos(\phi)$
        *   $V$: RMS voltage
        *   $I$: RMS current
        *   $\phi$: Phase angle between voltage and current
*   **Reactive Power (Q):** The power that oscillates between the source and the reactive components (inductors and capacitors). It is necessary for establishing magnetic and electric fields but does not perform useful work.
    *   **Unit:** Volt-Ampere Reactive (VAR)
    *   **Formula:** $Q = V \cdot I \cdot \sin(\phi)$
        *   In an inductive circuit, $Q$ is positive (lagging).
        *   In a capacitive circuit, $Q$ is negative (leading).
*   **Apparent Power (S):** The vector sum of real and reactive power. It is the total power supplied by the source.
    *   **Unit:** Volt-Amperes (VA)
    *   **Formula:** $S = V \cdot I$
    *   **Relationship:** $S^2 = P^2 + Q^2$ (Phasor diagram)

### 1.2. Power Factor (PF)

The power factor is the ratio of real power to apparent power. It represents the efficiency with which electrical power is being used.

*   **Formula:** $PF = \frac{P}{S} = \frac{P}{V \cdot I} = \cos(\phi)$
*   **Interpretation:**
    *   **PF = 1 (Unity):** All apparent power is real power. This is the ideal scenario. The phase angle ($\phi$) is 0 degrees.
    *   **PF < 1 (Lagging):** The current lags behind the voltage. This is typical of inductive loads (e.g., motors, transformers, transmission lines). The phase angle ($\phi$) is positive.
    *   **PF < 1 (Leading):** The current leads the voltage. This is typical of capacitive loads. The phase angle ($\phi$) is negative.

### 1.3. RL Series Circuit (Transmission Line Model)

A transmission line, especially at lighter loads or shorter lengths, can be approximated by a series RL circuit.

*   **Components:**
    *   **Resistance (R):** Represents the resistive losses in the conductors.
    *   **Inductance (L):** Represents the magnetic field generated by the current flowing through the conductors.
*   **Impedance (Z):** The total opposition to current flow in an AC circuit.
    *   **Formula:** $Z = R + jX_L$
        *   $X_L = 2\pi fL$ (Inductive Reactance)
        *   $f$: Frequency of the AC supply
    *   **Magnitude of Impedance:** $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2}$
*   **Phase Angle ($\phi$):**
    *   **Formula:** $\phi = \arctan\left(\frac{X_L}{R}\right)$
    *   In an RL circuit, the current **lags** the voltage, so $\phi$ is positive.

### 1.4. Power Factor Improvement

The goal of power factor improvement is to increase the power factor, typically towards unity, by compensating for the lagging reactive power drawn by inductive loads.

*   **Method:** Adding capacitors in parallel with the inductive load.
*   **Reasoning:** Capacitors provide leading reactive power, which cancels out the lagging reactive power drawn by the inductive load.
*   **Desired Outcome:** The net reactive power drawn from the source is reduced, leading to a higher power factor.

### 1.5. Power Triangle

The relationship between real power (P), reactive power (Q), and apparent power (S) can be represented by a right-angled triangle.

*   **For an inductive load:**
    *   Adjacent side: Real Power (P)
    *   Opposite side: Lagging Reactive Power ($Q_L$)
    *   Hypotenuse: Apparent Power (S)
    *   Angle between P and S: Phase angle $\phi$
    *   $PF = \cos(\phi) = \frac{P}{S}$

*   **After adding a capacitor:**
    *   The capacitor supplies leading reactive power ($Q_C$).
    *   The new net reactive power ($Q_{net}$) is $Q_{net} = Q_L - Q_C$.
    *   The new apparent power ($S_{new}$) and phase angle ($\phi_{new}$) are calculated using $P$ and $Q_{net}$.
    *   The new power factor is $PF_{new} = \cos(\phi_{new}) = \frac{P}{S_{new}}$.

## 2. Calculating the Power Factor of the Given RL Series Circuit

To calculate the power factor of a given RL series circuit, we need to determine the real power (P) and either the apparent power (S) or the phase angle ($\phi$).

### 2.1. Methods for Calculation

**Method 1: Using Measured Voltage, Current, and Power**

This is the most direct method in a lab setting.

1.  **Measure:**
    *   RMS Voltage across the circuit ($V$) using a voltmeter.
    *   RMS Current flowing through the circuit ($I$) using an ammeter.
    *   Real Power consumed by the circuit ($P$) using a wattmeter.
2.  **Calculate Apparent Power (S):**
    *   $S = V \cdot I$ (in VA)
3.  **Calculate Power Factor (PF):**
    *   $PF = \frac{P}{S}$ (This will be a lagging PF since it's an RL circuit).

**Method 2: Using Measured Voltage, Current, and Phase Angle (if available)**

1.  **Measure:**
    *   RMS Voltage across the circuit ($V$)
    *   RMS Current flowing through the circuit ($I$)
    *   Phase Angle ($\phi$) between voltage and current. This can be measured using an oscilloscope with appropriate probes or a power quality meter.
2.  **Calculate Power Factor (PF):**
    *   $PF = \cos(\phi)$

**Method 3: Using Circuit Parameters (R and L)**

If the values of resistance (R) and inductance (L) are known, and the supply voltage (V) and frequency (f) are known:

1.  **Calculate Inductive Reactance ($X_L$):**
    *   $X_L = 2\pi fL$
2.  **Calculate Impedance (Z):**
    *   $Z = R + jX_L$
    *   Magnitude of Impedance: $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2}$
3.  **Calculate Current (I):**
    *   $I = \frac{V}{|Z|} = \frac{V}{\sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2}}$
4.  **Calculate Real Power (P):**
    *   $P = I^2 \cdot R$
5.  **Calculate Apparent Power (S):**
    *   $S = V \cdot I$
6.  **Calculate Power Factor (PF):**
    *   $PF = \frac{P}{S} = \frac{I^2 \cdot R}{V \cdot I} = \frac{I \cdot R}{V} = \frac{I \cdot R}{I \cdot |Z|} = \frac{R}{|Z|} = \frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2}}$

**Example:**

Consider a transmission line model with:
*   Resistance, $R = 5 \Omega$
*   Inductance, $L = 50 \text{ mH} = 0.05 \text{ H}$
*   Supply Voltage, $V = 230 \text{ V}$ (RMS)
*   Frequency, $f = 50 \text{ Hz}$

**Calculation using Method 3:**

1.  **$X_L = 2\pi fL = 2 \pi (50 \text{ Hz}) (0.05 \text{ H}) = 15.71 \Omega$**
2.  **$|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2} = \sqrt{(5 \Omega)^2 + (15.71 \Omega)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 246.80} = \sqrt{271.80} = 16.49 \Omega$**
3.  **$I = \frac{V}{|Z|} = \frac{230 \text{ V}}{16.49 \Omega} = 13.95 \text{ A}$**
4.  **$P = I^2 \cdot R = (13.95 \text{ A})^2 \cdot 5 \Omega = 194.6 \cdot 5 = 973 \text{ W}$**
5.  **$S = V \cdot I = 230 \text{ V} \cdot 13.95 \text{ A} = 3208.5 \text{ VA}$**
6.  **$PF = \frac{P}{S} = \frac{973 \text{ W}}{3208.5 \text{ VA}} = 0.303$ (lagging)**

**Alternatively, using the formula:**
$PF = \frac{R}{|Z|} = \frac{5 \Omega}{16.49 \Omega} = 0.303$ (lagging)

**Phase Angle:**
$\phi = \arctan\left(\frac{X_L}{R}\right) = \arctan\left(\frac{15.71}{5}\right) = \arctan(3.142) = 72.35^\circ$ (lagging)
$PF = \cos(72.35^\circ) = 0.303$

## 3. Designing the Capacitance Required to Improve Power Factor

Once the initial power factor is calculated, we can design the capacitor bank to improve it to a desired level.

### 3.1. Objective

To reduce the lagging reactive power drawn from the source by adding a capacitor in parallel with the RL circuit.

### 3.2. Design Steps

Let:
*   $PF_{initial}$: Initial power factor (lagging)
*   $PF_{desired}$: Desired power factor (e.g., 0.95 or 0.98 lagging)
*   $P$: Real power (remains constant if only reactive compensation is added)
*   $Q_{L, initial}$: Initial lagging reactive power
*   $Q_{C}$: Leading reactive power supplied by the capacitor
*   $Q_{L, final}$: Final lagging reactive power after compensation

**Step 1: Calculate Initial Reactive Power ($Q_{L, initial}$)**

From the power triangle:
*   $Q_{L, initial} = P \cdot \tan(\phi_{initial})$
    *   Where $\phi_{initial} = \arccos(PF_{initial})$

**Step 2: Calculate Desired Reactive Power ($Q_{L, final}$)**

The desired power factor will have a new phase angle, $\phi_{desired}$.
*   $Q_{L, final} = P \cdot \tan(\phi_{desired})$
    *   Where $\phi_{desired} = \arccos(PF_{desired})$

**Step 3: Calculate the Required Capacitive Reactive Power ($Q_C$)**

The capacitor must supply the difference between the initial and final reactive power:
*   $Q_C = Q_{L, initial} - Q_{L, final}$
*   Substituting the formulas from Steps 1 and 2:
    $Q_C = P \cdot \tan(\phi_{initial}) - P \cdot \tan(\phi_{desired})$
    $Q_C = P (\tan(\phi_{initial}) - \tan(\phi_{desired}))$

**Step 4: Calculate the Capacitance Value (C)**

We know that for a capacitor:
*   $Q_C = V^2 / X_C$
    *   $X_C$ is the capacitive reactance.
*   We also know that $X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi fC}$.

Therefore:
*   $Q_C = V^2 \cdot (2\pi fC)$
*   Rearranging to solve for C:
    $C = \frac{Q_C}{V^2 \cdot 2\pi f}$

**Step 5: Calculate the New Apparent Power and Current (Optional, for verification)**

*   $S_{final} = \sqrt{P^2 + Q_{L, final}^2}$
*   $I_{final} = \frac{S_{final}}{V}$ or $I_{final} = \frac{P}{V \cdot PF_{desired}}$

---

### Example (Continuing from the previous example)

Given:
*   $R = 5 \Omega$, $L = 0.05 \text{ H}$, $V = 230 \text{ V}$, $f = 50 \text{ Hz}$
*   Initial calculations: $P = 973 \text{ W}$, $I_{initial} = 13.95 \text{ A}$, $PF_{initial} = 0.303$ (lagging)

**Goal:** Improve the power factor to $PF_{desired} = 0.95$ (lagging).

**Step 1: Calculate Initial Reactive Power ($Q_{L, initial}$)**
*   $\phi_{initial} = \arccos(0.303) = 72.35^\circ$
*   $Q_{L, initial} = P \cdot \tan(\phi_{initial}) = 973 \text{ W} \cdot \tan(72.35^\circ) = 973 \text{ W} \cdot 3.142 = 3057.7 \text{ VAR}$

*   *Verification:*
    $S_{initial} = V \cdot I_{initial} = 230 \text{ V} \cdot 13.95 \text{ A} = 3208.5 \text{ VA}$
    $Q_{L, initial} = \sqrt{S_{initial}^2 - P^2} = \sqrt{(3208.5)^2 - (973)^2} = \sqrt{10300872 - 946729} = \sqrt{9354143} = 3058.4 \text{ VAR}$ (Slight difference due to rounding)

**Step 2: Calculate Desired Reactive Power ($Q_{L, final}$)**
*   $\phi_{desired} = \arccos(0.95) = 18.19^\circ$
*   $Q_{L, final} = P \cdot \tan(\phi_{desired}) = 973 \text{ W} \cdot \tan(18.19^\circ) = 973 \text{ W} \cdot 0.3287 = 319.9 \text{ VAR}$

**Step 3: Calculate the Required Capacitive Reactive Power ($Q_C$)**
*   $Q_C = Q_{L, initial} - Q_{L, final} = 3057.7 \text{ VAR} - 319.9 \text{ VAR} = 2737.8 \text{ VAR}$

**Step 4: Calculate the Capacitance Value (C)**
*   $C = \frac{Q_C}{V^2 \cdot 2\pi f} = \frac{2737.8 \text{ VAR}}{(230 \text{ V})^2 \cdot 2\pi (50 \text{ Hz})}$
*   $C = \frac{2737.8}{52900 \cdot 314.16} = \frac{2737.8}{16616184} = 0.0001647 \text{ F}$
*   **$C \approx 164.7 \mu \text{F}$**

**Step 5: Calculate the New Apparent Power and Current (Verification)**
*   $S_{final} = \frac{P}{PF_{desired}} = \frac{973 \text{ W}}{0.95} = 1024.2 \text{ VA}$
*   $I_{final} = \frac{S_{final}}{V} = \frac{1024.2 \text{ VA}}{230 \text{ V}} = 4.45 \text{ A}$

**Conclusion for the example:** To improve the power factor from 0.303 to 0.95 lagging, a capacitor bank of approximately $164.7 \mu \text{F}$ connected in parallel with the RL circuit is required. This would reduce the current drawn from the source from 13.95 A to 4.45 A.

## 4. Practical Implementation in the Lab

### 4.1. Equipment Needed

*   AC Voltage Source (Variable)
*   Resistor (R)
*   Inductor (L)
*   Capacitors (various values or a variable capacitor)
*   Ammeter (to measure current)
*   Voltmeter (to measure voltage)
*   Wattmeter (to measure real power)
*   Oscilloscope (optional, for phase angle measurement)
*   Connecting wires

### 4.2. Procedure

1.  **Setup the RL Circuit:** Connect the resistor and inductor in series with the AC voltage source.
2.  **Measure Initial Parameters:**
    *   Set the voltage source to the desired operating voltage (e.g., 230V).
    *   Measure and record the voltage ($V$) across the series RL combination.
    *   Measure and record the current ($I$) flowing through the circuit using the ammeter.
    *   Measure and record the real power ($P$) consumed by the circuit using the wattmeter.
3.  **Calculate Initial Power Factor:** Use the measured $V$, $I$, and $P$ to calculate the initial power factor ($PF_{initial} = P / (V \cdot I)$).
4.  **Determine Desired Power Factor:** Decide on the target power factor (e.g., 0.95 lagging).
5.  **Design the Capacitor:**
    *   Calculate the initial reactive power ($Q_{L, initial} = P \cdot \tan(\arccos(PF_{initial}))$).
    *   Calculate the desired reactive power ($Q_{L, final} = P \cdot \tan(\arccos(PF_{desired}))$).
    *   Calculate the required capacitor reactive power ($Q_C = Q_{L, initial} - Q_{L, final}$).
    *   Calculate the required capacitance ($C = Q_C / (V^2 \cdot 2\pi f)$).
6.  **Connect the Capacitor:** Connect the calculated capacitor (or combination of capacitors) in parallel with the series RL circuit.
7.  **Measure Final Parameters:**
    *   With the capacitor connected, measure and record the new current ($I_{final}$) flowing from the source.
    *   Measure and record the new real power ($P_{final}$) consumed by the circuit (it should remain approximately the same as $P$).
    *   Measure and record the new apparent power ($S_{final} = V \cdot I_{final}$).
8.  **Calculate Final Power Factor:** Calculate the final power factor ($PF_{final} = P_{final} / S_{final}$) and compare it with the desired value.

## 5. Practice Questions and Answers

**Question 1:**
A series RL circuit has a resistance of $10 \Omega$ and an inductance of $0.05 \text{ H}$. It is connected to a $240 \text{ V}, 50 \text{ Hz}$ AC supply.
a) Calculate the inductive reactance.
b) Calculate the impedance of the circuit.
c) Calculate the current flowing through the circuit.
d) Calculate the real power consumed by the circuit.
e) Calculate the initial power factor.
f) If it is desired to improve the power factor to 0.95 lagging by adding a capacitor in parallel, what is the required capacitance?

**Answer 1:**
Given: $R = 10 \Omega$, $L = 0.05 \text{ H}$, $V = 240 \text{ V}$, $f = 50 \text{ Hz}$.

a) **Inductive Reactance ($X_L$):**
   $X_L = 2\pi fL = 2 \pi (50)(0.05) = 15.71 \Omega$

b) **Impedance (Z):**
   $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2} = \sqrt{10^2 + 15.71^2} = \sqrt{100 + 246.8} = \sqrt{346.8} = 18.62 \Omega$

c) **Current (I):**
   $I = \frac{V}{|Z|} = \frac{240 \text{ V}}{18.62 \Omega} = 12.89 \text{ A}$

d) **Real Power (P):**
   $P = I^2 R = (12.89 \text{ A})^2 (10 \Omega) = 166.15 \times 10 = 1661.5 \text{ W}$
   *(Alternatively: $\phi = \arctan(X_L/R) = \arctan(15.71/10) = 57.54^\circ$. $P = V I \cos(\phi) = 240 \times 12.89 \times \cos(57.54^\circ) = 240 \times 12.89 \times 0.5367 = 1661.6 \text{ W}$)*

e) **Initial Power Factor (PF):**
   $PF = \frac{P}{V \cdot I} = \frac{1661.5 \text{ W}}{240 \text{ V} \cdot 12.89 \text{ A}} = \frac{1661.5}{3093.6} = 0.537$ (lagging)
   *(Alternatively: $PF = \cos(57.54^\circ) = 0.537$)*

f) **Required Capacitance (C):**
   *   $PF_{initial} = 0.537 \implies \phi_{initial} = \arccos(0.537) = 57.54^\circ$
   *   $Q_{L, initial} = P \tan(\phi_{initial}) = 1661.5 \text{ W} \times \tan(57.54^\circ) = 1661.5 \times 1.563 = 2597.4 \text{ VAR}$
   *   $PF_{desired} = 0.95 \implies \phi_{desired} = \arccos(0.95) = 18.19^\circ$
   *   $Q_{L, final} = P \tan(\phi_{desired}) = 1661.5 \text{ W} \times \tan(18.19^\circ) = 1661.5 \times 0.3287 = 546.4 \text{ VAR}$
   *   $Q_C = Q_{L, initial} - Q_{L, final} = 2597.4 - 546.4 = 2051 \text{ VAR}$
   *   $C = \frac{Q_C}{V^2 \cdot 2\pi f} = \frac{2051 \text{ VAR}}{(240 \text{ V})^2 \cdot 2\pi (50 \text{ Hz})} = \frac{2051}{57600 \times 314.16} = \frac{2051}{18095616} = 0.0001133 \text{ F}$
   *   $C \approx 113.3 \mu \text{F}$

**Question 2:**
A purely inductive load draws $15 \text{ A}$ at a power factor of $0.6$ lagging from a $400 \text{ V}, 50 \text{ Hz}$ supply.
a) Calculate the real power consumed by the load.
b) Calculate the reactive power consumed by the load.
c) Calculate the apparent power of the load.
d) What value of capacitance is required to improve the power factor to $0.95$ lagging?

**Answer 2:**
Given: $I = 15 \text{ A}$, $PF_{initial} = 0.6$ (lagging), $V = 400 \text{ V}$, $f = 50 \text{ Hz}$.

a) **Real Power (P):**
   $P = V \cdot I \cdot PF_{initial} = 400 \text{ V} \cdot 15 \text{ A} \cdot 0.6 = 3600 \text{ W}$

b) **Reactive Power ($Q_{L, initial}$):**
   $\phi_{initial} = \arccos(0.6) = 53.13^\circ$
   $Q_{L, initial} = V \cdot I \cdot \sin(\phi_{initial}) = 400 \text{ V} \cdot 15 \text{ A} \cdot \sin(53.13^\circ) = 6000 \times 0.8 = 4800 \text{ VAR}$
   *(Alternatively: $Q_{L, initial} = P \tan(\phi_{initial}) = 3600 \text{ W} \times \tan(53.13^\circ) = 3600 \times 1.333 = 4798.8 \text{ VAR}$)*

c) **Apparent Power (S):**
   $S = V \cdot I = 400 \text{ V} \cdot 15 \text{ A} = 6000 \text{ VA}$
   *(Alternatively: $S = \sqrt{P^2 + Q_{L, initial}^2} = \sqrt{3600^2 + 4800^2} = \sqrt{12960000 + 23040000} = \sqrt{36000000} = 6000 \text{ VA}$)*

d) **Required Capacitance (C):**
   *   $PF_{desired} = 0.95 \implies \phi_{desired} = \arccos(0.95) = 18.19^\circ$
   *   $Q_{L, final} = P \tan(\phi_{desired}) = 3600 \text{ W} \times \tan(18.19^\circ) = 3600 \times 0.3287 = 1183.3 \text{ VAR}$
   *   $Q_C = Q_{L, initial} - Q_{L, final} = 4800 \text{ VAR} - 1183.3 \text{ VAR} = 3616.7 \text{ VAR}$
   *   $C = \frac{Q_C}{V^2 \cdot 2\pi f} = \frac{3616.7 \text{ VAR}}{(400 \text{ V})^2 \cdot 2\pi (50 \text{ Hz})} = \frac{3616.7}{160000 \times 314.16} = \frac{3616.7}{50265600} = 0.00007195 \text{ F}$
   *   $C \approx 71.95 \mu \text{F}$

## 6. Important Points to Remember

*   **Low power factor is detrimental:** It increases current, leading to higher I²R losses, reduced voltage regulation, and reduced system capacity.
*   **Inductive loads cause lagging power factor:** This is the most common scenario in industrial and transmission systems.
*   **Capacitors are used to improve lagging power factor:** They supply leading reactive power, counteracting the inductive reactive power.
*   **Real power (P) remains approximately constant** when power factor correction is applied using static capacitors.
*   **The power triangle is essential** for understanding the relationship between P, Q, and S and for performing calculations.
*   **The phase angle ($\phi$) is directly related to the power factor:** $PF = \cos(\phi)$.
*   **Always specify whether the power factor is lagging or leading.** For RL circuits, it's always lagging.
*   **In practice, capacitors are chosen from standard available ratings.** You might need to use a combination of capacitors to achieve the exact desired value.
*   **Over-correction should be avoided:** Connecting too much capacitance can lead to a leading power factor, which also has its own set of problems (e.g., overvoltage, resonance).
