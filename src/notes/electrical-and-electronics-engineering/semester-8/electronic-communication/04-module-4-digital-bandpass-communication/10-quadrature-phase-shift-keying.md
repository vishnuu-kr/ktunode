# Electronic Communication: Module 4 - Digital Bandpass Communication

## Topic: Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK)

---

### **Introduction**

Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK) is a digital bandpass modulation scheme that offers a more efficient way to transmit digital data compared to simple Phase Shift Keying (PSK) by utilizing two carrier signals in phase quadrature. This allows for the transmission of two bits of data simultaneously, effectively doubling the data rate for a given bandwidth. QPSK is a form of Phase Shift Keying (PSK) where the phase of the carrier wave is shifted by one of four possible values (0, π/2, π, 3π/2), with each phase shift corresponding to a specific combination of two bits.

---

### **1. Working Principle of QPSK**

QPSK modulates the phase of a carrier wave by one of four different values. These four phase values correspond to the four possible combinations of two bits.

*   **Data Grouping:** Incoming serial binary data is first grouped into pairs of bits (dibits).
*   **Dibit-to-Phase Mapping:** Each dibit is mapped to a specific phase shift. A common mapping is:
    *   00 -> 0 radians (0 degrees)
    *   01 -> π/2 radians (90 degrees)
    *   10 -> π radians (180 degrees)
    *   11 -> 3π/2 radians (270 degrees)
*   **Carrier Modulation:** A carrier signal, $s(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_c t)$, is modulated such that its phase $\phi$ is shifted according to the mapped dibit.

---

### **2. Mathematical Representation of QPSK**

A QPSK signal can be represented mathematically as:

$s(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_c t + \phi(t))$

where:
*   $A$ is the amplitude of the carrier wave.
*   $f_c$ is the carrier frequency.
*   $\phi(t)$ is the phase of the carrier wave, which changes in discrete steps corresponding to the dibits.

Alternatively, and more insightfully, a QPSK signal can be expressed as a sum of two PSK signals in phase quadrature:

$s(t) = d_1(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) - d_2(t) \sin(2\pi f_c t)$

where:
*   $d_1(t)$ and $d_2(t)$ are the two data streams generated by splitting the original serial binary data into two parallel streams.
    *   $d_1(t)$ carries the first bit of each dibit.
    *   $d_2(t)$ carries the second bit of each dibit.
*   The amplitude of $d_1(t)$ and $d_2(t)$ is typically $\pm \sqrt{E_b/T_b}$, where $E_b$ is the energy per bit and $T_b$ is the bit duration. For QPSK, since two bits are transmitted in $T_s = 2T_b$, the symbol energy $E_s = 2E_b$. The amplitude for QPSK symbols is $\pm \sqrt{E_s/T_s}$.

**Explanation of the two-component representation:**

The terms $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ and $-\sin(2\pi f_c t)$ represent two carrier signals that are orthogonal to each other.

*   When $d_1(t) = \sqrt{E_s/T_s}$ and $d_2(t) = 0$, the signal is $\sqrt{E_s/T_s} \cos(2\pi f_c t)$. This corresponds to a dibit like '00' (assuming a common mapping).
*   When $d_1(t) = 0$ and $d_2(t) = \sqrt{E_s/T_s}$, the signal is $-\sqrt{E_s/T_s} \sin(2\pi f_c t)$. This corresponds to a dibit like '01'.
*   When $d_1(t) = -\sqrt{E_s/T_s}$ and $d_2(t) = 0$, the signal is $-\sqrt{E_s/T_s} \cos(2\pi f_c t)$. This corresponds to a dibit like '10'.
*   When $d_1(t) = 0$ and $d_2(t) = -\sqrt{E_s/T_s}$, the signal is $\sqrt{E_s/T_s} \sin(2\pi f_c t)$. This corresponds to a dibit like '11'.

This representation is crucial for understanding the QPSK modulator and demodulator design.

---

### **3. QPSK Modulator**

A typical QPSK modulator consists of the following blocks:

1.  **Serial-to-Parallel Converter:** Splits the incoming serial data stream into two parallel streams, creating $d_1(t)$ and $d_2(t)$.
2.  **Dibit-to-Phase/Amplitude Converters (Data Inversion/Suppression):** These circuits manipulate the parallel data streams to generate the baseband signals that will multiply the carrier and quadrature carriers.
    *   For instance, if dibit '00' maps to 0 phase, it might result in $d_1(t) = +X$ and $d_2(t) = 0$.
    *   If dibit '01' maps to $\pi/2$ phase, it might result in $d_1(t) = 0$ and $d_2(t) = +X$.
    *   If dibit '10' maps to $\pi$ phase, it might result in $d_1(t) = -X$ and $d_2(t) = 0$.
    *   If dibit '11' maps to $3\pi/2$ phase, it might result in $d_1(t) = 0$ and $d_2(t) = -X$.
    (Where $X = \sqrt{E_s/T_s}$)
3.  **Carrier Generators:** Generate two carrier signals that are in phase quadrature, i.e., $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ and $\sin(2\pi f_c t)$.
4.  **Mixers (Multipliers):**
    *   The $d_1(t)$ signal is multiplied with the $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ carrier.
    *   The $d_2(t)$ signal is multiplied with the $\sin(2\pi f_c t)$ carrier.
5.  **Summer:** The outputs of the two mixers are added together to produce the final QPSK modulated signal.

**Block Diagram of a QPSK Modulator:**

```
     Serial Data Input
             |
             V
     Serial-to-Parallel Converter
        /         \
       /           \
      V             V
   (Bit 1)       (Bit 2)
      |             |
      V             V
   Data Mapping  Data Mapping
   (e.g., +/- X) (e.g., +/- X)
      |             |
      V             V
  d1(t)---------| Mixer 1 |-------+
                |        |       |
  Carrier cos(2πfct)------|       |
                           |       |------- Summation -------> QPSK Output Signal
  d2(t)---------| Mixer 2 |-------+
                |        |       |
  Carrier sin(2πfct)------|       |
                           |       |
```

### **4. QPSK Demodulator**

The QPSK demodulator aims to recover the original dibits from the received QPSK signal. It typically uses a coherent detection scheme.

1.  **Carrier Synchronization:** The receiver must have a local oscillator that is synchronized in frequency and phase with the incoming carrier.
2.  **Mixers:** The received signal is mixed with two locally generated carrier signals that are in phase quadrature: $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ and $\sin(2\pi f_c t)$.
    *   Received signal multiplied by $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$.
    *   Received signal multiplied by $\sin(2\pi f_c t)$.
3.  **Low-Pass Filters (LPFs):** After mixing, the output contains both the desired baseband signals and unwanted high-frequency components. LPFs are used to remove these high-frequency terms.
    *   The output of the first LPF will approximate $d_1(t)$.
    *   The output of the second LPF will approximate $d_2(t)$.
4.  **Decision Circuits (Comparators/Threshold Detectors):** These circuits compare the filtered outputs with a threshold level.
    *   If the output of the first LPF is positive, it's decided as '0' for the first bit; if negative, it's '1'.
    *   If the output of the second LPF is positive, it's decided as '0' for the second bit; if negative, it's '1'.
5.  **Parallel-to-Serial Converter:** The two parallel output bits are combined to reconstruct the original serial data stream.

**Block Diagram of a Coherent QPSK Demodulator:**

```
      QPSK Input Signal
             |
             |---------| Mixer 1 |-------+
                       |        |       |
   Carrier cos(2πfct)---|        |       |
                           |       |------- LPF 1 --------> Decision Circuit 1 ----> Bit 1
                           |       |                         (for d1(t))
      QPSK Input Signal    |       |
             |             |       |
             |---------| Mixer 2 |-------+
                       |        |       |
   Carrier sin(2πfct)---|        |       |
                           |       |------- LPF 2 --------> Decision Circuit 2 ----> Bit 2
                           |       |                         (for d2(t))
```

### **5. QPSK Constellation Diagram**

A constellation diagram visually represents the possible symbols in a digital modulation scheme. For QPSK, there are four possible symbols, each corresponding to a unique phase. The symbols are equally spaced around a circle in the complex plane.

*   Each point in the constellation represents a dibit.
*   The horizontal axis represents the In-phase (I) component (related to $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$).
*   The vertical axis represents the Quadrature (Q) component (related to $\sin(2\pi f_c t)$).

| Dibit | Phase (radians) | I Component | Q Component | Point in Complex Plane |
| :---- | :-------------- | :---------- | :---------- | :--------------------- |
| 00    | 0               | $\sqrt{E_s/T_s}$ | 0           | $(\sqrt{E_s/T_s}, 0)$  |
| 01    | $\pi/2$         | 0           | $\sqrt{E_s/T_s}$ | $(0, \sqrt{E_s/T_s})$  |
| 10    | $\pi$           | $-\sqrt{E_s/T_s}$| 0           | $(-\sqrt{E_s/T_s}, 0)$ |
| 11    | $3\pi/2$        | 0           | $-\sqrt{E_s/T_s}$| $(0, -\sqrt{E_s/T_s})$ |

*(Note: The mapping of dibits to phases can vary. The above is a common Grey coding example where adjacent symbols differ by only one bit.)*

**Example of a QPSK Constellation Diagram:**

Imagine a circle centered at the origin. There are four points on this circle:
*   On the positive real axis (0 degrees).
*   On the positive imaginary axis (90 degrees).
*   On the negative real axis (180 degrees).
*   On the negative imaginary axis (270 degrees).

These points are equidistant from the origin, meaning all QPSK symbols have the same amplitude. The key difference is their phase.

### **6. Performance of QPSK**

#### **6.1 Bandwidth Efficiency**

Bandwidth efficiency is a measure of how much data can be transmitted per unit of bandwidth. It is typically measured in bits per second per Hertz (bps/Hz).

*   **Data Rate:** $R_b$ bps.
*   **Bandwidth:** For QPSK, the required bandwidth is approximately $B \approx R_s = R_b/2$, where $R_s$ is the symbol rate. A common approximation is $B = R_s = R_b/2$ (one-sided bandwidth) or $2R_s = R_b$ (null-to-null bandwidth for raised cosine filtering). Using the Nyquist criterion, the minimum bandwidth required for $N$ bits per symbol is $B \ge R_s = R_b/N$. For QPSK, $N=2$.
*   **Bandwidth Efficiency ($\eta$) = Data Rate / Bandwidth**
    $\eta = R_b / B$

If we consider the minimum Nyquist bandwidth $B = R_b/2$, then for QPSK:
$\eta = R_b / (R_b/2) = 2$ bps/Hz.

This means QPSK is twice as bandwidth-efficient as BPSK (which has $\eta = 1$ bps/Hz).

#### **6.2 Power Efficiency (Bit Error Rate - BER)**

The power efficiency of a digital modulation scheme is determined by the Bit Error Rate (BER) as a function of the received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). For QPSK, the probability of bit error is approximately:

$P_b \approx Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E_b}{N_0}}\right)$

where:
*   $E_b$ is the energy per bit.
*   $N_0$ is the noise power spectral density.
*   $Q(x)$ is the Q-function, defined as $Q(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_x^\infty e^{-t^2/2} dt$.

**Key Point:** QPSK has the same BER performance as BPSK for the same energy per bit ($E_b$) if transmitted at the same bit rate. This is because QPSK effectively transmits two bits in parallel using two orthogonal carriers, and the decision for each bit is made independently based on its respective carrier component.

**Comparison with BPSK:**
If we compare QPSK with BPSK at the *same symbol rate*, QPSK would have twice the bit rate, meaning each symbol carries two bits instead of one. For the same bit rate ($R_b$), QPSK requires $E_b$ energy per bit, just like BPSK.

**Power Reduction:**
While the BER performance for a given $E_b$ is the same, QPSK can achieve the *same bit rate* as BPSK with *half the average transmitted power*. This is because for QPSK, $E_s = 2E_b$. The average transmitted power is proportional to $E_s$. If we compare QPSK transmitting at $R_b$ bps with BPSK transmitting at $R_b$ bps:
*   BPSK: Transmits 1 bit per symbol. Symbol duration $T_s = T_b$. Energy per symbol $E_s = E_b$.
*   QPSK: Transmits 2 bits per symbol. Symbol duration $T_s = 2T_b$. Energy per symbol $E_s = 2E_b$.
However, the energy per bit is $E_b$ for both.

The advantage of QPSK is that for a given bandwidth (which limits the symbol rate $R_s$), QPSK can transmit data at twice the bit rate ($R_b = 2 R_s$) compared to BPSK ($R_b = R_s$). This means QPSK is more bandwidth-efficient.

#### **6.3 Symbol Error Rate (SER)**

The symbol error rate (SER) is the probability that an entire symbol is received incorrectly. For QPSK, where each symbol carries two bits, a symbol error can result in one of several possible bit errors.

The SER for QPSK is approximately:
$P_s \approx 2 Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{E_s}{N_0}}\right)$

Since $E_s = 2E_b$, this becomes:
$P_s \approx 2 Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E_b}{N_0}}\right)$

This formula represents the probability that a symbol is in the wrong quadrant. For QPSK, it's known that $P_b = P_s / 2$ (assuming Grey coding), which leads to the BER formula above.

### **7. Types of QPSK**

While the basic QPSK described above is common, there are variations:

*   **Offset QPSK (OQPSK):** In OQPSK, the two data streams ($d_1(t)$ and $d_2(t)$) are staggered by one bit period ($T_b$). This means that the transitions in the two streams are never simultaneous.
    *   **Advantage:** Reduces the rate of phase changes, leading to a smoother envelope and reducing spectral spreading due to nonlinear amplification.
    *   **Modulator:** Requires a delay element for one of the data streams.
    *   **Demodulator:** Similar to standard QPSK, but with the data streams processed with the time offset.

*   **Differential QPSK (DQPSK):** In DQPSK, the dibit to be transmitted is encoded in the *change* of phase from the previous symbol, rather than the absolute phase.
    *   **Advantage:** Does not require carrier phase synchronization at the receiver, making it simpler to implement. It uses differential detection.
    *   **Disadvantage:** BER performance is slightly worse than coherent QPSK (typically by 1-3 dB).
    *   **Working:**
        *   A dibit is encoded as a phase difference.
        *   The modulator applies the phase difference to the carrier.
        *   The demodulator detects the phase difference between consecutive received symbols.

### **8. Applications of QPSK**

QPSK is widely used in various communication systems due to its good balance of bandwidth efficiency and implementation complexity.

*   **Satellite Communications:** Common in digital satellite TV broadcasting (e.g., DVB-S) and data transmission.
*   **Wireless LANs (Wi-Fi):** Used in older Wi-Fi standards (like 802.11b/g) for higher data rates.
*   **Cellular Communications:** Used in 2G (GSM) and some 3G systems.
*   **Modems:** Used in various data modems.
*   **Deep Space Communications:** Used by NASA for its deep space missions.

### **10. Important Points to Remember**

*   **Dibits:** QPSK transmits data in groups of two bits (dibits).
*   **Four Phases:** It uses four distinct phase shifts of the carrier.
*   **Bandwidth Efficiency:** 2 bps/Hz (twice that of BPSK).
*   **Power Efficiency:** Same BER as BPSK for the same $E_b/N_0$. Can achieve higher bit rates in the same bandwidth.
*   **Constellation Diagram:** Four points, equally spaced on a circle, representing the four dibits.
*   **Modulator:** Uses two quadrature carriers multiplied by two orthogonal data streams.
*   **Demodulator:** Typically coherent, using mixers with synchronized quadrature carriers.
*   **OQPSK:** Smoother envelope, better for non-linear amplifiers.
*   **DQPSK:** No carrier synchronization needed, but slightly worse BER.

### **11. Practice Questions and Answers**

**Question 1:** What is the primary advantage of QPSK over BPSK in terms of bandwidth?
**Answer:** QPSK offers twice the bandwidth efficiency of BPSK, achieving 2 bps/Hz compared to BPSK's 1 bps/Hz. This means QPSK can transmit data at twice the rate in the same bandwidth.

**Question 2:** How many bits are transmitted simultaneously in QPSK?
**Answer:** QPSK transmits data in dibits, meaning two bits are transmitted simultaneously.

**Question 3:** Describe the role of the mixers in a QPSK modulator.
**Answer:** The mixers multiply the two parallel data streams ($d_1(t)$ and $d_2(t)$) with the quadrature carrier signals ($\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ and $\sin(2\pi f_c t)$ respectively) to shift the data onto the carrier frequencies.

**Question 4:** If a QPSK receiver receives a signal corresponding to the point $(-\sqrt{E_s/T_s}, 0)$ in the complex plane, what dibit was likely transmitted? (Assume a standard mapping where 00 is at (A,0), 01 at (0,A), 10 at (-A,0), 11 at (0,-A), where $A = \sqrt{E_s/T_s}$).
**Answer:** The point $(-\sqrt{E_s/T_s}, 0)$ corresponds to the third quadrant of the complex plane (negative real axis). Based on the assumed mapping, this corresponds to the dibit '10'.

**Question 5:** What is the approximate required bandwidth for a QPSK signal transmitting at a bit rate of 1 Mbps?
**Answer:** For QPSK, the bandwidth efficiency is 2 bps/Hz. Therefore, Bandwidth = Bit Rate / Bandwidth Efficiency = 1 Mbps / 2 bps/Hz = 0.5 MHz (or 500 kHz).

**Question 6:** What is the main difference between OQPSK and standard QPSK, and what is its benefit?
**Answer:** In OQPSK, the two data streams are staggered (offset) by half a symbol period (or one bit period). This reduces the rate of phase changes, leading to a smoother envelope and less spectral spreading, which is beneficial for transmission through non-linear amplifiers.
