# Electromagnetic Theory: Module 2 - Magnetostatics

## Topic: Magnetostatics

This module introduces the fundamental laws governing magnetic fields produced by steady currents, focusing on Biot-Savart's Law and Ampere's Circuital Law. We will explore the magnetic field generated by various current distributions and delve into the concepts of magnetic potentials.

### 1. Biot-Savart's Law

**Key Concept:** Biot-Savart's Law provides a way to calculate the magnetic flux density ($\mathbf{B}$) produced by a steady current. It's analogous to Coulomb's Law for electric fields.

**Definition:** For a differential current element $Id\mathbf{l}$, the differential magnetic flux density $d\mathbf{B}$ at a point located by position vector $\mathbf{r}$ relative to the current element is given by:

$d\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{Id\mathbf{l} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{\mathbf{r}^2}$

where:
*   $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space ($\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ H/m).
*   $I$ is the current.
*   $d\mathbf{l}$ is the differential length vector of the current element, pointing in the direction of current flow.
*   $\mathbf{r}$ is the vector from the current element to the point where $\mathbf{B}$ is being calculated.
*   $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \frac{\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|}$ is the unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{r}$.

**Derivation (Conceptual):** Biot-Savart's Law can be viewed as a result of experimental observations, similar to Coulomb's Law. It quantifies the magnetic field contribution from a tiny segment of a current-carrying wire.

**Total Magnetic Field:** To find the total magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ at a point, we integrate the contributions from all current elements:

$\mathbf{B} = \int_{L} d\mathbf{B} = \int_{L} \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{Id\mathbf{l} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{\mathbf{r}^2}$

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   The direction of $\mathbf{B}$ is perpendicular to both $Id\mathbf{l}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, determined by the right-hand rule.
*   $\mathbf{B}$ is proportional to the current $I$.
*   $\mathbf{B}$ is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the current element.
*   The law is applicable for steady currents (magnetostatics).

### 2. Ampere's Circuital Law

**Key Concept:** Ampere's Circuital Law provides a convenient way to calculate magnetic flux density ($\mathbf{B}$) when the current distribution possesses symmetry. It relates the line integral of $\mathbf{B}$ around a closed path to the total current enclosed by that path.

#### 2.1 Integral Form

**Definition:** The line integral of the magnetic flux density $\mathbf{B}$ around any closed path $C$ is equal to $\mu_0$ times the total current $I_{enc}$ enclosed by the path:

$\oint_{C} \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$

where:
*   $C$ is a closed loop.
*   $d\mathbf{l}$ is a differential length vector along the path $C$.
*   $I_{enc}$ is the net current passing through any surface bounded by the closed path $C$.

**Derivation (Conceptual):** This law is derived from Biot-Savart's Law by considering current distributions with specific symmetries. It embodies the principle of superposition for magnetic fields.

**Application:** Ampere's Law is most useful when we can choose a path (Amperian loop) such that $\mathbf{B}$ is constant in magnitude and either parallel or perpendicular to $d\mathbf{l}$ along the path. This simplifies the integral.

**Textbook References:**
*   **Sadiku:** Chapter 5, Section 5.4
*   **Hayt & Buck:** Chapter 5, Section 5.2

#### 2.2 Point Form

**Definition:** The point form of Ampere's Circuital Law is obtained by applying Stokes' Theorem to the integral form:

$\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J}$

where:
*   $\nabla \times$ is the curl operator.
*   $\mathbf{J}$ is the current density (current per unit area, $\mathbf{J} = \rho_v \mathbf{v}$, where $\rho_v$ is charge density and $\mathbf{v}$ is velocity).

**Derivation:**
1.  Start with the integral form: $\oint_{C} \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$
2.  Use Stokes' Theorem: $\oint_{C} \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_{S} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$
3.  The enclosed current $I_{enc}$ can be expressed as the surface integral of the current density $\mathbf{J}$ over any surface $S$ bounded by $C$: $I_{enc} = \int_{S} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$
4.  Equating the two expressions: $\int_{S} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \mu_0 \int_{S} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$
5.  Since this holds for any arbitrary surface $S$, the integrands must be equal: $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J}$

**Important Points to Remember:**
*   The point form highlights that non-zero current density ($\mathbf{J}$) is the source of curl in the magnetic field.
*   This equation is one of Maxwell's equations for magnetostatics.

### 3. Magnetic Field due to Infinite Line Current

**Application of Biot-Savart and Ampere's Law:** This is a classic example demonstrating the power of Ampere's Law due to symmetry.

**Scenario:** An infinitely long, straight wire carrying a steady current $I$ along the z-axis.

#### 3.1 Using Ampere's Law

1.  **Symmetry:** The magnetic field lines will be concentric circles around the wire. The magnitude of $\mathbf{B}$ will be constant at a fixed radial distance from the wire.
2.  **Amperian Loop:** Choose a circular Amperian loop of radius $\rho$ in the xy-plane, centered on the wire.
3.  **Applying Ampere's Law:**
    *   $\oint_{C} \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$
    *   Along the circular loop, $\mathbf{B}$ is parallel to $d\mathbf{l}$ and constant in magnitude.
    *   $\oint_{C} B dl = B \oint_{C} dl = B (2\pi\rho)$
    *   The enclosed current $I_{enc}$ is simply the current $I$ flowing through the wire.
    *   Therefore, $B (2\pi\rho) = \mu_0 I$
    *   $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi\rho}$
4.  **Direction:** Using the right-hand rule, if the current is in the +z direction, the magnetic field points in the $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ direction (azimuthal direction).

**Result:** $\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi\rho} \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$

#### 3.2 Using Biot-Savart's Law (Conceptual - more complex)

This would involve integrating $d\mathbf{B}$ over the entire length of the infinite wire, which is mathematically more involved but yields the same result.
