# ENERGY STORAGE SYSTEMS

## Module 3: Types of Renewable Energy Sources: Wave, Wind, Tidal, Hydroelectric, Solar Thermal, and Photovoltaics

**Description:** This module explores various renewable energy sources, with a focus on how they relate to energy storage systems. We will examine wave, wind, tidal, hydroelectric, solar thermal technologies, and photovoltaics, understanding their fundamental principles and their synergy with energy storage.

**Learning Outcomes:**

*   Understand the principles of wave, wind, tidal, hydroelectric, solar thermal technologies, and photovoltaics.
*   Analyze the intermittent nature of these renewable sources and the need for energy storage.
*   Identify the role of energy storage in integrating these renewable sources into the power grid.
*   Compare the operational characteristics and energy storage requirements of different renewable energy sources.

### 1. Wave Energy

**1.1 Introduction**
Wave energy harnesses the kinetic and potential energy of ocean surface waves. It is a renewable and abundant resource, but its conversion and storage present significant technical challenges.

**1.2 Principles of Wave Energy Conversion**
Waves possess both kinetic energy (due to water particle motion) and potential energy (due to the elevation difference between crests and troughs). Devices convert this energy into mechanical motion, which is then typically used to drive a generator to produce electricity.

**1.3 Types of Wave Energy Converters (WECs)**

*   **Point Absorbers:** Buoyant structures that absorb energy from waves approaching from any direction. They can be tethered to the seabed or moored.
    *   *Example:* Pelamis wave energy converter.
*   **Attenuators:** Long, snake-like structures that float on the surface and flex at the joints as a wave passes. The flexing motion drives hydraulic pumps.
    *   *Example:* Pelamis.
*   **Terminators/Oscillating Water Columns (OWCs):** Structures that face the waves. As waves enter a chamber, they cause the water level to rise and fall, compressing and decompressing the air above it. This oscillating airflow drives a turbine.
    *   *Example:* LIMPET (Land Installed Marine Power Energy Transmitter).
*   **Overtopping Devices:** Structures that channel waves into a reservoir situated above sea level. The water then flows back to the sea through a conventional low-head hydropower turbine.
    *   *Example:* Wave Dragon.

**1.4 Energy Storage Considerations for Wave Energy**

*   **Intermittency and Variability:** Wave energy is subject to changes in wave height, period, and direction, making it inherently variable.
*   **Storage Needs:** Energy storage is crucial to smooth out the output of wave energy converters and provide a reliable supply of electricity when wave conditions are not optimal.
*   **Storage Technologies:**
    *   **Batteries:** Can be used for short-to-medium term storage.
    *   **Pumped Hydro Storage (PHS):** Can be a viable option if suitable elevated reservoirs can be constructed near wave farms.
    *   **Compressed Air Energy Storage (CAES):** Potentially applicable, especially if offshore OWCs can utilize compressed air storage.
*   **[Ter-Gazarian, 2011]:** Discusses the integration challenges of variable renewable sources like wave energy and the role of storage in managing this variability. Chapter 4 likely provides insights into this.
*   **[Díaz-González, Sumper, Gomis-Bellmunt, 2016]:** Likely covers the challenges of integrating variable renewables and the types of storage suitable for grid stabilization.

**1.5 Practice Question:**
Describe how an Oscillating Water Column (OWC) converts wave energy into electricity and explain why energy storage is essential for such a system.

**1.6 Answer:**
An OWC converts wave energy by capturing incoming waves into a chamber. The rising and falling water level within the chamber compresses and de-compresses the air above it. This oscillating airflow is then used to drive a bi-directional turbine, which is connected to a generator to produce electricity. Energy storage is essential because wave conditions are not constant. Fluctuations in wave height and frequency lead to intermittent power generation. Storage systems can smooth out these fluctuations, store excess energy when wave conditions are favorable, and release it when wave energy is low, thereby ensuring a more stable and reliable power supply.

### 2. Wind Energy

**2.1 Introduction**
Wind energy converts the kinetic energy of moving air into mechanical energy, which is then used to drive a generator for electricity production. It is one of the most mature and widely deployed renewable energy technologies.

**2.2 Principles of Wind Energy Conversion**
Wind turbines capture the kinetic energy of wind using rotor blades. The wind causes the blades to rotate, which in turn spins a shaft connected to a gearbox (in most designs) and then a generator.

**2.3 Types of Wind Turbines**

*   **Horizontal Axis Wind Turbines (HAWTs):** The most common type, with the rotor axis parallel to the ground.
    *   *Upwind Turbines:* Rotor is upstream of the tower.
    *   *Downwind Turbines:* Rotor is downstream of the tower.
*   **Vertical Axis Wind Turbines (VAWTs):** The rotor axis is perpendicular to the ground. Less common but can capture wind from any direction without yawing.
    *   *Darrieus Turbines:* Lift-based, egg-beater shape.
    *   *Savonius Turbines:* Drag-based, S-shaped rotor.

**2.4 Energy Storage Considerations for Wind Energy**

*   **Intermittency and Variability:** Wind speed is highly variable and unpredictable, leading to fluctuating power output.
*   **Storage Needs:** Energy storage is critical for grid integration of wind power to:
    *   **Firm capacity:** Provide a predictable and dispatchable power source.
    *   **Peak shaving:** Store excess energy during periods of high wind and low demand, and discharge it during peak demand.
    *   **Grid stability:** Provide frequency regulation and voltage support.
*   **Storage Technologies:**
    *   **Batteries (Lithium-ion, flow batteries):** Increasingly used for short-to-medium duration storage, offering fast response times.
    *   **Pumped Hydro Storage (PHS):** Large-scale storage option, suitable for regions with suitable geography.
    *   **Compressed Air Energy Storage (CAES):** Can store large amounts of energy for longer durations.
    *   **Flywheels:** Suitable for rapid, short-duration energy buffering.
    *   **Hydrogen Production:** Wind energy can be used to electrolyze water, producing hydrogen for later use in fuel cells or combustion.
*   **[Ter-Gazarian, 2011]:** Dedicates significant chapters to the integration of wind power and the role of various storage technologies in mitigating its variability. Chapters 5 & 6 are highly relevant.
*   **[Díaz-González, Sumper, Gomis-Bellmunt, 2016]:** Provides detailed analysis of wind power integration and the necessity of energy storage for grid stability and reliability.

**2.5 Practice Question:**
Discuss the primary challenges associated with integrating large-scale wind power into the grid and explain how energy storage can help overcome these challenges.

**2.6 Answer:**
The primary challenges of integrating large-scale wind power are its inherent variability and intermittency. Wind speed is not constant and can change rapidly, leading to fluctuating power output that doesn't always match electricity demand. This variability can cause grid instability, frequency deviations, and voltage fluctuations. Energy storage helps overcome these challenges by:
1.  **Smoothing Output:** Storing excess energy when wind speeds are high and discharging it when wind speeds are low, providing a more consistent power output.
2.  **Providing Ancillary Services:** Offering services like frequency regulation and voltage support, which are crucial for grid stability.
3.  **Dispatchability:** Enabling wind power to be dispatched when needed, even if the wind is not blowing, thus contributing to firm capacity.
4.  **Arbitrage:** Storing energy when electricity prices are low (due to high wind generation) and selling it when prices are high.

### 3. Tidal Energy

**3.1 Introduction**
Tidal energy harnesses the energy from the rise and fall of sea levels caused by the gravitational forces of the Moon and Sun. It is a predictable and consistent source of energy.

**3.2 Principles of Tidal Energy Conversion**

*   **Tidal Barrages:** Dams built across estuaries or bays. As the tide rises and falls, water flows through turbines in the barrage, generating electricity. This works on the principle of a hydroelectric dam.
*   **Tidal Stream Generators:** Underwater turbines, similar to wind turbines, placed in areas with strong tidal currents. These capture the kinetic energy of flowing water.

**3.3 Types of Tidal Energy Converters**

*   **Tidal Barrages:**
    *   *Impoundage (Ebb/Flood):* Water is impounded behind the barrage during high tide and released through turbines during low tide (ebb generation), or water is released during high tide (flood generation).
    *   *Run-of-Tide:* No barrage; turbines are placed in the tidal stream.
*   **Tidal Stream Generators:**
    *   *Horizontal Axis Turbines:* Similar to wind turbines, rotated by tidal flow.
    *   *Vertical Axis Turbines:* Can capture flow from any direction.
    *   *Oscillating Hydrofoils:* Blades that oscillate up and down like wings.

**3.4 Energy Storage Considerations for Tidal Energy**

*   **Predictability vs. Intermittency:** Tidal energy is highly predictable, but its generation is intermittent due to the cyclical nature of tides (typically two high and two low tides per day). The power output often peaks during ebb and flood cycles, with periods of zero generation at slack tide.
*   **Storage Needs:**
    *   **Smoothing Generation:** To provide a more continuous power supply and fill the gaps during slack tides.
    *   **Load Matching:** To shift generation to times of peak demand.
*   **Storage Technologies:**
    *   **Pumped Hydro Storage (PHS):** A very suitable technology, especially if barrages can incorporate pumped storage capabilities. The barrage itself can act as the upper reservoir, and a lower reservoir can be used.
    *   **Batteries:** For shorter durations to smooth output and provide ancillary services.
    *   **CAES:** Potentially applicable for larger-scale storage.
*   **[Ter-Gazarian, 2011]:** While wave energy is more extensively covered, the principles of managing intermittent but predictable sources would apply. Storage for such sources is discussed in relation to grid stability.
*   **[Díaz-González, Sumper, Gomis-Bellmunt, 2016]:** Likely discusses the integration of predictable but intermittent sources and the role of storage in improving their dispatchability.

**3.5 Practice Question:**
Explain the difference between tidal barrages and tidal stream generators and discuss the role of energy storage in improving the reliability of tidal energy generation.

**3.6 Answer:**
Tidal barrages are essentially dams built across estuaries. They work by impounding water at high tide and releasing it through turbines at low tide (or vice versa), similar to conventional hydropower. Tidal stream generators, on the other hand, are submerged turbines placed in areas with strong tidal currents. They capture the kinetic energy of the moving water directly, much like underwater wind turbines.

Energy storage is crucial for tidal energy to improve its reliability because, despite being predictable, tidal generation is intermittent. There are periods of slack tide when there is no water flow, resulting in zero power generation. Energy storage systems, such as pumped hydro storage (especially when integrated with barrages) or batteries, can store energy generated during peak tidal flow and discharge it during slack tides or periods of high electricity demand. This smooths out the power output, making tidal energy a more consistent and dispatchable source of electricity.

### 4. Hydroelectric Power

**4.1 Introduction**
Hydroelectric power is one of the oldest and most established forms of renewable energy. It converts the potential energy of water stored at a height into kinetic energy as it flows downwards, driving turbines to generate electricity.

**4.2 Principles of Hydroelectric Power Generation**
Water is impounded behind a dam, creating a reservoir. When electricity is needed, water is released from the reservoir through penstocks to drive turbines, which are connected to generators. The height difference (head) and the flow rate of water determine the power output.

**4.3 Types of Hydroelectric Power Plants**

*   **Impoundment Hydropower:** The most common type, utilizing dams and reservoirs.
*   **Run-of-River Hydropower:** Plants that use the natural flow of a river with little to no storage. They have a more continuous but often lower power output, dependent on river flow.
*   **Pumped Storage Hydropower (PSH):** Considered a major energy storage technology itself. It uses surplus electricity (often from other intermittent sources like wind or solar) to pump water from a lower reservoir to an upper reservoir. When demand is high, water is released from the upper reservoir back to the lower one through turbines, generating electricity.

**4.4 Energy Storage Considerations for Hydroelectric Power**

*   **Storage Capability:**
    *   **Reservoir Storage:** The primary form of energy storage for impoundment and pumped storage hydro. It allows for significant capacity and duration of energy storage.
    *   **Dispatchability:** Hydroelectric power, particularly with reservoirs, is highly dispatchable and can respond quickly to changes in demand.
*   **Role in Renewable Integration:**
    *   **Balancing Intermittent Renewables:** PSH is an excellent tool for storing excess energy from solar and wind and releasing it when needed, thus improving the grid integration of these variable sources.
    *   **Grid Stability:** Hydro plants can provide rapid response for frequency regulation and voltage control.
*   **[Ter-Gazarian, 2011]:** Chapter 3 extensively covers conventional and pumped-storage hydropower as key storage technologies, discussing their characteristics and applications in supporting grid stability and integrating renewables.
*   **[Díaz-González, Sumper, Gomis-Bellmunt, 2016]:** Similar to Ter-Gazarian, this book emphasizes PSH as a critical energy storage technology for renewable energy integration, detailing its operational modes and benefits.
*   **[Rastler, 2010]:** Likely categorizes PSH as a dominant large-scale storage technology with high capacity and duration.

**4.5 Practice Question:**
Explain how Pumped Storage Hydropower (PSH) functions as both a generator of electricity and an energy storage system.

**4.6 Answer:**
Pumped Storage Hydropower (PSH) operates in two main modes:
1.  **Generation Mode (Discharging):** When there is high electricity demand and/or low electricity prices (e.g., when solar or wind generation is abundant), water is released from an upper reservoir, flows through turbines, and generates electricity before returning to a lower reservoir.
2.  **Storage Mode (Pumping):** When there is low electricity demand and/or surplus electricity available (e.g., from intermittent renewables or off-peak grid power), this surplus energy is used to power pumps that move water from the lower reservoir back up to the upper reservoir, effectively storing potential energy.

Thus, PSH acts as an energy storage system by converting electrical energy into potential energy (stored water) and can then convert this potential energy back into electrical energy on demand. This makes it invaluable for balancing the grid and integrating variable renewable energy sources.

### 5. Solar Thermal Technologies

**5.1 Introduction**
Solar thermal technologies harness solar radiation to generate heat, which can then be used for various purposes, including electricity generation, water heating, and space heating.

**5.2 Principles of Solar Thermal Energy Conversion**

*   **Concentrating Solar Power (CSP):** Uses mirrors or lenses to concentrate sunlight onto a small area (receiver). The concentrated sunlight heats a fluid (e.g., oil, molten salt, water/steam), which is then used to produce steam to drive a turbine and generator.
*   **Solar Water Heating:** Uses solar collectors to absorb sunlight and heat water directly or indirectly (via a heat transfer fluid).

**5.3 Types of Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) Systems**

*   **Parabolic Troughs:** Long, curved mirrors focus sunlight onto a receiver tube running along the focal line. The fluid in the tube is heated.
    *   *Example:* The Solana Generating Station in Arizona.
*   **Solar Towers (Power Towers):** A field of mirrors (heliostats) tracks the sun and reflects sunlight onto a central receiver located at the top of a tower. The heat transfer fluid is often molten salt, which can reach very high temperatures.
    *   *Example:* Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System in California.
*   **Parabolic Dishes:** Dish-shaped reflectors focus sunlight onto a receiver at their focal point, typically powering a Stirling engine or a microturbine.
*   **Linear Fresnel Reflectors:** Use long, flat or slightly curved mirrors to focus sunlight onto a receiver tube.

**5.4 Energy Storage Considerations for Solar Thermal Technologies**

*   **Intermittency and Variability:** Solar radiation is intermittent (day/night cycle) and variable (cloud cover, seasonal changes).
*   **Storage Needs:**
    *   **Providing Power after Sunset:** CSP systems with thermal energy storage (TES) can continue to generate electricity for several hours after the sun has set.
    *   **Smoothing Output:** Storing heat to provide a more consistent power output during periods of fluctuating solar irradiance.
*   **Storage Technologies:**
    *   **Thermal Energy Storage (TES):** This is the primary storage method for CSP.
        *   **Molten Salt Storage:** Most common for solar towers and parabolic troughs. Molten salts (e.g., sodium nitrate and potassium nitrate) can store large amounts of heat at high temperatures. These systems typically use a two-tank system (hot and cold storage).
        *   **Sensible Heat Storage:** Storing heat by raising the temperature of a material (e.g., rocks, concrete, water).
        *   **Latent Heat Storage:** Storing heat during a phase change (e.g., solid to liquid).
    *   **Batteries:** Can be coupled with solar thermal plants to store electricity generated by the plant, providing electrical storage rather than thermal storage.
*   **[Ter-Gazarian, 2011]:** Likely discusses TES as a specific and effective storage solution for CSP plants, enabling them to provide dispatchable power.
*   **[Díaz-González, Sumper, Gomis-Bellmunt, 2016]:** Would cover CSP and its unique integration with TES, highlighting how it bridges the gap between intermittent solar resource and demand.

**5.5 Practice Question:**
Explain the concept of Thermal Energy Storage (TES) in the context of Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) and its advantages.

**5.6 Answer:**
Thermal Energy Storage (TES) in CSP systems involves storing the heat generated by concentrated solar radiation. In most CSP plants, this heat is used to heat a fluid (commonly molten salt). This heated fluid is then stored in insulated tanks. When electricity generation is needed, the hot fluid is drawn from the storage, used to produce steam to drive a turbine and generator, and then returned to a cold storage tank.

The main advantage of TES for CSP is that it allows the plant to continue generating electricity for several hours after the sun has set or during periods of cloud cover. This significantly improves the dispatchability and reliability of solar power, making it more competitive with traditional power sources and enabling it to contribute to grid stability even when direct solar irradiance is not available.

### 6. Photovoltaics (PV)

**6.1 Introduction**
Photovoltaics (PV) is a technology that converts sunlight directly into electricity using semiconductor materials. It is the most rapidly growing renewable energy technology globally.

**6.1 Principles of Photovoltaic Conversion**
PV cells, typically made from silicon, utilize the photovoltaic effect. When photons from sunlight strike the semiconductor material, they excite electrons, creating an electric current. A PV panel consists of multiple PV cells connected together.

**6.2 Types of Photovoltaic Technologies**

*   **Crystalline Silicon PV:** The most dominant technology, accounting for over 90% of the market.
    *   *Monocrystalline Silicon:* Made from a single silicon crystal; high efficiency.
    *   *Polycrystalline Silicon:* Made from multiple silicon crystals; slightly lower efficiency but generally cheaper.
*   **Thin-Film PV:** Uses thin layers of photovoltaic material deposited on a substrate.
    *   *Cadmium Telluride (CdTe):* High efficiency for thin-film.
    *   *Copper Indium Gallium Selenide (CIGS):* Flexible and efficient.
    *   *Amorphous Silicon (a-Si):* Lower efficiency but flexible and good in low light.
*   **Emerging PV Technologies:** Perovskites, organic PV, multi-junction cells.

**6.3 Energy Storage Considerations for Photovoltaics (PV)**

*   **Intermittency and Variability:** PV output is directly dependent on sunlight availability, meaning it is zero at night and varies significantly with cloud cover, time of day, and season.
*   **Storage Needs:** Energy storage is essential for PV systems to:
    *   **Provide Power at Night/Low Light:** Store excess daytime energy for use during evening peak demand or overnight.
    *   **Grid Stabilization:** Help manage the rapid ramps of PV generation and provide ancillary services.
    *   **Reduce Curtailment:** Store excess PV generation that might otherwise be curtailed (wasted) if the grid cannot absorb it.
    *   **Increase Self-Consumption:** Allow homeowners and businesses to use more of the solar energy they generate.
*   **Storage Technologies:**
    *   **Batteries (Lithium-ion, Lead-acid, Flow batteries):** The most common and rapidly growing storage solution for PV systems, ranging from residential to utility-scale. Lithium-ion batteries are particularly popular due to their high energy density and good performance.
    *   **Hydrogen Production:** PV electricity can power electrolyzers to produce hydrogen, which can be stored and used later in fuel cells or for other applications.
    *   **Thermal Storage (for PV-thermal hybrid systems):** Less common than electrical storage, but some hybrid systems can store heat generated by PV panels.
*   **[Ter-Gazarian, 2011]:** Chapter 7 discusses solar PV and its integration challenges, highlighting the critical role of batteries and other storage solutions in addressing its intermittency.
*   **[Díaz-González, Sumper, Gomis-Bellmunt, 2016]:** Provides a comprehensive overview of PV systems, their grid integration issues, and the significant contribution of battery storage systems to mitigate these challenges.
*   **[Denholm, Ela, Kirby, Milligan, 2010]:** This NREL report focuses on the role of energy storage, with PV being a prime example of a renewable source requiring storage for effective integration.
*   **[Rastler, 2010]:** Likely details various battery technologies and their suitability for PV applications.

**6.4 Practice Question:**
Why is battery energy storage particularly well-suited for mitigating the intermittency of photovoltaic (PV) power generation, and what are the key benefits it provides to the grid?

**6.5 Answer:**
Battery energy storage is well-suited for mitigating the intermittency of PV power generation because:
1.  **Fast Response:** Batteries can discharge and charge rapidly, allowing them to quickly absorb excess PV generation during sudden cloud cover changes or ramp up to meet demand when the sun sets.
2.  **Scalability:** Battery systems can be scaled from small residential units to large utility-scale installations, making them adaptable to various PV applications.
3.  **Modular Design:** They can be easily deployed and expanded as needed.
4.  **High Efficiency:** Modern battery technologies offer good round-trip efficiency.

Key benefits provided to the grid by battery storage coupled with PV include:
1.  **Increased Reliability:** Providing power during non-solar hours, smoothing output, and improving the overall reliability of the PV supply.
2.  **Grid Stability:** Supplying ancillary services like frequency regulation and voltage support, which are vital for maintaining grid stability, especially with high PV penetration.
3.  **Reduced Curtailment:** Storing excess PV energy that would otherwise be wasted when the grid cannot absorb it.
4.  **Peak Shaving:** Discharging during peak demand periods, reducing stress on the grid and potentially lowering electricity costs.
5.  **Improved Grid Integration:** Enabling higher penetrations of PV on the grid by managing its variable output.

### Important Points to Remember:

*   **Intermittency is Key:** The primary driver for energy storage with most renewable sources (wave, wind, solar PV, solar thermal) is their intermittent and variable nature.
*   **Predictability Matters:** Tidal and Hydroelectric power are predictable but still have intermittent generation patterns (slack tide, reservoir availability) that storage can help optimize.
*   **Storage Solutions are Source-Specific:** While some storage technologies are versatile (e.g., batteries), others are more closely tied to specific renewable sources (e.g., TES for CSP, PSH for hydro).
*   **Synergy:** Energy storage is not just about filling gaps; it's about creating synergy, enabling higher penetrations of renewables, and enhancing grid stability and reliability.
*   **Cost and Efficiency:** The economic viability and efficiency of storage technologies are crucial factors in their deployment alongside renewable energy.
