# Digital System Design - Module 2: ASM Chart and its Realization

## Topic: Races in ASM Charts

### 1. Introduction to Races in Sequential Circuits

Sequential circuits are susceptible to timing-related issues, especially when their state depends on the arrival of multiple signals. A "race condition" or "race" occurs when the final state of a sequential circuit is unpredictable because of the unequal propagation delays of different logic paths that affect the state transition. In the context of ASM charts, races can manifest during state transitions, leading to unintended behavior.

**Key Concept:** A race condition arises when two or more inputs or feedback paths intended to change the state of a flip-flop arrive at different times due to varying propagation delays.

**Relevance to ASM Charts:** ASM charts represent the behavior of a sequential circuit. The transitions between states in an ASM chart are triggered by input conditions. If these conditions involve multiple signals with different delays, or if the state feedback itself is subject to delays, races can occur.

**Knowledge Level:** This topic directly supports **CO1: Analyze asynchronous and clocked synchronous sequential circuits** by understanding the potential timing pitfalls in sequential logic design.

### 2. Types of Races in Sequential Circuits

While the term "race" is general, it can be further categorized based on how the signals interact. In the context of ASM charts and their realization, we can consider these types:

*   **Non-critical Race:** In a non-critical race, even if the inputs arrive at different times, the final state of the flip-flop will be the same, regardless of the order of arrival. This is generally not a problem.

*   **Critical Race:** This is the problematic type. In a critical race, the final state of the flip-flop depends on the order in which the inputs arrive. This leads to unpredictable behavior and is a major concern in sequential circuit design.

    *   **Example:** Consider a JK flip-flop. If both J and K inputs are supposed to change from 0 to 1 simultaneously, but due to delays, J becomes 1 slightly before K, the flip-flop might toggle. If K becomes 1 before J, it might remain in its current state (depending on the flip-flop's exact behavior). The final state is uncertain.

### 3. Races in ASM Chart Realization

When an ASM chart is translated into hardware, typically using flip-flops and combinational logic, the potential for races arises from:

*   **Combinational Logic Delays:** The logic gates that generate the next-state signals and output signals for the flip-flops have propagation delays. If multiple paths contribute to a next-state signal, variations in these delays can cause issues.
*   **Clock Skew:** In synchronous circuits, the clock signal might not arrive at all flip-flops at precisely the same instant. This difference is known as clock skew. While not directly a race within the combinational logic generating next states, it can affect when flip-flops transition, potentially interacting with combinational delays.
*   **Feedback Path Delays:** The signals that feedback from the output of flip-flops to the combinational logic also have delays.

**How Races Affect ASM Charts:**

Consider a transition in an ASM chart where the next state depends on an input condition `A` and `B`. If the combinational logic for `A` and `B` has different delays, and the transition to the next state is sensitive to the relative arrival times of `A` and `B`, a race can occur.

**Example Scenario:**

Suppose an ASM chart dictates a transition to State 2 if `Input1 = 1` AND `Input2 = 1`.

```
Current State: State 1
Input Condition: (Input1=1) AND (Input2=1)
Next State: State 2
```

If `Input1` is generated by a combinational path with a delay of $2\tau$ and `Input2` is generated by a path with a delay of $4\tau$, and both are fed into an AND gate that determines the transition to State 2.

*   If the flip-flop controlling the state transition is sensitive to the arrival of both signals at the same time, the delay difference can cause a race.
*   A more subtle race can occur if the next-state logic is designed in a way that if a signal momentarily glitches or changes state incorrectly due to delays before settling to its final value, the flip-flop might capture this incorrect intermediate value.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Wakerly, John F. "Digital Design."** Wakerly's book often emphasizes the importance of timing analysis in sequential circuit design. He would likely discuss how combinational logic delays directly impact the reliable operation of flip-flops and state machines, leading to race conditions.

### 4. Identifying and Preventing Races

Preventing races is crucial for reliable digital system design. Several techniques are employed:

#### 4.1. Hazard Elimination in Combinational Logic

Hazards are unwanted transient output changes in combinational circuits caused by differing delays of logic paths.

*   **Static Hazard:** A change in input should not cause the output to change.
*   **Dynamic Hazard:** A change in input should cause the output to change only once.
*   **Essential Hazard:** A single input change can cause the output to change multiple times before settling. These are the most problematic for sequential circuits.

**Prevention:**

*   **Static and Dynamic Hazards:** Can be eliminated by adding redundant gates to the Karnaugh map (K-map) minimization, ensuring that each input transition is covered by at least two product terms in the sum-of-products expression.
*   **Essential Hazards:** These are more complex and typically occur in circuits where the output is a function of multiple inputs that change simultaneously. Prevention often involves ensuring that the feedback paths in the sequential circuit stabilize before the next clock edge. This is achieved by adding delays to certain feedback paths or by careful design of the next-state logic.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Givone, Donald G. "Digital Principles & Design."** Chapter 4 on combinational logic circuits extensively covers hazards and their elimination using K-maps.
*   **Yarbrough, John M. "Digital Logic Applications and Design."** Yarbrough would also detail hazard identification and elimination, possibly with a focus on practical design considerations.

#### 4.2. Synchronous Design with Edge-Triggered Flip-Flops

The most robust method to avoid races is to design **fully synchronous sequential circuits**.

*   **Synchronous Design:** All state transitions occur only on the active edge (rising or falling) of a single, global clock signal.
*   **Edge-Triggered Flip-Flops:** These flip-flops sample their data inputs only at the clock edge. This means that any changes in the next-state logic due to input arrival times are only captured if they have stabilized by the clock edge.

**How it helps:**

In a synchronous system, the next-state logic must settle to its correct value within the setup time of the flip-flop before the clock edge arrives. If there are propagation delays in the next-state logic, they must be accounted for. The clock period is chosen to be longer than the longest combinational delay path plus the flip-flop's propagation delay and hold time.

**Example:**

Consider the ASM transition from State 1 to State 2: `(Input1=1) AND (Input2=1)`.
If `Input1` and `Input2` are generated by combinational logic, the output of the AND gate becomes the D input to a D flip-flop.

*   **Unsafe:** If the AND gate output is directly connected to a level-sensitive latch, and `Input1` and `Input2` change asynchronously, the latch could transition multiple times.
*   **Safe:** If the AND gate output is connected to the D input of an edge-triggered D flip-flop, and the clock period is long enough for the AND gate output to stabilize after `Input1` and `Input2` change, the flip-flop will capture the correct next state on the clock edge.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Mano & Ciletti, "Digital Design with an introduction to HDL, VHDL and Verilog."** This book emphasizes synchronous design principles throughout, particularly in its coverage of VHDL and Verilog, which are inherently geared towards synchronous design.
*   **Wakerly, John F. "Digital Design."** Wakerly's approach strongly advocates for synchronous design to ensure predictable behavior and ease of analysis, directly addressing the problem of races.

#### 4.3. Ensuring Setup and Hold Times

Edge-triggered flip-flops have critical timing requirements:

*   **Setup Time ($t_{su}$):** The data input must be stable for a certain minimum time *before* the clock edge.
*   **Hold Time ($t_h$):** The data input must remain stable for a certain minimum time *after* the clock edge.

**Preventing Races via Timing:**

*   **Longer Clock Period:** A sufficiently long clock period ensures that the combinational logic producing the next-state signals has enough time to settle before the clock edge, satisfying the setup time.
*   **Careful Layout and Routing:** In physical design, minimizing wire lengths and avoiding signal contention can reduce propagation delays and clock skew, thereby mitigating race conditions.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Givone, Donald G. "Digital Principles & Design."** Chapter 5 or later chapters on sequential circuit analysis will detail flip-flop timing parameters.
*   **Mano & Ciletti, "Digital Design with an introduction to HDL, VHDL and Verilog."** Timing analysis, including setup and hold times, is a core concept for reliable sequential circuit design in their text.

#### 4.4. Using Delay Elements (Less Common in Modern Design)

In older or specific asynchronous designs, designers might strategically insert delay elements into feedback paths to equalize signal arrival times and prevent races. However, this approach can make timing analysis complex and is generally avoided in favor of synchronous design.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Biswas, N. N. "Logic Design Theory."** Theoretical texts might discuss such techniques as part of a comprehensive treatment of sequential circuit design, including asynchronous approaches.

### 5. Races in ASM Chart Examples and Analysis

Let's consider an ASM chart and how a race might manifest during its hardware realization.

**ASM Chart Fragment:**

```
+-----------+
| State S0  |
| Output Y=0|
+-----------+
    |
    | Condition: X = 1
    v
+-----------+
| State S1  |
| Output Y=1|
+-----------+
    |
    | Condition: X = 0
    v
+-----------+
| State S0  |
| Output Y=0|
+-----------+
```

**Realization with a D Flip-Flop:**

Let the state S0 be represented by a logic value of '0' and S1 by '1'. The D input to the flip-flop will determine the next state.

*   **Next State Logic for S1:** $D = X$ (when in S0, if X=1, go to S1)
*   **Next State Logic for S0:** $D = 0$ (when in S1, if X=0, go to S0)

This can be combined: $D = S \oplus X$, where $S$ is the current state encoded by the flip-flop. Let's simplify and assume state encoding is directly $S$. So, if state is S0 (represented by 0) and X=1, D=1. If state is S1 (represented by 1) and X=0, D=0.

If we use a D flip-flop, the next state is given by the output of the combinational logic that computes $D$. Let's say the flip-flop is at the output of a logic gate.

**Scenario Leading to a Race:**

Suppose the state is S0 (flip-flop output $Q=0$). The input $X$ arrives.
*   If $X$ changes from 0 to 1.
*   The combinational logic for $D$ calculates $D=1$.

Now, consider the transition from S1 back to S0. State is S1 ($Q=1$). Input $X$ changes from 1 to 0.
*   The combinational logic for $D$ calculates $D=0$.

A race can occur if the flip-flop is sensitive to input changes other than the clock edge, or if there are internal feedback loops within the combinational logic that cause oscillations.

**More complex race example:**

Consider a state transition where the next state depends on two inputs, $I_1$ and $I_2$, which are generated by separate logic blocks.

```
ASM Chart:
State A
  | Condition: I1 AND I2
  v
State B
```

Hardware realization:
*   `I1` is output of Logic Block 1 (delay $T_{L1}$)
*   `I2` is output of Logic Block 2 (delay $T_{L2}$)
*   An AND gate combines `I1` and `I2` to produce the $D$ input for a flip-flop.

If $T_{L1} \neq T_{L2}$:
When `I1` and `I2` are both supposed to become '1', the AND gate output will depend on which signal arrives first. If $T_{L1} < T_{L2}$, `I1` becomes '1' first. If the AND gate is sensitive at this moment, its output might momentarily be '0' (if the flip-flop is still in a state where a '0' is latched) before `I2` arrives and the output becomes '1'. If the flip-flop captures this intermediate '0', it could lead to an incorrect state.

**How to prevent this specific race:**

1.  **Synchronous Design:** Ensure the inputs `I1` and `I2` are sampled by flip-flops at the clock edge. The combinational logic for the next state then uses these stable flip-flop outputs. The entire next-state logic (Logic Block 1, Logic Block 2, AND gate) must settle within the clock period before the next clock edge.
2.  **Hazard Elimination:** Ensure the combinational logic generating `I1` and `I2` is hazard-free for static and dynamic hazards.
3.  **Equalize Delays:** While less common, one could add delay elements to Logic Block 1 to match the delay of Logic Block 2, ensuring `I1` and `I2` arrive at the AND gate simultaneously. This is complex and less preferred.

**Textbook Reference:**
*   **Wakerly, John F. "Digital Design."** Wakerly is known for his clear explanations of timing diagrams and critical path analysis, which would be used to identify such races.
*   **Mano & Ciletti, "Digital Design with an introduction to HDL, VHDL and Verilog."** Their HDL examples implicitly show how to structure designs to avoid these issues by relying on clocked assignments.

### 6. Races in Asynchronous vs. Synchronous Circuits

The primary difference is where races are most problematic:

*   **Synchronous Circuits (Clocked):** Races are generally **prevented** by using edge-triggered flip-flops and ensuring all combinational logic settles within the clock period. If a race condition exists, it's usually due to improper design (e.g., not meeting setup/hold times, incorrect clocking, or allowing asynchronous inputs to directly control state).
*   **Asynchronous Circuits (Clockless):** Races are **inherent** potential problems because state transitions are directly triggered by the arrival of input signals. The design of asynchronous circuits relies heavily on techniques like delay elements and specific logic structures (e.g., Huffman's method for state minimization) to manage and avoid races.

**ASM Charts and their realization:**

ASM charts are a behavioral description. Their realization can be synchronous or asynchronous.
*   **Synchronous Realization:** The standard approach where races are managed by the clock.
*   **Asynchronous Realization:** Less common, but possible. If an ASM chart is realized asynchronously, then the analysis of races becomes much more critical, similar to general asynchronous sequential circuit design.

**Course Outcomes Alignment:**
*   **CO1 (Analyze asynchronous and clocked synchronous sequential circuits):** Understanding races is fundamental to analyzing the behavior and potential failures of both types of sequential circuits.
*   **CO2 (Design hazard-free digital circuits):** Hazards in combinational logic are a direct cause of races in sequential circuits. Therefore, designing hazard-free circuits is a preventative measure.

### 7. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1:**
Define a "race condition" in the context of sequential circuit design. What is the difference between a critical and a non-critical race?

**Answer:**
A race condition occurs when the final state of a sequential circuit is unpredictable due to unequal propagation delays of signals that determine the state.
*   **Critical Race:** The final state depends on the order in which signals arrive.
*   **Non-critical Race:** The final state is the same regardless of signal arrival order.

**Question 2:**
List two common causes of race conditions when realizing an ASM chart in hardware.

**Answer:**
1.  **Unequal propagation delays in combinational logic paths** that generate next-state signals.
2.  **Clock skew** (though this affects when flip-flops capture data rather than signal arrival at combinational logic inputs directly, it can interact with delays).

**Question 3:**
How does using edge-triggered flip-flops and a synchronous clock help prevent race conditions?

**Answer:**
Edge-triggered flip-flops sample data only at the clock edge. By ensuring that all combinational logic producing the next-state signals settles to its correct value *before* the clock edge (within the setup time), and that the clock period is long enough to accommodate these delays, the flip-flop captures a stable, correct next state, thus preventing races.

**Question 4:**
Consider a simple ASM transition where a state change occurs if $Input_A = 1$ and $Input_B = 1$. $Input_A$ is generated by a path with delay $5$ ns, and $Input_B$ by a path with delay $8$ ns. Both are fed into an AND gate that controls the $D$ input of a flip-flop. If the clock period is $20$ ns, and the flip-flop requires $3$ ns setup time and $1$ ns hold time.
a) Will a race condition likely occur if this is the only factor?
b) How can you ensure this transition is safe?

**Answer:**
a) Yes, a race condition is likely. When $Input_A$ and $Input_B$ are both supposed to become 1, $Input_A$ will arrive at the AND gate first (after 5 ns), and $Input_B$ will arrive later (after 8 ns). If the AND gate output is sensitive during this interval, and the flip-flop is sensitive to the changes, an incorrect state could be captured if the signals don't settle simultaneously before the clock edge. The delay difference is 3 ns.

b) To ensure this transition is safe:
1.  **Synchronous Design:** Use edge-triggered flip-flops and ensure the entire combinational path (including the generation of $Input_A$ and $Input_B$ and the AND gate) settles within the clock period before the next clock edge.
    *   Total delay for next-state logic: (Max delay of $Input_B$ generation) + (AND gate delay) + (Flip-flop propagation delay) $\le$ Clock Period - Setup Time.
    *   Assuming a reasonable AND gate delay (e.g., 2 ns) and flip-flop propagation delay (e.g., 5 ns):
        $8 \text{ ns} + 2 \text{ ns} + 5 \text{ ns} = 15 \text{ ns}$.
    *   This must be less than $20 \text{ ns} - 3 \text{ ns} = 17 \text{ ns}$. Since $15 \text{ ns} < 17 \text{ ns}$, a 20 ns clock period is likely sufficient.
2.  **Hazard Elimination:** Ensure the logic generating $Input_A$ and $Input_B$ is hazard-free.
3.  **Equalize delays:** Add a 3 ns delay to the path generating $Input_A$ to match $Input_B$'s delay. This is a less preferred method.

**Question 5:**
What is an "essential hazard" and how does it relate to races in sequential circuits?

**Answer:**
An essential hazard is a condition in a combinational circuit where a single input change can cause the output to change multiple times before settling. This is particularly problematic for sequential circuits because if these transient changes occur within the active window of a latch or before the clock edge for a flip-flop, they can lead to a critical race condition, causing the circuit to enter an incorrect state.

**Important Points to Remember:**

*   **Synchronous design is key:** Employing a single clock and edge-triggered flip-flops is the most effective way to manage timing and prevent races.
*   **Timing analysis is crucial:** Always consider setup and hold times, propagation delays of combinational logic, and clock skew.
*   **Hazards must be eliminated:** Hazards in combinational logic directly contribute to race conditions in sequential circuits.
*   **ASM charts are behavioral:** The realization of an ASM chart determines whether races become an issue.
*   **Asynchronous circuits require meticulous delay management:** Races are a fundamental concern in clockless designs.
