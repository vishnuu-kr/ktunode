## 1. Introduction to Vehicle Energy Sources

Vehicles require energy to overcome forces such as rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag, and to provide acceleration. The **energy source technology** refers to the system that stores and delivers this energy to the vehicle's propulsion system.

### 1.1. Conventional Vehicle Energy Sources: The Internal Combustion Engine (ICE)

Conventional vehicles primarily rely on **Internal Combustion Engines (ICEs)**, which convert the chemical energy stored in fuel into mechanical energy to drive the wheels.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Internal Combustion Engine (ICE):** A heat engine where the combustion of a fuel occurs with an oxidizer (usually air) in a combustion chamber that is an integral part of the working fluid flow circuit. The expansion of the high-temperature and high-pressure gases produced by combustion applies direct force to some component of the engine, such as pistons or turbine blades.
*   **Fuel:** Typically a hydrocarbon-based fuel like gasoline (petrol) or diesel.
*   **Combustion:** A rapid chemical reaction between a substance and an oxidant, usually accompanied by the production of heat and light.
*   **Mechanical Energy:** Energy derived from the motion of objects. In an ICE, this is typically rotational energy from the crankshaft.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Chau, K. T. (2015). *Electric Vehicles Machines and Drives- Design, Analysis and Application*.** While primarily focused on EVs, Chapter 1 and early sections often provide context on conventional powertrains for comparison.
*   **Miller, J. M. (2010). *Propulsion Systems for Hybrid Vehicles*.** This book extensively details ICE technology as it forms a significant part of hybrid powertrains, providing a strong basis for understanding its performance characteristics.

### 1.2. Energy Storage and Delivery

The energy stored in the fuel needs to be efficiently delivered to the engine.

**Key Components:**

*   **Fuel Tank:** Stores the liquid fuel.
*   **Fuel Pump:** Delivers fuel from the tank to the engine.
*   **Fuel Injectors (or Carburetor):** Mixes fuel with air in the correct ratio for combustion.
*   **Air Intake System:** Provides air for combustion.

**Example:**

A typical gasoline car has a fuel tank made of plastic or metal, a fuel pump that pressurizes the fuel, and fuel injectors that spray atomized fuel into the engine cylinders at precisely timed intervals.

### 1.3. ICE Working Principles (Four-Stroke Cycle)

The most common ICE configuration is the four-stroke cycle:

1.  **Intake Stroke:** The piston moves down, and the intake valve opens, drawing a mixture of fuel and air into the cylinder.
2.  **Compression Stroke:** Both valves close, and the piston moves up, compressing the fuel-air mixture.
3.  **Power (Combustion) Stroke:** The spark plug ignites the compressed mixture, causing a rapid expansion of gases that pushes the piston down, generating mechanical power.
4.  **Exhaust Stroke:** The exhaust valve opens, and the piston moves up, pushing the burnt gases out of the cylinder.

### 1.4. Energy Density and Power Density of Conventional Fuels

When comparing energy sources, two key metrics are energy density and power density.

**Key Concepts & Definitions:**

*   **Energy Density:** The amount of energy stored per unit of volume (volumetric energy density) or per unit of mass (gravimetric energy density). Higher energy density means more energy can be stored in a given space or weight.
*   **Power Density:** The amount of power that can be delivered per unit of volume (volumetric power density) or per unit of mass (gravimetric power density). Higher power density allows for quicker acceleration and higher peak performance.

**Comparison (Illustrative Values):**

| Energy Source | Gravimetric Energy Density (MJ/kg) | Volumetric Energy Density (MJ/L) | Typical Power Density (kW/kg for engine) |
| :------------ | :--------------------------------- | :------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| Gasoline      | ~44                                | ~32                               | 1-2 (for ICE)                            |
| Diesel        | ~45                                | ~37                               | 1-1.5 (for ICE)                          |
| **Electric Vehicle Battery (Lithium-ion)** | **~0.5 - 1**                       | **~1 - 2.5**                      | **1-5+ (for electric motor)**            |

**Important Note:** These are simplified values for comparison. Actual values can vary significantly based on specific formulations and battery technologies.

*   **Mi, C., Masrur, M. A., Gao, D. W. (2011). *Hybrid Electric Vehicles – Principles and applications with practical perspectives*.** This book will provide comparative data between ICEs and electric propulsion systems.

### 1.5. Performance Characteristics of ICE Vehicles

The performance of a conventional vehicle is heavily influenced by its energy source and powertrain.

**Key Aspects:**

*   **Torque Curve:** The relationship between engine speed (RPM) and torque output. ICEs typically produce peak torque in a specific RPM range.
*   **Horsepower:** A measure of the rate at which work is done, often associated with top speed.
*   **Fuel Efficiency:** Measured in miles per gallon (MPG) or kilometers per liter (km/L), indicating how much fuel is consumed to travel a certain distance.
*   **Emissions:** The byproducts of combustion, including greenhouse gases (CO2) and pollutants (NOx, particulate matter), are a significant drawback.

**Example:**

A sports car engine might be designed for high horsepower and a broad torque band for strong acceleration across a wide range of speeds. A truck engine, conversely, might be optimized for high torque at low RPMs for towing heavy loads.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Hussein, I. (2003). *Electric and Hybrid Vehicles: Design Fundamentals*.** This book will likely touch upon the performance metrics of conventional vehicles as a point of reference.

### 1.6. Limitations and Environmental Impact of ICE Energy Sources

While ICEs have powered transportation for over a century, they have significant drawbacks.

**Limitations:**

*   **Lower Energy Conversion Efficiency:** A large portion of the fuel's chemical energy is lost as heat. Typical ICE efficiency is around 20-30%.
*   **Dependence on Fossil Fuels:** Primarily rely on finite and non-renewable resources.
*   **Emissions:** Contribute to air pollution and climate change.
*   **Noise and Vibration:** ICEs are inherently noisy and produce vibrations.
*   **Maintenance:** Complex mechanical systems require regular maintenance.

**Environmental Impact:**

*   **Greenhouse Gas Emissions:** Release of CO2, a major contributor to global warming.
*   **Air Pollution:** Emission of NOx, SOx, particulate matter, and carbon monoxide, which are harmful to human health and the environment.
*   **Resource Depletion:** Reliance on fossil fuels that are finite resources.

**Textbook Reference:**

*   **Chau, K. T. (2015). *Electric Vehicles Machines and Drives- Design, Analysis and Application*.** Chapter 1 will likely highlight the environmental drivers for EV adoption.
*   **Mi, C., Masrur, M. A., Gao, D. W. (2011). *Hybrid Electric Vehicles – Principles and applications with practical perspectives*.** Discusses the motivation for hybrid and electric powertrains, often driven by emissions concerns.

## 2. Key Concepts and Definitions Summary

*   **Internal Combustion Engine (ICE):** Converts chemical energy of fuel into mechanical energy through combustion within the engine.
*   **Energy Density:** Energy stored per unit of mass or volume.
*   **Power Density:** Power delivered per unit of mass or volume.
*   **Four-Stroke Cycle:** The common operating cycle of gasoline and diesel engines (Intake, Compression, Power, Exhaust).
*   **Torque:** Rotational force produced by the engine.
*   **Horsepower:** Rate of work done.

## 3. Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1 (K2):**

What is the primary function of an internal combustion engine in a conventional vehicle?

**Answer:** The primary function of an internal combustion engine is to convert the chemical energy stored in fuel (like gasoline or diesel) into mechanical energy (rotational force) that can be used to propel the vehicle.

**Question 2 (K2):**

List the four strokes of a typical four-stroke internal combustion engine in their correct order.

**Answer:** The four strokes are: Intake, Compression, Power (or Combustion), and Exhaust.

**Question 3 (K2):**

Define "energy density" in the context of vehicle energy sources.

**Answer:** Energy density refers to the amount of energy stored in a given system or region of space, typically expressed as energy per unit mass (gravimetric energy density) or energy per unit volume (volumetric energy density).

**Question 4 (K2):**

Besides CO2, name two other significant pollutants emitted by conventional internal combustion engines.

**Answer:** Other significant pollutants include Nitrogen Oxides (NOx), Sulfur Oxides (SOx), Carbon Monoxide (CO), and particulate matter.

**Question 5 (K2):**

Compare the typical energy conversion efficiency of a gasoline ICE with that of an electric motor. (Requires prior knowledge from the module or research)

**Answer:** A typical gasoline ICE has an energy conversion efficiency of around 20-30%, meaning only 20-30% of the fuel's energy is converted into mechanical work. Electric motors, on the other hand, have much higher efficiencies, typically in the range of 85-95%.

## 4. Important Points to Remember

*   **ICEs are the backbone of conventional vehicles:** Understanding their operation and limitations is crucial for appreciating the advantages of EVs.
*   **Energy density vs. Power density:** These metrics are key for comparing different energy storage technologies. Conventional fuels excel in energy density, allowing for long ranges and quick refueling.
*   **The four-stroke cycle is fundamental:** Knowing the sequence of events within an ICE cylinder is essential.
*   **Environmental impact is a major driver for EV adoption:** The emissions from ICEs are a significant concern.
*   **Efficiency matters:** The lower efficiency of ICEs means more energy is wasted as heat compared to electric powertrains.
