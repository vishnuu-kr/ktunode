# POWER ELECTRONICS AND DRIVES LAB - MODULE 2: HIGH FREQUENCY DIODE

## Topic: High Frequency Diode - Measurement of Power Loss and Reverse Recovery Time

---

### **1. Introduction to High-Frequency Diodes**

High-frequency diodes are specialized semiconductor devices designed to operate efficiently at switching frequencies significantly higher than standard rectifier diodes. Their performance characteristics, particularly power loss and reverse recovery time, become critical in high-frequency applications like switch-mode power supplies (SMPS), resonant converters, and high-frequency inverters.

**Key Concepts:**

*   **Switching Frequency:** The rate at which a diode is turned on and off. High-frequency diodes are designed for frequencies in the tens of kHz to MHz range.
*   **Power Loss:** The energy dissipated by the diode during its operation, contributing to heating and reduced efficiency.
*   **Reverse Recovery Time ($t_{rr}$):** The time it takes for a diode to transition from a conducting state (forward biased) to a non-conducting state (reverse biased) after the voltage across it reverses. This is a crucial parameter affecting switching losses.

**Importance in Power Electronics:**

*   **Efficiency:** Minimizing power loss is paramount for improving the efficiency of power electronic converters.
*   **Reliability:** Excessive power dissipation can lead to device overheating and failure.
*   **Performance:** Fast switching speeds are essential for achieving the desired performance in high-frequency circuits.

---

### **2. Understanding Diode Power Loss**

Diode power loss can be categorized into several components:

**2.1. Forward Conduction Loss ($P_{forward}$)**

This is the power dissipated when the diode is conducting current in the forward direction. It is primarily due to the forward voltage drop ($V_F$) across the diode.

*   **Formula:** $P_{forward} = V_F \times I_{avg}$
    *   Where $I_{avg}$ is the average forward current.
*   **Behavior in High-Frequency Diodes:** High-frequency diodes often have lower $V_F$ values compared to standard diodes to reduce conduction losses at high currents.

**2.2. Switching Loss ($P_{switching}$)**

This is the most significant component of power loss in high-frequency diodes and occurs during the switching transitions (turn-on and turn-off).

*   **Turn-on Loss ($P_{on}$):** Occurs when the diode is turned on while still experiencing a reverse voltage. The overlap of voltage and current during this transition dissipates power.
*   **Turn-off Loss ($P_{off}$):** Occurs when the diode is turned off. This is dominated by the **reverse recovery loss**, which is directly related to the reverse recovery time ($t_{rr}$). During reverse recovery, the diode briefly conducts a reverse current ($I_{rr}$) before becoming non-conducting.

**2.3. Reverse Leakage Loss ($P_{leakage}$)**

This is the power dissipated when the diode is reverse biased. It is due to the small reverse leakage current ($I_R$) flowing through the diode.

*   **Formula:** $P_{leakage} = V_R \times I_R$
    *   Where $V_R$ is the reverse voltage.
*   **Behavior in High-Frequency Diodes:** High-frequency diodes typically have very low reverse leakage currents.

**Total Power Loss ($P_{total}$) = $P_{forward}$ + $P_{switching}$ + $P_{leakage}$**

### **3. Measurement of Power Loss**

Measuring diode power loss directly in a high-frequency circuit can be challenging due to the fast switching speeds. The most common and practical method involves measuring the voltage across and current through the diode simultaneously.

**3.1. Direct Power Measurement (Calorimetric Method)**

This method involves measuring the heat generated by the diode.

*   **Princ:** The power dissipated by the diode is converted into heat. By measuring the temperature rise of the diode and its thermal resistance, the power loss can be estimated.
*   **Setup:**
    1.  Mount the diode on a heatsink with a temperature sensor (e.g., thermocouple, thermistor) attached.
    2.  Operate the diode in the target circuit.
    3.  Measure the steady-state temperature of the diode (or heatsink) and the ambient temperature.
    4.  Knowing the thermal resistance of the diode and heatsink ($R_{th}$), calculate the power loss: $P_{loss} \approx \frac{T_{junction} - T_{ambient}}{R_{th}}$.
*   **Limitations:** This method provides an average power loss and doesn't separate different loss components. It also requires knowing accurate thermal parameters.

**3.2. Indirect Power Measurement (Voltage and Current Integration)**

This is the most common method for characterizing power loss in laboratory settings, especially when focusing on switching losses. It involves capturing the instantaneous voltage and current waveforms of the diode and integrating their product.

*   **Princ:** Instantaneous power dissipated by the diode is $p(t) = v(t) \times i(t)$. The average power loss is the integral of this instantaneous power over a switching cycle.
*   **Setup:**
    1.  **Diode Under Test (DUT):** The high-frequency diode to be tested.
    2.  **Switching Circuit:** A circuit that drives the diode at the desired high frequency (e.g., a buck converter, boost converter, or a simple PWM switching circuit).
    3.  **Current Probe:** A non-intrusive current probe (e.g., Hall effect sensor, Rogowski coil) to measure the instantaneous current through the diode.
    4.  **Voltage Probe:** A high-bandwidth voltage probe to measure the instantaneous voltage across the diode.
    5.  **Digital Storage Oscilloscope (DSO):** A DSO with at least two channels to capture both voltage and current waveforms simultaneously. It should have sufficient bandwidth and sampling rate.
    6.  **Mathematical Functions on DSO:** Many DSOs have built-in functions to perform multiplication and integration of waveforms.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Connect the current probe to measure the diode current ($i_{DUT}(t)$) and the voltage probe to measure the voltage across the diode ($v_{DUT}(t)$).
    2.  Set up the switching circuit to operate the diode at the desired frequency, duty cycle, and load conditions.
    3.  Capture the $v_{DUT}(t)$ and $i_{DUT}(t)$ waveforms over several switching cycles.
    4.  Use the DSO's mathematical functions to:
        *   Multiply the two waveforms to obtain the instantaneous power: $p_{DUT}(t) = v_{DUT}(t) \times i_{DUT}(t)$.
        *   Integrate the instantaneous power waveform over one or more switching cycles to obtain the total energy loss per cycle ($E_{loss}$).
        *   Calculate the average power loss: $P_{loss} = \frac{E_{loss}}{T_{switching}}$, where $T_{switching}$ is the switching period.
*   **Considerations:**
    *   **Probe Bandwidth and Rise/Fall Times:** Ensure that the probes and oscilloscope have sufficient bandwidth to accurately capture the fast switching transients.
    *   **Probe Loading:** Minimize the impact of probe capacitance and inductance on the circuit.
    *   **Synchronization:** Ensure accurate triggering and synchronization between voltage and current measurements.
    *   **Offset Calibration:** Properly calibrate the probes to avoid DC offsets.

**Example Setup for Measuring Power Loss:**

Consider a simple circuit where a diode is switched by a PWM signal.

```
     +Vcc ----> [Optional Series Resistor] ----> Diode (DUT) ----> Load ----> Ground
                    |                                    |
                  PWM Signal ---------------------------> Gate Drive
```

In this setup:
*   The current probe measures the current flowing into the anode of the diode.
*   The voltage probe measures the voltage across the diode (anode to cathode).

The DSO would then:
1.  Display the voltage and current waveforms.
2.  Calculate $p(t) = v(t) \times i(t)$.
3.  Calculate $P_{loss} = \frac{1}{T_{switching}} \int_{0}^{T_{switching}} v_{DUT}(t) \times i_{DUT}(t) dt$.

### **4. Understanding Reverse Recovery Time ($t_{rr}$)**

The reverse recovery time is a critical parameter for diodes operating in switched applications. It represents the time it takes for the diode to stop conducting current when the applied voltage reverses.

**4.1. Reverse Recovery Mechanism:**

When a diode is forward biased, charge carriers (electrons and holes) are injected into the depletion region and diffused into the neutral regions. When the voltage is suddenly reversed, these stored charge carriers must be removed or recombined before the diode can block reverse voltage. This process is called **reverse recovery**.

**4.2. Key Parameters of Reverse Recovery:**

*   **Reverse Recovery Time ($t_{rr}$):** The total time from the point where the forward current ($I_F$) drops to zero after the voltage reverses to the point where the reverse current falls to a specified percentage (usually 10% or 25%) of its peak value ($I_{rr}$).
*   **Peak Reverse Recovery Current ($I_{rr}$):** The maximum reverse current that flows during the reverse recovery interval. This peak current can be significantly larger than the forward current.
*   **Charge Storage ($Q_{rr}$):** The total charge that needs to be removed during reverse recovery. It's the integral of the reverse current over the reverse recovery interval.

**4.3. Reverse Recovery Waveform:**

1.  **Forward Conduction:** The diode conducts a forward current $I_F$.
2.  **Voltage Reversal:** The applied voltage reverses, causing the forward current to decrease.
3.  **Zero Crossing:** The forward current reaches zero.
4.  **Reverse Current Rise:** Due to stored charge, the diode starts conducting a reverse current, which rises rapidly to a peak value $I_{rr}$.
5.  **Reverse Current Decay:** The reverse current decays as the stored charge is removed by recombination or swept out.
6.  **Steady State Reverse Leakage:** The diode reaches a low leakage current state.

The most common model for the reverse recovery current is the **"S-shaped" or "hyperbolic" decay**, characterized by a relatively constant reverse current ($I_{rr}$) for a period and then a rapid decay.

**4.4. Impact of $t_{rr}$ on Switching Loss:**

The reverse recovery process is a major contributor to switching loss. During the $t_{rr}$ period, the diode is still conducting a significant reverse current while a reverse voltage is being applied. This overlap of voltage and current leads to a power dissipation described by:

$P_{rr} \approx \frac{1}{2} V_R \times I_{rr} \times t_{rr} \times f_{sw}$ (for simplified square wave switching)

Where:
*   $V_R$ is the reverse blocking voltage.
*   $I_{rr}$ is the peak reverse recovery current.
*   $t_{rr}$ is the reverse recovery time.
*   $f_{sw}$ is the switching frequency.

A lower $t_{rr}$ and $I_{rr}$ significantly reduce switching losses, which is crucial for high-frequency operation.

### **5. Measurement of Reverse Recovery Time ($t_{rr}$)**

Measuring $t_{rr}$ accurately requires a circuit that can rapidly switch the diode from forward conduction to reverse blocking.

**5.1. Test Circuit Configuration:**

A common test circuit uses a **reverse recovery test fixture** or a **fast switching circuit**. One such circuit involves a series combination of a resistor and the diode, driven by a pulsed voltage source.

*   **Princ:** Apply a large forward current pulse to the diode, then rapidly reverse the voltage across it and measure the resulting reverse current.
*   **Setup:**
    1.  **Diode Under Test (DUT):** The high-frequency diode.
    2.  **Forward Current Source/Pulse Generator:** To drive a known forward current ($I_F$) through the diode. This can be achieved using a current-limited voltage source or a dedicated pulse generator.
    3.  **Reverse Voltage Source:** To apply a reverse bias to the diode after the forward pulse.
    4.  **Series Resistor ($R_s$):** This resistor is critical. It limits the peak reverse recovery current ($I_{rr}$) and helps define the reverse voltage across the diode during the recovery period. A smaller $R_s$ will result in a higher $I_{rr}$ and potentially a shorter $t_{rr}$ if charge removal is the dominant factor, but also higher switching losses.
    5.  **Current Measurement:** A fast current probe or a small series sense resistor with a voltage probe to measure the instantaneous current through the diode.
    6.  **Voltage Measurement:** A fast voltage probe to measure the voltage across the diode.
    7.  **Digital Storage Oscilloscope (DSO):** To capture and analyze the voltage and current waveforms.

**5.2. Example Reverse Recovery Measurement Circuit:**

```
        +V_forward ----> Rs ----> Diode (DUT) ----> Ground
                         |             |
                         |             |
        [Optional: Reverse Voltage Source connected here after forward pulse]
```

A more common setup uses a single pulse generator to control both forward and reverse bias:

```
    PULSE GENERATOR (controlled)
         |
         |------> [R_s] ------> [Diode DUT] ------> [R_load (for reverse current measurement)] ------> Ground
```

In this setup:
*   The pulse generator first applies a forward voltage to drive $I_F$ through $R_s$ and the diode.
*   The pulse generator then switches to a reverse voltage, causing the diode to turn off.
*   A small resistor ($R_{sense}$) can be placed in series with the diode to measure current, or the current probe can be used directly.
*   A voltage probe measures the voltage across the diode.

**5.3. Procedure for $t_{rr}$ Measurement:**

1.  **Set Forward Current:** Configure the pulse generator to deliver a specific forward current pulse ($I_F$) through the diode. Ensure the forward current pulse is long enough to reach a steady state.
2.  **Reverse Bias Transition:** Program the pulse generator to quickly reverse the voltage, causing the diode to switch from forward conduction to reverse blocking. The rate of voltage reversal (dV/dt) is important.
3.  **Capture Waveforms:**
    *   Use the current probe to measure the instantaneous current through the diode.
    *   Use the voltage probe to measure the voltage across the diode.
4.  **Identify $t_{rr}$:** On the oscilloscope display:
    *   Locate the point where the forward current ($I_F$) drops to zero after the voltage reversal.
    *   Observe the reverse current, which will peak at $I_{rr}$ and then decay.
    *   **Measure $t_{rr}$:** The time interval from the zero-crossing of the forward current to the point where the reverse current has decayed to 10% of its peak value ($I_{rr}$), or as specified by the datasheet (e.g., $I_{rr}/10$). Some datasheets specify $t_{rr}$ as the time until the reverse current reaches $-0.1 \times I_F$.
5.  **Measure $I_{rr}$:** Identify the peak value of the reverse current during the recovery period.
6.  **Calculate $Q_{rr}$:** Integrate the reverse current waveform over the reverse recovery interval: $Q_{rr} = \int_{t_{zero}}^{t_{end}} |i_{rr}(t)| dt$.

**Important Considerations for $t_{rr}$ Measurement:**

*   **Forward Current ($I_F$):** $t_{rr}$ is dependent on the forward current. Higher $I_F$ generally leads to a longer $t_{rr}$ and higher $I_{rr}$.
*   **Rate of Voltage Reversal (dV/dt):** The rate at which the voltage reverses affects the shape and duration of the reverse recovery. A faster dV/dt can lead to a higher $I_{rr}$ but may not always significantly change $t_{rr}$ if charge removal is the limiting factor.
*   **Temperature:** $t_{rr}$ is also temperature-dependent.
*   **Diode Type:** Different types of high-frequency diodes (e.g., Schottky diodes, fast recovery diodes, Ultrafast recovery diodes) have vastly different $t_{rr}$ values. Schottky diodes, for instance, have very small $t_{rr}$ because they rely on majority carriers.
*   **Measurement Conditions:** Always refer to the diode's datasheet for the specific test conditions under which $t_{rr}$ is specified (e.g., $I_F$, $V_R$, temperature).

### **6. Practice Questions with Answers**

**Q1. What are the two primary components of switching loss in a diode operating at high frequencies?**

**Answer:** The two primary components of switching loss are turn-on loss and turn-off loss. Turn-off loss is primarily attributed to the reverse recovery phenomenon.

**Q2. Explain why reverse recovery time ($t_{rr}$) is crucial for high-frequency diode applications.**

**Answer:** Reverse recovery time is crucial because it directly impacts switching losses. During reverse recovery, the diode conducts a reverse current ($I_{rr}$) while a reverse voltage is applied, causing significant power dissipation ($P_{rr} \approx \frac{1}{2} V_R \times I_{rr} \times t_{rr} \times f_{sw}$). A shorter $t_{rr}$ reduces this power loss, leading to higher efficiency and less heating in high-frequency circuits.

**Q3. Describe the key steps involved in measuring the reverse recovery time ($t_{rr}$) of a diode using an oscilloscope.**

**Answer:**
1.  Set up a test circuit that can drive a known forward current ($I_F$) into the diode and then rapidly reverse the voltage.
2.  Use current and voltage probes to capture the diode's current and voltage waveforms on a DSO.
3.  Identify the point where the forward current drops to zero after voltage reversal.
4.  Observe the peak reverse current ($I_{rr}$).
5.  Measure the time interval from the forward current zero-crossing to when the reverse current falls to 10% of $I_{rr}$ (or as per datasheet specification). This time is $t_{rr}$.

**Q4. A diode has a forward current of 5A and a reverse recovery time of 50ns, with a peak reverse recovery current of 10A. If it is operated in a circuit with a reverse voltage of 100V and a switching frequency of 100kHz, estimate the reverse recovery power loss per cycle.**

**Answer:**
*   $I_F = 5 \text{A}$ (given, but not directly used in the power loss formula)
*   $t_{rr} = 50 \text{ ns} = 50 \times 10^{-9} \text{ s}$
*   $I_{rr} = 10 \text{ A}$
*   $V_R = 100 \text{ V}$
*   $f_{sw} = 100 \text{ kHz} = 100 \times 10^3 \text{ Hz}$
*   Switching period, $T_{sw} = 1/f_{sw} = 1/(100 \times 10^3) = 10 \mu\text{s}$

The reverse recovery power loss can be approximated as:
$P_{rr} \approx \frac{1}{2} V_R \times I_{rr} \times t_{rr} \times f_{sw}$
$P_{rr} \approx \frac{1}{2} \times 100 \text{ V} \times 10 \text{ A} \times (50 \times 10^{-9} \text{ s}) \times (100 \times 10^3 \text{ Hz})$
$P_{rr} \approx 0.5 \times 100 \times 10 \times 50 \times 10^{-9} \times 100 \times 10^3$
$P_{rr} \approx 0.5 \times 1000 \times 5000 \times 10^{-6}$
$P_{rr} \approx 5000 \times 10^{-3}$
$P_{rr} \approx 5 \text{ W}$

*Note: This is an approximation. The actual shape of the reverse recovery current and voltage overlap would influence the precise loss.*

**Q5. What factors influence the reverse recovery time ($t_{rr}$) of a diode?**

**Answer:** The factors influencing $t_{rr}$ include:
*   **Diode Material and Doping Profile:** The fundamental properties of the semiconductor material and how it is doped significantly impact charge storage.
*   **Forward Current ($I_F$):** Higher forward currents lead to more stored charge and generally longer $t_{rr}$.
*   **Temperature:** Device temperature affects carrier mobility and recombination rates.
*   **Reverse Voltage ($V_R$):** While $V_R$ doesn't directly cause the recovery, the rate of voltage reversal (dV/dt) can influence the peak reverse current ($I_{rr}$).
*   **Circuit Parameters:** The external circuit, particularly the series resistance and the speed of voltage reversal, can affect the measured $t_{rr}$.

### **7. Important Points to Remember**

*   **High-frequency diodes** are essential for efficient operation of power converters at high switching frequencies.
*   **Power loss** in diodes is mainly due to conduction loss ($V_F \times I_{avg}$) and switching loss ($P_{on} + P_{off}$).
*   **Reverse recovery loss** is the dominant component of switching loss, directly related to $t_{rr}$ and $I_{rr}$.
*   **Measuring power loss** involves capturing instantaneous voltage and current waveforms and integrating their product, or using calorimetric methods.
*   **Measuring $t_{rr}$** requires a fast switching circuit and accurate, high-bandwidth measurement of current and voltage transients.
*   Always refer to the **diode datasheet** for specified test conditions and parameter values.
*   **Schottky diodes** offer very low forward voltage drop and near-zero reverse recovery time, making them ideal for high-frequency applications.
*   **Fast/Ultrafast recovery diodes** are designed with specific doping profiles to minimize $t_{rr}$ and $Q_{rr}$.
