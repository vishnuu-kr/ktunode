### 1. Introduction to the Sine Function in the Complex Plane

The sine function, $w = \sin z$, is a fundamental complex function. Understanding its properties, such as its analyticity, periodicity, and relationship to other complex functions, is crucial for various applications in electrical and physical sciences, including signal processing, wave mechanics, and control systems. This topic will explore the behavior of $\sin z$ in the complex plane and its connection to the real-valued sine function.

---

### 2. Definition and Euler's Formula

**Key Concept:** The complex sine function is defined using Euler's formula, which relates exponential functions to trigonometric functions.

**Definition:**
For a complex number $z = x + iy$, where $x$ is the real part and $y$ is the imaginary part, the complex sine function is defined as:

$$
\sin z = \frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i}
$$

**Derivation from Euler's Formula:**
Recall Euler's formula:
$e^{i\theta} = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta$

Using this, we can write:
$e^{iz} = e^{i(x+iy)} = e^{ix - y} = e^{-y} e^{ix} = e^{-y}(\cos x + i \sin x)$
$e^{-iz} = e^{-i(x+iy)} = e^{-ix + y} = e^{y} e^{-ix} = e^{y}(\cos x - i \sin x)$

Substituting these into the definition of $\sin z$:
$$
\sin z = \frac{e^{-y}(\cos x + i \sin x) - e^{y}(\cos x - i \sin x)}{2i}
$$
$$
\sin z = \frac{\cos x (e^{-y} - e^{y}) + i \sin x (e^{-y} + e^{y})}{2i}
$$

Recall the hyperbolic functions:
$\cosh y = \frac{e^y + e^{-y}}{2}$
$\sinh y = \frac{e^y - e^{-y}}{2}$

Therefore, $e^{-y} - e^{y} = -2 \sinh y$ and $e^{-y} + e^{y} = 2 \cosh y$.

Substituting these back:
$$
\sin z = \frac{\cos x (-2 \sinh y) + i \sin x (2 \cosh y)}{2i}
$$
$$
\sin z = \frac{-2 \cos x \sinh y + 2i \sin x \cosh y}{2i}
$$
$$
\sin z = \frac{-2 \cos x \sinh y}{2i} + \frac{2i \sin x \cosh y}{2i}
$$
$$
\sin z = i \frac{\cos x \sinh y}{i} + \sin x \cosh y
$$
$$
\sin z = \cos x \sinh y (-i) + \sin x \cosh y
$$
$$
\sin z = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y
$$

This gives us the expression for $\sin z$ in terms of its real and imaginary parts.

**Real Part:** $\text{Re}(\sin z) = \sin x \cosh y$
**Imaginary Part:** $\text{Im}(\sin z) = \cos x \sinh y$

**Reference:** This derivation is a standard result and can be found in most complex analysis textbooks, including Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 13.3) and Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Section 2.3).

### 3. Analyticity of $w = \sin z$

**Key Concept:** A complex function is analytic (or holomorphic) if it is differentiable in a neighborhood of every point in its domain. Analyticity is crucial for conformal mapping (CO2).

**Theorem:** The complex sine function $w = \sin z$ is analytic everywhere in the complex plane.

**Proof using Cauchy-Riemann Equations:**
Let $w = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$, where $u(x,y) = \sin x \cosh y$ and $v(x,y) = \cos x \sinh y$.
We need to check if the Cauchy-Riemann equations are satisfied:
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$ and $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$

**Partial Derivatives:**
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\sin x \cosh y) = \cos x \cosh y$
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\sin x \cosh y) = \sin x \sinh y$
$\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\cos x \sinh y) = -\sin x \sinh y$
$\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\cos x \sinh y) = \cos x \cosh y$

**Checking Cauchy-Riemann Equations:**
1. $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \cos x \cosh y$ and $\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \cos x \cosh y$.
   So, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$. (Satisfied)

2. $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \sin x \sinh y$ and $-\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -(-\sin x \sinh y) = \sin x \sinh y$.
   So, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$. (Satisfied)

Since the Cauchy-Riemann equations are satisfied and the partial derivatives are continuous everywhere, $\sin z$ is analytic for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$.

**Derivative of $\sin z$:**
The derivative of $\sin z$ is $\cos z$.
$\frac{d}{dz}(\sin z) = \frac{d}{dz} \left( \frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i} \right) = \frac{1}{2i} (i e^{iz} - (-i) e^{-iz}) = \frac{1}{2i} (i e^{iz} + i e^{-iz}) = \frac{i}{2i} (e^{iz} + e^{-iz}) = \frac{e^{iz} + e^{-iz}}{2} = \cos z$.

**Reference:** Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 13.3) and Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Section 2.3) discuss the analyticity and derivative of $\sin z$.

### 4. Periodicity of $\sin z$

**Key Concept:** A function $f(z)$ is periodic with period $P$ if $f(z+P) = f(z)$ for all $z$ in the domain.

**Property:** The complex sine function is periodic with period $2\pi$.

**Proof:**
We want to show that $\sin(z + 2\pi) = \sin z$.
Using the definition:
$$
\sin(z + 2\pi) = \frac{e^{i(z+2\pi)} - e^{-i(z+2\pi)}}{2i}
$$
$$
\sin(z + 2\pi) = \frac{e^{iz} e^{i2\pi} - e^{-iz} e^{-i2\pi}}{2i}
$$
Recall that $e^{i2\pi} = \cos(2\pi) + i \sin(2\pi) = 1 + i(0) = 1$.
And $e^{-i2\pi} = \cos(-2\pi) + i \sin(-2\pi) = 1 + i(0) = 1$.

Substituting these values:
$$
\sin(z + 2\pi) = \frac{e^{iz}(1) - e^{-iz}(1)}{2i} = \frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i} = \sin z
$$
Thus, $\sin z$ has a period of $2\pi$.

**Other Periods:**
If $P$ is a period, then $nP$ is also a period for any integer $n$. So, $2n\pi$ are also periods for $\sin z$. The fundamental period is the smallest positive period, which is $2\pi$.

**Reference:** This is a fundamental property covered in all standard texts, e.g., Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 13.3).

### 5. Zeros of $\sin z$

**Key Concept:** The zeros of a function are the values of $z$ for which $f(z) = 0$.

**Property:** The zeros of $\sin z$ are $z = n\pi$, where $n$ is an integer.

**Proof:**
Set $\sin z = 0$:
$$
\frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i} = 0
$$
$$
e^{iz} - e^{-iz} = 0
$$
$$
e^{iz} = e^{-iz}
$$
Multiply both sides by $e^{iz}$:
$$
e^{2iz} = 1
$$
Let $2iz = w$. We are looking for solutions to $e^w = 1$.
In polar form, $1 = 1 \cdot e^{i(0 + 2k\pi)}$ for any integer $k$.
So, $w = i(0 + 2k\pi) = i 2k\pi$.

Substituting back $w = 2iz$:
$2iz = i 2k\pi$
$z = k\pi$

Thus, the zeros of $\sin z$ are $z = n\pi$, where $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**Example:**
$\sin(0) = 0$
$\sin(\pi) = 0$
$\sin(-\pi) = 0$
$\sin(2\pi) = 0$

**Reference:** Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Section 2.3) discusses the zeros of trigonometric functions.

### 6. Relationship to Hyperbolic Sine Function

**Key Concept:** There is a close relationship between the circular trigonometric functions and the hyperbolic trigonometric functions.

**Property:** $\sin(iy) = i \sinh y$ and $\sin(x) = \sinh(ix)$.

**Proof for $\sin(iy)$:**
Using the definition of $\sin z$:
$$
\sin(iy) = \frac{e^{i(iy)} - e^{-i(iy)}}{2i} = \frac{e^{-y} - e^{y}}{2i} = \frac{-(e^{y} - e^{-y})}{2i} = \frac{-2 \sinh y}{2i} = \frac{-\sinh y}{i}
$$
Multiply numerator and denominator by $i$:
$$
\sin(iy) = \frac{-\sinh y}{i} \cdot \frac{i}{i} = \frac{-i \sinh y}{-1} = i \sinh y
$$

**Proof for $\sin(x)$ (by replacing $y$ with $ix$ in the identity $\sin(z) = \sin(x+iy)$):**
We know $\sin z = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$.
Let $z = x$ (so $y=0$). This gives $\sin x = \sin x \cosh 0 + i \cos x \sinh 0 = \sin x (1) + i \cos x (0) = \sin x$.

Now consider $\sin(x)$ and relate it to hyperbolic functions.
From $\sin z = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$, if we let $z = x + iy$, we have:
$\sin(x+iy) = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$.

What if we consider $\sin z$ where $z$ is purely imaginary, say $z = iy$?
$\sin(iy) = \sin(0+iy) = \sin 0 \cosh y + i \cos 0 \sinh y = 0 \cdot \cosh y + i \cdot 1 \cdot \sinh y = i \sinh y$. This confirms the first part.

Now consider the second part: $\sin(x) = \sinh(ix)$.
Let's evaluate $\sinh(ix)$:
$$
\sinh(ix) = \frac{e^{ix} - e^{-ix}}{2}
$$
Using Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta$:
$$
\sinh(ix) = \frac{(\cos x + i \sin x) - (\cos(-x) + i \sin(-x))}{2}
$$
Since $\cos(-x) = \cos x$ and $\sin(-x) = -\sin x$:
$$
\sinh(ix) = \frac{(\cos x + i \sin x) - (\cos x - i \sin x)}{2}
$$
$$
\sinh(ix) = \frac{\cos x + i \sin x - \cos x + i \sin x}{2}
$$
$$
\sinh(ix) = \frac{2i \sin x}{2} = i \sin x
$$
This is not what we wanted. Let's recheck the identity.

The correct identity is:
$\sin(x+iy) = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$.

Let's consider purely real and purely imaginary arguments for $\sin$ and $\sinh$:
$\sin(x+iy) = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$

Let $z = x$.
$\sin x = \sin x \cosh 0 + i \cos x \sinh 0 = \sin x$.

Let $z = iy$.
$\sin(iy) = \sin 0 \cosh y + i \cos 0 \sinh y = i \sinh y$.

Now let's check the identity for $\sin x$ in terms of hyperbolic functions.
Consider the identity $\cos z = \cosh(iz)$.
Let's re-evaluate $\sinh(ix)$:
$\sinh(ix) = \frac{e^{ix} - e^{-ix}}{2}$. This is $i \sin x$.

The identity we are looking for is $\sin x = \frac{1}{i} \sinh(ix)$.
$$
\frac{1}{i} \sinh(ix) = \frac{1}{i} (i \sin x) = \sin x
$$
This works.

Also, we can show:
$\cos(iy) = \cosh y$.
Proof: $\cos(iy) = \frac{e^{i(iy)} + e^{-i(iy)}}{2} = \frac{e^{-y} + e^{y}}{2} = \cosh y$.

And $\cosh(iy) = \cos y$.
Proof: $\cosh(iy) = \frac{e^{i(iy)} + e^{-i(iy)}}{2} = \frac{e^{-y} + e^{y}}{2}$. Wait, this is $\cosh y$.
$\cosh(iy) = \frac{e^{i(iy)} + e^{-i(iy)}}{2} = \frac{e^{-y} + e^{y}}{2}$.
Let's re-evaluate $\cosh(iy)$:
$\cosh(iy) = \frac{e^{iy} + e^{-iy}}{2} = \cos y$.

So, the relationships are:
* $\sin(iy) = i \sinh y$
* $\cos(iy) = \cosh y$
* $\sinh(iy) = i \sin y$
* $\cosh(iy) = \cos y$

And from these, we can derive:
$\sin x = \frac{\sinh(ix)}{i} = -i \sinh(ix)$
$\cos x = \cosh(ix)$

**Reference:** Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 13.3) extensively covers these relationships. Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Section 2.4) also discusses hyperbolic functions.

### 7. Magnitude and Argument of $\sin z$

**Key Concept:** Understanding the magnitude and argument of $w = \sin z$ helps visualize its mapping properties and is relevant to analyticity and conformal mapping (CO2).

We have $\sin z = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$.
So, $u = \sin x \cosh y$ and $v = \cos x \sinh y$.

**Magnitude $|\sin z|$:**
$$
|\sin z|^2 = u^2 + v^2 = (\sin x \cosh y)^2 + (\cos x \sinh y)^2
$$
$$
|\sin z|^2 = \sin^2 x \cosh^2 y + \cos^2 x \sinh^2 y
$$
Using $\cosh^2 y = 1 + \sinh^2 y$ and $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$:
$$
|\sin z|^2 = \sin^2 x (1 + \sinh^2 y) + (1 - \sin^2 x) \sinh^2 y
$$
$$
|\sin z|^2 = \sin^2 x + \sin^2 x \sinh^2 y + \sinh^2 y - \sin^2 x \sinh^2 y
$$
$$
|\sin z|^2 = \sin^2 x + \sinh^2 y
$$

Alternatively, using $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$ and $\sinh^2 y = \cosh^2 y - 1$:
$$
|\sin z|^2 = (1 - \cos^2 x) \cosh^2 y + \cos^2 x (\cosh^2 y - 1)
$$
$$
|\sin z|^2 = \cosh^2 y - \cos^2 x \cosh^2 y + \cos^2 x \cosh^2 y - \cos^2 x
$$
$$
|\sin z|^2 = \cosh^2 y - \cos^2 x
$$

Using $\cosh^2 y = 1 + \sinh^2 y$ and $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$:
$|\sin z|^2 = (1 + \sinh^2 y) - (1 - \sin^2 x) = 1 + \sinh^2 y - 1 + \sin^2 x = \sin^2 x + \sinh^2 y$.

This confirms our result.

$$
|\sin z| = \sqrt{\sin^2 x + \sinh^2 y} = \sqrt{\cosh^2 y - \cos^2 x}
$$

**Argument $\text{arg}(\sin z)$:**
The argument of $\sin z$ is given by $\arctan\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)$, being careful about the quadrant.
$$
\text{arg}(\sin z) = \arctan\left(\frac{\cos x \sinh y}{\sin x \cosh y}\right) = \arctan\left(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} \frac{\sinh y}{\cosh y}\right)
$$
$$
\text{arg}(\sin z) = \arctan(\cot x \tanh y)
$$
This needs to be adjusted based on the signs of $u$ and $v$.

**Important Point:** $|\sin z| \ge |\sin x|$ and $|\sin z| \ge |\sinh y|$.
* When $y=0$, $|\sin z| = |\sin x|$, which is the real sine function.
* When $x=0$, $|\sin z| = |\sinh y|$.
* When $x=\pi/2$, $|\sin z| = |\cosh y|$.
* When $y$ is large, $|\sin z|$ grows exponentially like $\frac{1}{2}e^{|y|}$.

**Reference:** Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 13.3) provides the formulas for magnitude and argument.

### 8. Conformal Mapping of $w = \sin z$

**Key Concept:** A mapping $w = f(z)$ is conformal at a point $z_0$ if it preserves angles between curves passing through $z_0$ (both magnitude and orientation). This requires $f(z)$ to be analytic and $f'(z_0) \neq 0$. This directly addresses CO2.

**Analyticity:** We have shown that $\sin z$ is analytic everywhere.

**Derivative:** $f'(z) = \frac{d}{dz}(\sin z) = \cos z$.

**Points of Non-Conformality:** The mapping is not conformal where $f'(z) = 0$, i.e., where $\cos z = 0$.
$\cos z = 0$ when $z = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$, where $n$ is an integer.
At these points, angles are not preserved.

**Mapping Properties:**
The sine function maps strips to regions.

*   **Strip $0 < x < \pi/2$, $y > 0$:** This region is mapped to the upper half of the $w$-plane.
    *   The vertical lines $x = c$ are mapped to ellipses in the $w$-plane.
    *   The horizontal lines $y = c$ are mapped to hyperbolas in the $w$-plane.

*   **Strip $0 < x < \pi$, $y > 0$:** This region is mapped to the upper half of the $w$-plane.
    *   The line segment $x \in [0, \pi]$ on the real axis ($y=0$) is mapped to the segment $[-1, 1]$ on the real axis of the $w$-plane.
    *   The line $x=0$ ($0 \le y < \infty$) is mapped to the positive imaginary axis in the $w$-plane ($u=0, v = \sinh y \ge 0$).
    *   The line $x=\pi$ ($\pi \le y < \infty$) is mapped to the negative imaginary axis in the $w$-plane ($u=0, v = \sinh y \ge 0$). This statement is incorrect. Let's re-evaluate.
        *   If $x = \pi$, $\sin(\pi + iy) = \sin \pi \cosh y + i \cos \pi \sinh y = 0 \cdot \cosh y + i (-1) \sinh y = -i \sinh y$.
        So, the line $x=\pi$, $y \in [0, \infty)$ maps to the negative imaginary axis ($u=0, v = -\sinh y \le 0$).

    *   The line $y=0$ ($0 \le x \le \pi$) maps to the real axis segment $[-1, 1]$.
    *   The line $x=0$, $y \in (-\infty, \infty)$ maps to the imaginary axis ($u=0, v = \sinh y$).
    *   The line $x=\pi$, $y \in (-\infty, \infty)$ maps to the imaginary axis ($u=0, v = -\sinh y$).

    Consider the strip $0 < x < \pi$ for $y$ any real value.
    *   The line $x=c$ for $c \in (0, \pi)$ is mapped to an ellipse in the $w$-plane.
        We have $u = \sin c \cosh y$ and $v = \cos c \sinh y$.
        $\frac{u}{\sin c} = \cosh y$ and $\frac{v}{\cos c} = \sinh y$.
        Using $\cosh^2 y - \sinh^2 y = 1$, we get:
        $\left(\frac{u}{\sin c}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{v}{\cos c}\right)^2 = 1$.
        This is the equation of a hyperbola if $\sin c \neq 0$ and $\cos c \neq 0$.
        Let's recheck the mapping of vertical lines.
        If $0 < c < \pi/2$, $\sin c > 0$, $\cos c > 0$. The mapping is to the right branch of a hyperbola.
        If $\pi/2 < c < \pi$, $\sin c > 0$, $\cos c < 0$. The mapping is to the left branch of a hyperbola.
        Ah, I confused the roles of $x$ and $y$.
        Let's use the identity $\sin z = \sin(x+iy) = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$.
        For a fixed $x=c$ (where $\sin c \neq 0$ and $\cos c \neq 0$), and varying $y$:
        $u = \sin c \cosh y$
        $v = \cos c \sinh y$
        $\cosh y = u / \sin c$
        $\sinh y = v / \cos c$
        $(\frac{u}{\sin c})^2 - (\frac{v}{\cos c})^2 = \cosh^2 y - \sinh^2 y = 1$.
        This is indeed a hyperbola with foci on the real axis if $\sin c = 0$ or $\cos c = 0$.
        If $\sin c > 0$ and $\cos c > 0$ ($0 < c < \pi/2$), the transverse axis is along the $u$-axis, and the equation is $\frac{u^2}{\sin^2 c} - \frac{v^2}{\cos^2 c} = 1$. The vertices are at $(\pm \sin c, 0)$. As $y$ varies from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, $u$ spans $[\sin c, \infty)$ and $v$ spans $(-\infty, \infty)$. This maps to the right branch of a hyperbola.

    *   For a fixed $y=c$ (where $\cosh c \neq 0$ and $\sinh c \neq 0$), and varying $x \in (0, \pi)$:
        $u = \sin x \cosh c$
        $v = \cos x \sinh c$
        $\sin x = u / \cosh c$
        $\cos x = v / \sinh c$
        $(\frac{u}{\cosh c})^2 + (\frac{v}{\sinh c})^2 = \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$.
        This is an ellipse with semi-axes $\cosh c$ and $\sinh c$.
        As $x$ varies from $0$ to $\pi$, $\sin x$ goes from $0$ to $1$ and back to $0$, while $\cos x$ goes from $1$ to $-1$.
        So, $u$ goes from $0$ to $\cosh c$ and back to $0$.
        $v$ goes from $\sinh c$ to $0$ and back to $-\sinh c$.
        This maps the interval $[0, \pi]$ to the upper half of the ellipse.

*   **Mapping of the first quadrant ($x>0, y>0$):**
    The positive real axis ($y=0, x>0$) maps to the positive real axis ($v=0, u>0$).
    The positive imaginary axis ($x=0, y>0$) maps to the positive imaginary axis ($u=0, v>0$).
    The strip $0 < x < \pi/2$, $y>0$ maps to the first quadrant of the $w$-plane.
    The mapping is conformal in the open strip.

**Reference:** Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 14.4) and Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Section 7.2) provide detailed discussions and diagrams of conformal mappings, including $\sin z$.

### 9. Complex Integration of $\sin z$

**Key Concept:** Complex integrals of analytic functions can be computed using Cauchy's Integral Theorem and Cauchy's Integral Formula (CO3).

**Cauchy's Integral Theorem:** If $f(z)$ is analytic in a simply connected domain $D$, then for every closed contour $C$ in $D$, $\oint_C f(z) dz = 0$.

Since $\sin z$ is analytic everywhere, for any closed contour $C$, $\oint_C \sin z \, dz = 0$.

**Cauchy's Integral Formula:** If $f(z)$ is analytic in a simply connected domain $D$, and $C$ is a simple closed contour in $D$, then for any point $z_0$ inside $C$, $f(z_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_C \frac{f(z)}{z-z_0} dz$.

**Example 1:** Evaluate $\oint_C \sin z \, dz$, where $C$ is the circle $|z|=1$.
Since $\sin z$ is analytic everywhere, and the circle $|z|=1$ is a closed contour, by Cauchy's Integral Theorem:
$\oint_{|z|=1} \sin z \, dz = 0$.

**Example 2:** Evaluate $\oint_C \frac{\sin z}{z - \pi/2} dz$, where $C$ is the circle $|z|=2$.
Here, $f(z) = \sin z$ and $z_0 = \pi/2$.
Since $\sin z$ is analytic in the disk $|z| \le 2$, and $\pi/2$ is inside the circle $|z|=2$, we can use Cauchy's Integral Formula:
$\oint_C \frac{\sin z}{z - \pi/2} dz = 2\pi i \cdot f(\pi/2) = 2\pi i \cdot \sin(\pi/2) = 2\pi i \cdot 1 = 2\pi i$.

**Example 3:** Evaluate $\oint_C \frac{\sin z}{z - 2\pi} dz$, where $C$ is the circle $|z|=1$.
Here, $f(z) = \sin z$ and $z_0 = 2\pi$.
Since $z_0 = 2\pi$ is outside the circle $|z|=1$, and $\sin z$ is analytic inside and on $C$, by Cauchy's Integral Theorem, the integral is 0.
$\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{\sin z}{z - 2\pi} dz = 0$.

**Reference:** Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 14.2) and Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Section 4.1, 4.2) cover these theorems and their applications.

### 10. Series Expansion of $\sin z$

**Key Concept:** Complex functions can be represented by Taylor and Laurent series around points where they are analytic or have singularities (CO4).

**Taylor Series:** Since $\sin z$ is analytic everywhere, its Taylor series expansion around any point $z_0$ exists and converges everywhere.

**Taylor Series around $z_0 = 0$:**
The Taylor series for $\sin z$ around $z_0=0$ (Maclaurin series) is:
$$
\sin z = z - \frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^5}{5!} - \frac{z^7}{7!} + \dots = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n z^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}
$$
This series converges for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$.

**Proof:** We can derive this using the Taylor series for $e^{iz}$ and $e^{-iz}$.
$e^{w} = 1 + w + \frac{w^2}{2!} + \frac{w^3}{3!} + \dots$
$e^{iz} = 1 + iz + \frac{(iz)^2}{2!} + \frac{(iz)^3}{3!} + \frac{(iz)^4}{4!} + \frac{(iz)^5}{5!} + \dots$
$e^{iz} = 1 + iz - \frac{z^2}{2!} - i\frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^4}{4!} + i\frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots$

$e^{-iz} = 1 - iz + \frac{(-iz)^2}{2!} + \frac{(-iz)^3}{3!} + \frac{(-iz)^4}{4!} + \frac{(-iz)^5}{5!} + \dots$
$e^{-iz} = 1 - iz - \frac{z^2}{2!} + i\frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^4}{4!} - i\frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots$

$\sin z = \frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i}$
$e^{iz} - e^{-iz} = (1 + iz - \frac{z^2}{2!} - i\frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^4}{4!} + i\frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots) - (1 - iz - \frac{z^2}{2!} + i\frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^4}{4!} - i\frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots)$
$e^{iz} - e^{-iz} = 2iz - 2i\frac{z^3}{3!} + 2i\frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots$

$\sin z = \frac{2iz - 2i\frac{z^3}{3!} + 2i\frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots}{2i} = z - \frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots$

**Laurent Series:** For functions with singularities, Laurent series are used. $\sin z$ has no singularities, so its Laurent series is just its Taylor series.

**Example:** Find the Taylor series of $\sin z$ around $z_0 = \pi/2$.
We know $\sin z = \sin(\pi/2 + (z - \pi/2))$.
Using the angle addition formula $\sin(A+B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B$:
$\sin z = \sin(\pi/2) \cos(z - \pi/2) + \cos(\pi/2) \sin(z - \pi/2)$
$\sin z = 1 \cdot \cos(z - \pi/2) + 0 \cdot \sin(z - \pi/2)$
$\sin z = \cos(z - \pi/2)$

Now, use the Taylor series for $\cos w = 1 - \frac{w^2}{2!} + \frac{w^4}{4!} - \dots$ with $w = z - \pi/2$.
So, the Taylor series of $\sin z$ around $z_0 = \pi/2$ is:
$\sin z = 1 - \frac{(z - \pi/2)^2}{2!} + \frac{(z - \pi/2)^4}{4!} - \frac{(z - \pi/2)^6}{6!} + \dots$
$\sin z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n (z - \pi/2)^{2n}}{(2n)!}$

**Reference:** Kreyszig (10th ed., Section 12.2) and Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Section 6.1) cover Taylor series expansions.

### 11. Residue Theorem and Real Integrals

**Key Concept:** The Residue Theorem is a powerful tool for evaluating complex integrals and, consequently, real integrals (CO4).

**Residue of $\sin z$:** Since $\sin z$ is analytic everywhere, it has no poles and thus no residues at any finite point.

**Application to Integrals of $\sin z$:**
The Residue Theorem states that $\oint_C f(z) dz = 2\pi i \sum (\text{residues of } f(z) \text{ inside } C)$.
For $f(z) = \sin z$, the sum of residues inside any contour $C$ is 0. This aligns with Cauchy's Integral Theorem.

**Using $\sin z$ in Integrals involving $\sin z$:**
Consider an integral where $\sin z$ appears in the numerator or as part of the integrand.

**Example 1:** Evaluate $\oint_C \frac{\sin z}{z^2} dz$, where $C$ is the circle $|z|=1$.
Here, $f(z) = \sin z$ and we are integrating $\frac{f(z)}{z^2}$.
The pole is at $z=0$, which is inside $C$. This is a pole of order 2.
We need the residue of $\frac{\sin z}{z^2}$ at $z=0$.
We know the Taylor series of $\sin z$ around $z=0$:
$\sin z = z - \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^5}{120} - \dots$
So, $\frac{\sin z}{z^2} = \frac{1}{z^2} \left( z - \frac{z^3}{6} + \dots \right) = \frac{1}{z} - \frac{z}{6} + \dots$
The Laurent series has a term $\frac{1}{z}$ with coefficient 1.
The residue at $z=0$ is $\text{Res}\left(\frac{\sin z}{z^2}, 0\right) = 1$.

Using the Residue Theorem:
$\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{\sin z}{z^2} dz = 2\pi i \times \text{Res}\left(\frac{\sin z}{z^2}, 0\right) = 2\pi i \times 1 = 2\pi i$.

**Example 2:** Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{x^2+1} dx$ (This is an application of the real Fourier Transform where $\sin$ might be involved or related).
While the direct integral of $\sin z$ over simple closed curves is zero, $\sin z$ is critical when it's part of a larger integrand whose integral is non-zero.
Let's consider the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} dx$.
We can evaluate this using the residue theorem by considering the integral of $\frac{e^{iz}}{z}$ over a semi-circular contour in the upper half-plane.
Let $f(z) = \frac{e^{iz}}{z}$. The pole is at $z=0$.
Consider a contour $C$ consisting of the interval $[-R, -\epsilon]$ on the real axis, a semicircle $C_\epsilon$ of radius $\epsilon$ around the origin in the upper half-plane, the interval $[\epsilon, R]$ on the real axis, and a semicircle $C_R$ of radius $R$ in the upper half-plane.
$f(z)$ has a simple pole at $z=0$.
$\text{Res}(f(z), 0) = \lim_{z \to 0} z \frac{e^{iz}}{z} = e^{i0} = 1$.

By the Residue Theorem, $\oint_C f(z) dz = 2\pi i \times \text{Res}(f(z), 0)$ if the pole is inside.
However, $z=0$ is on the contour. For a pole on the real axis, we use the Bromwich contour or indent the contour.
Using the principal value and indenting around $z=0$:
$\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{x} dx = \oint_{C'} \frac{e^{iz}}{z} dz$, where $C'$ is the contour as described but with a semicircle of radius $\epsilon$ *around* the pole at $z=0$.
The integral over the large semicircle $C_R$ goes to 0 as $R \to \infty$ (Jordan's Lemma).
The integral over the small semicircle $C_\epsilon$ goes to $-i\pi \times \text{Res}(f(z), 0) = -i\pi(1) = -i\pi$ as $\epsilon \to 0$ (for a pole on the real axis, indented from above).
The integral over the real axis is $\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{x} dx$.
So, $\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{x} dx - i\pi = 2\pi i (0)$ (no poles inside the indented contour).
$\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{ix}}{x} dx = i\pi$.

Splitting $e^{ix} = \cos x + i \sin x$:
$\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{x} dx + i \text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} dx = i\pi$.

Equating real and imaginary parts:
$\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\cos x}{x} dx = 0$ (as expected, since $\frac{\cos x}{x}$ is odd).
$\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} dx = \pi$.
Since $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ is an even function, the principal value is the same as the standard integral.
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} dx = \pi$.

**Reference:** Kreyszig (10th ed., Chapter 16) and Zill & Shanahan (3rd ed., Chapter 8) cover the Residue Theorem and its applications in evaluating integrals. B.V. Ramana and B.S. Grewal also have extensive sections on these topics.

### 12. Fourier Transforms and $\sin z$

**Key Concept:** Fourier Transforms are used to decompose signals into their constituent frequencies. The sine function is a fundamental oscillatory component. CO1 is about Fourier Transforms.

The Fourier Transform of a function $f(t)$ is given by:
$\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\} = F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i\omega t} dt$.

The inverse Fourier Transform is:
$f(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F(\omega)\} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega) e^{i\omega t} d\omega$.

The sine function itself, $\sin(\omega_0 t)$, is not directly Fourier transformable in the standard sense because it's not absolutely integrable. However, its transform can be understood in the sense of distributions.

We know that $\sin t = \frac{e^{it} - e^{-it}}{2i}$.
Consider the Fourier transform of $e^{i\omega_0 t}$:
$\mathcal{F}\{e^{i\omega_0 t}\} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{i\omega_0 t} e^{-i\omega t} dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-i(\omega-\omega_0)t} dt = 2\pi \delta(\omega - \omega_0)$.
This is the Dirac delta function.

Using linearity of the Fourier transform:
$\mathcal{F}\{\sin(\omega_0 t)\} = \mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{e^{i\omega_0 t} - e^{-i\omega_0 t}}{2i}\right\}$
$= \frac{1}{2i} \mathcal{F}\{e^{i\omega_0 t}\} - \frac{1}{2i} \mathcal{F}\{e^{-i\omega_0 t}\}$
$= \frac{1}{2i} (2\pi \delta(\omega - \omega_0)) - \frac{1}{2i} (2\pi \delta(\omega - (-\omega_0)))$
$= \pi \delta(\omega - \omega_0) - \frac{\pi}{i} \delta(\omega + \omega_0)$
$= \pi \delta(\omega - \omega_0) + i\pi \delta(\omega + \omega_0)$.

This result shows that a pure sine wave at frequency $\omega_0$ is represented by delta functions at frequencies $+\omega_0$ and $-\omega_0$ in the frequency domain.

**Application in Signal Processing:**
*   **Filters:** The behavior of filters is often described in the frequency domain using their frequency response, which involves $\sin$ and $\cos$ terms.
*   **Lumped Element Models:** In circuits, sinusoidal steady-state analysis relies heavily on the properties of $\sin$ and $\cos$ functions.

**Reference:** Rao, Kim, Hwang's "Fast Fourier Transform - Algorithms and Applications" is directly relevant here. Also, general engineering mathematics texts like Kreyszig and Ramana will cover Fourier transforms.

**Question 1:** Express $\sin(2+3i)$ in the form $u+iv$.
**Answer:**
$z = 2+3i$, so $x=2$, $y=3$.
$\sin z = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$
$\sin(2+3i) = \sin(2) \cosh(3) + i \cos(2) \sinh(3)$.
Using calculator:

$\cosh(3) \approx 10.0677$, $\sinh(3) \approx 10.0179$
$\sin(2+3i) \approx (0.9093)(10.0677) + i (-0.4161)(10.0179)$

**Question 2:** Find the derivative of $\sin(3z^2+5)$ with respect to $z$.
**Answer:**
Using the chain rule:
$\frac{d}{dz} \sin(3z^2+5) = \cos(3z^2+5) \cdot \frac{d}{dz}(3z^2+5)$
$= \cos(3z^2+5) \cdot (6z)$
$= 6z \cos(3z^2+5)$.

**Question 3:** Evaluate $\oint_C \frac{\sin z}{z - \pi} dz$, where $C$ is the circle $|z - \pi/2| = 1$.
**Answer:**
The integrand is $f(z) = \sin z$, and the pole is at $z_0 = \pi$.
The contour is a circle centered at $\pi/2$ with radius 1.
The points on the contour are $z = \pi/2 + e^{i\theta}$.
The point $z_0 = \pi$ is at a distance $|\pi - \pi/2| = \pi/2 \approx 1.57$ from the center of the contour.
Since $1.57 > 1$, the pole $z_0 = \pi$ is *outside* the contour.
Since $\sin z$ is analytic everywhere, the integral is 0 by Cauchy's Integral Theorem.
$\oint_C \frac{\sin z}{z - \pi} dz = 0$.

**Question 4:** Find the residues of $f(z) = \frac{\sin z}{z^3}$ at $z=0$.
**Answer:**
Use the Taylor series of $\sin z$ around $z=0$:
$\sin z = z - \frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots$
$f(z) = \frac{1}{z^3} \left( z - \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^5}{120} - \dots \right)$
$f(z) = \frac{1}{z^2} - \frac{1}{6} + \frac{z^2}{120} - \dots$
The Laurent series has terms with powers $z^{-2}, z^0, z^2, \dots$.
The residue is the coefficient of $z^{-1}$. There is no $z^{-1}$ term.
Therefore, the residue is 0.

**Question 5:** What is the Fourier transform of $\cos(\omega_0 t)$?
**Answer:**
$\cos t = \frac{e^{it} + e^{-it}}{2}$.
$\mathcal{F}\{\cos(\omega_0 t)\} = \mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{e^{i\omega_0 t} + e^{-i\omega_0 t}}{2}\right\}$
$= \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{F}\{e^{i\omega_0 t}\} + \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{F}\{e^{-i\omega_0 t}\}$
$= \frac{1}{2} (2\pi \delta(\omega - \omega_0)) + \frac{1}{2} (2\pi \delta(\omega - (-\omega_0)))$
$= \pi \delta(\omega - \omega_0) + \pi \delta(\omega + \omega_0)$.

### 14. Important Points to Remember

*   **Definition:** $\sin z = \frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i}$.
*   **Real/Imaginary Parts:** $\sin z = \sin x \cosh y + i \cos x \sinh y$.
*   **Analyticity:** $\sin z$ is analytic everywhere in $\mathbb{C}$.
*   **Derivative:** $\frac{d}{dz}(\sin z) = \cos z$.
*   **Periodicity:** $\sin z$ is periodic with period $2\pi$.
*   **Zeros:** $\sin z = 0$ if and only if $z = n\pi$, where $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
*   **Magnitude:** $|\sin z|^2 = \sin^2 x + \sinh^2 y$.
*   **Conformal Mapping:** $\sin z$ is conformal everywhere except where $\cos z = 0$ (i.e., $z = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$).
*   **Complex Integration:** $\oint_C \sin z \, dz = 0$ for any closed contour $C$ in $\mathbb{C}$ by Cauchy's Integral Theorem.
*   **Taylor Series:** The Taylor series around $z=0$ is $\sin z = z - \frac{z^3}{3!} + \frac{z^5}{5!} - \dots$.
*   **Residues:** $\sin z$ has no poles, so its residue at any finite point is 0. Integrals involving $\sin z$ may require analyzing other parts of the integrand.
*   **Fourier Transform:** $\mathcal{F}\{\sin(\omega_0 t)\} = \pi (\delta(\omega - \omega_0) - i \delta(\omega + \omega_0))$ (this is a common form, but the sign in the second term depends on convention for the inverse transform). My derivation above yielded $\pi \delta(\omega - \omega_0) + i\pi \delta(\omega + \omega_0)$. Let's recheck the standard form. The standard FT pairs often use $e^{-i\omega t}$, and the inverse FT often has a $1/2\pi$.
    Let's use the convention:
    $F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i\omega t} dt$
    $f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega) e^{i\omega t} d\omega$
    $\mathcal{F}\{e^{it}\} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{it} e^{-i\omega t} dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-i(\omega-1)t} dt = 2\pi \delta(\omega - 1)$.
    $\mathcal{F}\{\sin t\} = \mathcal{F}\{\frac{e^{it} - e^{-it}}{2i}\} = \frac{1}{2i} [2\pi \delta(\omega-1) - 2\pi \delta(\omega-(-1))]$
    $= \frac{\pi}{i} [\delta(\omega-1) - \delta(\omega+1)] = -i\pi [\delta(\omega-1) - \delta(\omega+1)] = i\pi [\delta(\omega+1) - \delta(\omega-1)]$.
    So, $\mathcal{F}\{\sin(\omega_0 t)\} = i\pi [\delta(\omega+\omega_0) - \delta(\omega-\omega_0)]$.
    My earlier derivation had a sign flip. The key is consistent definition of FT and inverse FT.
