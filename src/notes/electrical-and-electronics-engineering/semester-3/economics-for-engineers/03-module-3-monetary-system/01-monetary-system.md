# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 3: Monetary System

## Topic: Monetary System

### Introduction to the Monetary System

The monetary system is the set of institutions, policies, and instruments that a government uses to manage its money supply and credit conditions. In essence, it's the framework that governs how money is created, distributed, and used within an economy. For engineers, understanding the monetary system is crucial for making sound economic decisions in their projects, understanding investment climates, and analyzing the financial feasibility of their endeavors.

**Learning Outcome:** Understand the fundamentals of various economic issues using laws and learn the concepts of demand, supply, elasticity and production function. (CO1, K2)

**Key Concepts:**
*   **Money:** Anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value.
*   **Monetary Policy:** Actions undertaken by a central bank to manipulate the money supply and credit conditions to stimulate or restrain economic activity.
*   **Financial Institutions:** Organizations that provide financial services, such as banks, credit unions, insurance companies, and investment firms.
*   **Financial Markets:** Arenas where financial instruments are traded, such as stock markets, bond markets, and foreign exchange markets.

**Reference:**
*   "Managerial Economics" by Geetika, Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015) provides a foundational understanding of money, its functions, and the role of financial institutions.
*   "Indian Financial System" by Khan M. Y. (2011) offers a detailed look at the structure and functioning of financial markets and institutions, particularly relevant for the Indian context.

### Functions of Money

Money serves three primary functions, which are fundamental to its role in an economy:

1.  **Medium of Exchange:** Money facilitates transactions by acting as an intermediary between buyers and sellers. This eliminates the need for barter, which is inefficient and cumbersome.
    *   **Example:** Instead of trading goods directly, you use money to buy a component for your engineering project.

2.  **Unit of Account:** Money provides a common measure of value, allowing for the comparison of the worth of different goods and services. Prices are expressed in monetary units.
    *   **Example:** The cost of steel, concrete, and labor for a construction project are all expressed in a common currency (e.g., dollars, rupees).

3.  **Store of Value:** Money can be saved and used for future purchases. While inflation can erode its purchasing power over time, it is generally a more convenient store of value than most physical goods.
    *   **Example:** You can save your earnings to invest in new equipment for your engineering firm later.

**Learning Outcome:** Understand the fundamentals of various economic issues using laws and learn the concepts of demand, supply, elasticity and production function. (CO1, K2)

**Important Point to Remember:** The effectiveness of money relies on its widespread acceptance and stability in value.

### Evolution of Money

The concept of money has evolved significantly over time:

*   **Barter System:** Direct exchange of goods and services without the use of money. Highly inefficient due to the "double coincidence of wants."
*   **Commodity Money:** Money backed by a commodity with intrinsic value, such as gold, silver, or salt.
    *   **Example:** Gold coins.
*   **Fiat Money:** Money declared legal tender by a government, not backed by a physical commodity. Its value is derived from trust and government decree.
    *   **Example:** Dollar bills, Euro notes.
*   **Near Money:** Assets that can be quickly converted into cash with little to no loss of value, such as savings accounts, money market accounts, and Treasury bills.

### Money Supply and Monetary Aggregates

The **money supply** refers to the total amount of money in circulation within an economy. Central banks closely monitor and manage the money supply as a key tool of monetary policy.

**Monetary Aggregates:** Different measures of the money supply, categorized by liquidity. Common aggregates include:

*   **M0 (Monetary Base):** Currency in circulation plus commercial banks' reserves held at the central bank.
*   **M1:** M0 plus demand deposits (checking accounts) and other checkable deposits. This is the most liquid form of money.
    *   **Example:** Cash in your wallet + money in your checking account.
*   **M2:** M1 plus savings deposits, small-denomination time deposits (CDs), and retail money market mutual fund balances. More broadly defined, it includes less liquid assets.
    *   **Example:** M1 + money in your savings account.
*   **M3:** M2 plus larger time deposits, institutional money market funds, and other larger liquid assets.

**Learning Outcome:** Outline the macroeconomic principles of monetary and fiscal systems, national income and stock market. (CO3, K2)

**Reference:**
*   "Managerial Economics" by Geetika, Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015) elaborates on money supply and its components.
*   "Indian Financial System" by Khan M. Y. (2011) provides specific details on monetary aggregates as defined by the Reserve Bank of India.

**Important Point to Remember:** The definition and components of monetary aggregates can vary slightly between countries and over time.

### Central Banking and Monetary Policy Tools

**Central Bank:** A national institution responsible for overseeing the monetary system, regulating banks, and implementing monetary policy. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is India's central bank, and the Federal Reserve System is the central bank of the United States.

**Monetary Policy Objectives:**
*   Price Stability (Controlling Inflation)
*   Full Employment
*   Economic Growth
*   Exchange Rate Stability

**Key Monetary Policy Tools:**

1.  **Open Market Operations (OMO):** The buying and selling of government securities by the central bank in the open market.
    *   **Buying Securities:** Increases money supply (injects liquidity), lowers interest rates.
    *   **Selling Securities:** Decreases money supply (withdraws liquidity), raises interest rates.
    *   **Example:** If inflation is too high, the central bank might sell government bonds to reduce the amount of money in circulation.

2.  **Reserve Requirements:** The fraction of a bank's deposits that it is legally required to hold in reserve, either as cash in its vault or as deposits at the central bank.
    *   **Increasing Reserve Requirements:** Reduces the amount of money banks can lend, thus decreasing the money supply.
    *   **Decreasing Reserve Requirements:** Increases the amount of money banks can lend, thus expanding the money supply.
    *   **Example:** If a bank has $100 million in deposits and a reserve requirement of 10%, it must hold $10 million in reserve. If the requirement increases to 15%, it must hold $15 million, reducing its lending capacity.

3.  **Discount Rate (or Policy Rate):** The interest rate at which commercial banks can borrow money directly from the central bank.
    *   **Increasing the Discount Rate:** Makes borrowing more expensive for banks, leading to a contraction in the money supply.
    *   **Decreasing the Discount Rate:** Makes borrowing cheaper for banks, encouraging lending and expanding the money supply.
    *   **Example:** If the central bank lowers its policy rate, banks can borrow funds more cheaply, potentially leading them to lend more to businesses and individuals.

4.  **Interest Rate Corridor (Repo and Reverse Repo Rates in India):**
    *   **Repo Rate:** The rate at which the central bank lends money to commercial banks against government securities. It's a key tool to manage liquidity.
    *   **Reverse Repo Rate:** The rate at which the central bank borrows money from commercial banks, absorbing excess liquidity.
    *   **Example:** A higher repo rate makes it more expensive for banks to borrow from the central bank, which can lead to higher lending rates for consumers and businesses.

**Learning Outcome:** Outline the macroeconomic principles of monetary and fiscal systems, national income and stock market. (CO3, K2)
**Learning Outcome:** Develop decision making capability by applying concepts relating to costs and revenue, and acquire knowledge regarding the functioning of firms in different market situations. (CO2, K3) - Understanding monetary policy impacts interest rates, which in turn affect investment decisions and the cost of capital.

**Reference:**
*   "Managerial Economics" by Geetika, Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015) provides detailed explanations of monetary policy tools.
*   "Indian Financial System" by Khan M. Y. (2011) offers insights into specific policy rates used by the RBI.
*   "Engineering Economy" by R. Paneerselvam (2012) might discuss how interest rates, influenced by monetary policy, affect project feasibility analysis.

**Important Point to Remember:** Monetary policy actions have a lagged effect on the economy, meaning their full impact may not be felt for several months.

### Interest Rates and Their Significance

**Interest Rate:** The cost of borrowing money or the return on lending money, typically expressed as a percentage of the principal amount.

**Types of Interest Rates:**
*   **Nominal Interest Rate:** The stated interest rate, without accounting for inflation.
*   **Real Interest Rate:** The nominal interest rate minus the rate of inflation. This reflects the actual purchasing power gained or lost on an investment.
    *   **Formula:** Real Interest Rate ≈ Nominal Interest Rate - Inflation Rate
    *   **Example:** If the nominal interest rate on a loan is 8% and inflation is 3%, the real interest rate is approximately 5%. This means the borrower's purchasing power has effectively increased by 5% on the amount borrowed.

**Role of Interest Rates:**
*   **Cost of Capital:** For businesses and engineers, interest rates directly influence the cost of borrowing funds for investment in new projects, equipment, or research and development.
*   **Investment Decisions:** Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive, potentially leading to fewer investments. Lower interest rates encourage borrowing and investment.
*   **Saving and Consumption:** Higher interest rates incentivize saving, while lower rates may encourage spending.
*   **Inflation Control:** Central banks use interest rates to manage inflation. Raising rates cools down the economy and reduces inflationary pressures.

**Learning Outcome:** Develop decision making capability by applying concepts relating to costs and revenue, and acquire knowledge regarding the functioning of firms in different market situations. (CO2, K3)
**Learning Outcome:** Make use of the possibilities of value analysis and engineering, and solve simple business problems using break even analysis, cost benefit analysis and capital budgeting techniques. (CO4, K3) - Understanding interest rates is critical for capital budgeting techniques like Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR).

**Reference:**

**Important Point to Remember:** The real interest rate is a more accurate measure of the true cost or return of borrowing or lending.

### Banking System and Financial Intermediation

**Banks:** Financial institutions that accept deposits from the public and provide loans. They play a crucial role in the monetary system by facilitating the flow of funds between savers and borrowers.

**Financial Intermediation:** The process by which financial institutions act as intermediaries between those who have surplus funds (savers) and those who need funds (borrowers).

**Key Functions of Banks:**
*   **Accepting Deposits:** Providing safe places for individuals and businesses to store their money.
*   **Granting Loans:** Providing credit to individuals, businesses, and governments for various purposes, including project financing.
*   **Facilitating Payments:** Handling transactions through checks, electronic transfers, and other payment systems.
*   **Creating Money:** Through the process of lending and deposit creation (the money multiplier effect).

**Money Multiplier Effect:** The process by which a new deposit in a bank can lead to a larger increase in the overall money supply.
*   **Formula:** Money Multiplier = 1 / Reserve Requirement Ratio
*   **Example:** If the reserve requirement is 10% (0.1), the money multiplier is 1 / 0.1 = 10. An initial deposit of $1000 could theoretically lead to a total increase in the money supply of $10,000 (the initial deposit plus $9000 in new loans).

**Learning Outcome:** Outline the macroeconomic principles of monetary and fiscal systems, national income and stock market. (CO3, K2)
**Learning Outcome:** Develop decision making capability by applying concepts relating to costs and revenue, and acquire knowledge regarding the functioning of firms in different market situations. (CO2, K3) - Understanding how banks create money and influence credit availability is important for business planning.

**Reference:**
*   "Managerial Economics" by Geetika, Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015) explains the role of banks and the money multiplier.
*   "Indian Financial System" by Khan M. Y. (2011) provides detailed information on the banking sector in India.

**Important Point to Remember:** The actual money multiplier is often smaller than the theoretical maximum due to factors like banks holding excess reserves and individuals holding cash.

### Financial Markets

Financial markets are where financial instruments are traded. They are essential for the efficient allocation of capital in an economy.

**Types of Financial Markets:**

1.  **Money Markets:** Markets for short-term debt instruments (maturity of less than one year).
    *   **Instruments:** Treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit.
    *   **Purpose:** To facilitate short-term borrowing and lending, and to manage liquidity.
    *   **Example:** A company might issue commercial paper to finance its short-term working capital needs.

2.  **Capital Markets:** Markets for long-term debt and equity instruments (maturity of one year or more).
    *   **Stock Markets (Equity Markets):** Where ownership shares (stocks) of companies are bought and sold.
        *   **Purpose:** To raise capital for companies and provide investment opportunities for individuals and institutions.
        *   **Example:** Buying shares of a manufacturing company on a stock exchange to potentially benefit from its growth and dividends.
    *   **Bond Markets (Debt Markets):** Where debt securities (bonds) are issued and traded.
        *   **Purpose:** To allow governments and corporations to borrow large sums of money from investors for long-term projects.
        *   **Example:** A government issuing bonds to finance infrastructure development, such as building a new highway.

3.  **Foreign Exchange Markets (Forex):** Markets where currencies are traded.
    *   **Purpose:** To facilitate international trade and investment by allowing for the conversion of one currency to another.
    *   **Example:** An engineering firm importing specialized equipment from another country will need to buy the foreign currency to pay for it.

**Learning Outcome:** Develop decision making capability by applying concepts relating to costs and revenue, and acquire knowledge regarding the functioning of firms in different market situations. ### Practice Questions and Exercises

**Question 1 (K2 - CO1):**
Define money and explain its three primary functions. Provide an example of each function relevant to an engineering project.

**Answer:**
*   **Money:** Anything generally accepted as a medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value.
*   **Functions:**
    1.  **Medium of Exchange:** Money allows for the purchase of raw materials (e.g., steel, concrete) for a construction project without direct barter.
    2.  **Unit of Account:** The cost of different engineering services (e.g., design, surveying) are expressed in a common currency, allowing for easy comparison and budgeting.
    3.  **Store of Value:** Savings accumulated from an engineering project can be held and used for future investments in advanced machinery.

**Question 2 (K2 - CO3):**
Explain how open market operations can be used by a central bank to influence the money supply.

**Answer:**
Open Market Operations (OMO) involve the central bank buying or selling government securities.
*   **To increase money supply:** The central bank *buys* securities from the public, injecting money into the economy.
*   **To decrease money supply:** The central bank *sells* securities to the public, withdrawing money from circulation.

**Question 3 (K3 - CO2, CO4):**
A manufacturing company is considering investing in new automated machinery that costs $500,000. The company plans to finance this investment partly through a bank loan. If the central bank increases the policy rate, how might this affect the company's decision to invest in the machinery? Explain the concept of the real interest rate in this context.

**Answer:**
An increase in the policy rate by the central bank typically leads to higher interest rates for commercial banks, which in turn will likely increase the interest rate on loans for the manufacturing company. This makes borrowing more expensive, increasing the cost of capital for the machinery. Consequently, the company might find the investment less attractive, potentially delaying or abandoning the project if the expected returns no longer justify the higher borrowing costs.

The **real interest rate** is the nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate. If the nominal interest rate on the loan rises, but inflation also rises significantly, the real cost of borrowing might not increase as much. Conversely, if inflation falls while the nominal rate stays the same or rises, the real interest rate increases, making the loan more costly in real terms. For investment decisions, the real interest rate is more relevant as it reflects the actual increase in purchasing power the company must repay.

**Question 4 (K2 - CO3):**
What are M1 and M2, and what is the primary difference between them?

**Answer:**
*   **M1:** Refers to the most liquid forms of money, including currency in circulation and demand deposits (checking accounts).
*   **M2:** Is a broader measure that includes M1 plus less liquid assets like savings deposits, small-denomination time deposits, and retail money market mutual fund balances.
*   **Primary Difference:** M2 includes assets that are not directly usable as a medium of exchange but can be readily converted into cash, making them "near money."

### Key Points to Remember

*   The monetary system is the foundation for financial transactions and economic activity.
*   Money's three functions (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value) are vital for efficient economic operation.
*   Central banks use monetary policy tools (OMO, reserve requirements, discount rate) to manage the money supply and influence economic outcomes like inflation and growth.
*   Interest rates are the cost of money and are crucial for investment decisions, capital budgeting, and understanding the time value of money in engineering projects.
*   Financial markets facilitate the flow of capital from savers to borrowers, supporting investment and economic development.
*   Understanding the monetary system is essential for engineers to make informed financial and economic decisions related to their projects and businesses.
