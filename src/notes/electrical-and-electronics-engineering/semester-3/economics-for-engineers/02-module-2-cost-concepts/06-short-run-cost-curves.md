# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts

## Topic: Short Run Cost Curves

---

### **Introduction to Short Run Costs**

In the short run, a firm operates with at least one factor of production that is fixed. This means the firm cannot change its scale of operations or its plant size. All other factors are variable. The costs incurred by a firm in the short run are known as short-run costs. These costs are crucial for understanding a firm's production decisions and profitability within a limited timeframe.

**Key Concept:** The distinction between fixed and variable factors of production defines the short run.

---

### **Total Cost (TC) in the Short Run**

Total Cost is the sum of all costs incurred by a firm in the short run to produce a given level of output. It can be broken down into two components:

*   **Total Fixed Cost (TFC):** Costs that do not vary with the level of output. These are incurred even if the firm produces zero output.
    *   **Examples:** Rent of factory building, salaries of permanent staff, insurance premiums, depreciation of machinery.
    *   **Characteristic:** TFC remains constant as output changes in the short run.
    *   *Refer to:* Managerial Economics by Geetika, Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015) for detailed discussion on fixed costs.

*   **Total Variable Cost (TVC):** Costs that vary directly with the level of output. These costs are zero when output is zero.
    *   **Examples:** Cost of raw materials, wages of casual labor, electricity consumed in production, fuel.
    *   **Characteristic:** TVC increases as output increases.
    *   *Refer to:* Engineering Economy by R. Paneerselvam (2012) for how variable costs relate to production processes.

**Formula:** $TC = TFC + TVC$

---

### **Short Run Cost Curves**

The behavior of TFC, TVC, and TC with respect to output can be graphically represented by their respective cost curves.

#### 1. Total Fixed Cost (TFC) Curve

*   **Shape:** A horizontal straight line.
*   **Explanation:** Since TFC is constant irrespective of output, the TFC curve is parallel to the output (X-axis).
*   *Example:* If a factory's rent is $1000 per month, TFC will be $1000 whether it produces 10 units or 100 units.

#### 2. Total Variable Cost (TVC) Curve

*   **Shape:** Initially, it rises at a decreasing rate, then at an increasing rate. It starts from the origin (zero output, zero TVC).
*   **Explanation:**
    *   **Increasing Returns (Decreasing Marginal Cost):** In the initial stages of production, as more variable factors are employed, output increases proportionally more than the increase in variable inputs. This leads to TVC rising at a decreasing rate.
    *   **Diminishing Returns (Increasing Marginal Cost):** Beyond a certain point, due to the law of diminishing marginal returns, employing more variable factors leads to smaller and smaller increases in output. This causes TVC to rise at an increasing rate.
*   *Refer to:* Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen and W. J. Fabrycky (1966) for the relationship between input and output and its cost implications.

#### 3. Total Cost (TC) Curve

*   **Shape:** Similar to the TVC curve, but it starts from the level of TFC on the Y-axis. It also rises at a decreasing rate initially and then at an increasing rate.
*   **Explanation:** Since TC is the sum of TFC and TVC, and TFC is a constant, the TC curve is essentially the TVC curve shifted upwards by the amount of TFC. The vertical distance between the TC curve and the TVC curve is constant and equal to TFC.

---

### **Per Unit Cost Concepts in the Short Run**

These are derived by dividing the total cost concepts by the quantity of output produced.

#### 1. Average Fixed Cost (AFC)

*   **Definition:** Total Fixed Cost divided by the output.
    *   **Formula:** $AFC = TFC / Q$
*   **Shape:** A rectangular hyperbola.
*   **Explanation:** As output (Q) increases, AFC continuously falls. The AFC curve never touches the X-axis, as TFC is always positive. The area under the AFC curve for any output level represents TFC.
*   *Example:* If TFC = $1000, then at Q=10, AFC=$100; at Q=20, AFC=$50; at Q=100, AFC=$10.

#### 2. Average Variable Cost (AVC)

*   **Definition:** Total Variable Cost divided by the output.
    *   **Formula:** $AVC = TVC / Q$
*   **Shape:** U-shaped.
*   **Explanation:**
    *   Initially, AVC falls due to increasing returns to the variable factor, meaning output increases faster than the increase in variable costs.
    *   It reaches a minimum point and then starts rising due to diminishing returns, where output increases at a slower rate than the increase in variable costs.
*   *Refer to:* Managerial Economics by Geetika, Ghosh, and Chodhury (2015) for the concept of U-shaped average cost curves.

#### 3. Average Total Cost (ATC) or Average Cost (AC)

*   **Definition:** Total Cost divided by the output, or the sum of AFC and AVC.
    *   **Formula:** $ATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC$
*   **Shape:** U-shaped.
*   **Explanation:**
    *   The ATC curve is also U-shaped because it is influenced by both the falling AFC and the initially falling then rising AVC.
    *   The minimum point of the ATC curve occurs at a higher output level than the minimum point of the AVC curve.
    *   The vertical distance between the ATC and AVC curves is AFC, which continuously decreases.
*   *Refer to:* Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park (2001) for the application of average cost concepts in engineering decisions.

#### 4. Marginal Cost (MC)

*   **Definition:** The change in Total Cost (or Total Variable Cost) resulting from producing one additional unit of output.
    *   **Formula:** $MC = \Delta TC / \Delta Q$ or $MC = \Delta TVC / \Delta Q$ (since TFC does not change)
*   **Shape:** U-shaped.
*   **Explanation:**
    *   MC initially falls as output increases because of increasing returns. The cost of producing an additional unit becomes lower.
    *   It reaches a minimum point and then rises due to diminishing returns. Producing an additional unit becomes more expensive.
*   **Relationship with TVC:** The MC curve is the slope of the TVC curve.
*   **Relationship with AVC and ATC:**
    *   MC intersects AVC at its minimum point.
    *   MC intersects ATC at its minimum point.
    *   When MC is below AVC or ATC, AVC and ATC are falling.
    *   When MC is above AVC or ATC, AVC and ATC are rising.

---

### **Interrelationship of Short Run Cost Curves**

The relationships between these curves are fundamental to understanding cost behavior:

*   **AFC always falls.**
*   **AVC falls initially, reaches a minimum, and then rises.**
*   **ATC falls initially, reaches a minimum, and then rises.**
*   **MC falls initially, reaches a minimum, and then rises.**
*   **MC intersects AVC at its minimum point.**
*   **MC intersects ATC at its minimum point.**
*   **The vertical distance between ATC and AVC is AFC, which decreases as output increases.**

**Important Point to Remember:** The U-shape of AVC, ATC, and MC curves is a consequence of the Law of Variable Proportions (Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns).

### **Illustrative Example**

Consider a firm that produces chairs. The fixed cost (rent, salaries) is $200 per day.

| Output (Q) | TFC ($) | TVC ($) | TC ($) | AFC ($) | AVC ($) | ATC ($) | MC ($) |
| :--------- | :------ | :------ | :----- | :------ | :------ | :------ | :----- |
| 0          | 200     | 0       | 200    | -       | -       | -       | -      |
| 1          | 200     | 100     | 300    | 200     | 100     | 300     | 100    |
| 2          | 200     | 180     | 380    | 100     | 90      | 190     | 80     |
| 3          | 200     | 240     | 440    | 66.67   | 80      | 146.67  | 60     |
| 4          | 200     | 320     | 520    | 50      | 80      | 130     | 80     |
| 5          | 200     | 450     | 650    | 40      | 90      | 130     | 130    |
| 6          | 200     | 660     | 860    | 33.33   | 110     | 143.33  | 210    |

**Observations from the table:**

*   TFC is constant at $200.
*   TVC increases with output.
*   AFC continuously falls.
*   AVC falls initially (up to output 3) and then rises.
*   ATC falls initially (up to output 4) and then rises.
*   MC falls initially (up to output 3) and then rises.
*   MC intersects AVC at output 4 (both $80).
*   MC intersects ATC at output 4 (both $130).

### **Answers to Practice Questions**

1.  **Definitions:**
    *   **Total Fixed Cost (TFC):** Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run.
    *   **Total Variable Cost (TVC):** Costs that change directly with the level of output in the short run.
    *   **Average Variable Cost (AVC):** Total Variable Cost divided by the total output ($AVC = TVC / Q$).
    *   **Marginal Cost (MC):** The addition to total cost resulting from the production of one more unit of output ($MC = \Delta TC / \Delta Q$).

2.  **Explanation of U-shaped Curves:**
    The U-shape of AVC, ATC, and MC curves is primarily due to the **Law of Variable Proportions** (or Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns).
    *   **Initial Falling Phase:** In the early stages of production, as variable factors are added, marginal productivity of the variable factor increases. This means output rises faster than costs, leading to falling average and marginal costs.
    *   **Minimum Point:** At a certain output level, the efficiency of variable factors reaches its peak.
    *   **Rising Phase:** Beyond this point, the Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns sets in. Each additional unit of the variable factor contributes less to output than the previous one. This means more variable input is required for each additional unit of output, causing average and marginal costs to rise.
    *   **AFC's Influence:** ATC is the sum of AFC (which always falls) and AVC (which is U-shaped). Initially, the falling AFC dominates, pulling ATC down. As output increases, the rising AVC becomes more significant, eventually causing ATC to rise.

3.  **Calculations:**
    *   TFC = Total Cost at 0 output = $500.
    *   At an output of 10 units:
        *   TC = $1500 (assuming the $1500 is the total cost at 10 units, based on the problem's structure, or if it were implied that the $1000 was TVC at 10 units, then TC would be $500 + $1000 = $1500)
        *   TVC = TC - TFC = $1500 - $500 = $1000
        *   AVC = TVC / Q = $1000 / 10 = $100 per unit.

4.  **Calculations:**

    | Output (Q) | Total Cost (TC) ($) | TFC ($) | TVC ($) | AFC ($) | AVC ($) | ATC ($) | MC ($) |
    | :--------- | :------------------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :------ | :----- |
    | 0          | 100                 | 100     | 0       | -       | -       | -       | -      |
    | 1          | 180                 | 100     | 80      | 100     | 80      | 180     | 80     |
    | 2          | 240                 | 100     | 140     | 50      | 70      | 120     | 60     |
    | 3          | 280                 | 100     | 180     | 33.33   | 60      | 93.33   | 40     |
    | 4          | 330                 | 100     | 230     | 25      | 57.5    | 82.5    | 50     |
    | 5          | 400                 | 100     | 300     | 20      | 60      | 80      | 70     |

    *   **TFC:** $100 (Cost at zero output).
    *   **TVC:** Calculated as TC - TFC.
    *   **AFC:** Calculated as TFC / Q.
    *   **AVC:** Calculated as TVC / Q.
    *   **ATC:** Calculated as TC / Q (or AFC + AVC).
    *   **MC:** Calculated as the change in TC divided by the change in Q. For example, MC for the 2nd unit is (240 - 180) / (2 - 1) = 60.

### **Key Takeaways for Engineers**

*   **Cost Minimization:** Understanding cost curves helps engineers identify the output levels where average costs are minimized, leading to more efficient production.
*   **Decision Making:** Knowledge of short-run costs is vital for making informed decisions about production volume, pricing, and resource allocation within the constraints of existing plant capacity.
*   **Profitability Analysis:** By combining cost information with revenue information, engineers can analyze the profitability of different production scenarios.
*   **Impact of Fixed Costs:** Engineers often work with plant capacity and equipment, which represent significant fixed costs. Understanding how these costs are spread over production is crucial.
