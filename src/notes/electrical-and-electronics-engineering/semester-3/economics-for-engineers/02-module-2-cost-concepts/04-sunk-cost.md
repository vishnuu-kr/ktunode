# ECONOMICS FOR ENGINEERS - Module 2: Cost Concepts

## Topic: Sunk Cost

### Introduction

In the realm of engineering and business decision-making, understanding different types of costs is crucial for making rational choices. One such cost concept that often influences decisions, sometimes incorrectly, is the **sunk cost**. This topic delves into the nature of sunk costs, their implications for decision-making, and how engineers should approach them.

### 1. What is a Sunk Cost?

A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of future decisions or actions. It is a past expenditure that is irrelevant to future choices.

**Key Characteristics:**

*   **Irrecoverable:** Once spent, the money or resources are gone.
*   **Past Expenditure:** It relates to past commitments, not future ones.
*   **Irrelevant to Future Decisions:** Because they cannot be changed, sunk costs should not influence decisions about the future.

**Definitions from Textbooks:**

*   **Managerial Economics by Geetika, Piyali Ghosh and Chodhury (2015):** While not explicitly defining "sunk cost" as a standalone term, the book emphasizes the importance of distinguishing between relevant and irrelevant costs in decision-making. Sunk costs fall under the category of irrelevant costs.
*   **Engineering Economy by H. G. Thuesen, W. J. Fabrycky (1966):** This classic text, foundational to engineering economics, implicitly discusses sunk costs by highlighting that past costs do not influence future economic choices. The focus is on incremental costs and benefits.
*   **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam (2012):** Defines sunk costs as "costs already incurred and which cannot be recovered." The book stresses that these costs should be ignored when making future decisions.

### 2. Why are Sunk Costs Important?

The importance of sunk costs lies in their potential to **mislead decision-makers**. This is often referred to as the "sunk cost fallacy" or "escalation of commitment."

**The Sunk Cost Fallacy:**

This fallacy occurs when individuals or organizations continue to invest in a project or venture solely because they have already invested significant resources (time, money, effort) into it, even when future prospects are bleak. The emotional attachment to past investments can override rational evaluation of future costs and benefits.

**Examples:**

*   **A student who has spent three years pursuing a degree in a field they no longer enjoy.** Despite the significant time and tuition already spent, if the student realizes they would be happier and more successful in a different field, continuing with the original degree solely due to the past investment would be succumbing to the sunk cost fallacy. The correct decision is to switch majors if the future benefits of switching outweigh the future costs.
*   **A company that has invested heavily in developing a new product that is proving to be unpopular in market testing.** If the company continues to pour money into marketing and production simply because of the R&D costs already incurred, rather than cutting their losses and developing a different product, they are falling prey to the sunk cost fallacy.
*   **Continuing to repair an old car that frequently breaks down.** The money spent on previous repairs is a sunk cost. The decision to keep repairing it should be based on the future repair costs versus the cost of buying a new car, not on the money already spent on past repairs.

### 3. Sunk Costs vs. Other Cost Concepts

It's essential to differentiate sunk costs from other relevant cost concepts to ensure proper decision-making.

*   **Sunk Cost vs. Opportunity Cost:**
    *   **Sunk Cost:** A past, irrecoverable expenditure.
    *   **Opportunity Cost:** The value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. This is a forward-looking cost and is highly relevant to decision-making. (Refer to **Managerial Economics by Geetika et al.** for a detailed discussion on opportunity costs).

*   **Sunk Cost vs. Relevant Cost (Incremental Cost):**
    *   **Sunk Cost:** Irrelevant to future decisions.
    *   **Relevant Cost:** A cost that differs between alternative courses of action. These are the costs that *should* be considered in decision-making. (Refer to **Engineering Economics by R. Paneerselvam** for emphasis on incremental costs).

*   **Sunk Cost vs. Fixed Cost:**
    *   **Sunk Cost:** Can include fixed costs that have already been incurred.
    *   **Fixed Cost:** A cost that does not change with the level of output. While some fixed costs can become sunk costs (e.g., depreciation on a machine already purchased), not all fixed costs are sunk costs in the context of future decisions. For instance, future fixed costs for a project are relevant.

### 4. Sunk Costs in Engineering Decision-Making

Engineers are often involved in evaluating projects, making investment decisions, and managing resources. Properly accounting for sunk costs is critical for economic efficiency and rational project management.

**How to Handle Sunk Costs:**

1.  **Identify:** Recognize which costs are truly sunk – those that have been incurred and cannot be recovered.
2.  **Ignore:** Mentally (or practically) set aside sunk costs when evaluating future options.
3.  **Focus on Future Costs and Benefits:** Base decisions on the incremental costs and benefits that will arise from each potential course of action.

**Implications for Project Evaluation:**

*   **Project Continuation:** If a project is already underway and has incurred sunk costs, the decision to continue or abandon it should be based on whether the *future* benefits of continuing outweigh the *future* costs. The initial investment (sunk cost) is irrelevant to this decision.
*   **New Project Selection:** When choosing between new projects, sunk costs from previous, unrelated projects should have no bearing on the selection.
*   **Technology Adoption:** A company that has invested heavily in a particular technology should not hesitate to adopt a superior, newer technology if the future economic benefits of the new technology (considering all future costs and benefits) are greater, even if it means abandoning the old technology and its associated sunk costs. (This aligns with the principles of **value analysis and engineering** discussed in **CO4**).

**Reference:**

*   **Contemporary Engineering Economics by Chan S. Park (2001)** emphasizes the importance of forward-looking analysis in engineering economics, where sunk costs are explicitly excluded from consideration in evaluating alternatives.
*   **Engineering Economy by Leland Blank P.E, Anthony Tarquin P. E. (7TH Edition)** provides practical examples and frameworks for decision-making that consistently highlight the irrelevance of sunk costs.

### 5. Examples in Engineering Context

*   **Scenario 1: A Construction Project**
    *   A company has spent \$1 million on land and initial site preparation for a new office building. Due to unexpected economic downturns, the cost of construction materials has doubled, making the project significantly less profitable than initially anticipated.
    *   **Sunk Cost:** The \$1 million already spent on land and site preparation.
    *   **Decision:** The company needs to decide whether to proceed with the construction. The decision should not be influenced by the \$1 million already spent. Instead, they must compare the *future* costs (remaining construction, financing, maintenance) with the *future* revenues and benefits. If the future costs outweigh the future benefits, the project should be abandoned, regardless of the initial \$1 million investment.

*   **Scenario 2: Software Development**
    *   An engineering firm has invested 5,000 man-hours in developing custom software for internal use. During the final testing phase, it becomes apparent that a commercially available software package offers superior features and functionality at a lower total cost of ownership (including implementation and training).
    *   **Sunk Cost:** The 5,000 man-hours of development time (representing labor costs).
    *   **Decision:** Should the firm proceed with its custom software or purchase the commercial package? The decision should be based on a comparison of the *future* costs and benefits of each option. The 5,000 man-hours are gone and cannot be recovered. The firm should choose the option that provides the greatest net future economic benefit.

### 6. Practice Questions

**Question 1:**
Define sunk cost and explain why it is irrelevant in decision-making.

**Question 2:**
A company invested \$500,000 in research and development for a new gadget. After extensive market research, it was found that consumer demand for the gadget is very low. The company is considering whether to proceed with production. The remaining production costs will be \$200,000, and the estimated revenue is \$150,000.
*   Identify the sunk cost in this scenario.
*   Should the company proceed with production based on economic principles? Explain your reasoning.

**Question 3:**
Differentiate between a sunk cost and an opportunity cost. Provide an example for each.

**Question 4:**
An engineer has spent two years designing a new type of bridge. The design is innovative but extremely complex and expensive to build. A new material has become available that would allow for a simpler, more cost-effective design for a similar bridge, offering comparable performance. The engineer is reluctant to abandon the current design because of the time already invested. Is this a rational approach? Explain your answer referencing the concept of sunk cost.

### 7. Answers to Practice Questions

**Answer 1:**
A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of future decisions or actions. It is irrelevant in decision-making because it represents a past expenditure that cannot be altered by any future choice. Decisions should be based on future costs and benefits, not on past, unrecoverable expenses.

**Answer 2:**
*   **Sunk Cost:** The \$500,000 invested in research and development.
*   **Decision:** No, the company should not proceed with production.
    *   **Reasoning:** The \$500,000 is a sunk cost and should not influence the decision. The decision to proceed or not should be based on comparing the *future* costs and *future* revenues.
    *   Future Costs = \$200,000
    *   Future Revenue = \$150,000
    *   Net Future Outcome = \$150,000 (Revenue) - \$200,000 (Costs) = -\$50,000.
    *   Since the future outcome is negative (a loss of \$50,000), the company should abandon production to avoid further losses.

**Answer 3:**
*   **Sunk Cost:** A cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. Example: Money spent on purchasing a specific piece of equipment that is now obsolete and cannot be sold.
*   **Opportunity Cost:** The value of the best alternative forgone when a choice is made. Example: If an engineer chooses to work on Project A, the opportunity cost is the salary and experience they would have gained by working on Project B (the next best alternative).

**Answer 4:**
No, this is not a rational approach. The two years of design effort represent a sunk cost. The engineer is falling victim to the sunk cost fallacy. The decision to continue with the complex design or switch to the simpler, more cost-effective design using the new material should be based on a comparison of the *future* costs, benefits, and risks associated with each design. If the new material and simpler design offer superior future economic benefits (lower construction cost, comparable performance, etc.), the engineer should embrace it, irrespective of the time already invested in the original design. This aligns with **CO4**'s emphasis on value analysis and engineering.

### 8. Important Points to Remember

*   **Sunk costs are past and irrecoverable.**
*   **They should never influence future decisions.**
*   **Recognizing and ignoring sunk costs is crucial for rational economic decision-making.**
*   **The sunk cost fallacy (escalation of commitment) is a common behavioral bias to guard against.**
*   **Focus on incremental costs and benefits when evaluating alternatives.**
