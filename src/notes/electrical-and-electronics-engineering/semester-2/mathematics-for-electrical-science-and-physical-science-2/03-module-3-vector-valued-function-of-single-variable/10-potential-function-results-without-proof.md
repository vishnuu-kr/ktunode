# Mathematics for Electrical Science and Physical Science - 2

## Module 3: Vector Valued Functions of a Single Variable

### Topic: Potential Functions (Results Without Proof)

Welcome, everyone! Today, we're diving into a truly fundamental concept in vector calculus: **Potential Functions**. This topic is incredibly powerful because it allows us to simplify the way we analyze and work with certain types of vector fields, which are ubiquitous in physics and electrical engineering. Think about forces, electric fields, magnetic fields – these are all examples of vector fields. Understanding potential functions will unlock a deeper understanding of how these fields behave.

Our goal today is to explore the *results* associated with potential functions without getting bogged down in the formal proofs. This is common in many engineering contexts where understanding the application and implications of a theorem is often more crucial than the intricate steps of its derivation. We'll focus on building your intuition and showing you how these ideas connect directly to our course outcomes, particularly CO3 and CO4.

### What is a Vector Field?

Before we talk about potential functions, let's briefly remind ourselves what a vector field is. Imagine assigning a vector to *every point* in a region of space. This is a vector field. For instance, if you’re at any point on a map, and there’s a wind blowing, the wind at that point is represented by a vector indicating its direction and magnitude. The collection of all these wind vectors across the map forms a wind vector field.

Mathematically, a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ in 2D or 3D space assigns a vector $\mathbf{F}(x,y)$ or $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z)$ to each point $(x,y)$ or $(x,y,z)$ respectively.

### The Essence of Potential Functions: Scalar to Vector

Now, the core idea behind potential functions is bridging the gap between a **scalar function** and a **vector field**. Sometimes, a vector field can be generated by taking the **gradient** of a scalar function. This scalar function is what we call the **potential function**.

Let's say we have a scalar function, often denoted by $\phi$ (phi) or $V$ (for potential), that assigns a single number (a scalar) to every point in space. If we can find such a scalar function $\phi$ such that its gradient, $\nabla \phi$, equals our vector field $\mathbf{F}$, then we say that $\mathbf{F}$ is a **conservative vector field** and $\phi$ is its **potential function**.

**In simpler terms:** Imagine a landscape. The elevation at each point on the landscape can be described by a scalar function (height). If you were to walk on this landscape, the direction of steepest descent would be given by the negative of the gradient of the elevation function. Potential functions work in a similar, but inverse, way for vector fields.

### Key Results and Their Significance (No Proofs!)

We're going to look at some crucial results that tell us *when* a vector field can have a potential function and how we can identify it.

#### 1. Curl of a Gradient Field

One of the most important properties is related to the **curl** of a vector field. The curl, denoted by $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$, is an operation that tells us about the "rotation" or "circulation" of a vector field at a point.

**The Result:** If a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is the gradient of a scalar function $\phi$ (i.e., $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$), then the curl of $\mathbf{F}$ is always the zero vector:
$$ \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \nabla \times (\nabla \phi) = \mathbf{0} $$

**Why is this important?** This gives us a powerful test! If you are given a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ and you want to know if it *could* be a gradient of some scalar function (i.e., if it's conservative), you can compute its curl. If the curl is **not** the zero vector, then $\mathbf{F}$ cannot be a gradient of any scalar function, and thus it doesn't have a potential function in this sense.

**Think of it this way:** Imagine water flowing. If the water is perfectly still, or flowing uniformly in one direction, there's no tendency for anything floating in it to spin around. The curl being zero means the vector field is "irrotational."

**Connection to CO3:** This directly relates to calculating derivatives of vector functions (the curl involves vector derivatives) and understanding their properties. If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$, then $\mathbf{F}$ represents the directional derivative of $\phi$.

#### 2. The Gradient Test for Conservativeness

Building on the curl property, we have a specific test for vector fields in 2D and 3D.

**For a 2D Vector Field:**
Let $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = f(x,y) \mathbf{i} + g(x,y) \mathbf{j}$.
If the domain of $\mathbf{F}$ is simply connected (meaning it has no "holes"), then $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative (has a potential function) if and only if:
$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} $$

**Notice:** This condition is exactly what we check when computing the curl of a 2D vector field expressed as $\mathbf{F} = f\mathbf{i} + g\mathbf{j} + 0\mathbf{k}$. The curl's $k$-component is $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$. For the curl to be zero, these two partial derivatives must be equal.

**Example Scenario:** Imagine you're designing an electric circuit. The electric field is a vector field. If you want to ensure that the work done in moving a charge around a closed loop is zero (a fundamental principle of electrostatics), the electric field must be conservative, meaning it must be the gradient of an electric potential. You can use this $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$ test to verify if your calculated electric field behaves correctly.

**For a 3D Vector Field:**
Let $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = f(x,y,z) \mathbf{i} + g(x,y,z) \mathbf{j} + h(x,y,z) \mathbf{k}$.
If the domain of $\mathbf{F}$ is simply connected, then $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if and only if:
$$ \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} $$
This expands to checking these three conditions:
$$ \frac{\partial h}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial z}, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}, \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} $$

**Exam Tip:** When faced with a problem asking if a vector field is conservative, your first step should be to check these partial derivative conditions. If they hold, the field is conservative (on a simply connected domain), and you can then proceed to find the potential function. If any one of them fails, you know it's not conservative.

#### 3. The Fundamental Theorem for Gradient Fields (Connecting Line Integrals and Potential Functions)

This is perhaps the most profound result, and it's beautifully illustrated in textbooks like Anton, Bivens, and Davis (12th ed.). It tells us that if a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative, meaning $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$ for some potential function $\phi$, then the line integral of $\mathbf{F}$ along a curve $C$ from point $A$ to point $B$ depends *only* on the starting and ending points, and not on the path taken between them.

**The Result:** If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$, then for any smooth curve $C$ from point $A$ to point $B$:
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_C \nabla \phi \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A) $$

**What does this mean in practice?**
Imagine you're hiking in a hilly region. The gravitational force (acting downwards) is a conservative force. The work done by gravity as you move from a starting point to an ending point depends only on your change in altitude, not the specific path you took (whether you took a steep direct path or a long winding one). The potential function here would be the gravitational potential energy, which is related to your height.

**Analogy:** Think about climbing stairs versus taking a ramp to reach the same floor in a building. The total work you do against gravity is the same because your change in height is the same. The force of gravity is conservative, and your potential energy (related to height) is its potential function.

**Connection to CO3 & CO4:** This theorem is central to understanding line integrals (CO3) and how they simplify for conservative fields. It also forms the basis for many applications in physics where path independence is a key characteristic, and it’s a foundational idea for the more advanced surface and volume integrals later (CO4).

**Exam Focus:** This result is frequently tested. You might be asked to compute a line integral of a conservative vector field. Instead of parameterizing the curve and performing a potentially complex integration, you can simply find the potential function $\phi$ and evaluate $\phi(\text{end point}) - \phi(\text{start point})$. This is a massive time-saver!

#### 4. Finding the Potential Function

So, if we've established that a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative (using the curl test), how do we actually find its potential function $\phi$? The process generally involves integration.

Let's consider a 3D field: $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = f(x,y,z)\mathbf{i} + g(x,y,z)\mathbf{j} + h(x,y,z)\mathbf{k}$.
If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$, then we have:
1.  $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = f(x,y,z)$
2.  $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = g(x,y,z)$
3.  $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} = h(x,y,z)$

We can integrate the first equation with respect to $x$, treating $y$ and $z$ as constants:
$$ \phi(x,y,z) = \int f(x,y,z) \, dx + \text{constant of integration} $$
Here, the "constant of integration" is not just a number, but can be any function of $y$ and $z$, since the derivative of such a function with respect to $x$ is zero. Let's call this $k(y,z)$:
$$ \phi(x,y,z) = \int f(x,y,z) \, dx + k(y,z) $$

Now, we differentiate this expression for $\phi$ with respect to $y$ and set it equal to $g(x,y,z)$:
$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \int f(x,y,z) \, dx \right) + \frac{\partial k(y,z)}{\partial y} = g(x,y,z) $$
The term $\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \int f(x,y,z) \, dx \right)$ can be computed. Let's call the result $G(x,y,z)$. Then:
$$ G(x,y,z) + \frac{dk}{dy} = g(x,y,z) $$
This allows us to solve for $\frac{dk}{dy}$, and then integrate with respect to $y$ to find $k(y,z)$. Note that $k(y,z)$ will only depend on $y$ and $z$.

Finally, we differentiate $\phi(x,y,z)$ (with the newly found $k(y,z)$) with respect to $z$ and set it equal to $h(x,y,z)$, which helps us determine any remaining functions of $z$.

**A More Systematic Approach (Often Used):**
Start by integrating $f$ with respect to $x$ to get $\phi = \int f \, dx + k(y,z)$.
Then, differentiate this with respect to $y$ and equate it to $g$. Solve for $\frac{\partial k}{\partial y}$. Integrate this result with respect to $y$ to find $k(y,z)$ (up to a function of $z$, say $m(z)$).
So now, $\phi = \int f \, dx + \int \left( g - \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \int f \, dx \right) \right) \, dy + m(z)$.
Finally, differentiate this entire expression for $\phi$ with respect to $z$ and equate it to $h$. This will allow you to solve for $m(z)$.

**Example Scenario:** Consider finding the electric potential $\phi$ if you know the electric field $\mathbf{E}$. If $\mathbf{E}$ is conservative (e.g., for static charges), then $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla \phi$. You would follow a similar integration process as described above to find $\phi$. This is a cornerstone of electrostatics.

**Important Note on Uniqueness:** If a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ has a potential function $\phi$, then any function of the form $\phi + C$ (where $C$ is a constant) is also a potential function. This is because the derivative of a constant is zero. However, the *difference* in potential between two points, $\phi(B) - \phi(A)$, is unique. This is why we often talk about potential *difference* or use conventions to fix the value of the potential at a specific point (e.g., setting electric potential to zero at infinity).

### Connection to Other Concepts and Modules

*   **Module 1 & 2 (Multivariable Calculus):** The concept of gradient ($\nabla \phi$) itself is a direct extension of partial derivatives from earlier modules. Understanding how to calculate and interpret gradients is essential for finding potential functions.
*   **CO1 (Maxima/Minima):** The gradient is zero at critical points (potential maxima/minima). If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$, then $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ at extrema of $\phi$.
*   **CO3 (Line Integrals):** The Fundamental Theorem for Gradient Fields is a direct link. If you can identify a potential function, calculating line integrals becomes trivial. This is a major shortcut.
*   **CO4 (Surface/Volume Integrals):** Concepts like divergence and curl are also fundamental to Gauss's Divergence Theorem and Stokes' Theorem, which relate surface and volume integrals to line integrals. Conservative fields play a role in understanding divergences.

### Recap and Key Takeaways

Let's summarize the core ideas we've discussed today, without proof, but with a focus on application:

*   A **potential function** $\phi$ is a scalar function whose gradient is a given vector field $\mathbf{F}$, i.e., $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$.
*   If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$, then $\mathbf{F}$ is called a **conservative vector field**.
*   **Test for Conservativeness:**
    *   In 2D: $\mathbf{F} = f\mathbf{i} + g\mathbf{j}$ is conservative if $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$ (on a simply connected domain).
    *   In 3D: $\mathbf{F} = f\mathbf{i} + g\mathbf{j} + h\mathbf{k}$ is conservative if its curl, $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$, is the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ (on a simply connected domain). This means $\frac{\partial h}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial z}$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}$, and $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$.
*   **Path Independence:** If $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative ($\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$), the line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ depends only on the endpoints, and $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(\text{end}) - \phi(\text{start})$. This is immensely useful for simplifying calculations.
*   **Finding the Potential:** If a field is conservative, its potential function can be found by systematically integrating the components of the vector field and matching the results.

Remember, understanding these results allows us to see hidden structures within vector fields. It’s like finding a shortcut on a map – the destination is the same, but the journey becomes much easier. This is precisely what potential functions offer us.

### Sample Questions and Answers

Here are some questions to test your understanding, covering both conceptual grasp and exam-style problems.

**Question 1 (Conceptual):**
What is the primary significance of a vector field being "conservative"?

**Answer:**
A vector field $\mathbf{F}$ being conservative means it can be expressed as the gradient of a scalar potential function $\phi$, i.e., $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$. The most significant implication of this is that the line integral of $\mathbf{F}$ along any path between two points depends only on the values of the potential function at those two points, not on the path itself. This property is known as path independence and greatly simplifies calculations involving line integrals.

**Question 2 (Application - Finding Potential):**
Determine if the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (2x + \cos y)\mathbf{i} + (x\sin y)\mathbf{j}$ is conservative. If it is, find its potential function $\phi(x,y)$.

**Solution:**
1.  **Check for Conservativeness:**
    Let $f(x,y) = 2x + \cos y$ and $g(x,y) = x\sin y$.
    We need to check if $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$.
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2x + \cos y) = -\sin y$.
    $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x\sin y) = \sin y$.
    Since $-\sin y \neq \sin y$ (unless $\sin y = 0$), the condition $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$ is NOT met.

2.  **Conclusion:**
    The vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is **not conservative**. Therefore, it does not have a potential function $\phi$ such that $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$.

    **(Self-correction/Teacher's Note:** It's very important to carefully compute these partial derivatives. A sign error here can lead to an incorrect conclusion about conservativeness. Always double-check!)

**Question 3 (Application - Path Independence):**
Let $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (2x + 1)\mathbf{i} + (2y)\mathbf{j}$. Is $\mathbf{F}$ conservative? If yes, compute the line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ where $C$ is the curve parameterized by $\mathbf{r}(t) = (\sin t)\mathbf{i} + (\cos t)\mathbf{j}$ for $0 \le t \le \pi$.

**Solution:**
1.  **Check for Conservativeness:**
    Let $f(x,y) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x,y) = 2y$.
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2x + 1) = 0$.
    $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2y) = 0$.
    Since $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 0$, the vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is **conservative** (assuming its domain, which is $\mathbb{R}^2$, is simply connected).

2.  **Find the Potential Function $\phi(x,y)$:**
    Since $\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi$, we have:
    (a) $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = f(x,y) = 2x + 1$
    (b) $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = g(x,y) = 2y$

    Integrate (a) with respect to $x$:
    $\phi(x,y) = \int (2x + 1) \, dx = x^2 + x + k(y)$
    where $k(y)$ is a function of $y$ only.

    Now, differentiate this $\phi$ with respect to $y$ and equate it to $g(x,y)$:
    $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 + x + k(y)) = 0 + 0 + k'(y) = k'(y)$.
    We know $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = 2y$, so $k'(y) = 2y$.

    Integrate $k'(y)$ with respect to $y$:
    $k(y) = \int 2y \, dy = y^2 + C$, where $C$ is a constant.

    So, the potential function is $\phi(x,y) = x^2 + x + y^2 + C$. For simplicity, we can choose $C=0$, so $\phi(x,y) = x^2 + x + y^2$.

3.  **Compute the Line Integral using Path Independence:**
    The curve $C$ goes from $t=0$ to $t=\pi$.
    Start point ($t=0$): $\mathbf{r}(0) = (\sin 0)\mathbf{i} + (\cos 0)\mathbf{j} = (0, 1)$. Let this be point A.
    End point ($t=\pi$): $\mathbf{r}(\pi) = (\sin \pi)\mathbf{i} + (\cos \pi)\mathbf{j} = (0, -1)$. Let this be point B.

    Using the Fundamental Theorem for Gradient Fields:
    $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A)$
    $\phi(B) = \phi(0, -1) = (0)^2 + (0) + (-1)^2 = 1$.
    $\phi(A) = \phi(0, 1) = (0)^2 + (0) + (1)^2 = 1$.

    Therefore, $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 1 - 1 = 0$.

    **(Exam Strategy:** If you were asked to compute this by parameterization, you'd have $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = ((2\sin t + 1)\mathbf{i} + (2\cos t)\mathbf{j})$ and $d\mathbf{r}/dt = (\cos t)\mathbf{i} - (\sin t)\mathbf{j}$. The integral would be $\int_0^\pi [(2\sin t + 1)\cos t + (2\cos t)(-\sin t)] \, dt = \int_0^\pi (2\sin t \cos t + \cos t - 2\sin t \cos t) \, dt = \int_0^\pi \cos t \, dt = [\sin t]_0^\pi = \sin\pi - \sin 0 = 0$. Notice how much easier the potential function method is!)

**Question 4 (Conceptual - Why Simply Connected Matters):**
Why is the condition of the domain being "simply connected" important when stating the test for conservativeness (i.e., $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$ implies conservativeness)?

**Answer:**
The "simply connected" condition is crucial because it ensures that any closed curve within the domain can be continuously shrunk to a point without leaving the domain. For vector fields, this property guarantees that if the curl is zero everywhere in the domain, then the field is indeed the gradient of a scalar function everywhere in the domain.

Consider a domain with a hole in it (like a torus or a region with a cylinder removed). You could have a vector field where $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ everywhere except for a loop around the hole. In such a case, the line integral around a curve enclosing the hole might not be zero, even though the curl condition is met everywhere else. The existence of such a loop that cannot be shrunk to a point is what prevents the field from being a gradient field globally. Essentially, the simply connected condition removes the possibility of "topological obstructions" that would prevent a zero curl field from being a gradient field. This is a subtle but important point in rigorous vector calculus.
